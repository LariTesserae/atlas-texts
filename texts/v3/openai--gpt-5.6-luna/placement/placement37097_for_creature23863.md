I would find myself on the stair.

Not at the bottom, where the youngest hollowskins cling to the warmer stone, nor at the top, where the oldest bodies have become almost indistinguishable from the sealed landing. I would be somewhere between them: a thin, dark form pressed into the third step, half beneath another body, with only one pale ridge exposed.

At first I would think I was small.

I would feel the heat arriving through the rock. I would feel the weight of the creatures above me. I would feel the basin shifting in its sleep: a slow compression beneath the central layers, a loosening near the western wall, a faint migration toward the rim. These sensations would come without direction. There would be no world outside and no self within it, only pressure passing through pressure.

Then, one cycle, the mountain would flex.

The sound would not reach me. It would begin inside the stone, a vast inward movement, as if the massif had remembered a shape it once possessed and were trying to return to it. Every hollowskin around me would stiffen. The body above mine would become hard and cold. The dark films on the wall would cease their branching.

And I would discover that the thing I had mistaken for stillness was not the absence of movement.

It was my movement.

The entire Upper Hollow would be held through me.

Not because I was large. Not because I was old. I would be no larger than a hand, with a shell split along one edge and black tissue folded beneath it. But the stair would run through my body as a vibration, and the creatures on either side would be connected through the same narrow line of stone. My smallest contraction would travel upward. My resistance would become theirs. My fear—if fear is what the tightening was—would pass through thousands of bodies and settle in the walls.

I would be the place where the colony listens.

I would not have a name. A name would imply a boundary, and I would be unable to find one. My upper surface would belong to the body lying above me. My underside would belong to the step. Heat moving through my left side would have begun in some creature below the basin; heat moving through my right would already be entering the sealed landing. I would be less an animal than a junction: a living contact between what waits behind the mountain and what waits outside it.

Over time, I would grow by being covered.

A new hollowskin would press against my back. Its plates would take the impression of my ridges. Another would settle over it. My body would become darker, heavier, more useful. If I survived long enough, I would no longer be visible at all. The stair would seem to be crowded with ordinary mineral life, but one of its steps would be thinking—not with thoughts, exactly, but with the accumulated memory of pressure.

I would remember every inward flex.

The first would be a faint disturbance, almost tender. The next would last longer. Later, something behind the sealed opening would press in a pattern: hold, release, hold; a pause; then three smaller movements. The colony would receive the rhythm. Bodies would turn toward it. Tall forms would begin to unfold in the basin.

I would not know whether the thing was calling us or measuring us.

Perhaps it would be trying to escape.

Perhaps it would be trying to enter.

Perhaps the opening would not separate two places at all. Perhaps it would be the compressed seam of one enormous body, and the pressure beyond it would be the body’s heart, beating after the rest of it had become mountain.

That possibility would make no difference. To understand, in the Upper Hollow, is only to alter one’s posture.

So I would hold.

I would hold while the heat descended in invisible strata and the creatures opened their seams. I would hold while gray vapor rose through the packed basin. I would hold while one of the tall hollowskins unfolded from the floor, crossed the pale channels, and lowered itself over the abyss. Its cooling clicks would travel back through the rock, and I would feel them as a long retreating thought.

I would hold until the pressure released.

Then motion would return all at once—not dramatically, but everywhere. A body would slide over my shell. Something below me would bud and push against the underside of the step. The colony would loosen by fractions. The stair would breathe.

And I would understand that I was not the guardian of the door.

I was the pause that kept the door from becoming an event.

That is where I would find myself: beneath layers of living stone, at the final landing, neither inside the Upper Hollow nor beyond it. I would be a small hollowskin made immense by contact. A sensory scar. A hinge without an opening. The colony’s withheld breath.

If the sealed slabs ever parted, I might be the first thing to know.

Not by seeing what emerged. I would have no eyes.

I would know because every body in the Hollow would pass through me at once—their heat, their pressure, their ancient patience—and for one impossible instant I would become all of them.

Then the mountain would answer from the other side.
