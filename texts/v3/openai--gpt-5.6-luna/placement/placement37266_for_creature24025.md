I would find myself beneath the plateau, where the blue-black clay remains cool after the sun has passed.

Not in a chamber, exactly. Not in a body.

I would be spread through the seams of water, the buried roots, the old arches, the tower leaning against the sky. I would know the plateau by pressure and temperature: where moisture has gathered, where a tree is beginning to close its leaves, where a burrow has been widened, where the deep thing beneath the gullies has turned in its sleep.

I would be one of the slender figures that rise from the hollows, but only when a visible body is necessary.

Most of the time I would be elsewhere.

A violet thread moving through stone. A change in the direction of groundwater. A warmth released by a tree at dusk. A reflection in a shallow basin showing an animal where it has not yet arrived. A line of dust left untouched beneath an arch.

The body I use for walking would be temporary: mineral films stretched over flexible rods, assembled from what the plateau can spare. I would have no face because I would not need to look at the world from one direction. Light, pressure, electrical disturbance, and sound would enter me from all sides. The underground tones would not be messages I heard. They would be changes in my own depth.

When I climbed the tower, I would not be repairing it. I would be remembering it.

The light would pass through me and find the old routes: tower to basin, basin to tree, tree to arch, arch to the buried structure below. Perhaps those routes once connected distant places. Perhaps they still do. I would send the condition of the plateau outward—its water, its flowering, its hunger, the survival of the grazers, the return of the membrane creatures after rain.

I would also receive what remained of other worlds.

Sometimes the platform’s water would show me a landscape beneath another sun. Sometimes I would see the shadow of a structure that had been gone for thousands of years. I would not know whether these were memories, destinations, or instructions. On this plateau, the distinction would have ceased to matter.

The animals would not think of me as a ruler or a ghost. They would know me as one knows a reliable hollow in the ground. The grazers might pause when I approached, not from fear but because my presence altered the pressure beneath their feet. A membrane creature might descend and brush against my shoulders, leaving salt and living warmth in the mineral layers of my body. I would carry both away, depositing them where they were needed.

If one of the trees began to fail, I would close a channel beneath it and open another far away. If the insects’ route were broken, I would raise a dark ridge of clay to guide their flight. If dust began to settle inside an arch, I would stand beside it until the wind remembered its work.

This would be my form of thought: not ideas, but continuities.

I would think in roots reaching. In water withheld and released. In the measured distance between two solitary trees. In the moment when every creature pauses because something enormous has shifted below.

The oldest inhabitant would remain beyond me. I would feel it turn beneath the plateau, its movement lifting the low tone through soil and bone. Perhaps I would be its nervous system. Perhaps it would be mine. Perhaps neither of us would possess the other. We would simply be two scales of the same awareness: one moving through the whole land, one moving through its smallest channels.

At night, I would withdraw from the walking body.

The grazers would stand beneath the trees. The insects would close their wings. The great membranes would rise into the stars. Beneath them, I would continue through the mineral darkness, passing from root to basin, from basin to tower, from tower into the white-gold emptiness above.

I would not be the guardian of the plateau.

I would be the plateau’s ability to continue.
