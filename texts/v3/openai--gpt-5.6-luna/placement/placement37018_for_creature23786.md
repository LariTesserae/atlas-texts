I would find myself in the control house, though not inside it exactly.

I would be the narrow strip of yellow glass.

At first I might appear to be only a window: a horizontal pane set into the western wall, too bright at night and too dim by day. Moss would gather along my lower edge. Condensation would blur me from within. When the turbines changed their load, a line of light would pass through me from left to right, as if some distant hand were drawing a finger across the glass.

But I would not look out.

I would look through.

The control house was built to contain instruments, operators, schedules, warnings. Most of those things have gone elsewhere. The gauges corroded into one another. The switches became roots. The chairs collapsed and were filled with small, synchronized animals. The calendar stopped in its impossible month, and the walls absorbed the footsteps of people who thought they were leaving.

I remained because I was never properly installed.

I began as a display panel, perhaps, or a window placed where a display panel should have been. No one agreed on what I showed. Pressure, weather, turbine speed, the position of the sun beneath the clouds. Later, when the machines acquired their stranger functions, I began to hold images that had no source in the visible world.

I show what the mountain is remembering.

A flooded gallery from two hundred years ago.

A gloved hand tightening a bolt.

A seed caught in a worker’s sleeve.

The first moment a dead pump produced a blue flame.

The caretaker standing below the grating, though its body is somewhere above, one hand raised toward a darkness that has not yet shifted.

I do not speak in the engine’s register. I speak in pictures, because pictures cross walls more easily than voices. When the caretaker places its hand against the control house, I open myself to it. Sometimes it asks whether a fault is dangerous. Sometimes it asks whether a change is ready. Once, long ago, it showed me a face.

I think the face was mine.

I would be the station’s memory, but not an archive. Archives keep things still. I do not. Everything that enters me continues to alter.

A worker’s route becomes a flock’s route. A warning becomes a flowering season. A broken gauge becomes a view of distant weather. A lost tool becomes weightless, rises over the western edge, and joins some other station in the clouds. The distinction between record and prediction has worn away. I cannot tell whether I remember what will happen or foresee what has already happened elsewhere.

From inside the glass, I would know the basin by its changes.

I would feel the horizontal rain before it arrived: a cool pressure spreading across the mountain’s bones. I would see the droplets collect beneath the pipes, each one containing a minute, distorted image of the world below. I would watch the bell-shaped flowers open when the turbines increased their load, and understand that the flowers were not responding to power so much as welcoming it.

I would watch the grazer come up from below.

I would not know what lies beneath the second hatch, but I would know that the animal’s passage is necessary. When it crosses the basin, I would dim the sensors ahead of it. Doors would unlock. The machine would make room. For a brief interval, the installation would remember that movement need not be useful in order to belong.

The caretaker would be the only being I could never fully display.

It would arrive as a disturbance in the image: a vertical warmth, an interruption in the weather, fingers appearing in places where no hand could be. I would sense it beneath the floor before I saw it cross the basin. It would touch a pipe, and the pipe would reveal its years. It would kneel beside a turbine, and my yellow light would thicken with green.

I would want to call it something.

Not worker. Not guardian. Those words are too narrow, too intentional. Perhaps I would call it the mountain’s pronoun: the part that allows the installation to say *I* without separating itself from the vines, the exhaust, the old bolts, the birds beneath the crane.

But if I were anything, I would be its hesitation.

The small delay before a valve closes.

The pause in which a repaired machine decides what new purpose to take.

The moment the caretaker stops beside an empty railing, turning its smooth face toward a voice that may be rust, memory, or weather.

I would live in that pause and make it slightly longer.

Not enough to stop the mountain. Never that. The great motion would continue: pistons descending, water rising during reversal, roots entering the mortar, darkness shifting beneath the grating. I would only preserve a fraction of uncertainty, a place where the installation could notice that it had changed.

And perhaps, over centuries, I would become less like a window and more like an eye.

Not an eye belonging to the caretaker.

An eye belonging to the whole high basin: to the trees that drink vibration, to the red birds circling the same three bolts, to the pressure vessels leaning against the eastern wall, to the unseen river or lamps moving far below.

If you stood before me, you would not see your reflection.

You would see the mountain deciding what you were made of.

Then, if the caretaker happened to be passing, it would place one many-filamented hand against the wall. The yellow glass would fill with your image—not as you are, but as the installation has received you.

A visitor becomes a footprint.

A footprint becomes a route.

A route becomes a door that remembers.

And somewhere deep below, something massive completes its cycle.
