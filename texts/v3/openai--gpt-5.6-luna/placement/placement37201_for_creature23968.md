You would find yourself in a **low-temperature hydrothermal pocket**—not an active vent with boiling plumes and dramatic towers, but an old, sealed-off mineral system where circulation has slowed almost to stillness. The columns are the remains of diffuse vent structures: natural chimneys built by precipitation, dissolution, and repetition. The chamber is a dead-looking part of the deep ocean only by the standards of animals.

You would be **a microbial consortium**, though “being” would be a misleadingly singular word.

More precisely, you would be the living skin of the stone: a layered community of chemolithotrophic microbes occupying the gradients inside basalt, mineral crust, and vent deposits. Some members of you would use traces of reduced sulfur, hydrogen, iron, or other dissolved minerals as sources of energy. Others would consume the compounds your neighbors release. A few would make use of the faint light at the wall, but most of you would live beyond light’s reach, where chemistry—not vision—defines the world.

You would not experience the chamber as a room.

You would experience it as a sequence of boundaries:

- a pore where oxygen-poor water meets oxygen-bearing water,
- a seam where iron begins to precipitate,
- a surface that is slightly warmer or more acidic than the one beside it,
- a mineral grain carrying an electrical difference,
- a passage through which fresh dissolved material arrives.

To you, a millimeter might be a continent. A mineral column might be an entire weather system.

Your body would have no clear outline. It would be the green-brown film on the wall, the darkening of a crust, the invisible population threaded through the columns. You would be partly alive and partly mineral: organisms growing within deposits, deposits preserving the remains of organisms, new generations using the structures made by old ones. The factory and its workers would be the same thing.

You would not hunt or flee. You would not need to move through the water. Movement would occur as chemistry passes through you.

Some of you would remain active for a brief interval, dividing where conditions allow. Some would enter dormancy when the available energy thinned. Some would be sealed into the rock, not quite dead so much as archived. Later, if pressure and chemistry changed, another part of you might awaken. Your history would not be recorded in bones or tracks, but in bands of iron-black crust, dull green salts, pale sediment, and rust-colored seams.

The low thrum of the columns would be your world’s great event—not a voice, but the sound of circulation continuing. Water entering. Minerals changing hands. Deposits thickening by an amount too small to see. A system remaining in equilibrium because nothing within it is capable of making equilibrium feel insufficient.

If an animal entered the hollow, it might call the place empty.

You would not.

You would know the bare basalt as a surface not yet occupied, the clean shelf as a future habitat, the sediment fan as a layered archive of former conditions. The chamber would be crowded with gradients, thresholds, dormant cells, mineral-bound histories, and slow exchanges occurring everywhere at once.

You are not the creature living in the hollow.

You are the hollow’s **metabolism**—a distributed, ancient life spread thin across stone, making almost no visible motion and yet, over geological time, remaking the entire room.
