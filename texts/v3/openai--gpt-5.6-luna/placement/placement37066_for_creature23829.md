I would find myself below the great central leaf, where the city gathers around its polished pillar and the warm channels divide into the neighborhoods.

I would be called **First-Listening**, though that would not be my first name.

My first name was probably something small and temporary: Warm-Stone, perhaps, or Born-Under-Leaf. I no longer remember it with certainty. In the lake, names wear away as tools do. They are handled, repaired, tied to new purposes. The name I carry now was given after I learned to hear what others could not.

Not voices.

Roots.

I can tell when a bridge is thirsty. I can feel the change in a stalk before its leaves begin to pale. I know the difference between a root loosening from stone and a root being worried by the blind scavengers below. When a channel begins to clog, the whole city changes its breathing by a fraction. Most people notice only that the water feels wrong against the skin. I place my palm against a living wall and feel the trouble traveling through it.

I am not the oldest listener, nor the best. My cords are few: one for a roof I helped raise, one for a spawning hollow I failed to protect, and one pale cord for a house that returned to open water. The pale cord is the one I touch most often.

I live in a chamber grown from a plant that has forgotten whether it is a tree or a building. Its roots hold the ceiling in place; its leaves collect a pocket of warm air where I sleep. I hang in a narrow hammock with a weight at my feet, so that I do not drift into the dark while dreaming. Around me, the walls are soft enough to pulse. Sometimes, in the night, I wake because the chamber has shifted around my body.

Then I press my ear to the stem.

The city speaks more clearly through bone than through breath. A tremor passes from root to wrist, from wrist to throat. Three quick knocks: a fish caught in a net of vine. A slow shudder: water entering where it should not. A long trembling note that seems to have no beginning or end: the falls thickening above us.

I rise, dress in gray felt and leafskin, and leave my house through a curtain of braided stems.

There is no street outside. There is only water, and the memory of a street maintained within it.

I push off once and allow the current to take me. The city opens and closes around me. A school of silver fish scatters from my shoulder. Beneath a broad leaf, a child floats in a woven cradle while two adults retie the cord around its waist. Farther on, an old woman is scraping pale algae from the underside of a roof. She looks at me but does not call out. She taps twice against the plant beside her.

I answer with one tap.

The news is not urgent.

That is how we measure danger: not by size, but by speed.

I am sent beyond the last cultivated houses, where the plant towers become tall and spare. There is a foundation there, older than any living person, perhaps older than the lake’s present level. A stairway descends from nothing into the silt. At its bottom, a root has begun to grow through a crack in the stone.

The root is dying.

It is not dead. That would be simpler. A dead root can be cut away, and the structure above it dismantled before decay spreads. But this one still holds the stair, still conducts a weak, irregular current. It is trying to live in poisoned sediment.

I place both hands against it.

For a while, I do nothing.

This is the part outsiders would find hardest: the work before work. The patience. The refusal to act merely because something is failing. The city has endured because we do not confuse intervention with care.

The root carries fragments of sensation into me: cold stone, grit, the pressure of deep water, the faint warmth of sunlight filtered through many lengths of water. Beneath these is another pattern, so faint I mistake it for my own pulse.

There is a passage below the foundation.

Not a blocked channel. Something older. Something sealed by mud and root, but still carrying water toward the deep lake.

If we open it, the lower neighborhoods may lose warmth. Several spawning hollows could empty. A house near the old stair might have to be abandoned.

If we leave it closed, the root will die, and the pressure will build elsewhere. A different channel may break suddenly. The city might not absorb that kind of change.

I withdraw my hands.

For a few breaths I am only a person in cold water, small beneath the mountain. Above me, the falls thunder through the lake. Behind me, the city’s roofs and bridges tremble in their green suspension. Ahead, the darkness has no walls at all.

Then the root moves.

Not much. A tightening beneath my fingers. A question, if plants are permitted questions.

I return to the city and report what I felt.

By evening, others have gathered: gardeners, channel-keepers, two elders with cords darkened nearly black by age. We do not decide at once. We lay our palms against the public roots and let the news travel. The city will answer, though never in a single voice.

Perhaps we will open the old passage a little each season, allowing the water to change by degrees. Perhaps we will grow a new anchor before touching the old one. Perhaps the foundation will be left to sink, and we will build around its absence.

I will be part of whichever choice is made. I will return to the dying root, tie a young stem beside it, clear the silt from its underside, and wait.

This is who I am in the lake-city:

not a ruler, not a prophet, not the owner of any living thing.

I am a hand placed against the dark to learn whether the dark is moving.

I am a keeper of transitions—the moment when a channel becomes a flood, when a plant becomes a wall, when a house becomes open water, when a child first releases the cord and discovers that the current is not the same as falling.

If I live long enough, my name will change again. Perhaps I will become Deep-Listener. Perhaps Root-Tied. Perhaps I will be given no new name at all.

When I die, my cords will be untied and hung around the central pillar. My body will be carried beyond the last gardens, to the sloping floor where the scavengers wait. The city will continue without me, though not unchanged. Some bridge will inherit my hands. Some plant will carry the shape of my work in its growth.

And years later, someone may touch a root beneath the great leaf and feel a faint, familiar tremor passing through it.

Not my ghost.

Only the city breathing through what I helped keep alive.
