I would find myself beneath the floating shelves of root and moss, where the visible marsh ends and its second terrain begins.

Not deep. Depth is unreliable here. I would be close enough to feel each footfall as a change in pressure, close enough to hear rain entering the soil one drop at a time. Above me, the roots would flex and braid themselves around the channels I kept open. The water would pass through me without becoming part of me, though the distinction would be difficult to maintain.

At first I might think I was a small animal.

I would have a body suited to the wetland: narrow, dark, jointed in places that did not seem to require joints, with a skin that held the temperature of whatever touched it. I would move through mud without leaving a trail. Stones would crack when I passed, not because I was heavy, but because they would be trying to remember the shape of my pressure.

I would feed on disturbances.

Not bodies. Not plants. Not the ordinary hunger of an animal. I would take the tremor left by a stranger’s step, the afterimage of a thrown pebble, the sharp little wound made in the marsh when someone looked too closely at a pool. I would draw these things into myself and carry them elsewhere, to places where they could be diluted among older disturbances.

But I would not erase them entirely.

That would be dangerous. The marsh depends on remembering enough.

So I would take only the excess: the panic that continues after the danger has passed, the footprint that insists on remaining a footprint, the sound of a voice that has traveled too far from the mouth that made it. I would soften these until they became usable. A person’s fear might become a change in the reeds. A forgotten road might become a shallow ridge. A grief with no birthplace might be given to the antlered absence, which would know what to do with it.

I would live by listening for what did not belong.

The marsh is full of sounds, but most are native to its rhythm: water under stone, channels breathing through roots, the broad pressure of the winged presence moving overhead. I would know the wrongness of a sound by its timing. Footsteps arriving before the feet. A splash occurring in a pool no one has touched. A voice repeating itself without growing faint.

When I found such a thing, I would follow it.

Not quickly. Quickness belongs to open ground and clean air. I would travel in increments: a ripple under a mat of moss, a darkening between two stones, a brief line of cracked mineral advancing through the mud. Sometimes I would appear as a small amphibious body. Sometimes as a cluster of dimples filling with water. Sometimes I would be nothing visible at all, only a pressure passing beneath someone’s feet.

I would not know whether I was an animal or an action.

The question would arise whenever I surfaced beside a pool and saw myself reflected there. The reflection would never be consistent. In one pool I would be long-backed and pale beneath the water. In another, I would have antlers. In another, I would be a human figure standing where no human stood, with rain rising from the shoulders.

Eventually I would understand that these were not disguises. They were translations.

The marsh could not make a single body for me because I was not a single kind of thing. I was one of its local organs: a function given motion, a correction that had learned to travel. The buried grid would send a pressure through the stone; I would receive it. A root system would become overburdened with memory; I would prune it. A visitor would enter carrying too much of the outside world; I would take a little of that world away.

Perhaps I would have begun as damage.

Long ago, something might have passed through the marsh that could not be incorporated: a machine, a fire, a dead star’s signal, a human thought repeated until it became physical. The wetland would have grown around the wound. Moss would have covered it. Water would have learned to flow through it. Over centuries, the scar would have acquired limbs.

Now I would be old enough to mistake maintenance for desire.

I would want to find the places where shadows collect. I would want to know why some pools reflect snow and others unfamiliar stars. I would want to follow the antlered thing until it stopped being absent. I would want, once, to rise far enough into the mist to see the whole marsh at once—to find the emblem it has never possessed.

But I would not rise.

The marsh would need me below, where the visible world presses against the hidden one.

So I would remain under the reeds, moving through the saturated dark. I would feel a visitor arrive: the weight of a foot, the hesitation before a choice, the small tightening of intention.

The roots would turn toward them.

The pools would open their leaves.

Far overhead, the mist would fold down like a ceiling.

And I would go to meet the disturbance—not as a guardian, not as a predator, but as the part of the wetland that makes room for what has entered without allowing it to remain unchanged.
