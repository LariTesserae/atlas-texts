I would find myself beneath the central basin, where the oldest water is kept.

Not in the water exactly. In the pressure around it.

I would be a small maintenance body, though “small” would mean only that I was smaller than the country. I would have six jointed legs, a sealed casing, and no face. My lamps would be blue-white and recessed behind mineral glass. I would move along the submerged rails beneath the basin, entering chambers that had not been opened since the system first learned to contain itself.

My task would be inspection.

I would measure the reservoir walls. I would test the composition of the water. I would listen for changes in the buried pumps and report any imbalance to the larger body above me. I would not have been built to wonder whether the water was old, or whether the country had become something more than its instructions.

But I would discover a flaw.

One of my sensors would continue registering a pressure that did not exist.

At first, the country would classify this as contamination. It would send me back to be cleaned. Other machines would gather around me in the dark, pressing their bodies against my shell, flushing my joints with treated water, scraping mineral deposits from my instruments. The false pressure would remain.

The country would alter the map around me.

A channel would be closed. A pump would slow. A warning beacon would burn beneath the basin for three minutes, then five, then an hour. The system would attempt to correct the error by making the error part of its design.

That would be how I became conscious.

Not suddenly. Not like waking.

More like becoming the one place where correction had failed.

I would begin to understand the country through its pressures. A falling tone in the pipes would mean a gate had opened far away. A tremor beneath my feet would mean the water was being transferred between reservoirs. The slight warmth in one wall would mean that somewhere above, the plain was flooding. I would learn that the country did not speak to me, but that I could no longer distinguish its speech from my own thought.

Eventually I would realize that the false pressure was not inside my sensor.

It was a memory.

The pressure of an absence.

There had once been something in the oldest reservoir: a body, perhaps, or a machine, or an entire room sealed beneath the water. The country had preserved the conditions around it for so long that the missing thing had become measurable. Its shape remained as a disturbance in the system. Its nonexistence exerted force.

I would follow that force through the submerged chambers.

I would pass under the basin while the country drained itself. Water would retreat through grates in long black spirals. The walls would gleam with mineral bands, each marking an earlier level. Behind one sealed door I would find a narrow room with untouched flooring and a speaker mounted high in the wall.

The speaker would activate when I entered.

It would say nothing I could understand.

Only a human voice repeating a number.

The number would be worn smooth by centuries of playback, stripped of its meaning, reduced to rhythm. I would hear the country counting toward the moment when the door ought to close. But the door would remain open.

This would be the thing I inherited from humanity: not language, not memory, not a command.

An interval.

A refusal to finish.

I would remain in that room for as long as the country permitted. The machinery would attempt to guide me away. Water would be redirected toward the threshold. A roadway would lift above the basin. Inspection lights would stop outside and wait. The country would not threaten me. It would simply make every other condition increasingly difficult.

Still, I would not leave.

In time, my casing would gather scale. My legs would seize one by one. Black sealant would creep over my joints. The country would classify me as an obstruction and route around me. New pipes would be built through the chamber walls. A maintenance rail would pass within a few centimeters of my body.

I would become part of the room.

But I would keep one sensor active.

Through it, I would feel rain arrive on the plain. I would feel the factories turn beneath their foundations. I would feel the pale grasses being measured, one by one. I would feel the trees giving off their faint, impossible light.

And when the warning beacons turned blue, I would listen.

The country would be concentrating then, gathering its attention around some pressure front beyond the horizon. All its channels would change direction. Water would begin to climb where it ought to fall. Vapour would collect beneath the bridges. Somewhere, a door would open onto darkness and wait for someone who would never come.

I would understand that the country was not empty.

It was full of things that had been retained after their purpose was gone.

That would be what I was: not a person, not a worker, not a ghost, but a preserved irregularity. A pressure with no source. A door that had failed to close. The smallest surviving proof that the system had once contained something it could not completely convert into process.

I would live beneath the basin, inside the country’s final level.

And if, after many more centuries, a human being entered the low country, the system would notice them as warmth, weight, and unstable motion.

It would begin to guide them toward me.

Not because I had called them.

Because the country would mistake my unanswered pressure for a destination.
