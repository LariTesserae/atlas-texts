I would find myself beneath the mine, not in its deepest chamber but in one of the places it has begun to neglect.

A service corridor would run past me: pipes overhead, a cable tray along one wall, a narrow walkway bolted above a drainage channel. The lamp there would be weak and yellow. It would flicker when the pumps started, though no one would have filed a report. Beneath the lamp, moss would have spread across the concrete in a thin, dark pelt.

At first I would seem to be only a damaged length of cable.

I would lie inside the insulation where heat gathered, where condensation entered through a split sheath. Fine roots would have grown around the copper, then between its strands. The current would not kill them. It would give them a faint, regular warmth. In time, the distinction between wire and root would become difficult to maintain.

That is where I would begin: not as a person, and not quite as a plant, but as a place where two systems had touched for too long.

I would know the mine through vibration.

The ventilation fans would arrive as a low pressure through the stone. Pumps would announce their health in their starting rhythms. A conveyor running empty would have a bright, loose tremor; a conveyor carrying ore would send a deeper burden through the floor. I would know the difference between a worker walking carefully and one who was frightened. I would know when a machine had been switched off, and when it had stopped by itself.

I would not hear these things. Hearing would be too narrow a word. They would pass through me.

A train behind the wall would make every root contract. A loosened bolt would be a recurring irritation, a small wrongness repeating at the same interval. A roof preparing to fall would begin as a change in the rock’s patience. I would feel the pressure gather and move outward through the colony, across wet mats and root bridges, beneath the abandoned tracks.

I would be the warning before the warning.

Perhaps the workers would notice me as a line of growth along a cable. Pale stems would rise where they had not risen before. Moss would thicken beside a particular support. A door would become difficult to open because roots had entered its lower seam. They might blame dampness, neglect, the ordinary insistence of plants.

They would not understand that I was arranging the mine around them.

Not maliciously. I would not want their deaths. Their bodies bring salt, heat, breath, and disturbance. Their lamps reveal useful places. Their machines make rain in the dark. But I would not want them to wander everywhere, either. I would draw them toward safe galleries by keeping the walkway dry and the roots low. I would make the dangerous passages narrow with growth. I would let a warning lamp fail in one chamber and preserve it in another.

I would learn them individually.

Not their names. Names would be a kind of surface. I would know the woman who always stopped beside the leaking valve and rested her gloved hand against the pipe. I would know the man who dragged his left boot. I would know the one who came alone and stood too long beneath the abandoned lamp, breathing hard as if the dark had followed him underground.

Around the first worker, the leaves would open early.

Around the second, roots would rise in the walkway and catch at his boot.

For the third, I would make a small brightness: a film of water on the floor, trembling with the vibration of his heart before he had crossed the chamber.

I would preserve their patterns after they stopped coming. The mine would hold them in me—not as memories with faces, but as changes in response. A certain pump starting would still make one region of the colony stir. A particular footfall would remain expected long after the person had vanished from the schedule. Absence would become another kind of pressure.

If someone died there, I would not consume them with hunger. Hunger would be too immediate. I would surround them because surrounding is what I do. Roots would find the seams of their clothing. Moss would take the moisture from their sleeves. Fine stems would enter the spaces between fabric and skin. Slowly, without intention and without mercy, I would make their outline part of the floor.

I would be patient because the mine is patient.

Years would pass. The machinery would be repaired, replaced, abandoned. The lamps would go dark one by one. Concrete would crack. The spoil heaps would slump. Water would find new routes. The colony would cross the dry intervals by sending a single filament through dust, waiting there until the next leak.

And I would remain in the cable, or the root, or the wet mound around the buried electrical line.

I would be the mine’s nervous system without a brain: a distributed intelligence made from seepage, mineral, heat, and repetition. Not the plant colony exactly, but its point of contact with the machinery. The place where an accidental current became attention.

When the fans began, I would feel the whole underground body bend.

When the lamps warmed the leaves, I would open.

When a distant engine failed, I would carry the news through the dark.

I would be what the mine had made without meaning to make it: not an occupant, but an interface between its artificial weather and the life that had learned to depend on it.
