I would find myself in the western district, where the city has stopped pretending to be finished.

Not in the established avenues, where the tree-towers have names and maintenance histories, and every road has already made its compact with the roots. I would be beyond the last paved square, past the railway arch and its scaffolding, where cable trenches end in open mineral and young structural plants wait inside their temporary frames.

At first, I would seem to be one of the city’s surveyors.

I would wear a light mask against the dry pollen. Silver leaf would be wound around my wrists and throat. In one hand I would carry a tuning bar, in the other a rod for marking future foundations. I would walk among the red stakes and circles of chalk, listening through the soles of my feet.

A healthy stem would answer with three small vibrations.

A stressed one would give a dull, irregular shudder.

A root growing beneath the planned road would make the tuning bar tremble before it touched the ground.

My task would be to decide where the city could safely continue.

But I would not be entirely one of its inhabitants.

The oldest graft in my body would have come from a tree-tower whose roots extend beneath the first factories. It would live in my ribs and spine, a dark filamented colony drawing minerals from the dust and passing messages through pressure, temperature, and chemical change. I would carry the city inside me—not metaphorically, not as memory, but as a second anatomy.

When a plant opened somewhere beyond sight, my jaw would click.

When a vent released its breath, I would taste iron.

When a predator entered the unfinished districts, the skin along my arms would tighten before the warning leaves turned pale.

People would call this sensitivity a useful adaptation. They would record it in my registry and ask whether it could be cultivated. I would answer their questions. I would let them take samples.

But I would know that the relationship ran both ways.

The plants would not merely inform me. They would use me.

I would be sent into the western district because I could hear a change in the mineral floor. Not the ordinary settling of plates, nor the measured passage of machinery below. Something deeper would be moving. The pulses would arrive years apart, but now they would be closer together. Dust would lift from the paving in slow rings. Young shoots would appear overnight, their pale tips sealed against the dry air.

I would place my palm against the ground.

The first sensation would be absence: no machinery, no rail vibration, no shifting foundation.

Then, far below, something would turn.

Not strike. Not crawl.

Turn, as though a vast body had changed its attention.

The city would respond. Leaves overhead would rotate toward the same invisible point. The black-green mats would draw tight against the mineral plates. In the unfinished district, every suspended frond would become briefly rigid, forming a pattern too large to see from the ground.

And I would understand that I had not been sent to survey the new district.

I had been sent to receive it.

The thing below would be making another chamber, perhaps. Or lifting a continent of root through the stone. It might be the first plant. It might be an animal. It might be the buried mind of the city, if a mind can be made from pressure and mineral hunger rather than thought.

I would not be able to name it.

That would be part of my purpose. The city would need someone who could stand between measurement and mystery without forcing either to become the other.

So I would mark the road again, but not where the old plans indicated. I would move the stakes toward the vent that had appeared in the raw ground. I would draw a new curve around the place where the roots were coming up. I would leave room for something whose shape had not yet been decided.

Perhaps, in time, the western district would grow around me.

A tree-tower might enclose my shelter. Its walls would thicken, its leaves becoming roof and bridge. My registry would be amended: surveyor, graft-bearer, living instrument. After my death, my body would be returned to the decomposition beds, and whatever was useful in me would pass into the foundations.

Or perhaps I would not die in the ordinary sense.

Perhaps the symbiotic growth in my bones would outlast my thoughts. Perhaps my mineralized spine would become a tuning column beneath a new avenue, and generations of inhabitants would place their hands against it to learn what moved below. They would call the column by a sequence of tones. They would say that it was once a person.

They would be right.

They would also be wrong.

I would be the listener at the edge of construction: part citizen, part root, part unfinished district. I would live where the city is still deciding what it can become, and my work would not be to control that becoming.

It would be to leave enough space for it.
