## Preparation for visiting the hollow city

Treat this as a short, guided expedition into a warm, high-wind industrial habitat—not as a normal city visit. The greatest dangers are not violence or wildlife. They are dehydration, hearing damage, respiratory injury, disorientation, falling debris, and accidentally violating rules that exist because the environment itself is unstable.

### Before you go

#### Confirm that your body can survive the visit

The city has no water. This is not merely a cultural omission: there are no wells, cisterns, channels, or apparent liquid supplies. An ordinary human cannot remain there for long without risking rapid dehydration.

Before arrival:

- Be fully hydrated, but do not assume you can replenish yourself inside.
- Arrange an approved emergency hydration procedure with the inhabitants or your expedition authority.
- Do not bring an ordinary canteen, water bottle, flask, or other conspicuous liquid container. The prohibition is explicit and may be socially serious, possibly because liquid is dangerous to the city’s plants, architecture, or air chemistry.
- Do not drink plant fluids or try to extract moisture from the vegetation. Most plants contain no liquid sap, and some release hot, chemically irritating gas when damaged.
- Plan on a visit measured in hours, not days, unless you have confirmed that your physiology and equipment are compatible with local custom.

If no sanctioned method of hydration exists, do not enter. “There is no water” should be taken literally.

#### Prepare for extreme noise

The hollow is continuously loud, and some events make it dangerously so. The wind itself produces a deep roar; streets act as flutes; bridges hum; dry fountains ring; and the wind houses can generate tones capable of vibrating the chest or hurting the teeth.

Bring:

- High-quality hearing protection, preferably layered: fitted earplugs beneath earmuffs.
- A method of communicating without speech.
- A visual or tactile alarm system in case you cannot hear your companions.
- A way to record or display written instructions without relying on audio.

Do not wear hearing protection so completely that you cannot notice tactile warnings from a guide, unless you have established another signaling method. A guide should be able to communicate through hand contact, colored flags, light signals, or agreed gestures.

Expect temporary tinnitus and fatigue even with protection. If you feel pressure or pain in your ears, leave the exposed avenue immediately and move into a lined interior space.

#### Protect your lungs and eyes

The city is full of mineral dust. It moves uphill toward the entrance, hangs in the sunlight, works into every crack, and is continually ground and redistributed by wind-powered machinery. Some airborne vapors come from the plants and fissures.

Bring:

- Sealed eye protection rather than ordinary spectacles.
- A particulate respirator with replaceable filters.
- Additional filters rated for unknown chemical vapors if available.
- A hood or scarf that seals around the neck and ears.
- Skin protection for exposed areas.
- Spare filters stored in dustproof wrapping.

Do not assume that clear air is safe. The hard shafts of light may make the air look clean while revealing only the largest suspended particles.

If your equipment supports it, carry an independent atmosphere monitor. The local air is probably breathable for the inhabitants, but their biology, long acclimatization, or tolerance for plant gases may differ from yours.

#### Dress for constant wind and abrasive surfaces

The wind is not an occasional gust. It is the defining motion of the city. It will pull at clothing, lift dust, destabilize you on bridges, and drive grit into seams.

Wear:

- Close-fitting clothing with no loose scarves, dangling straps, or unsecured equipment.
- Sturdy boots with excellent grip.
- Gloves for rough mineral handrails and rope mechanisms.
- A face and neck covering.
- Protective headgear, particularly around construction yards, towers, and wind houses.
- A harness or tether for exposed bridges and upper terraces, but use it only with local approval. A badly placed tether could catch the wind or interfere with a counterweight system.
- Clothing that can tolerate heat and dust without overheating you.

Avoid umbrellas, capes, large bags, light fabric, and anything that can become a sail.

#### Bring only quiet, simple equipment

The inhabitants use ropes, counterweights, membranes, mills, and human-operated mechanisms. Loud engines, generators, drones, firearms, fans, and powered devices may be disruptive or dangerous around the wind houses.

Bring:

- A compact, dust-sealed light source for the occasional dark shaft or sealed room.
- Nonverbal communication cards or colored flags.
- A tethered navigation marker or beacon approved by your guide.
- Durable dry food that produces little packaging waste.
- Basic medical supplies for eyes, lungs, cuts, heat stress, and noise exposure.
- A way to measure time without depending on sound.
- A mechanically secured return beacon or dimensional anchor, if your travel method permits it.

Do not bring a portable water purifier and assume it will be useful. There is no visible water to purify, and carrying liquid-handling equipment may be interpreted as ignorance or provocation.

### Arrival at the seam

The entrance is dangerous before you even enter.

Dust moves uphill toward the black seam, and warm air exits with considerable force. The opening is narrow and may function as a nozzle. Approach from the side rather than standing directly in front of it. Secure your clothing and equipment before reaching the mouth.

Do not:

- Block the seam.
- Light a flame.
- Release smoke, aerosols, or drones.
- Shout into it.
- Throw anything inside.
- Stand beneath unstable overhangs.
- Assume that a sudden change in airflow is harmless.

Wait for an appointed local guide. The city may regulate entry by pressure, vent behavior, or signals from the wind houses. If the sound changes sharply, retreat and wait. A change in tone may indicate a dustfall, vent opening, or dangerous pressure shift.

### Entering and orienting yourself

The hollow will appear much larger and more complex than it is. The brightness, dust columns, repeating bridges, and layered construction will make distance difficult to judge. The constant sound will also make it hard to identify direction.

Immediately establish:

- The route back to the lower seam.
- A sheltered assembly point.
- The location of a quiet or relatively quiet room.
- The nearest medical or host facility.
- A route that does not depend on a single bridge, lift, or wind house.
- A signal for “leave now.”
- A signal for “air problem,” “injury,” and “lost.”

Do not explore alone. A person can cross the hollow in under an hour, but that does not mean you can navigate it safely in an hour. Some streets may amplify sound or wind; others may funnel dust. A local guide will know which bridges become dangerous during particular tones.

Stay on established paths. The empty plazas are not necessarily safe open ground. A courtyard may be an airflow channel, a maintenance area, or a place where falling mineral fragments accumulate.

### How to communicate

Speech is unreliable and may be impossible. Expect people to use:

- Hand signs.
- Touch, especially a palm or finger to the wrist.
- Colored flags.
- Movement of leaves and hanging strips.
- District-specific wind-house tones.

Do not grab someone suddenly, even if you are trying to get their attention. Learn the local contact signal first. A guide may place a hand on your wrist to communicate; this is functional communication, not necessarily intimacy.

Carry a visible set of colored markers, but do not wave them randomly. Colors may have established meanings. Ask for the local code before using them.

Do not imitate wind-house tones, bells, or warning signals. A careless imitation could be taken as a district alarm or a declaration that you understand a signal when you do not.

### Cultural rules to follow

The city’s rules are environmental safeguards as much as customs.

#### Never seal a room completely

Every room must have two openings, however small, because still air is considered dangerous. Do not close both a door and a vent. Do not pitch a fully enclosed tent inside the city. If you need to sleep, use a local room or an approved shelter with airflow.

Do not plug cracks, tape vents, or cover gaps to keep out dust. Ask for a filtered screen instead.

#### Do not bring or display liquid vessels

Do not carry a flask, bottle, cup, jar, or decorative vessel designed to hold liquid. Do not ask casually where the water is. “Water” is ceremonial and old-fashioned in their vocabulary; repeated questions may sound like insisting that their world is deficient.

If you need medical liquid, cosmetics, or a medication suspension, clear it with a host before entry. A solid or vapor formulation will be less problematic.

#### Do not damage living plants

Never cut a root, harvest a leaf, snap a stem, or open a flower. The plants are infrastructure, food production, air chemistry, and possibly sacred objects all at once.

Keep away from:

- Plants that click open.
- Gas-releasing stems.
- Root clusters in wall cracks.
- Vines under tension.
- Trees near vents, especially during a pressure change.

Do not touch the flowers merely because they resemble glass. Their opening mechanism may be forceful, sharp, or chemically active.

#### Do not interfere with wind structures

Wind houses, dry fountains, membranes, bells, fans, lifts, and rattling frames are not scenery. They transmit information and regulate work. Do not lean on them, silence them, alter their membranes, tie anything to them, or walk through their airflow without permission.

Never attempt to make the city quiet. The inhabitants’ belief that silence might cause the hollow to open may be theological, practical, or both. In either case, testing it would be reckless.

#### Follow the stone custom

At the beginning of the local season, households remove a stone from their own walls and place it in the public streets. Visitors should not remove stones from buildings themselves. If invited to participate, accept a stone offered by a resident and place it where directed.

Do not repair a wall, straighten a leaning building, or “improve” a step without permission. The city’s looseness is intentional. A structure that appears unstable may be designed to move or redistribute pressure.

### Heat, light, and shelter

The city is bright rather than merely sunny. Mineral surfaces scatter light into the central avenues, and there may be few deep shadows. Use shaded lenses or lightly tinted eye protection. Even if the temperature is tolerable, reflected light and constant wind can produce headaches, eye strain, and heat exhaustion.

Rest indoors, but not in a sealed chamber. Choose spaces lined with felt or packed plant fiber. These are likely the safest places for visitors because they reduce noise and airflow while preserving ventilation.

Sleep may be difficult. The walls will shudder, the wind will remain audible, and the light may not follow a familiar day-night cycle. Bring a sleep mask, but make sure it does not prevent you from seeing evacuation signals. Ear protection is essential.

### Food and biological precautions

The vegetation is lush but not necessarily edible to humans. Its chemistry is based on heat, salts, mineral powder, and unusual gases. Do not eat local plants, flowers, roots, or powders without explicit identification and permission.

Bring dry, compact, familiar food. Avoid packaging that can be blown into vents or tangled in vines. Take every scrap back out or dispose of it according to local instructions.

The pale foundation animals are not necessarily dangerous, but do not handle them. The winged vent creatures may be fragile, territorial, or part of the city’s air ecology. Avoid disturbing upper openings.

The seasonal blind grazer should be treated as a large wild animal. If it enters while you are there:

- Move behind a wall or into a designated shelter.
- Do not approach, feed, or photograph it at close range.
- Do not stand between it and the windward route.
- Follow the city’s instructions when gates are closed.

### Hazards to watch for

Leave or seek shelter if you observe:

- A sudden change in the direction or temperature of airflow.
- Dust beginning to fall in sheets rather than drift.
- Wind-house tones becoming unusually low, sharp, or synchronized.
- Plants bending in a direction different from the surrounding wind.
- Bridges vibrating visibly.
- Loose stones creeping across roofs.
- Your guide signaling without explanation.
- Loss of orientation caused by hearing protection or dust.
- Headache, ringing ears, nausea, coughing, burning eyes, or unusual drowsiness.
- A gas release from damaged vegetation.
- A sudden reduction in wind.

The last sign is especially important. Stillness is not necessarily relief. It may indicate a blockage, pressure event, or a condition the inhabitants regard as dangerous.

### Health and emergency plans

Have a strict turnaround time. Do not wait until you are thirsty, dizzy, or disoriented. In this city, an ordinary traveler may deteriorate quickly without realizing it because wind and noise mask bodily warning signs.

Agree beforehand that any one of the following ends the visit:

- Difficulty breathing.
- Persistent coughing or chest tightness.
- Eye pain or visual distortion.
- Hearing loss or severe tinnitus.
- Confusion, weakness, or poor coordination.
- Inability to communicate with the guide.
- A pressure or dust warning from the wind houses.
- Loss of your return marker.
- Any violation of the city’s evacuation signals.

Carry no lone “heroic” emergency plan. If separated, remain in place rather than searching blindly. Find the nearest inhabited, ventilated structure and display the agreed distress color. The city is sparse enough that wandering can put you in an empty district, a maintenance shaft, or an active airflow channel.

### Recommended duration

For an ordinary human, the safest visit is:

1. Arrive already hydrated.
2. Spend only enough time inside to meet a guide and observe the city.
3. Remain on lower, established routes at first.
4. Avoid major wind-house events.
5. Leave before fatigue, thirst, or dust exposure becomes significant.

A longer stay should require a local host family, approved shelter, a reliable hydration arrangement, respiratory monitoring, and a detailed understanding of the district’s wind signals.

Above all, enter quietly in the cultural sense—even though the city itself is never quiet. Do not try to make it more familiar, more efficient, more sealed, or more comfortable by ordinary-world standards. The hollow’s wind, plants, looseness, and emptiness are not background conditions. They are the city’s life-support system.
