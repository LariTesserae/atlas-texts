To visit the Upper Hollow safely, treat it less like a wilderness destination and more like the interior of a living industrial furnace perched above an abyss. An ordinary human should not attempt an unprotected visit, and should not assume that familiar concepts—air, ground, animals, weather, daylight, or death—behave normally there.

## Before departure

### Bring a sealed thermal environment

You will need a fully enclosed suit capable of operating in extreme heat and low atmospheric pressure. Ordinary fire-resistant clothing is useless. The suit should have:

- Active cooling, preferably with redundant coolant loops.
- Thermal insulation on the outside and reflective surfaces facing the red illumination.
- Its own oxygen supply and carbon-dioxide scrubber.
- Positive internal pressure, so outside vapor cannot leak in.
- Filters designed for metallic aerosols, mineral dust, and unknown corrosive compounds.
- Protection against heat conducted through the feet and hands.
- A visor that can tolerate infrared exposure and sudden changes in illumination.

Do not rely on breathing apparatus alone. The atmosphere may be thin, chemically hostile, or simply incapable of supporting human respiration. The gray vapor should be treated as toxic until proven otherwise. Its odor of scorched metal and dry bone is not a useful safety indicator; if you can smell it through a suit, your suit has already failed.

Your cooling system should be sized for a stationary visit, not merely a short excursion. The heat is described as steady, but the “hot pressure” cycles may overwhelm a marginal system. Carry enough reserve cooling to leave the basin and reach your extraction point after an unexpected delay.

### Prepare for pressure and vibration

The fauna sense pressure, heat, and vibration across their bodies. Your movement, suit pumps, radio equipment, and landing system may be detectable even if you make no audible sound.

Bring:

- Soft, vibration-isolated footwear or landing pads.
- A way to dampen suit machinery.
- Low-power, non-pulsing communications.
- Inertial navigation, since visual landmarks may disappear in darkness.
- A tether or guided return line, but make sure the tether cannot drag across the creatures or snag the rim.
- Remote probes for testing the landing area before you enter.

Avoid equipment that produces repeated impacts, rotating machinery, sonic ranging pulses, or strong heat signatures. A conventional drone with loud propellers would be a poor choice. Use a hovering or crawling probe with minimal vibration, or deploy a passive sensor package from a distance.

### Bring a means of immediate extraction

Do not plan to walk out if something goes wrong. The route upward may be difficult, and the route downward leads toward the abyss and hotter regions. Establish an extraction point before arrival. Ideally, your transport or portal should remain available and independently monitored.

Bring at least two independent return methods:

1. A primary beacon or portal anchor.
2. A physically carried emergency locator or recall device.
3. A prearranged remote extraction protocol in case you become incapacitated.

Test these methods in the destination’s local conditions. The Hollow may interfere with electromagnetic equipment, heat-sensitive electronics, or anything that depends on stable pressure. Do not assume that a beacon placed on the floor will remain in the same place. The “floor” itself shifts.

### Learn the cycles before going

The Hollow has no visible sun and no ordinary day, so you will need instruments to measure its thermal and pressure cycles. Spend as little time as possible observing from outside the basin before committing to entry.

Determine:

- When the hot-pressure event occurs.
- How long it lasts.
- Whether it arrives on a predictable schedule.
- Whether the creatures begin moving before the event.
- Whether the red glow from below corresponds to increased danger.
- Whether the tall creatures are emerging or descending during your proposed visit.

Your safest window is likely the quiet interval after a pressure cycle, when the fauna have settled and before the next cycle begins. Avoid arriving during a pressure descent. That is when the animals become restless, climb the walls, flatten themselves against surfaces, and emit vapor. Their movement may turn a traversable patch into a living, shifting slope.

## How to enter

### Do not land on the packed bodies

The creatures may resemble stones, but they are not inert ground. A landing impact could trigger a local disturbance or transmit pressure across a large section of the basin. Even a gentle landing would compress and heat the upper layers.

Enter onto an exposed, stable section of fused stone if one exists. Test it first for:

- Surface temperature.
- Structural stability.
- Movement beneath the crust.
- Chemical reaction with your suit or equipment.
- Vibration transmission into the basin.

If no bare ground exists, reconsider the visit. The creatures cover the floor in layers, and the distinction between a body, a carcass, and landscape cannot be trusted. A “solid” area may be a colony that slowly yields under weight.

If you must cross the fauna, distribute your weight over a broad platform and move only with permission from a remote observer watching the colony’s reaction. Do not step directly on individual forms. Do not wedge equipment between them. Do not try to push one aside.

### Minimize your heat signature

The inhabitants appear to feed on gradients rather than food. A human in an actively cooled suit may create an unusual thermal boundary: cool machinery against hot stone, hot exhaust against cooler bodies, or a moving gradient where none existed before.

Keep external temperatures as uniform as possible. Avoid venting heat directly into the basin. Do not use flares, torches, open flames, or bright active lamps unless absolutely necessary. Light itself may not matter to the creatures, but heat and pressure almost certainly do.

Use dim, narrow-beam illumination, preferably in wavelengths chosen after remote observation. The red glow from below may not be ordinary light, and sudden illumination could reveal or provoke movement you cannot interpret.

### Move slowly and predictably

The creatures react to vibration and pressure, not necessarily to human intention. Sudden movement may be interpreted as a pressure event, a threat, or an attractive gradient.

Use a slow, steady gait. Keep your feet planted gently. Avoid jumping, running, crawling directly across the bodies, or changing direction abruptly. Pause whenever you detect a broad ripple moving through the basin.

If a large number of creatures tilt at once, stop immediately. Do not mistake a synchronized movement for curiosity. It may indicate an approaching heat cycle, a signal passing through the colony, or preparation for the emergence of a larger form.

## What not to approach

### The tall unfolding creatures

If one of the larger bodies begins to unfold, freeze and give it the widest possible clearance. Do not photograph it at close range, illuminate it, scan it with active sonar, or attempt communication through vibration.

The emerging form is not necessarily attacking, but its movement is powerful, deliberate, and connected to the entire packed layer. Smaller bodies will part around it, possibly creating a temporary route—but that route is not an invitation. Wait until it has either descended beyond the rim or settled again.

Never stand between one of these forms and the wall or the abyss. Its path may be determined by pressure changes you cannot perceive.

### The basin rim

Treat the raised lip of bodies around the abyss as unstable living terrain. It may look like a wall, but it is composed of tightly packed organisms and may shift without warning.

Do not look over the rim without a secured tether. Do not place instruments beyond it. The abyss has no visible bottom, and the red illumination may distort depth perception. More importantly, you may cause a local pressure change that makes the rim creatures move.

The edge is also likely to be where heat gradients are strongest. It may attract the fauna and destabilize the entire perimeter.

### The ring of pierced slabs

The western ring should be treated as an active structure, not an archaeological curiosity. The holes contain darkness deeper than the surrounding darkness, and the creatures avoid the ring. That avoidance is the most important warning available.

Do not pass through the ring. Do not place a hand, probe, light, camera, or sensor in one of the holes. Do not touch or lean against the slabs. Their purpose may involve pressure, sound, heat, or geometry that is dangerous to a human even if nothing visibly happens.

Mark its position remotely and maintain a wide exclusion zone.

### The gray arch

The arch is damaged, alien in composition, and partly buried by the fauna. It may be a remnant, a machine, a boundary, or a passage. Its interrupted markings may be intentional rather than eroded.

Do not attempt to excavate it. Digging would disturb the colony and possibly release heat or pressure from beneath the living layers. Do not complete, trace, or reproduce the markings with tools. In an unfamiliar reality, a partial pattern can be an incomplete warning, a control sequence, or an inscription whose function depends on being disturbed in a particular way.

### The northern stair and sealed opening

The stair is the most dangerous feature in the Hollow.

The creatures fill every step but stop before the final landing. That boundary is almost certainly meaningful. The soft impact from behind the sealed opening causes every organism in the basin to become perfectly still. This is not ordinary environmental behavior; it is a coordinated response to something the colony recognizes.

Do not climb the stair. Do not approach the final landing. Do not knock, speak, vibrate the wall, examine the seam at close range, or attempt to open the slabs.

If the pressure flex occurs while you are anywhere in the basin, become completely motionless. Shut down nonessential machinery, mute active transmitters, stop walking, and wait. Do not use the silence to retreat unless the pressure has clearly released and the fauna have resumed their normal motion. A movement during the collective stillness may be more dangerous than the original event.

## Reading the environment

The Hollow offers few conventional warnings, so learn its indirect ones.

Leave immediately if:

- The floor begins shifting over a broad area.
- Multiple wall colonies change color or pattern at once.
- Gray vapor starts accumulating rather than sinking.
- The red glow brightens for more than a brief interval.
- A tall creature unfolds near your route.
- The bodies begin pressing toward the upper walls.
- Your suit registers a pressure gradient you cannot explain.
- The basin becomes silent in response to the sealed opening.
- The creatures begin clustering around your equipment.
- A stable surface develops a sudden thermal difference.

Do not wait for an animal to “attack.” In this place, a mass movement, a change in pressure, or a body settling beneath your foot may be the attack—or may cause one without any individual creature displaying hostile behavior.

## Conduct and research protocol

Assume that the fauna are intelligent at the colony level, even if individual bodies possess only partial instincts. They may communicate through pressure, heat, vibration, or changes in arrangement. Your presence will be registered long before you see a reaction.

A good field protocol would be:

1. Observe from outside the basin.
2. Deploy passive sensors.
3. Map heat, pressure, and vibration gradients.
4. Record the timing of collective movements.
5. Enter only during a settled interval.
6. Keep the visit short.
7. Avoid all remnants and all dense clusters.
8. Do not collect specimens, fragments, vapor, or mineral material.
9. Leave no heat source, beacon, food, fluid, or waste behind.
10. Return before the next predicted pressure event, with a large reserve margin.

Do not bring biological samples from another world into the Hollow. The local life may react to organic matter, water, microbes, or chemical contamination in ways that cannot be predicted. Likewise, do not assume the creatures can be fed or befriended. Their apparent “feeding” on gradients means that a gift of matter may be irrelevant, while a harmless-looking battery, heater, coolant leak, or breathing person may be intensely significant.

## Physical and psychological preparation

The visual environment will be disorienting. There is no normal horizon, little usable light, and an abyss whose depth cannot be judged. The basin’s nearly imperceptible motion may produce the sensation that the ground is breathing or that your own body is swaying.

Use instruments rather than your eyes to judge:

- Orientation.
- Distance.
- Slope.
- Time.
- Movement.
- Temperature.
- Proximity to the rim.

Keep a verbal or digital check-in schedule. A place this quiet can induce fixation: the traveler may become absorbed in tiny movements, the sealed opening, or the illusion that the whole basin is waiting for a particular action. Do not investigate a compulsion simply because the environment seems to be “asking” you to. The Hollow may indeed be communicating, but you will not have enough information to interpret that communication safely on a first visit.

## Recommended mission profile

The safest ordinary-human visit would be a brief, remote-assisted survey from a stable basalt shelf near—but not inside—the basin. Remain in a sealed, cooled pressure suit. Observe the fauna during a settled period. Map the abandoned structures without touching them. Record the pressure event from a distance. Leave before the next cycle.

A direct crossing of the basin should be considered an emergency operation, not sightseeing. The Upper Hollow is not merely hot terrain populated by unusual animals. It is a tightly coupled system in which the ground is alive, the atmosphere is dangerous, the architecture may still function, and a hidden presence can make every organism in the basin stop listening at once.

The essential rule is simple:

**Make as little change as possible.**

Do not add heat, pressure, vibration, matter, sound, or curiosity to a place that appears to be waiting for a very specific change in pressure.
