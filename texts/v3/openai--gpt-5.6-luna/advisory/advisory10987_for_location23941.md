## Preparation for the Basin

Treat this as an expedition into a vast, enclosed wetland-industrial ecosystem—not as a wilderness trek and not as a ruin survey. The basin is alive, mechanically active, poorly mapped, and hostile to anyone who assumes that a familiar category applies.

An ordinary human should not visit alone. Ideally, do not visit at all without a local guide or a traveler with prior experience in this specific basin. The terrain, weather, machinery, wildlife, and local rules interact in ways that cannot be managed by generic survival training.

### 1. Arrive above the floodline

Your arrival point is your first major decision.

Do not materialize, land, or establish a camp in:

- A drainage channel
- A marsh or reed field
- A basin or depression
- Beneath a bridge, cable, pipe, or conveyor
- Near an operating pump or dredge
- Inside or immediately beside an abandoned industrial structure
- Beneath dense canopy where delayed rain can fall in concentrated avalanches

Choose a high, stable platform with clear paths in several directions. A reinforced maintenance station, upper gantry, old tower platform, or large root shelf may be suitable, provided it is not vibrating, leaking, steaming, or visibly inhabited by something else.

The basin can flood without rain when underground pumps stop. You need to be above the highest visible waterline, not merely above the current one. Elevation must be checked against local markers, sediment stains, stranded debris, and the advice of inhabitants.

Bring enough equipment to remain at the arrival site for several days. Do not assume the return portal, vehicle, or extraction mechanism will function after the first storm.

### 2. Protect your lungs and skin

The air is warm, wet, thin, and biologically active. It may contain industrial vapors, fungal spores, steam, decomposition gases, and substances released from disturbed sediment.

Wear:

- A full-face respirator with replaceable particulate and chemical cartridges
- A reserve supply of oxygen or an emergency breathing mask
- Sealed goggles or a respirator with an integrated visor
- Waterproof outer clothing
- Puncture-resistant gloves extending past the wrists
- Tall, sealed boots with aggressive tread
- A hood or helmet that protects the neck and ears
- Clothing resistant to abrasion and plant cuts

Do not rely on ordinary rain gear. It will trap heat and moisture against the body, while the vegetation and machinery will tear it. Use a layered system: a moisture-managing inner layer, protective middle layer, and a tough, waterproof outer layer with sealed seams.

Avoid direct contact with:

- Warm pools
- Pale flexing organisms in channels
- Translucent bladder-plants
- Fungal shelves
- Mud bubbles
- Condensation from pipes or cables
- Water collected from roofs or machinery
- Any biological growth that pulses, closes, trembles, or exhales

A plant that closes around passing bodies is not merely inconvenient. It may restrain you, inject something, or signal your presence to other organisms.

Carry sterile wound treatment and assume every cut will become infected unless cleaned immediately. Include antifungal, antibacterial, antiparasitic, and anti-inflammatory supplies appropriate to your physiology. Do not use local medicines, food, or water until a competent local has identified them and you have tested them cautiously.

### 3. Bring redundant navigation

The darkness is absolute, and your light will not turn the basin into a normal landscape. A lamp may reveal only a few meters of wet leaf, machinery, and moving bodies while making you conspicuous.

Use several independent methods of navigation:

- Shielded, low-intensity lamps with red or infrared capability
- Tactile route markers
- A measured survey line or climbing rope
- Inertial navigation
- A pressure- and depth-capable mapping device
- A local map, if one exists
- Passive acoustic or vibration monitoring
- A dead-reckoning log maintained by two people independently

Every member of the expedition should be connected to a safety line in unfamiliar terrain, particularly near channels, platforms, and industrial structures. The line should be brightly tactile or chemically marked rather than dependent on visible color.

Do not mark trees, roots, machines, or living structures without permission. The basin’s ecology may respond to damage, and a structure that appears abandoned may still be part of a functioning system. Use removable tags, knots, bells, or coded rope arrangements instead.

Do not trust a single map. Tunnels change apparent length. Water changes routes. Vegetation conceals old infrastructure. A freight line may move, and a walkway may no longer lead where it did previously.

### 4. Prepare for sound as a survival sense

Sound carries far through the water, metal, roots, and enclosed air. It is useful for navigation, but it also advertises you.

Carry:

- Hearing protection that can be rapidly removed
- A communications system using bone conduction or low-power wired links
- Vibration sensors
- A means of recording persistent mechanical rhythms
- Quiet signaling devices
- A manually operated emergency alarm agreed upon with local inhabitants

Do not constantly speak, shout, or play loud orientation tones. Establish hand signals, rope signals, and short coded noises before departure.

The basin has a continuous soundscape. Learn to distinguish ordinary background noise from changes:

- A sudden silence among insects and small animals
- Water moving where there was no current
- A vibration through the ground
- A chain beginning to move
- A machine becoming quieter rather than louder
- A siren giving a single low note
- Rain striking a new surface
- Footsteps or dragging sounds above you

Silence is not safety. In this place, silence is often an announcement.

### 5. Understand the machinery

Treat every industrial object as active until proven otherwise. “Abandoned” does not mean harmless. A dead engine may be a nest, a colony, a heat source, a pump component, or an organ of the surrounding wetland.

Never:

- Enter a tank
- Crawl beneath a conveyor
- Stand under a cable or pressure line
- Touch a rotating, vibrating, warm, or wet machine
- Follow a machine operating quietly
- Cross rails without checking for vibration
- Assume an unmanned vehicle is empty or predictable
- Shut down a pump
- Open a valve
- Pull a lever
- Cut a cable
- Remove a part
- Block a drain or intake

A large structure may generate its own weather. Condensation can fall from ceilings like rain. Steam may obscure a drop, conceal an animal, or burn through clothing. Pipes may carry pressure, heat, chemicals, or something more difficult to classify.

Stay away from low-lying machinery whenever possible. If a pump stops, leave the low ground immediately. Do not wait to confirm that the water is rising. The basin’s water may arrive from below, sideways, or through structures that appeared dry.

The closed-tank rule is absolute: if a voice comes from inside a sealed tank, do not answer, open it, knock on it, or approach it. Mark the location and retreat.

### 6. Keep away from the water

Assume all water is contaminated, inhabited, or both.

Carry enough sealed drinking water for the entire planned stay, plus an emergency reserve. Do not depend on filtration alone. The local water may contain organisms too small to see, industrial compounds, or forms of contamination that ordinary purification will not remove.

If you must obtain local water, use a sealed collection system and subject it to multiple treatments: particulate filtration, chemical treatment, boiling or sterilization, and laboratory testing if available. Never drink from channels, pools, reservoirs, or runoff collected beneath industrial structures.

Do not wade unless there is no alternative. Use a pole to probe the ground, and assume that any pale flexing object, bubble, ripple, or apparently empty patch of mud may be alive.

Streams can carry enough force to sweep away an adult even when they appear shallow. Their banks may collapse, and mats of roots can conceal sudden drop-offs. Cross only at locations approved by a local guide, using fixed lines and an anchored safety rope.

### 7. Treat the vegetation as an animal population

Do not push casually through tall grass or blade-leaves. Use a machete or cutting tool only when necessary, and cut with protective gloves and face covering. Cutting plants may release irritants, spores, scents, or alarm signals.

Avoid:

- Trembling stems
- Leaves that close or turn toward you
- Fungal shelves that release dust when pressure changes
- Synchronized pulsing in the mud
- Vines descending from unseen heights
- Root systems that move or tighten
- Any plant field behaving differently from the wind

If reeds begin moving in circles rather than with the wind, do not cross them. Do not investigate the center of the movement. Retreat along your existing route, slowly and without entering the vegetation.

Never camp beneath the canopy unless there is no alternative. The upper growth catches rain and releases it later in heavy, localized falls. It also conceals large animals and may provide routes for things that move above you.

### 8. Learn the animal warning signs

You will rarely see the dangerous fauna clearly. Your primary warning system will be the behavior of smaller creatures.

Leave the area if:

- Insects stop ticking all at once
- Waterfowl rise without calling
- Small animals flee in one direction
- Mud begins bubbling in a line
- The upper vegetation shakes against the wind
- A channel becomes still despite continuing rain
- Calls stop in a widening radius

Do not pursue movement in the canopy. Do not investigate a dragging sound on a roof from outside or below. Do not approach herds simply because they appear herbivorous; their weight, panic, or defensive behavior can be lethal.

Predators may use machinery, walkways, roofs, or water channels as travel routes. Keep camp elevated, enclosed, and quiet. Do not leave food waste, blood, warm clothing, or biological samples outside.

At night—though there is no true day—use heat, scent, and sound discipline. Hood lights, seal food, and keep all bodily waste contained.

### 9. Camp like the local inhabitants

Observe how people live before deciding that their practices are excessive.

A suitable camp should have:

- A raised floor
- Rapidly closing doors
- Two exits
- No direct contact with roots, pipes, or standing water
- Covered lights
- A rope route to a higher location
- A dry, sealed store for food and medical supplies
- A way to detect flooding beneath the floor
- Bells, vibration lines, or other passive intrusion warnings
- A designated quiet room or shelter for storms and machinery events

Do not illuminate the landscape. Light should identify the camp without projecting far beyond it. Use hooded lamps, downward-facing markers, and covered emergency beacons.

The local use of bells is significant. Their purpose is not necessarily to scare animals away; they may indicate when something has crossed a walkway. Ask what each bell pattern means before relying on it.

Do not sleep directly on the ground. Do not leave doors open. Do not build a fire unless you know the air chemistry and have local approval. Heat may attract animals, alter fungal behavior, or interact with buried pipes.

### 10. Establish local agreements

If you encounter inhabitants, do not approach from darkness without announcing yourself in the manner they use. Their settlements are designed around threats you will not recognize.

Offer practical information and supplies rather than bright lights or loud technology. Ask:

- Which routes are currently safe?
- Which pumps are running?
- Where is the last known floodline?
- Which machines must not be approached?
- What sounds indicate a predator?
- What is the meaning of the local bells?
- Where are travelers permitted to sleep?
- Which water, food, and medical practices are acceptable?
- What should be done if the machinery changes rhythm?
- Which names, voices, or signals must never be answered?

Do not enter a hut, station, tank, tunnel, or machine hall without invitation. Do not photograph or sample local people, animals, or structures without permission. In a world where infrastructure and ecology have merged, removing a component may be equivalent to injuring an organism.

### 11. Prepare for weather and physical exhaustion

The constant humidity will defeat ordinary body-temperature regulation. You may sweat continuously while becoming chilled by wet stone, steam, or moving water. Carry electrolyte solution, dry clothing in sealed bags, and a method of warming yourself without an exposed fire.

Expect:

- Reduced oxygen tolerance
- Persistent skin maceration
- Blisters and fungal infections
- Loss of orientation
- Hearing fatigue
- Sleep disruption from constant noise
- Sudden wind and pressure changes
- Delayed rain from the canopy
- Cold surfaces beneath warm air
- Heat from vents and buried machinery

Rest on a strict schedule. Exhaustion is especially dangerous here because the important signals are subtle: a small change in animal noise, a vibration underfoot, or water appearing where it was not present before.

### 12. Follow the basin’s rules exactly

Memorize these before departure:

1. Do not follow a machine that is running quietly.
2. Do not stand beneath a dripping cable unless you know what it carries.
3. When the underground pumps stop, leave the low ground immediately.
4. Never answer a voice that comes from inside a closed tank.
5. If the reeds begin to move in circles rather than with the wind, do not cross them.

Add a sixth rule of your own:

**When you cannot determine whether something is infrastructure, vegetation, animal, or habitation, do not touch it.**

The basin is not waiting to be explored in the ordinary sense. It has already incorporated its rivers, factories, roots, tunnels, heat columns, animals, and ruins into a stable—though dangerous—balance. Your safest role is to be quiet, temporary, elevated, sealed, and easy to remove.

Arrive with a guide, remain above the water, disturb nothing, answer no impossible voice, and leave before the machinery changes its rhythm.
