## Prepare as if you are entering a sealed geothermal ruin, not a city

This is not a place for ordinary travel clothes, improvised camping gear, or an untested atmosphere mask. It is a hot, dry, enclosed environment with intermittent pressure events, abrasive mineral dust, unstable architecture, intense darkness, and no obvious source of water. Treat it as a hazardous industrial site combined with a desert and a living alien ecosystem.

### Before entering

**Bring a reliable way back.**  
The chamber has no horizon and many streets lead into ledges, shafts, terraces, and unmapped lower spaces. Bring a beacon, line, or navigation marker that can function through dust, heat, and poor visibility. A physical guide rope anchored outside would be preferable to relying entirely on electronics. Mark every major junction on the way in. Do not assume you can retrace your route by memory.

If your method of multiversal travel permits it, establish an exit point outside the blister and keep it fixed. Do not place the return point inside the city unless you have confirmed that the chamber’s pressure pulses cannot displace, bury, or heat it.

**Study the breathing cycle.**  
Observe the site from outside for as long as possible. The warm air pulses, the opening and closing of shutters, and the red brightening of the central vent are warnings that a major activity period is approaching. Enter during the longest quiet interval, and never remain near the central vent when the city begins to respond.

You should assume that the timing is irregular until proven otherwise. Carry an independent clock, a pressure sensor, and a temperature sensor. Do not trust the old vanes or mechanisms as a complete weather forecast; they may react locally and may themselves be damaged.

**Determine whether the air is breathable.**  
The description gives no reason to assume that it is. “Dry” does not mean safe. The air may contain volcanic gases, metal vapors, acidic dust, spores, pollen, or fine ceramic particles. Bring a sealed environmental suit with:

- Positive-pressure breathing apparatus  
- Replaceable filters for particulates and unknown chemical vapors  
- An independent oxygen supply, not merely a filter  
- A carbon dioxide monitor  
- Toxic-gas alarms  
- Eye protection sealed against dust  
- A way to communicate through the helmet  

Do not remove the mask simply because the air feels empty or clean. There are no animals to consume oxygen or produce carbon dioxide, but that does not make the atmosphere suitable for human lungs.

### Clothing and equipment

Wear a fully enclosed, heat-resistant suit with reflective outer layers. Ordinary fabric will collect mineral dust, snag on rigid vegetation, and become uncomfortable or dangerous near hot masonry. The suit should protect against both radiant heat and sudden blasts of furnace air.

Use:

- Heat-resistant gloves with good grip  
- Hard boots with soles suited to polished stone and loose mineral powder  
- A helmet with a face shield, preferably with independent cooling  
- Ear protection or active noise reduction  
- A cooling vest or phase-change system  
- A hydration pack inside the suit, with more water than you expect to need  
- Electrolyte replacement  
- A compact emergency shelter or insulated thermal wrap  
- Redundant lamps, including low-glare lamps for preserving night vision  
- Spare batteries kept insulated from heat  
- A dustproof camera and mapping device  
- A pressure-resistant first-aid kit  

The city is not wet, but dehydration will be severe. The dry air, heat, exertion, and respirator will draw water from you quickly. Bring water for the return journey and an emergency reserve. There is no usable water in the environment, and collecting condensation is not a realistic plan.

Do not count on the local plants as food, medicine, shade, or a source of moisture. Their tissues may be ceramic, caustic, electrically active, or biologically incompatible with you.

### Protect your hearing and balance

The chamber amplifies sound. A single structural crack, pressure rupture, or mechanism slamming shut may become a citywide report. The low subterranean murmur may also make it difficult to distinguish a nearby hazard from a distant one.

Use hearing protection, but not so much that you lose all awareness of alarms and structural movement. A suit-mounted vibration sensor would be valuable. Watch for:

- Sudden changes in the low rumble  
- Vanes beginning to turn  
- Shutters moving  
- Plants flattening or widening their pores  
- Dust lifting from the floor without an obvious breeze  
- The central vent becoming visibly red  
- A delay between a sound and its returning echo  

The last sign is particularly important: if echoes become stronger, longer, or strangely delayed, you may be near a large unseen chamber, a changing pressure pocket, or an opening below the streets.

### Entering the city

Move slowly and stay on the broadest, most intact routes. The paving is old, and the city is being altered from below by mineral growth. A stairway may be supported by a living structure that is slowly lifting it. A bridge that appears sound may be weakened by heat, dust, or unseen cavities.

Avoid:

- The central vent and all direct vent channels  
- Freshly cracked paving  
- Black openings whose depth cannot be judged  
- Bridges with missing or warped supports  
- Areas beneath loaded roofs  
- Narrow ledges during a pressure pulse  
- Loose mineral plates  
- Mechanisms that are moving, even slowly  
- Plants growing directly from hot vents or kiln mouths  

Do not lean on walls merely because they look massive. The city’s masonry may be stable in compression but brittle at its joints. A small disturbance could release accumulated dust, dislodge a plate, or start a local collapse.

Keep one hand free and never separate from your guide line or partner. The darkness, dust veils, and strange perspective will make distances unreliable. A tower across the basin may look close while being separated by a deep drop or inaccessible terraces.

### Treat the vegetation as hazardous infrastructure

The plants are not passive scenery. They react rapidly to the earth’s pulses, grow through masonry, shed hard flakes, release pollen, and may be structurally integrated into roofs and bridges.

Do not touch them barehanded. Their leaves may cut like glass, carry static charge, shed irritants, or snap shut. Their glittering pollen should be treated as a serious respiratory and contamination hazard even through a filter. Avoid disturbing flowering bodies or seed-cases. A cloud released in a narrow street could blind you, clog your filters, or make the floor dangerously slippery.

Do not cut, break, or uproot anything. Some growth may be holding up a wall. Some plants may function as part of the city’s old ventilation system. Even a small injury to one could trigger a mechanical or biological response.

Keep specimens sealed if you must collect them, and quarantine all equipment before bringing it back through the multiverse. The plants are adapted to an environment without animals; they may have no defenses that resemble familiar ecology, or may possess defenses your biology cannot tolerate.

### Understand the city’s “weather”

The mineral dust is not merely inconvenient. It can abrade seals, obscure vision, damage bearings, foul filters, enter joints, and conceal holes. When a pulse begins, get under a robust roof or behind a thick structural wall, but first verify that the shelter is not adjacent to a vent or dependent on an unstable bridge.

Do not shelter in a kiln-house or storage chamber without checking for:

- A second opening or escape route  
- Heat buildup  
- Old fuel or reactive material  
- Collapsing ceilings  
- Accumulated dust  
- Mechanisms that may close automatically  

A pulse may bring hot air upward, black grains, glowing particles, and abrupt movement throughout the city. It may also create downdrafts in some passages. A place that was safe during stillness may become a wind tunnel minutes later.

The safest response to early activity is usually retreat, not curiosity. If the vanes begin turning, the plants flatten toward the vent, or the red radiance strengthens, head back toward the marked exit immediately.

### Navigation and exploration discipline

Use a strict turnaround rule. The city invites exploration because every route seems to continue behind walls, beneath terraces, or down unmapped ramps. Do not spend all your reserve reaching an interesting structure. Keep enough time, light, cooling, air, and water to return during conditions worse than those you entered.

Record:

- Temperature and pressure at each level  
- The direction and strength of airflow  
- Locations of intact shelters  
- Structural cracks and unstable bridges  
- The timing of plant and mechanism activity  
- Any changes in the central vent  
- The composition and behavior of dust  
- Signs that the floor is hollow beneath you  

Leave markers that cannot be mistaken for native objects. Bright colored tape may disappear under dust; physical tags, reflective strips, or coded light beacons are better. Avoid leaving biological material, food, or chemicals behind.

The old diagrams and measurements may be useful, but they describe a city whose physical and biological systems have continued changing without its makers. Treat every map as historical, not authoritative.

### What to do if something goes wrong

**If your air system fails:**  
Do not attempt to continue or “hold your breath” through a long passage. Seal your suit, switch to the backup supply, and return along the line. If the suit is breached, get to the nearest verified shelter and use the emergency oxygen while transmitting your position.

**If a pulse begins in the open:**  
Drop low only if there is no better shelter; protect your face, seal exposed joints, and move behind solid stone. Do not stand beneath bridges, towers, vanes, or roofless lintels. Wait for the pressure event to pass before moving, because the most dangerous structural shifts may occur after the first blast.

**If visibility disappears:**  
Stop. Do not wander through the dust. Anchor yourself to the guide line, check your instruments, and proceed only when you can confirm the route. The apparent street ahead may be a ledge or shaft.

**If you hear a collapse:**  
Assume more movement is coming. Move away from walls, bridges, and sloped debris. Watch for secondary pressure changes from newly opened chambers.

**If pollen or plant fragments enter the suit:**  
Do not open the helmet to inspect them. Isolate the contaminated layer if possible, return immediately, and quarantine the suit, samples, and equipment outside the site.

### Psychological preparation

The place will feel inhabited even though it contains no animals or people. Vanes will turn, plants will unfold, walls will groan, dust will move like surf, and old machinery will resume work without anyone directing it. Do not interpret every motion as intentional, but do not assume that apparent automation is harmless. The city’s systems have been operating for a very long time under conditions their builders may never have anticipated.

Expect spatial disorientation. There is no sky, horizon, rain, or familiar biological activity to orient you. Darkness will be warm rather than comforting. Silence will be interrupted by subterranean reports that seem to come from everywhere. Work in pairs, maintain scheduled check-ins, and establish a firm rule that either traveler may order an immediate withdrawal without debate.

### The simplest safe plan

Enter briefly during a quiet interval, remain in the upper and best-preserved streets, avoid the central vent, do not touch the plants or mechanisms, collect no unsealed samples, and leave at the first sign of a major pulse.

The city is fascinating precisely because it is not dead. It is a functioning balance of pressure, heat, mineral growth, and inherited machinery. An ordinary human should visit it as one would enter a vast kiln that has learned to garden: sealed, cooled, tethered, observant, and prepared to retreat before the earth takes its next breath.
