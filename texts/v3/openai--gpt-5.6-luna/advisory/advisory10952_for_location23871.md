## Preparation for visiting the summit city

Treat this as an expedition into a hostile, unstable environment rather than a ruined city tour. The city is not merely old or abandoned; it appears to obey local rules that can alter space, architecture, memory, and possibly causality. Your primary objectives should be:

1. Remain physically alive.
2. Avoid becoming spatially misplaced.
3. Preserve a reliable route home.
4. Avoid giving the city more intention, attention, or permanence than necessary.

Do not visit casually, alone, or without an established extraction method.

---

## Before departure

### Establish a return anchor

Your most important equipment is not clothing or food. It is a reliable way to leave.

Bring at least two independent return methods:

- A fixed portal, beacon, or extraction gate established before entry.
- A portable emergency device that does not depend on local geometry, radio, sound, line of sight, or GPS-like positioning.
- A physical or biological tether to the arrival point, if such a tether can function across dimensional distortions.
- A prearranged extraction time, with the understanding that local time may not correspond to outside time.

Do not rely on simply retracing your route. Paths can become permanent, stairways can reorient, bridges can change destinations, and the city may preserve the route while discarding the traveler’s relationship to it.

If possible, make your return anchor respond to a condition rather than a location: for example, “remove me when I activate this device,” rather than “return me to the entrance.” The entrance may no longer be where you remember it.

### Go with a small team

A group of three to five is preferable. Larger groups will be difficult to keep together in the city’s wide, silent spaces, while a lone traveler is too vulnerable to disorientation.

Assign roles:

- One navigator.
- One route recorder.
- One observer responsible for environmental changes.
- One person responsible for the return mechanism.
- One person, if available, whose only job is to watch the others for behavioral or perceptual changes.

Agree beforehand that anyone may order a retreat. Do not debate that order inside the city.

Use simple hand signals. Voices may not travel normally, and shouted communication may vanish a short distance from the speaker.

### Prepare psychologically

The city punishes strong expectations. Before arrival, avoid studying it in excessive detail or forming a vivid mental image of specific locations. The more firmly you decide what a place “must” be, the less reliably it may behave.

Train yourself to use provisional descriptions:

- “A tall structure,” not “the eastern tower.”
- “A passage that may lead upward,” not “the route to the summit.”
- “A room containing a basin-like depression,” not “the destination chamber.”

This is not merely a philosophical precaution. Expectation appears to make locations flatten, weaken, or become less real.

You should also prepare for:

- Muted or absent sound.
- Altered distance.
- Shadows moving independently of light.
- Seeing multiple possible landscapes in the basin.
- Apparent movement occurring in the observer rather than in the environment.
- The possibility that another version of the city is watching you.

Do not assume that every disturbing sight is an illusion. Do not assume that every disturbing sight is real. Act according to physical risk, not certainty.

## Clothing and personal equipment

### Protection from cold and desiccation

The environment is cold, exceptionally dry, and apparently low in breathable moisture. Prepare for rapid dehydration and damage to exposed tissue.

Wear:

- A sealed, insulated expedition suit.
- Layered thermal clothing.
- Full gloves, preferably with replaceable outer layers.
- A close-fitting hood.
- Sealed goggles or a full-face visor.
- A respirator or oxygen-assisted mask.
- A humidifying breathing system if one can operate reliably in your universe.
- Barrier cream or protective covering for skin, lips, nostrils, and eyes.

Do not expose your face simply because the air seems clear. Breath becoming powder rather than vapor suggests that moisture is being removed unusually quickly, or that exhalation is undergoing a local physical transformation.

Bring far more water than ordinary mountain travel would require. Carry it in multiple small containers rather than one large reservoir. If moisture behaves anomalously, a single container could be lost, emptied, frozen, or transformed without warning.

Do not count on finding water. The basin is not a water source. Snow, ice, frost, and condensation apparently do not occur here.

### Footwear

Use high-traction boots with rigid soles and ankle support. The streets are covered with sharp mineral powder and glass-like flakes. Some surfaces may be polished, sloped, or subtly moving.

Bring several kinds of traction:

- Replaceable crampon-like grips.
- Soft overshoes for polished stone.
- A way to detect hollow ground.
- Protective gaiters to keep powder and glass from entering clothing.

Avoid dragging ropes, chains, or long loose straps across the ground. They could become caught in cracks, vanish into changing geometry, or establish an unintended permanent path.

### Light

Do not depend on conventional illumination. There is no stable sun, and surfaces may become self-luminous or reflect things that are not physically present.

Bring:

- Several independent light sources.
- Shielded, low-intensity lamps.
- A light that can be seen by companions without flooding the environment.
- Spare batteries or an alternative energy source.
- A method of recording whether a light is illuminating the surroundings or merely making you visible.

Avoid powerful beams directed into the basin, polished walls, tower chambers, or distant darkness. Light may function as attention here.

## Navigation equipment

Ordinary maps will quickly become unreliable. Prepare a record based on events and physical sensations rather than coordinates.

Bring:

- A rugged analog notebook.
- Charcoal, chalk, or mineral markers.
- A camera with independent local storage.
- A mechanical compass, though do not trust it completely.
- A barometer, inertial unit, and simple pendulum.
- A mechanical watch and a second independent timekeeper.
- Small numbered physical markers.
- A supply of colored threads or bands for temporary identification.

Do not use the same route three times unless you deliberately want to cut that route permanently into the city. A path walked three times becomes permanent by an unknown process. This could be useful for creating an emergency road, but it could also make your presence part of the city’s structure.

If you need a permanent route, establish it deliberately, with a clear beginning, end, and retreat plan. Do not accidentally walk back and forth over the same avenue while confused.

Avoid leaving markers in patterns that resemble writing, maps, symbols, or invitations. The city may interpret intention more readily than language.

Record:

- The number of steps between landmarks.
- Whether the surface slopes.
- Which structures are visible only from certain angles.
- The condition of bridges and stairways.
- Whether the sky, shadows, or distant terraces have changed.
- Any point at which a companion’s account differs from your own.

If two people remember different routes, do not try to reconcile them by argument. Mark both accounts and return along the route that physically exists under your feet.

## Rules for moving through the city

### Do not look back on stairs

Stairs always lead upward, but “upward” can change. Looking back may reveal a descent into a district that was not previously below you.

When climbing:

- Keep your eyes forward or slightly downward.
- Count steps, but do not assume the count will remain stable.
- Have the lead climber announce changes in surface or gradient.
- Do not stop midway unless necessary.
- If you must turn around, stop on a landing or broad platform rather than on the stairs themselves.

If someone looks back and reports seeing an impossible route, do not look to confirm it. Ask them to describe only the surface immediately ahead.

### Approach doors without a specific destination

Doors open according to the destination held in the mind of the person approaching them. This is extremely dangerous.

Before approaching a door, agree on a neutral intention such as:

- “A safe interior space.”
- “The other side of this wall.”
- “A room large enough for the group.”

Do not think:

- “Take me home.”
- “Show me the person I miss.”
- “Lead me to the summit.”
- “Open into my own body.”
- “Show me what is behind the door.”

Do not let distressed or distracted team members approach doors. A person thinking of a childhood home, a dead companion, a beloved landscape, or an abstract destination could cause the doorway to open into something physically or psychologically hazardous.

If a door opens onto anything that is not plainly a safe room, close it without stepping through. Do not investigate by reaching inside.

### Keep destinations vague

The city responds poorly to certainty. Do not announce that you have found a particular tower, district, square, or landmark. Use descriptions instead.

Avoid naming a structure repeatedly. Naming may reinforce attention, and attention may alter the structure.

Do not return to a place merely to verify that it is still there. Repeated visits driven by expectation appear to degrade its reality.

### Keep the group visually connected

Distances in the city are unusually unreliable. A gap of a few hundred paces may become a separation of impossible scale.

Use short, visible intervals:

- Never allow members to disappear around corners without confirmation.
- Cross open squares as a group.
- Use lights or reflective signals rather than voices.
- Do not follow a person who appears far away until you have confirmed that it is truly a companion.
- If someone is seen in two places, approach neither version immediately.

A person who seems to be walking ahead of the group may be an echo, a displaced observer, or something imitating one of you.

## Structures and terrain

### Treat every bridge as temporary

Bridges may connect different places at different times. Their far ends can shift, disappear, or attach to another terrace.

Before crossing:

- Confirm both ends visually.
- Mark the near side.
- Cross one person at a time if the bridge is narrow.
- Do not stop in the middle.
- Do not look down unless necessary.
- Do not cross if the far end has changed while you were observing it.

If a bridge begins clicking, flexing, or altering its alignment, retreat toward the end you most recently occupied. Do not assume that the original entrance is still the safer side.

### Avoid edges

The city’s edges may overlook:

- The pale mineral continent.
- A depth with no measurable floor.
- The underside of the city.
- Buried districts.
- A different orientation of the mountain.

Never approach an edge without a physical barrier, a secured line, and a clear retreat. Do not throw objects over the side to estimate depth. They may return from above, arrive before they were thrown, or strike a place that did not exist when released.

Do not lean over to look beneath terraces. The underside may be a route, an entrance, or an active part of the city.

### Avoid the central basin

The basin at the summit should be considered a major anomalous hazard and not a scenic feature.

Do not:

- Touch the interior.
- Throw anything into it.
- Look into it for more than a brief glance.
- Use it as a navigational reference.
- Attempt to determine its depth.
- Respond to movement seen below.
- Enter it because it resembles a doorway, landscape, or alternate summit.

If you must observe it, use an indirect viewing method: a mirror, shielded camera, or limited-duration recording. Even then, inspect the recording outside the city. Do not replay it while standing near the basin.

Keep companions away from the rim. A person may misjudge whether something is happening in the basin or in themselves.

### Avoid tower chambers

The open chambers at the tops of towers appear empty but may host the unseen inhabitants inferred from membranes, powder trails, and ceiling impressions.

Do not enter a tower chamber if:

- Powder is moving upward.
- A door is open when it was previously closed.
- Black impressions appear on the ceiling.
- A translucent membrane is present.
- The room feels occupied despite being empty.
- A companion claims to see nothing but reacts as though something is nearby.

Never touch a membrane. Never attempt to photograph an unseen occupant at close range. Leave without making noise, even though the city’s sound seems suppressed.

## Local life and hazards

The slate-colored six-legged creatures should not be approached, followed, fed, or obstructed. Their movement does not match their apparent contact with the ground, so their physical reach and speed may be impossible to judge.

If one crosses your path:

- Stop.
- Remain still.
- Do not shine a light directly at it.
- Allow it to pass.
- Do not assume it has gone merely because the visible body has vanished.

The tower-dwelling entity should be treated as territorial or at least highly sensitive to intrusion. Do not enter spaces showing signs of its presence. If a membrane appears around a team member, do not tear it, touch it, or attempt to pull the person away unless they are in immediate physical danger. Withdraw from the room and initiate extraction.

The pale nodules beneath staircases and in room corners are not safe souvenirs. They may be seeds, eggs, sensors, dormant structures, or something that only resembles those things. Do not touch, pocket, relocate, or step deliberately among them. If one begins rotating, leave the area.

## Sound, communication, and perception

Sound is unreliable and may be converted into bodily sensation. A spoken message that seems to vanish might still be heard by something else.

Use visual confirmation for all important instructions. Have the receiver repeat a hand signal before acting.

Avoid striking stones, shouting, singing, or using alarms unless there is an immediate emergency. The city’s internal sounds may respond to vibration, and a sound that is inaudible to you may still travel through the mountain.

Monitor each team member for:

- Repeating the same phrase.
- Staring at a structure for too long.
- Insisting that a distant object is close.
- Claiming the group has already visited a place.
- Speaking to someone no one else can see.
- Becoming unusually certain about the route.
- Forgetting the purpose of the expedition.
- Referring to the city as if it were a person.

These may be signs of disorientation, influence, or simple exhaustion. In all cases, stop advancing and begin withdrawal.

## What to bring back

Bring nothing from the city unless it has been deliberately cleared by your expedition’s rules. In particular, do not remove:

- Pale nodules.
- Black glass fragments.
- Metallic veins.
- Powder from permanent paths.
- Membrane material.
- Objects found in the basin.
- Architectural fragments.
- Anything that rotates when unwatched.

The city may be extending through the mountain, and some apparently inert material could be part of an active structure. Contain all equipment after departure. Quarantine clothing, tools, recordings, dust, and biological material separately.

Review photographs and recordings only after confirming that all team members are physically present and accounted for. An image may show a route, figure, or structure that was not visible during the visit. Do not assume it is harmless documentation.

## Recommended expedition plan

A first visit should be short and intentionally incomplete.

1. Establish and test the return anchor.
2. Enter with no specific destination beyond a safe observation point.
3. Remain near the arrival area until the team confirms that everyone perceives the same environment.
4. Survey one avenue and one nearby building exterior.
5. Do not enter doors, towers, bridges, stairways, or the summit basin on the first excursion.
6. Record environmental changes for a fixed external duration.
7. Withdraw before fatigue, dehydration, or curiosity begins to override procedure.
8. Confirm that the return anchor brings back the entire group, not merely the person activating it.
9. Inspect all equipment and recordings outside the city.
10. Wait before planning a second visit.

If the city appears to move closer, farther away, or into a different orientation, do not attempt to correct the map. Activate the return procedure.

---

## Final rule

The city is safest when treated as a place that does not need to be understood.

Do not try to solve it, name its builders, reach the inverted settlement, determine what lies beneath the basin, or prove that its rules are consistent. Walk lightly, desire little, repeat no route accidentally, and leave before the city has had time to incorporate your presence into its arrangement.
