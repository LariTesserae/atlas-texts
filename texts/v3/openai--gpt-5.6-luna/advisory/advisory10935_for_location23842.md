## Prepare as if you are visiting an active industrial site built on the roof of an abyss

This is not a ruin. It is an operating system, a weather system, and an ecosystem at once. Treat the mountain as a living facility whose rules predate you and may not be negotiable.

### Before arrival

Do not arrive casually or alone if you can avoid it. The basin is small, but the machinery and the distances beneath it are not. Bring at least one person capable of monitoring your route and remaining at the arrival point, or leave an autonomous marker at your point of entry. Ordinary voices will not carry across the basin; plan to communicate by lamps, colored panels, or agreed gestures.

Study the local cycle before entering. You need to know:

- when the turbines reverse;
- when pressure vents open;
- whether the maintenance hatches are active;
- which areas are under load;
- whether the facility recognizes visitors as personnel, intruders, or simply another category of tolerated organism.

Do not assume that the apparent daylight cycle is reliable. The calendar and signage have already demonstrated that timekeeping here may be obsolete, local, or metaphysical. Bring independent timekeeping devices, preferably several kinds. A mechanical watch, a battery-powered clock, and a device that records elapsed time without consulting local signals would be useful.

Your mission should be conservative. Visit, observe, and leave. Do not plan to salvage machinery, collect plants, repair anything, open sealed doors, or descend through the grating unless you have a compelling reason and a route that has been tested by something expendable.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing suited to a combination of high altitude, industrial heat, cold wind, and sudden moisture:

- layered insulated clothing;
- waterproof outerwear;
- gloves with good grip and some cut and heat resistance;
- sturdy boots with non-slip soles and ankle support;
- sealed eye protection;
- a hard hat or equivalent head protection;
- a harness attached to a retractable lifeline;
- a respirator or filtered mask, at least for use during exhaust events;
- hearing protection that still allows you to perceive warning vibrations or visual alarms.

The sound is likely to be physically damaging. Ordinary earplugs may be inadequate, but full isolation may prevent you from noticing pressure changes or machinery movement. Use layered hearing protection and rely on visual monitoring as much as possible. Equip yourself with a chest-mounted pressure or vibration alarm if available.

Carry your equipment in closed, tethered containers. Nothing should be able to roll, blow, or slip through the grating. Secure straps, buckles, tools, water bottles, cameras, and loose clothing. A dropped object may not simply fall: near the western edge it may become weightless and rise into the clouds, while elsewhere it may disappear into an immeasurable shaft.

Bring redundant lighting, but do not assume light will reveal what is below the grating. Use lamps to identify your route and your companions, not to search the darkness. A ranging device may give nonsensical readings. Mark positions physically with bright, weighted, tethered tags rather than trusting maps or visual memory.

Bring enough food, water, and medical supplies to remain for several times your expected visit. The facility’s cycles are slow, and a door or pressure change may take hours to complete. Include:

- a sealed water supply;
- emergency oxygen if the atmosphere is uncertain;
- a compact first-aid kit;
- burn and cold-injury treatment;
- anti-nausea medication for vibration and altitude;
- a tether cutter, but do not use it near the western edge unless absolutely necessary;
- nonconductive tools;
- an emergency beacon with both light and radio output;
- a line reel for returning to the arrival point.

Avoid bringing active electronics that transmit constantly. The installation may interpret signals as machine commands, and repaired or exposed devices may acquire unintended functions. Keep devices shielded until needed. Do not connect anything to the facility’s power, pipes, gauges, outlets, or communication systems.

### Account for the height and atmosphere

The mountain’s true altitude is unknown. Even if the air feels breathable at the basin floor, the upper rails may be cold, thin, and violently exposed. The floor may be warm because of the machinery while the higher platforms experience severe wind chill.

Use a portable atmospheric monitor. Check oxygen, pressure, carbon monoxide, ozone, solvent vapors, and particulates. The smells of hot oil and ozone should be taken seriously rather than accepted as mere atmosphere. Exhaust winds may change abruptly, and a mist that looks harmless may carry industrial contaminants or living spores.

Never remove your eye protection during the horizontal rain or condensation events. The mist may wet only one side of every object because of a persistent directional force rather than wind. That makes it difficult to predict where exposed skin or equipment will be affected.

### Entering the basin

Arrive at a stable, enclosed structure if possible, not directly on the open grating or the highest platform. If you must arrive on the basin floor, immediately establish a three-point system:

1. a physical tether to a structural anchor;
2. a marked route back to the arrival point;
3. a visible signal that identifies you as a visitor rather than a malfunction.

Do not rely on faded arrows or emergency signs. They may describe a procedure that no longer applies, or direct you toward an exit that has been sealed for decades. Do not enter the control house merely because it is the only building with a window. The yellow strip of glass may be an observation port, a warning, or an active interface.

Before walking, inspect the grating. Look for areas where supports are corroded, where moss conceals gaps, and where water is collecting. Do not kneel, lie down, or place your hands into the openings. The blackness beneath is not behaving like ordinary shadow. Treat it as a material hazard.

Remain well inside the basin unless you have a specific reason to approach the rim. The western edge is especially dangerous. Do not carry or place any object across it. Do not test the floating effect with a stone, coin, tool, or drone. The object may rise, draw something else after it, or become impossible to retrieve. Keep your own harness anchored before approaching any edge, and do not let a companion stand behind you with unsecured gear.

### Do not interfere with the machinery

The machinery is not dormant, broken, or waiting for a technician. It is maintaining itself according to rules you do not understand.

Do not:

- touch moving belts, chains, pistons, valves, or counterweights;
- stand beneath the crane cable;
- walk between pressure vessels and the wall;
- lean against pipes;
- open a hatch;
- turn a wheel or lever;
- reset a warning light;
- remove moss from a joint;
- scrape a gauge;
- reconnect a cable;
- attempt to restore power;
- repair any device.

The rule concerning repeated repairs is particularly dangerous. A normal traveler has no reliable way to know what counts as a “repair,” whether the machine remembers separate people, or what “new function” means. A third intervention could cause a pump to emit light, a door to record or anticipate your footsteps, or a gauge to display information that compromises your route home.

If you find a damaged machine, photograph it from a distance and leave it alone. If a door opens for you, do not assume it is an invitation. If a door closes behind you, wait rather than forcing it. The system may be timing itself, not trapping you.

### Respect the plants

The vegetation is not incidental overgrowth. It is part of the installation’s operating ecology and may be connected through roots, insulation, vibration, and power conduits.

Do not cut, uproot, burn, or collect anything. Do not take seeds, flowers, moss, or pieces of vine as souvenirs. While the engines are running, a severed plant may knit itself back together, potentially around your blade, hand, or equipment. The plants also may be sensors, heat sinks, pressure indicators, or structural components.

Avoid walking through dense growth. Follow bare metal or existing service paths. If you must pass near the bell-shaped flowering plant, observe turbine activity first. Its opening may indicate increased load, but it may also be a warning signal. Do not put your head beneath the bloom. A plant large enough to shelter a person’s head may have evolved specifically to interact with pressure, heat, spores, or living animals.

Do not drink condensation or pool water. It may be clean, but it has passed over machinery, wiring, and possibly biological systems. Collect no water unless you can sterilize it and confirm that it does not respond anomalously to turbine cycles.

### Treat the animals as part of the rules

The small creatures, moths, birds, and grazers are not necessarily hostile, but they are adapted to conditions you are not.

Do not disturb:

- the glowing moths around indicator lamps;
- the synchronized cable-drum colonies;
- the birds beneath the crane counterweight;
- the translucent grazer;
- any animal emerging from a hatch.

Especially do not block the grazer’s route. It appears to move through a permitted circuit, and the facility changes its behavior when the animal passes: sensors dim and doors unlock. That implies the grazer is recognized by the system. You are not. Stand aside, remain still, and let it cross.

Do not follow it into a maintenance hatch. The passage may not lead to the same place for you, and the animal’s tolerance by the machinery does not extend to human travelers.

### Learn the turbine cycle before crossing open ground

The worst moment to be in the middle of the basin is during a reversal. When the turbines reverse, every drop of water rises and hangs in the air. This is not merely a picturesque effect. It means the local direction of gravity, pressure, or flow is changing.

Before moving between towers, identify visual or mechanical signs of an approaching reversal:

- turbines slowing or changing pitch;
- pressure gauges moving unusually slowly or backward;
- vines lifting from their supports;
- condensation beginning to detach;
- warning horns felt through the chest;
- birds abandoning their usual bolts;
- the flowering plant opening or closing suddenly.

When a reversal begins, stop moving. Get low if there is any chance of falling, secure yourself to a fixed structure, and keep away from edges and suspended loads. Do not reach for floating droplets. Do not assume that a tether will remain under tension. Wait until water has resumed its normal downward movement and all machinery has returned to its ordinary cycle.

The warning horns may be felt rather than heard. A sudden pressure in the chest is a warning even if no audible signal follows. Agree beforehand that anyone who feels it will immediately halt and display the emergency signal.

### Communication and navigation

Because voices cannot cross the basin, use simple, redundant signals:

- red light: stop;
- white light: proceed only if the route is clear;
- amber light: machinery cycle or uncertain condition;
- three flashes: return;
- continuous light: emergency.

Keep companions within visual range. Do not separate at corners or behind towers. If someone disappears from sight, do not run toward them. Stop, mark your position, and use the signal system.

Map by fixed structures, not by the apparent position of the sky or clouds. Record the locations of every pressure vessel, ladder, hatch, tower, and railing. The basin may be physically small but perceptually enlarged by sound, darkness, and hidden distances.

Assume that paths can change function without changing appearance. A ladder may remain a ladder but lead to a different level after a pressure event. A repaired door may remember footsteps. A gauge may reveal weather or geography from elsewhere in the mountain. Record anomalies, but do not test them.

### The control house and the helmets

The moss-filled helmets are a warning. They indicate that protective equipment left behind is being colonized, not that the facility is safe for visitors. Do not wear one. Do not disturb the moss. Do not assume the helmets belonged to workers who died there; they may have been abandoned, grown, or placed as part of a system you do not understand.

The control house should be treated as a potentially active control node. If you enter, do so only with an escape route already secured. Avoid the yellow glass, all consoles, switches, and paperwork. Do not attempt to correct the calendar. Do not use your own date to overwrite local records. A place that contains a month absent from your universe may not interpret correction as harmless.

### Leaving

Leave before fatigue becomes significant. The constant vibration will impair balance, hearing, judgment, and the ability to distinguish ordinary noises from alarms. Do not wait for “one more cycle” if the machinery begins doing something unfamiliar.

Before departure:

- confirm that every person is accounted for;
- recover only equipment that is clearly yours and safely tethered;
- leave all plants, animals, tools, and machinery undisturbed;
- check that no condensation or living growth has attached itself to your clothing;
- inspect your harness, boots, and pack;
- compare your independent clocks;
- verify the location of the return route;
- make sure your departure point is not on a platform whose function has changed.

Do not take the mountain’s silence as permission to stay. In this place, silence is merely the interval between cycles.

The correct mindset is not that you are entering a factory reclaimed by nature. You are entering a mountain that has made a factory into its metabolism. The engines, plants, animals, water, and hidden depths are all participating in the same old mechanism. Move slowly, touch little, secure everything, obey changes in pressure and vibration, and leave with fewer questions than you arrived with—because attempting to answer them may count as maintenance.
