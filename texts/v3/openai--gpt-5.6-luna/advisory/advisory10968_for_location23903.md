## Prepare as though you are entering a flooded construction site, a high-altitude cave, and an unstable physics anomaly at the same time.

This is not a normal underwater cavern. Ordinary cave-diving procedures will keep you alive only if they are supplemented by mountaineering, confined-space rescue, and anomaly precautions. The safest visit is a short, heavily instrumented observation mission conducted from a stable upper shelf. Do not plan to explore the whole chamber, reach the central shaft, or construct anything permanent.

### 1. Prepare for the approach

Find the mountain from above, not from the apparent level of the chamber. The exterior fissure is unstable and may close or relocate while you are looking at it.

Bring:

- A climbing team and fixed ropes outside the entrance
- Multiple independent anchors placed well away from the seam
- A marked retreat route to the ledge and cloud basin
- High-altitude clothing, gloves, eye protection, and breathing support
- Oxygen or a supplemental breathing system for the approach and exit
- A way to monitor barometric pressure, temperature, and the movement of the rock
- No explosives, drills, or heavy impacts

The ledge beyond the fissure may be more dangerous than the chamber. Expect low oxygen, violent wind, rapid weather changes, and no useful vegetation or soil. Establish a sheltered base below the exposed ledge if possible. Do not leave equipment where wind can carry it into the cloud basin.

The entrance should be observed for several hours, preferably across changing light conditions. Record when the seam opens, how long it remains passable, and whether the surrounding rock moves in a pattern. A small autonomous probe should enter first. If the probe does not return, do not send a person immediately; the passage may have closed behind it, or distance may have changed.

### 2. Do not enter without a redundant tether system

The water, gravity, and apparent distance cannot be trusted. Use at least two independent lines:

1. A primary climbing line anchored outside the chamber
2. A secondary safety line anchored to a separate point
3. A personal tether to the route line or to a guide line placed by the team

Mark the line at frequent intervals with physical tags and active beacons. Do not rely on your memory of the route. A corridor may lengthen, shorten, tilt, or become blocked while you are in it.

The line must be strong enough for a falling person and abrasion-resistant against newly formed stone. Protect it from sharp shelves and moving cracks. Do not anchor to anything that looks newly warm, translucent, hollow, or crystalline. Those surfaces may harden under touch, shift under load, or detach without a visible fracture.

Avoid knots or devices that require gravity to function in a particular direction. Use locking systems that remain secure if pulled upward, sideways, or suddenly unloaded.

### 3. Use a sealed breathing system, not ordinary scuba

Although the water is brightly lit, visibility is not the main problem. The currents reverse without warning, and bubbles may descend, hang motionless, or move sideways. A conventional open-circuit scuba setup will produce an unreliable bubble trail and may make entanglement more likely.

Use a closed-circuit or rebreather system with:

- Several hours of reserve gas
- An independent emergency cylinder
- A full-face mask or helmet
- A dry suit with hard protection over the limbs
- A rigid or semi-rigid helmet with face and throat protection
- Redundant depth, pressure, and inertial instruments
- A physical breathing-gas indicator that does not depend on normal gravity

The water’s depth may not correspond consistently to pressure. Test the gas system in a controlled section before proceeding. Bring a pressure-resistant, self-contained emergency hood in case the primary breathing equipment is damaged by stone or turbulence.

Do not remove your mask to taste or sample the water. Its chemistry is unknown, and mineral vents may release irritants, toxic gases, or superheated fluid. Keep all skin covered. The chamber’s “fresh” water may not be biologically or chemically compatible with ordinary human tissue.

### 4. Protect yourself from the rock

The newly exposed surfaces are more dangerous than old cave walls. They may be razor-sharp, brittle, hot from below, or deceptively soft. Wear:

- Cut- and puncture-resistant gloves
- Reinforced boots with replaceable soles
- Knee, elbow, and shin protection
- A buoyant but controllable harness
- A helmet with a face shield
- Thermal layers beneath the dry suit
- A medical kit for lacerations, crush injuries, hypothermia, and decompression-like symptoms

Do not touch a soft-looking surface casually. It may crystallize around your hand or tighten after contact. Use a remote probe to test any ledge before putting weight on it.

Keep your body away from cracks and mineral seams. A crack may widen while you are holding it, or a warm white seam may emit a sudden jet of water or gas. Never place your hand inside a fissure to judge its depth.

### 5. Treat gravity as local and temporary

Carry instruments that detect acceleration and orientation, but do not trust them completely. Combine them with physical indicators:

- Weighted pendulums in multiple orientations
- Floating markers
- Tension sensors on the tether
- Small objects released only in controlled conditions
- A wrist-mounted inertial recorder
- A constant contact line

Before moving through open water, observe the behavior of a test marker for at least a minute. A stone may remain suspended and then accelerate unexpectedly. A person who begins to fall may strike a shelf before the motion is visually apparent.

Avoid jumping, pushing off, or swimming directly across open spaces. Move by hand-over-hand progress along a secure line whenever possible. Keep your tether short enough that a gravity reversal cannot fling you into a wall or shaft.

Do not assume “down” is where the floor is. The strongest gravity may be near a newly forming surface, while open water may pull in another direction.

### 6. Avoid the water’s memory effects

The water appears to retain outlines and traces of recently introduced objects. This may be harmless, but it could also create persistent currents, false obstacles, or physical imitations capable of interfering with you.

For that reason:

- Do not drop stones, tools, flares, or personal objects
- Do not repeatedly move your hands through the water
- Keep deliberate gestures to a minimum in confined passages
- Do not test the phenomenon with your own body
- Retrieve every piece of equipment you can safely recover
- If a hollow outline remains after an object is removed, withdraw and wait rather than passing through it

Use a small number of preselected markers instead of improvising. Their shapes and materials should be documented before entry. Do not throw anything toward the central shaft.

### 7. Do not rely on speech or ordinary radio

Voices do not return as words. They return as pressure and vibration, sometimes in a tone that is not yours. This creates a serious communication and psychological hazard.

Use:

- Tactile signal lines
- Light signals with a fixed code
- Short-range digital transmitters with bone-conduction or helmet-mounted receivers
- Independent timing beacons
- Prearranged emergency signals that do not require speech

Do not answer sounds from the walls. Do not interpret a returning tone as a person calling, a guide, or an instruction. If a team member reports hearing their own voice—or another voice—treat it as an environmental event and confirm all instructions through the agreed visual or tactile system.

Because the chamber is deafening, maintain constant line contact. A person who loses the team may be unable to shout effectively, and the sound may be distorted or returned from the wrong direction.

### 8. Stay away from the active water-making zones

The narrow columns, sideways jets, hanging globes, and sudden local tides are likely to be the most dangerous features in the chamber. Do not attempt to cross in front of them merely because the current looks manageable.

Watch each zone for a long period before approaching. Record:

- Direction of flow
- Reversal interval
- Changes in turbulence
- Temperature
- Mineral discharge
- Whether nearby gravity changes when the flow changes

Assume every hanging globe is about to collapse. Assume every apparently calm patch can become a current. Keep at least one retreat route open at all times; never allow the team to be trapped between two active walls or a shifting shelf.

If the entire room begins moving, abandon exploration and follow the tether out. Do not attempt to “wait it out” on an untested ledge.

### 9. Treat the living things as unknown and fragile

The translucent animals may not be dangerous, but their nearly invisible bodies and threadlike feeding structures make accidental contact likely. Do not pursue, capture, illuminate intensely, or touch them.

The green, white, and iron-red growth should be treated as an isolated ecosystem. It may be adapted to the mineral vents and could be chemically hazardous. Do not collect samples without a sealed, remote sampling system and explicit permission from whoever governs the location. Even a small disturbance may destroy one of the chamber’s few established biological footholds.

If a larger pale shape passes overhead, do not follow it. Its movement may indicate a larger connected body of water, a pressure change, or an entity for which the chamber is only a passage.

### 10. Treat the primitive structures as warnings, not infrastructure

The platform, stakes, bowl, tools, and cords are not proof that the chamber is habitable. They are proof that someone reached it and left behind objects that have survived under unusual conditions.

Do not climb onto the platform until every stone has been tested. Do not trust the stakes as anchors. Their notches may record changes in the chamber, tides, visitors, or something else entirely. Photograph and map them before approaching.

The black crystals in the bowl deserve particular caution. Since they continuously rearrange themselves and indicate different cracks, they may be responding to water flow, mineral fields, seismic stress, or the presence of living observers. Do not touch, pocket, scatter, or reposition them. If they move toward your equipment, withdraw the equipment rather than disturbing the bowl.

Do not add your own stake, mark, offering, or inscription. The existing builders understood that permanence was foolish here. Follow their example.

### 11. Never approach the central shaft

The shaft is the obvious focus of the chamber and the least suitable place for an ordinary visitor.

Water moving in both directions at once suggests conflicting pressure, flow, or geometry. The ring around it is narrow, partly smooth, and partly serrated. The stakes may be counting time, eruptions, water reversals, visitors, or stages in the mountain’s formation. You will not know which before standing there, and standing there may alter the count.

Establish a hard exclusion zone well before the ring. Do not throw objects down the shaft. Do not lower a camera, line, drone, or instrument unless you are prepared to lose it and possibly change the behavior of the shaft. Do not step across the stake line, even if it appears stable.

The shaft may be the place where the chamber is still forming most rapidly. A slight change in the mountain could turn the ring into a falling slab, a rising column, or a sealed pocket.

### 12. Plan a short visit with a strict abort schedule

The chamber is not a place to spend a full day collecting observations. Set a maximum interior time before entering, and obey it even if nothing has gone wrong.

Withdraw immediately if:

- The entrance seam changes shape
- The tether loses tension or begins pulling in an unexpected direction
- A familiar surface changes texture or position
- The light from above becomes colorless, red, or absent
- A current reverses outside its observed pattern
- A new crack opens
- The water retains a human-shaped outline
- The black crystals form a line toward your route
- Any team member becomes confused, disoriented, or unable to distinguish signals
- The chamber’s sound changes from continuous turbulence to an unfamiliar quiet

The brief “silence” behind an exposed wall is not a safe resting period. It is likely a pressure transition. If the water withdraws from a wall, move away from that wall and prepare for its return.

### 13. Prepare for the exit as carefully as the entry

The exterior ledge will require high-altitude climbing immediately after a cold, exhausting immersion. Have dry clothing, warmed oxygen, shelter, and medical support ready outside. Do not remove the breathing system or climb into the wind while wet.

Check all personnel for:

- Hypothermia
- Cuts contaminated by unknown water
- Ear, sinus, lung, or pressure injuries
- Confusion or memory gaps
- Unusual skin crystallization, numbness, or irritation
- Symptoms appearing after return to normal gravity

Quarantine water, organisms, crystals, and mineral fragments in sealed containers. Ideally, bring no biological material out at all. Clean and isolate every suit, line, camera, and tool before taking it into another environment.

### 14. The appropriate mindset

Enter as an observer, not an explorer.

The mountain is not finished, the chamber has no stable geometry, and the water behaves less like a passive substance than like an active process. Every shelf may be temporary. Every route may become a trap. The safest human achievement here is not reaching the farthest point; it is leaving with the team intact and the location unchanged.

A successful visit would consist of:

1. Establishing a secure external base  
2. Sending in remote instruments  
3. Entering only with redundant lines and sealed breathing gear  
4. Remaining near a known upper shelf  
5. Mapping currents, light, and structural changes  
6. Avoiding the central shaft and all active vents  
7. Disturbing nothing living or constructed  
8. Returning before the mountain’s behavior has time to become familiar  

Do not mistake familiarity for safety. In this chamber, the most dangerous moment may be the one when the water seems calm, the light seems clear, and the unfinished mountain appears to have accepted you.
