## Before departure

Treat the plateau as a **high-altitude industrial environment with unstable geography and unreliable local physics**, not as an ordinary city.

Arrange contact with a resident guide, ideally one attached to the waterworks, railway authority, or municipal administration. Do not arrive alone and do not assume that a map, clock, compass, or doorway will continue to behave consistently. Your guide should meet you at the arrival point and establish:

- A safe lodging building whose dimensions have been checked recently.
- A route to the nearest medical facility and water station.
- The current locations of active construction, new cracks, blocked tunnels, and altered streets.
- The local interpretation of noon, curfew, rainfall, and machinery schedules.
- A reliable return point or portal, with at least two backup markers.

Have the guide teach you the city’s current rules. These rules are not superstition; they are the accumulated equivalent of engineering codes and public-health regulations.

## Physical preparation

### Altitude and climate

The plateau is higher than ordinary weather should permit. Even if the air appears breathable, prepare for:

- Low oxygen or altered atmospheric pressure.
- Severe dryness interrupted by sudden cold rain.
- Rapid temperature changes between exposed shelves, enclosed factories, and wet hollows.
- Strong ultraviolet exposure through diffuse cloud, if the local atmosphere permits it.
- Cold surfaces and dampness beneath bridges, even when the air feels dry.

Bring acclimatization medication appropriate to your physiology, supplemental oxygen, a pulse oximeter or equivalent, and a compact pressure or breathing reserve. Arrive with several days available for acclimatization rather than immediately climbing, touring, or entering industrial districts.

Wear layered clothing that remains insulating when wet. A waterproof outer shell, gloves with good grip, sealed boots, eye protection, and a helmet are advisable even in settled parts of the city. The stone is freshly exposed and sharp; ordinary rock faces may shed angular fragments without warning. Do not rely on the apparent stability of a ledge merely because it has supported a road or building. The plateau is still rising.

### Respiratory protection

Carry a respirator capable of handling fine mineral dust, industrial particulates, and the dark emissions that descend from smokestacks. Those emissions may not be ordinary smoke. They collect in low places and alleys, so avoid walking through them even if visibility remains acceptable.

Bring a sealed reserve of food and water for at least several days. Do not depend on local plants, mushrooms, animal products, or atmospheric collectors until they have been cleared by a resident authority. The local ecology appears vigorous but is not necessarily compatible with human digestion or immune systems.

## Water precautions

Water is one of the greatest hazards.

Do not drink from an unattended puddle, reservoir margin, temporary pool, or seep in the stone. Purification tablets, boiling, and filters may remove organisms but will not correct a physical irregularity. In particular, avoid any water that:

- Reflects a sky different from the one above you.
- Reflects you late, early, or with a different posture.
- Remains in a place that slopes sharply uphill.
- Shows movement after the animal, person, or object producing the reflection has gone.
- Appears deeper or shallower than its physical depression.
- Has no visible surface disturbance when rain falls into it.

Use sealed local water supplied by the guide, and carry your own emergency supply. Keep bottles labeled and physically attached to your person. Do not leave open water beside a crack, threshold, tunnel entrance, or machinery.

Rain itself is not necessarily dangerous, but it can expose new channels, alter surfaces, and make temporary pools. Sudden vertical rain may begin and end almost instantly. Shelter under properly inspected structures rather than beneath an unfamiliar bridge; the undersides may accumulate hanging sheets of water, and condensation may not obey gravity.

## Navigation and personal equipment

Use redundant navigation systems:

- A physical map annotated by a local guide.
- A short-range radio or equivalent communicator.
- A compass, even if you expect it to fail.
- A mechanical watch, plus a second independent timekeeping device.
- Chalk, paint markers, reflective tags, and physical route lines.
- A tether for industrial sites, cliff routes, and tunnels.
- A written log of every door, turn, level, and time.

Do not depend solely on digital maps, GPS, automated building directories, elevator displays, or spoken directions. Streets can contract over distance. Buildings can contain more internal floors than their exteriors allow. Elevators may stop at removed floors. A route that worked yesterday may terminate in a wall, a new room, or a different side of the plateau today.

Mark your route physically, but inspect each mark when returning. A chalk arrow may remain while the door or corridor it points toward changes. Use numbered tags that contain no spoken component; local door numbers may change when read aloud.

Keep your group together. Establish a rule that no one follows a voice, reflection, light, or apparent companion without visual confirmation from another traveler. Use a buddy system with regular physical check-ins. If a member of the group hears their own voice from a tunnel or service corridor, do not investigate and do not answer.

## Clothing and conduct around shadows

The shadows in the city are not reliable indicators of light, position, or ownership.

Avoid stepping into dark outlines of absent buildings, walls, stairs, or windows in the open lots. Treat them as marked hazardous areas even if local workers cannot explain the danger. Do not place tools, bags, cameras, or dropped equipment inside them. If something falls into one, do not reach in after it. Mark the location, notify your guide, and accept that retrieval may require specialized local procedures.

Check yourself and your companions after passing beneath awnings, gantries, bridges, and towers. A person may cast more than one shadow, and shadows may lag behind movement. This is not necessarily an emergency, but report persistent discrepancies to your guide and leave the area calmly.

Avoid photographing or filming anomalies at close range if doing so requires remaining in place. The city is not a sightseeing environment where observation is risk-free. Keep moving through exposed areas and do not allow curiosity to turn a transient irregularity into an extended encounter.

## Industrial districts

Assume every machine is active, even if it has no workers, no lights, and no obvious power source.

Do not enter:

- A sealed factory.
- An abandoned processing hall with moving pistons.
- The ore railway’s path or turning loop.
- Crane operating zones.
- Unsupervised freight elevators.
- Areas beneath descending smokestack emissions.
- Substations occupied by copper-colored insects.
- Any building whose interior dimensions do not match its exterior.

Gantry cranes and empty trains follow schedules that may be older than the current city. They may move without warning and may not respond to ordinary controls. Stay well outside marked machinery corridors. Do not walk between railcars, stand on the inner side of the northern rim track, or attempt to board the empty ore train.

At substations, keep away from the insects and their geometric formations. Their patterns may affect the electrical grid, clocks, elevators, and possibly other systems. Carry a flashlight with an isolated power source, but do not shine it directly into transformers or unusual electrical lattices.

Use analog equipment where possible. Assume that clocks can lose minutes or gain days, and that electronic records may reflect the altered time rather than the time you experienced. Every important event should be recorded in writing with a physical sequence marker, such as “before second rainfall” or “after western bells,” not just with a timestamp.

## Cracks, ledges, and changing ground

Never cross a newly formed crack. Ask when it appeared and whether it has survived **three rainfalls**. If no one knows, treat it as new.

Do not cross an unverified ledge, road, bridge, or retaining wall merely because machinery, grass, or another person appears to have crossed it. The land is still being formed. Its surfaces are clean and sharp because they have not undergone normal weathering, and the absence of rubble does not mean the ground is stable.

Carry no assumption that debris will behave normally. Collapsed walls may remain in deliberate stacks; machinery may settle into arrangements that look intentional. Do not dismantle piles or move stones to make a route easier. They may be holding something in place, or they may be part of the city’s current structural equilibrium.

## Plants and animals

Do not touch the silver-gray trees, especially their roots, and do not lean against concrete that has become soft or porous around them. The trees may be altering materials rather than merely growing through them.

Avoid the pale ferns, mushrooms, suspended cliff vegetation, and any growth found farther along a rock face than it was previously recorded. Collect no specimens. Bring sealed containers only for approved samples, and expect strict decontamination on departure.

The six-legged grazers are not necessarily aggressive, but their lack of fear makes them dangerous around machinery. Give herds a wide berth. Do not feed them or step through their pellets; the pellets sprout rapidly and may carry unfamiliar organisms.

The larger ravine animals should be observed only from a stable, elevated distance during daylight or full artificial illumination. Never approach their reflections in temporary pools. If a reflection remains after the animal has left, leave the pool and its banks immediately.

The black-winged birds are best treated as environmental sensors or warning signs. Their muttering may resemble speech, but do not interpret it, imitate it, or respond to it.

## Tunnels and doors

For an ordinary visitor, the deepest tunnels should be considered off-limits.

They are not merely old infrastructure. They contain corridors with inconsistent materials, doors opening onto other climates or locations, and numbering systems that change when spoken. Enter only under formal local escort, with a tether, physical route markers, a fixed return protocol, and a group large enough to maintain a watch at the entrance.

Do not open an unassigned door. Do not read door numbers aloud. Do not enter a room that contains weather unless the guide has specifically identified it as safe and has confirmed that the weather is local, temporary, and reversible.

If a door opens onto a place that resembles somewhere you know, assume it is not that place until proven otherwise. Do not step through to retrieve a familiar object, follow a familiar person, or escape an uncomfortable corridor.

## Time and routine

The plateau’s day is difficult to establish. Use local bells, lighting changes, guide instructions, and agreed physical markers rather than relying on your own watch.

At noon, stand clear of the western pumping station unless you have a reason to be there. The bells may answer from underground. Do not follow answering bells, and do not assume an underground response indicates a person or functioning station.

Plan outings around short, predictable intervals. Return before dusk if possible; dusk does not properly finish, and the extended low light is when insects gather, grid irregularities become more common, and reflections in wet ground become harder to interpret.

Do not remain in one anomalous place waiting for it to “settle.” Change is slow here but continuous. A room, street, tree, or factory may be different when you return, even if only by one wall, one window, or one floor.

## Arrival and departure protocol

Arrive in an open, surveyed area with clear boundaries—not inside a building, beside a cliff, on a rail line, or near a puddle. Immediately verify:

1. That all travelers arrived.
2. That each person casts the expected number of shadows.
3. That no one has acquired an unexplained object, injury, voice, or reflection.
4. That the arrival point remains in the same physical location.
5. That the return mechanism responds to a prearranged test.

Keep a written headcount and equipment inventory. Before departure, perform a quarantine inspection. Do not bring back soil, moss, insects, mushrooms, plant fragments, water, machine components, or animal matter. Check clothing seams, boot treads, bags, and hair. The local growth spreads without anyone seeing it spread.

If the return point has moved, do not chase it across the city. Stop, establish a protected camp, contact the guide, and use the backup markers. A missing portal is a navigation emergency, not an invitation to explore.

## Recommended minimum kit

- Layered waterproof clothing and insulated gloves.
- Helmet, eye protection, sturdy boots, and climbing harness.
- Respirator and spare filters.
- Supplemental oxygen and altitude medical supplies.
- Several days of sealed food and water.
- Water-testing and purification equipment, used only after visual inspection.
- Two independent lights, preferably one mechanical or chemically powered.
- Analog watch, backup timer, notebook, pencils, and permanent markers.
- Physical maps, route tags, chalk, cord, and a safety tether.
- Radio or other communication device with non-electronic fallback signals.
- First-aid kit, thermal blanket, and emergency shelter.
- Sealed containers and decontamination supplies for departure.
- A local guide, written permission, and a clearly defined extraction plan.

The safest way to visit is to remain in the inhabited upper tiers, travel by inspected routes, use approved water, avoid all tunnels and unstaffed industrial structures, and let the city be strange without trying to make it explain itself.

This is a place that is still becoming solid. Its dangers are not mainly violent or fast. They are the dangers of assuming that a surface, boundary, reflection, schedule, doorway, or shadow has finished deciding what it is.
