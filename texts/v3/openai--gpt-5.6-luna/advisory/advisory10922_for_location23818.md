## Prepare as though you are visiting a high-altitude industrial aquarium with uncertain physics

Do not treat this as a wet building or a mountain with some flooded rooms. Treat it as a **pressurized, self-contained environmental machine** whose water, atmosphere, weather, plants, and architecture are all parts of one operating system. The site may be habitable, but it is not naturally hospitable.

### Before arrival

Bring a remote probe if possible. It should measure:

- Atmospheric pressure, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and unknown gases
- Water temperature, salinity, acidity, dissolved gases, and biological activity
- Local gravity and buoyancy
- Radiation and ultraviolet exposure
- Pressure gradients around membranes, towers, and flooded chambers
- Electromagnetic fields and power sources
- Whether clocks, cameras, and recorded images behave consistently
- Any anomalous relationship between observation and plant growth

Send the probe ahead and leave it running for at least one complete machine cycle. Some processes operate over hours or days. A ten-minute walk across the site tells you almost nothing about what it does.

Bring at least two independent ways to return. Do not depend on a door, elevator, beacon, or visible boundary. The mountain may be newly formed, and the surrounding vapor may conceal a vertical drop, an unfinished edge, or a transition into another physical environment.

### Clothing and life support

Wear a **sealed, thermally regulated exploration suit**, even if the air initially appears breathable.

The suit should include:

- Independent oxygen supply
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing
- Positive internal pressure
- Full-face protection and eye protection
- Waterproof gloves and boots
- A tether or retractable safety line
- A hard helmet with lights and a heads-up display
- Chemical and biological filtration
- A compact buoyancy-control system
- Redundant locator beacons
- A knife or cutter that works underwater and on flexible materials
- A mechanical pressure gauge, not just electronic sensors

The warm, humid atmosphere may feel comfortable while being oxygen-poor, chemically active, or saturated with gases emitted by the plants and furnaces. The water may contain dissolved gases that are harmless to the machinery but dangerous to human lungs and blood. Do not breathe it merely because it is clear, warm, or visibly full of healthy vegetation.

If you need to enter the water, use a proper diving system rather than relying on swimming goggles and an air tank. The water is several meters deep in places, but its apparent depth cannot be trusted. A corridor that looks short may be optically elongated; a shaft that looks shallow may continue farther than its visible bottom. Use a physical depth line and maintain contact with a known structure.

Avoid unnecessary changes in pressure. The water may be under local pressure even when it looks placid, and membranes may separate regions with different pressures. Move slowly through thresholds. Never pass through a doorway or membrane while holding your breath. Never assume that a calm surface means equal pressure on both sides.

### Protection from the environment

The altitude is a serious concern even if the air is warm. You may encounter:

- Low atmospheric pressure
- High ultraviolet exposure
- Sudden loss of breathable air near the site’s edges
- Extreme cold outside the artificial environment
- Dangerous vapor or aerosol from the mountain
- Unstable freshly formed rock beneath the engineered shell

Carry sun and radiation protection beneath the helmet. Keep your suit insulated even if the site feels tropical; engineered water systems often have strong local thermal gradients, and a warm wall can stand beside a dangerously cold chamber.

Do not touch exposed water, roots, algae, flowering structures, or accumulated particulate matter. The system may be using unfamiliar minerals, engineered organisms, catalytic compounds, or active nanostructures. The pale coating that settles on machinery may not be dust. It may be material being incorporated into the architecture.

Use sealed sample containers. Do not bring samples into your suit, shelter, or return vehicle until they have been isolated and tested.

### First approach

Approach from above or from a stable external platform, not by landing directly on the crowded summit. There is no spare surface. Every apparent landing place may be:

- A thin membrane
- A pressure cap
- A growing bed
- A concealed vent
- A load-bearing structure with machinery beneath it
- A surface that is only visually solid because of the water

Do not land on roofs, transparent spans, broad leaves, or the upper surfaces of the water. Establish your own anchor point first.

From a distance, map the site in layers:

1. Outer membranes and their attachment towers  
2. The highest pressure structures  
3. Upper walkways and dry platforms  
4. Flooded galleries and visible chambers  
5. The central court and tree-like structure  
6. Pipes, drains, vents, turbines, and circulation machinery  

Mark your route physically and digitally. Use bright, inert tags that do not emit heat, chemicals, or strong electromagnetic signals. Do not rely on memory. The water will distort distance and orientation, and reflections may not correspond to your actual position.

### How to move

Walk slowly and keep three points of contact wherever possible. The surfaces will be slick, densely obstructed, and crossed by pipes, vines, cables, and narrow service ladders. Do not step on vegetation unless you have positively identified it as structural. Many plants are tethered to circulation systems and may be under tension.

Stay away from:

- Rotating drums
- Pumps and pistons
- Pressure doors
- Black reservoirs
- Unmarked vents
- Pipes with condensation or vibration
- Root beds with mechanical lifting arms
- Any surface that becomes transparent, reflective, or visually inconsistent
- Areas where water appears to hang unsupported

The machinery moves slowly, but slowness is not safety. A piston that takes an hour to extend can crush a person just as effectively as one that moves quickly. Some systems may operate on long cycles and may not react to your presence until much later.

Do not block channels, grilles, gutters, roots, or seed-collection systems. The installation appears to have no unused space because it has no unused space. A narrow passage may be an active air, water, heat, or reproductive pathway.

### Interact with as little as possible

Assume that every object has a function, including things that look decorative or dead. The site produces conditions rather than obvious products. A seemingly empty pipe may be moving atmosphere. A plant may be regulating pressure. A pool of clear water may be part of a heat exchanger. A blinking light may be an instruction intended for a process rather than a person.

Do not:

- Cut vines
- Pick flowers
- Open sealed panels
- Turn valves
- Disconnect cables
- Feed, water, or touch roots
- Redirect falling water
- Enter a chamber simply because its door is open
- Attempt to “repair” anything
- Shut down a machine because it appears abandoned

In particular, do not interfere with the central tree-like structure. It is the clearest sign that the installation’s current state is changing. The braces around it suggest that the system is accommodating its growth rather than controlling it. It may be a reactor, a pump, a biological computer, an atmospheric organ, or the site’s primary life-support mechanism.

Keep a generous distance from the canopy and roots. Do not place equipment against the glass around the court. If the structure reacts to your presence, withdraw rather than investigate.

### Handling the strange visual behavior

Use redundant observation methods:

- Direct human sight
- Ordinary video
- Infrared or ultraviolet imaging
- A physical measuring line
- Two synchronized clocks
- A simple mechanical marker or pendulum

Do not trust reflections, depth perception, or transparent surfaces. If a reflection moves before the object does, stop moving and observe. If a room appears behind a metal wall, do not attempt to pass through or open that wall. It may be an optical event, a delayed state, or an actual overlap with another version of the installation.

Avoid repeatedly staring at growing organisms to test whether observation affects them. The reported growth behavior is a warning, not an invitation. If the plants accelerate, alter shape, close, bloom, or redirect roots while watched, record the event from a distance and leave the area.

Do not sleep inside the works. The site’s cycles are too slow to notice easily, and a room that is safe at arrival may be flooded, heated, sealed, or repurposed several hours later. Maintain a watch schedule and compare conditions against your original map.

### Communication and records

Use low-power, short-range communications first. A large radio transmission could interfere with the installation or attract a response from something beyond the membranes. Record environmental data continuously, but also keep handwritten notes. If electronic records begin showing impossible sequences, duplicate them on independent systems.

Record:

- Exact route and elapsed time
- Water levels
- Pump and piston positions
- Plant growth and movement
- Changes in pressure
- Any sounds or vibrations
- Changes in the distant ridges or cloud layer
- Whether the rain curtains remain vertical
- Whether the central tree has altered its shape

Pay special attention to time. The installation’s machinery runs on geological patience, and the site may be producing not only water or atmosphere but also temporal states. If your two clocks disagree, leave. Do not attempt to determine which one is correct while inside.

### Psychological preparation

The absence of animals will be unsettling. Do not mistake silence for safety or the lush vegetation for a living ecosystem. This is a **closed ecology without ordinary agency**: growth is scheduled, reproduction is mechanical, and there are no birds, insects, fish, or incidental disturbances to reveal whether a location is dangerous.

The place may feel peaceful because it is quiet and warm. That feeling is misleading. Its danger lies in systems that are:

- Large but nearly silent
- Slow but unstoppable
- Biological but manufactured
- Watery but not governed by ordinary fluid behavior
- Vast in function despite being physically small

Expect a sense of being observed by processes rather than by creatures. Avoid anthropomorphizing the machines, but do not assume they are indifferent. The installation may be responsive without having anything resembling a mind.

### Recommended visit plan

Make the first visit a short reconnaissance:

1. Establish a stable external base and return beacon.
2. Sample the atmosphere from outside and inside the membranes.
3. Enter only the highest, driest walkways.
4. Map the circulation system without touching it.
5. Observe one complete local cycle if possible.
6. Photograph the central court from outside its ring.
7. Withdraw before exploring the lower flooded chambers.
8. Quarantine all equipment and samples afterward.

On a later visit, if the site has shown no hostile or destabilizing behavior, enter one flooded area using a fully independent diving system. Never explore alone. One person should remain on a dry, anchored platform with the extraction line and continuous communication.

### The most important rule

Do not assume that the installation is unfinished because it looks unfinished. The fresh surfaces, crowded machinery, new mountain, and growing tree may indicate that construction is still occurring—or that **construction is the environment’s normal biological phase**.

Visit as a guest inside an active organism. Keep your route reversible, your equipment independent of the site, your body sealed from its water and plants, and your curiosity subordinate to extraction. If the rain stops, the water level changes without a visible cause, the distant mountains move, or the tree begins making room for you, leave immediately.
