Treat this as an expedition into a functioning, pressurized underwater industrial continent—not as a dive site or a ruin. An ordinary human should not enter the open water here without a dedicated submersible or a rigid pressure suit connected to a support system.

## Before you go

### Establish that you are legally and operationally expected

The factories, pumps, transit lines, pressure doors, and unfinished construction zones are still active. Find out:

- Who controls the basin.
- Which areas are public, inspected, abandoned, or restricted.
- Whether visitors require an escort, beacon, identification transponder, or work authorization.
- Which corridors are used by trains, cargo vehicles, maintenance craft, or whales.
- Where emergency refuges and pressure-safe chambers are located.
- Whether the local operators know what a multiverse traveler is and whether they permit biological visitors.

Do not assume that an apparently empty district is abandoned. A tower with no visible workers may still contain active turbines, automated cranes, electrical equipment, compressed-water systems, or life-support machinery.

Obtain a current structural and hydraulic map. The landscape is too large for landmarks alone, and many features will look identical: towers, pylons, inspection lights, pressure housings, cable curtains, and wide concrete channels.

### Bring a vessel, not just diving equipment

Ordinary open-circuit scuba gear is unsuitable. The depths may be extreme, the water cold, and the currents powerful enough to turn a diver into debris. Use one of the following:

- A small, heavily built submersible with a pressure-rated cabin.
- A rigid atmospheric diving suit.
- A work-class remotely operated vehicle controlled from a safe vessel.
- A properly rated saturation-diving system, if you have an experienced local support operation.

A flexible wetsuit or ordinary drysuit is not adequate protection against the environment. The water may be merely cold by oceanic standards, but prolonged exposure, wet gloves, and repeated contact with metal will still produce hypothermia and loss of dexterity.

Your submersible should have:

- Redundant life support and power.
- Independent navigation and depth systems.
- Sonar capable of detecting walls, cables, pylons, fish schools, large animals, and approaching vehicles.
- Strong external lights with adjustable intensity.
- A physical tether or reliable homing system.
- A cutting tool for cables and nets.
- External pressure and water-quality sensors.
- Flooding alarms and a way to reach an enclosed refuge.
- A beacon that transmits your position even through murk, silt, and machinery noise.
- Mechanical rather than purely electronic emergency controls.
- A way to attach to approved guide rails or refuge stations without being pulled into a current.

Carry enough supplies to remain isolated for considerably longer than your planned excursion. A door may stay closed, a train corridor may be blocked, or a pump failure may make a supposedly short return journey impossible.

## Protect yourself from the water and pressure

Do not descend or enter a lower chamber until you understand the pressure regime. The basin contains enclosed areas, pressure doors, flooded shafts, deep transport routes, and possibly sections maintained at different pressures. A seemingly open passage may become dangerous when a gate cycles or a neighboring chamber equalizes.

Avoid:

- Standing or hovering in front of pressure doors.
- Entering a trench during a pump startup.
- Passing through a gate while lights or alarms indicate cycling.
- Approaching openings in ruptured pipelines.
- Exploring elevator shafts, intake tunnels, or unfinished channels.
- Assuming that calm water means stable water.

Water can begin moving violently with little visual warning. A failed pump, opened pressure gate, ruptured line, or changing circulation pattern could produce a current strong enough to pin or crush a vehicle against a wall.

Use a conservative depth and pressure schedule. If your equipment requires decompression, have a saturation-capable medical team and multiple decompression shelters. Never improvise ascent procedures from the basin to the open ocean. The route may involve major pressure changes, and the surface itself is not necessarily safe: it is a moving, metal-gray ceiling with wave action, weather, and possible shipping or launch activity above it.

## Plan around currents and “underwater weather”

The basin has regional-scale circulation. Treat current forecasts the way you would treat wind and storm forecasts on land.

Before each excursion, obtain the status of:

- Cooling systems and warm outflows.
- Major pumps and turbines.
- Pressure doors.
- Cargo and passenger rail traffic.
- Construction lifts.
- Sediment-clearing operations.
- Intake and discharge stations.
- Any known leaks or ruptures.

A storm-like sediment event can reduce visibility to nothing, obscure navigation lights, foul machinery, and drive loose cables into the water column. If a brown cloud begins to advance, return to a hardened refuge or stop in a designated safe position. Do not attempt to “push through” merely because the current looks slow from a distance.

Carry redundant inertial navigation, sonar mapping, and a physical route line where feasible. GPS will not work underwater. Ordinary radio may be unreliable through water and steel; use approved acoustic communication, low-frequency beacons, and tethered fiber where possible.

Do not trust visual range. A structure that appears nearby may be hundreds of meters away, and a wall concealed by blue water or silt may be directly ahead. The enormous open spaces will encourage you to travel farther than you can safely return.

## Treat every machine as live

The environment is not quiet, dead infrastructure. It is an operating industrial system.

Keep clear of:

- Turbine housings and cooling intakes.
- Propellers and transit channels.
- Rail tubes and their junctions.
- Crane arms, suspended loads, and automated manipulators.
- Electrical conduits and exposed cable bundles.
- Waste outlets and drainage mouths.
- Venting or pressure-release structures.
- Areas marked by flashing lamps, rising bubbles, or strong vibration.
- Unfinished construction zones.

Do not touch exposed cables, even if they appear inactive. Water, metal, and damaged insulation can make electrical hazards difficult to identify. Do not wedge open a door or hatch. The door may be balancing pressure, isolating contamination, or protecting an entire district from flooding.

Stay away from the tracks and transparent rail housings. A train that seems slow may have immense mass and little ability to stop or maneuver. Its headlights may appear long before the vehicle is audible through the surrounding machinery—or may be confused with other lights.

## Prepare for a harsh sensory environment

The sound will be physically tiring. Pumps, turbines, pressure gates, propellers, impacts, and animal calls will travel through the hull and your body. A vessel should provide acoustic insulation, and any suit should include hearing protection compatible with alarms and communication.

Use lights carefully. Intense illumination attracts fish and smaller organisms, but it can also startle large animals or destroy your night vision. Keep one low-intensity navigation mode and one powerful search mode. Avoid continuously sweeping powerful beams into animals’ eyes.

Expect constant vibration. It can cause fatigue, nausea, headaches, and difficulty distinguishing your own vehicle’s condition from the background machinery. Schedule quiet recovery periods in a stable, dry habitat.

The scale may also be psychologically difficult. Long stretches contain almost nothing but engineered floor, distant lights, and blue darkness. Then a factory tower, whale, or moving storm may appear without warning. Keep a companion, maintain a fixed return schedule, and do not continue exploration while tired or disoriented.

## Respect the ecology

The animals are not incidental scenery. They use the industrial landscape as habitat and may be accustomed to machinery but not to you.

Do not:

- Chase, feed, illuminate, or corner animals.
- Enter warm outflows or breeding areas.
- Approach sleeping seals or large predators.
- Block migration corridors.
- Put hands into pipe racks, bolt recesses, cable trenches, or grilles.
- Assume small animals are harmless; eels, crustaceans, and colonies around machinery may defend territory or carry unfamiliar pathogens.
- Collect organisms, algae, barnacles, or equipment without permission.

Give whales and whale-sized animals especially wide clearance. In a corridor of pillars and pipes, even a peaceful animal can injure a submersible by turning, surfacing, or reacting to thrusters. A large animal may also be following a current or hunting route that you cannot perceive.

Bring sealed sampling equipment if biological contact is necessary. Avoid releasing anything from your own world. A single microorganism, spore, parasite, or chemical contaminant could alter an ecosystem that is still in an unstable transition from industrial site to artificial reef.

## Wear contamination protection

The water may contain industrial chemicals, hydraulic fluids, corrosion products, heavy metals, biological films, waste discharge, and unfamiliar marine pathogens. Even clear water is not necessarily clean.

Use:

- A sealed suit or pressure vessel.
- External decontamination before entering a habitat.
- Separate tools for clean and contaminated zones.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity without exposing skin.
- Eye and respiratory protection during any surface transfer or maintenance operation.
- A quarantine period after returning to your origin world.

Do not eat local fish, plants, filter-feeders, or farm remnants. Their tissues may accumulate industrial contaminants, and their biochemistry may be incompatible with yours even if they resemble familiar marine life.

## Choose a sensible route

Your first visit should be a controlled survey, not a grand crossing.

A good initial itinerary would be:

1. Enter through a maintained, well-lit access station.
2. Remain near a staffed or automated refuge.
3. Travel along a mapped maintenance corridor.
4. Observe one active processing district from outside its safety perimeter.
5. Visit a quiet open chamber only with a guide and current report.
6. Return before the next scheduled pressure-door cycle or pump transition.

Do not begin at the outer edge, in the deepest chamber, near an unfinished platform, or along the hundred-kilometer highway. Those areas are visually compelling but offer fewer rescue options and more ways to become lost.

Keep a hard turnaround time. Do not allow “one more tower” or “just beyond that wall” to consume your reserve power, oxygen, or navigation margin.

## Pack for emergencies

At minimum, your expedition should have:

- Two independent communication methods.
- Two independent navigation methods.
- A locator beacon with a timed automatic distress signal.
- Spare power and emergency life support.
- A tether, line launcher, or approved guide cable.
- Cutting and release tools.
- Thermal protection.
- First-aid and decompression support appropriate to the equipment.
- A compact shelter or refuge capability.
- External markers visible to sonar and low-light cameras.
- A printed or mechanically stored map, in case electronics fail.
- A local emergency code sheet.
- A way to signal “do not approach” if you are trapped near wildlife, machinery, or a current.

Establish a check-in schedule with someone outside the basin. If you miss a check-in, they should know when to contact operators, seal the route, and begin recovery. Do not rely on a single automated rescue system; this environment contains too many active systems that may continue operating while a person is in danger.

## Expect unfinishedness

The most important mental preparation is to stop thinking of the basin as a dead city. It is a new and still-changing territory. A clean wall may be new construction. A dark facility may be in standby rather than abandoned. A field of pale concrete may be a future industrial district. A pipe covered in life may still carry hot, pressurized flow.

The place will feel both empty and crowded: huge distances with no people, but constant machinery, moving cargo, animals, currents, and invisible operations. You will not be visiting a museum. You will be entering a working machine that is gradually becoming an ecosystem.

Go slowly, stay within mapped and monitored zones, leave nothing behind, and make your first objective simply to return with an intact vehicle, uncontaminated equipment, and a reliable account of what you saw.
