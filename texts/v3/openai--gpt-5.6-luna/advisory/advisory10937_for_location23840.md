## Prepare as though you are visiting an extraterrestrial industrial ocean—not a high-altitude coastline.

An ordinary human should not attempt to arrive here unprotected, especially not by appearing on the plateau, in the water, or near the rim. The location combines a thin atmosphere, abnormal gravity, immense hydrostatic pressure, deafening continuous vibration, vast distances, dangerous machinery, and an ecosystem that may not recognize human biology as alive.

Your minimum safe arrangement is a **sealed, pressure-capable, independently powered submersible expedition system**, supported by an orbital or high-altitude logistics platform if possible.

---

# 1. Confirm the local physics before entering

The first danger is that the description does not obey ordinary terrestrial gravity.

The ocean remains on a continent miles above the surrounding world. Water falls from the rim without simply draining away. At least one of the following is true:

- local gravity is weaker;
- gravity changes with altitude or location;
- the plateau is enclosed by an unknown force or topological boundary;
- water is being continuously redirected;
- inertia, buoyancy, or “down” behaves differently near the rim.

Do not assume that conventional instruments will give a complete answer. Bring redundant measurements for:

- local gravitational acceleration;
- buoyancy and fluid density;
- inertial reference;
- atmospheric pressure;
- the direction of “down” at several depths and distances from the edge;
- whether the water’s surface and the falling curtain share the same gravitational behavior;
- whether objects crossing the rim conserve velocity, fall, rise, or transition into another medium.

Conduct initial observations from a remote probe. Do not send a person until you know what happens to a test mass, a drone, a tether, and a sealed capsule at the edge.

The rim should be considered a **nonlinear physical boundary**, not a scenic overlook.

---

# 2. Arrive above the plateau, not on it

The atmosphere above the high continent is thin and intensely clear. An ordinary human exposed to it would likely suffer rapid hypoxia, decompression injury depending on the actual pressure, severe cold, and possibly ultraviolet exposure.

Use a staged descent vehicle with:

- a fully pressurized cabin;
- independent oxygen reserves;
- carbon dioxide scrubbing;
- pressure regulation;
- heating and thermal insulation;
- radiation and ultraviolet protection;
- a high-altitude flight system that does not depend on local weather;
- enough thrust or buoyancy to remain stationary over a chosen landing site.

Do not land close to the ocean’s margin. The edge produces a continent-scale falling curtain, immense spray, ice, vapor, turbulence, electrical effects, and pressure oscillations. Even if the falling water appears stable, the air around it may contain violent vertical currents and suspended ice or mineral particles.

Establish your first camp on a high, dry, stable basalt shelf well inland, preferably near neither a fault line nor an industrial site. Use unmanned probes to verify that the plateau is not slowly shifting, opening, or lifting around you.

---

# 3. Never enter the water in a suit alone

The water is shallow in a geological sense, but “shallow” here may still mean hundreds or thousands of meters. In addition, the seabed is sharply fractured, and the pressure may become dangerous very quickly.

A human diver would need much more than a conventional diving suit. Use a rigid, one-person or multi-person submersible with:

- a pressure hull rated well beyond the intended depth;
- multiple independent buoyancy systems;
- mechanically protected thrusters;
- redundant life support;
- internal thermal regulation;
- external lights in several wavelengths;
- sonar and terrain-mapping systems;
- automatic ascent capability;
- a detachable emergency shelter or escape capsule;
- no single-point dependence on a tether.

The water’s pressure is not the only concern. Currents in trenches are described as having the force of engines. A current may carry a submersible into a fracture, turbine, cable system, or animal migration lane before its pilot can react. The fact that the currents are permanent makes them easier to map, but it also means they never conveniently weaken.

Do not rely on an anchor. An anchored vessel may be pulled sideways for centuries by a fixed current. Do not rely on a surface buoy unless you have verified that the surface itself remains stationary relative to the seabed.

Use a **current map**, a **depth map**, and a **return corridor** before leaving any safe zone.

---

# 4. Prepare for noise as a physical hazard

The soundscape is not merely loud. It is continuous, broad-spectrum vibration generated by:

- the falling ocean;
- currents passing through trenches;
- animals;
- industrial equipment;
- cables, hulls, and rock resonances.

Ordinary hearing protection will not be enough, and may be useless against low-frequency vibration transmitted through the water and the vehicle hull.

Your vehicle and habitats should include:

- acoustic isolation between the pressure hull and external frame;
- vibration-damping mounts;
- active cancellation for internal compartments;
- sealed communication systems;
- visual, tactile, or text-based communication;
- alarms delivered through displays and redundant haptic signals;
- hearing protection for all exposed personnel;
- medical monitoring for vertigo, nausea, fatigue, disorientation, and internal vibration injury.

Do not expect to speak through an open helmet, across a platform, or underwater by voice. Even a nearby person may be inaudible. Establish communication through fiber-optic cable, short-range encoded radio where possible, laser links, or hardwired terminals.

Do not use sound as your primary navigation system. The environment may saturate sonar with echoes and standing vibrations. Every object may seem to have a continuous acoustic presence.

---

# 5. Bring extraordinary navigation redundancy

The horizon and terrain are deceptive. Clear water, repeated ridges, mineral shelves, and immense scale will make nearby areas look familiar and distant areas appear close. A person can become disoriented even with excellent visibility.

Every expedition member and vehicle should carry:

- inertial navigation;
- depth and pressure logging;
- magnetic and gravitational mapping;
- optical terrain recognition;
- a continuously updated three-dimensional map;
- physical or optical markers at every safe staging point;
- a return beacon independent of the main power system;
- a clock synchronized to the expedition network;
- a dead-reckoning backup;
- a record of current direction and speed.

Do not assume a compass will work. The plateau’s newly fractured basalt, industrial electrical infrastructure, and possible anomalous physics may make magnetic navigation unreliable.

Do not leave a facility without marking:

1. where you are;
2. where the nearest safe ascent route lies;
3. which currents are safe to cross;
4. how much power remains;
5. whether your route will still be traversable on the return journey.

Although the world appears unchanging, your own supplies, batteries, seals, body temperature, and oxygen are changing. The landscape will not remind you of that.

---

# 6. Keep far away from the animals at first

The creatures do not behave like ordinary schools. They occupy fixed pressure territories and migration routes, sometimes with miles between individuals. That spacing should be treated as a warning: it may represent territorial, sensory, or hydrodynamic requirements.

Do not approach a large animal merely because it is slow. A massive creature may generate:

- pressure pulses capable of damaging a small submersible;
- electrical discharges;
- wake forces;
- magnetic or acoustic interference;
- defensive reactions to lights or sonar.

Avoid shining bright lights directly at animals until their responses are understood. Avoid loud active sonar near them. Avoid releasing food, waste, coolant, blood, or warm water. Do not cross a migration lane simply because it appears empty; the animals may be separated by distances that make the lane seem vacant.

The small metallic-looking forms may be animals, machines, mineral organisms, or something capable of damaging a hull. Treat all moving objects as potentially intelligent and hazardous.

The best first-contact policy is:

- observe remotely;
- maintain a fixed depth and heading;
- do not block a route;
- do not chase;
- do not collect specimens;
- do not touch anything;
- do not interpret silence as passivity.

If an animal begins matching your position rather than its apparent route, retreat.

---

# 7. Treat the industrial sites as active, not abandoned

The facilities are functioning, but there are no visible workers. That means they may be:

- autonomous;
- maintained by unseen inhabitants;
- operating on schedules independent of human time;
- executing obsolete routines;
- guarding something;
- preserving a system whose purpose is unknown;
- capable of recognizing, detaining, or destroying intruders.

Do not assume a lit window means hospitality. Do not enter through an open door. Do not disconnect a cable, block a conveyor, remove a mineral deposit, or shut down a pump. These systems may be part of the equilibrium that keeps the plateau, sea, or atmosphere stable.

Before approaching a structure, observe it for a long period and map:

- power flows;
- moving components;
- pressure changes;
- electromagnetic fields;
- heat output;
- fluid intake and discharge;
- door cycles;
- signals or transmissions;
- changes in nearby animal behavior.

Bring no bare biological samples into a facility. Use sterile, disposable external probes and maintain a quarantine boundary. If the machines are ancient or nonhuman, your microbial contamination may be as dangerous to them as their contamination is to you.

Do not trust an apparent control panel. A machine that resembles a console may be a power regulator, a sensor array, a lock, or an interface whose activation changes conditions hundreds of miles away.

---

# 8. Plan for the chemistry of the water and stone

The water may not be Earthlike. Dissolved minerals, pressure chemistry, electrical gradients, and vent products could make it corrosive, toxic, conductive, or biologically incompatible.

Bring:

- full chemical analysis capability;
- sealed sample containers;
- external manipulators;
- replaceable hull coatings;
- corrosion-resistant tools;
- particulate filtration;
- contamination monitors;
- radiation and electrical-field sensors;
- decontamination chambers;
- medical supplies for unknown toxins and pressure effects.

The colorless films on the rock should be treated as active life until proven otherwise. They may metabolize pressure, charge, or chemical differences and could react to metal, heat, oxygen, or human waste.

Do not drink the water. Do not breathe compressed local water mixtures. Do not assume boiling will sterilize it; unknown chemistry and organisms may survive, and boiling may concentrate dangerous substances.

The constant mineralization of dead animals also means that sediment and biological remains may be chemically active. Avoid stirring them.

---

# 9. Protect against permanent environmental exposure

There are no storms or seasons, but that does not make conditions mild. The environment is permanently extreme.

Prepare for:

- cold from high altitude and evaporative water loss;
- intense sunlight and ultraviolet exposure;
- glare from the water and pale mineral shelves;
- ice and high-velocity spray near the rim;
- electrical discharge around cables, animals, and water curtains;
- pressure fluctuations;
- mineral dust or aerosols;
- uninterrupted vibration;
- total absence of natural darkness.

Use polarized, UV-filtering eye protection and maintain artificial day-night cycles inside the habitat. Without an imposed schedule, crew members may lose sleep and temporal orientation. The unchanging light and noise will make days indistinguishable.

At night—or rather, during your artificial night—cover all unnecessary windows. The faint gleam in the deep may be biologically or mechanically meaningful, and continuous observation can produce fatigue and fixation.

---

# 10. Build a real refuge before exploring

Your base should be a sealed, vibration-isolated habitat with enough supplies to survive without outside rescue. It should have:

- two or more pressure compartments;
- independent oxygen and power systems;
- water purification;
- food for a long mission;
- spare hull seals and structural repair equipment;
- a medical bay;
- radiation, toxin, and infection controls;
- local and off-site communications;
- a rescue vehicle;
- a second, physically separate emergency shelter.

Place it away from:

- cliff edges;
- active faults;
- industrial discharge points;
- migration corridors;
- high-current trenches;
- falling-water spray;
- unexplained lights or structures.

Because the world is locked into equilibrium, you may be tempted to believe it is safe. It is not. A stable hazard is still a hazard, and a system that has not changed for a very long time may be difficult to interrupt or escape.

---

# 11. Prepare psychologically for a place without progression

This may be the most important preparation.

The environment does not offer the ordinary signs of time:

- no weather;
- no tides;
- no seasons;
- no erosion;
- no growth;
- no changing coast;
- no broad ecological succession;
- no visible aging of the machines;
- no natural arrival of night.

Your mind will expect gradual change and find none. Distances will lose meaning. A journey of days may cross a landscape that appears identical to the place you left. The permanent sound will erase silence. The absence of vegetation will make everything look exposed and unfinished. The abundance of animals will not make the place feel alive in a familiar way; most of the time, you will be alone in an ocean full of life.

Bring strict procedures for:

- sleep;
- exercise;
- timekeeping;
- social contact;
- journal and video logs;
- scheduled mission termination;
- psychological assessment;
- enforced breaks from observation;
- avoiding solitary excursions.

Do not let explorers remain alone for long periods. The setting encourages dissociation: the water, machines, animals, and fractures all move, but nothing develops. A traveler may begin to feel that they are the only source of change and therefore must investigate everything. That impulse is dangerous.

---

# 12. Set hard rules before departure

An ordinary human traveler should agree in advance to the following:

1. **No unprotected exposure**, even for a short look.
2. **No approach to the rim** until the gravity transition is understood.
3. **No solo travel.**
4. **No entry into industrial facilities without remote mapping and an exit plan.**
5. **No contact with animals unless they initiate sustained, nonaggressive interaction.**
6. **No biological samples return to the habitat without quarantine.**
7. **No shutdown or alteration of functioning machinery.**
8. **No route dependent on a single power source, tether, beacon, or vehicle.**
9. **Turn back at a preselected fuel, oxygen, power, or psychological threshold—not when the situation already feels dangerous.**
10. **Leave before curiosity becomes the mission.**

The safest itinerary would be deliberately modest:

- establish a high-altitude observation station;
- map the plateau and rim remotely;
- land well inland;
- analyze atmosphere and rock;
- deploy autonomous submersibles;
- observe one industrial site from a distance;
- observe one migration lane without entering it;
- descend only to a known shallow ridge;
- return before approaching the edge or deep trenches.

Do not try to “see what lies below the waterfall” on your first visit. The falling ocean is not merely a landmark. It is the place where the world’s rules become least trustworthy.

The central preparation is to abandon the idea that this is a place one can casually visit. It is a sealed, functioning system whose equilibrium predates your arrival and may depend on conditions you cannot perceive. Enter as a remote scientist, not a tourist; as a temporary, isolated pressure vessel, not a swimmer; and as someone who is prepared to leave without understanding the whole of it.
