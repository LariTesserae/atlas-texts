Prepare as though you are entering a functioning industrial megastructure during an unannounced maintenance cycle—not an abandoned ruin and not a natural cavern. The main danger is not any single monster or disaster. It is that the entire landscape is operating according to rules you do not yet understand.

## Before you go

Do not arrive alone, and do not rely on ordinary human infrastructure. Bring at least:

- A local guide or someone who understands the facility’s operating cycles.
- Two independent communication systems, preferably one wired or hardline.
- A physical map marked with safe platforms, flood channels, pressure gates, lifts, refuge rooms, and routes that remain usable during a shutdown.
- A clock synchronized with the facility’s cycle, if such a cycle can be determined.
- A return beacon or tether. Distances and echoes will make your sense of direction unreliable.
- Enough food, water, batteries, and thermal protection for considerably longer than your planned visit. Assume exits may close or become inaccessible.
- Written instructions. Screens may fail, lights may be misleading, and spoken communication will be unreliable.

Find out, if possible, what the place is built to do. In particular, learn:

1. Which channels carry water during a pressure release.
2. Which gates close automatically.
3. Which floor markings indicate evacuation routes and which indicate future demolition, flooding, or machinery clearance.
4. How to identify live electrical equipment.
5. Whether the atmosphere is breathable everywhere.
6. Whether the elevators and ramps connect to a known surface—or simply to deeper levels.
7. What happens when the warning lamps change color.
8. Whether the central sealed structure is restricted, monitored, or considered hazardous.

Do not assume that a facility’s operators will be visible. The machinery may be unattended, remotely controlled, or managed by systems that no longer distinguish between personnel and trespassers.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear layered, insulated clothing beneath a waterproof outer shell. The cold will be persistent, and the wetness will not come only from puddles. Condensation will fall from above, seep through seams, and collect inside your clothing. Use:

- Waterproof, steel-toed boots with aggressive, non-slip soles.
- Chemical-resistant gloves, preferably with cut protection.
- A helmet with a secured chin strap.
- Eye protection or a full-face visor.
- Hearing protection that still permits radio communication.
- A full-face respirator with filters suitable for industrial vapors, particulates, mold, and unknown aerosols. If the atmosphere has not been tested, carry a supplied-air or self-contained breathing system for emergencies.
- High-visibility outer markings, but not so much reflective material that you become confused with automated maintenance targets.
- A harness and fall-arrest line for gantries, elevated walkways, and inspection towers.
- Waterproof headlamps with red and white modes, plus spare lamps. Never depend on the facility’s lights.
- A rugged, waterproof notebook and grease pencil. Electronics may fail in condensation, electromagnetic noise, or flooded areas.
- A gas monitor capable of detecting oxygen depletion, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane, chlorine-like compounds, and volatile industrial solvents.
- A personal alarm that produces a distinctive signal not easily confused with the facility’s alarms.
- Marking tape, chalk, or removable tags—provided marking the structure is permitted.
- A compact tool kit, but do not touch exposed cables, valves, panels, or control mechanisms unless explicitly authorized.
- A first-aid kit, thermal blanket, chemical burn supplies, and treatment for lacerations and contaminated-water exposure.

Bring sealed containers for samples only if you have a compelling reason. The water may contain oil, metal particles, corrosive compounds, biological growth, or substances that react when exposed to air. Do not taste it. Do not wash wounds in it. Do not assume clear water is safe.

## Personal precautions

Treat every surface as wet, conductive, and potentially unstable.

Do not stand in drainage channels, even when they appear empty. They may fill abruptly when a distant pressure gate opens. The same applies to broad, clean concrete areas that seem safer than the machinery: their painted arrows may mark a temporary route that becomes a flood path or machinery-clearance zone during the next cycle.

Stay on raised walkways or designated service roads. Cross open floors only when necessary, and do so with a guide watching the lights, pipes, and channel levels. Keep away from:

- Unfenced pits.
- The undersides of walkways.
- Corroded railings and newly exposed reinforcement.
- Transparent panels behind which older chambers are visible.
- Pipes that are ticking, sweating, icing, or vibrating unusually.
- Any floor with fresh mineral crust, oil sheen, metal filings, or hairline cracks.
- Machinery that starts without an obvious control signal.
- Water moving against the apparent grade.
- The sealed structure at the center.

Assume that a “temporary” walkway has been left in place beyond its rated service life. Test nothing with your weight or your hands. Use a pole, remote camera, or sacrificial instrument if you must investigate a gap, puddle, or panel.

Do not touch the pale moss, translucent plants, geometric films, fungus, insects, or anything moving in the drains. Some may be harmless, but the ecosystem is adapted to conditions that may be hostile to you. The bluish phosphorescence associated with machine vibration is especially worth avoiding: it suggests a biological or chemical response to machinery, not ordinary light-producing fungus.

## Navigation

The place will defeat casual orientation.

The far walls are unreliable reference points, echoes return from unexpected directions, and the apparent “up” of one section may lead to another depth rather than the surface. Use a formal navigation system:

- Record every turn, elevation change, gate, lamp color, and pump station.
- Mark time as well as distance.
- Keep a physical line or beacon network where possible.
- Never separate from your group’s lights.
- Establish a maximum distance from the last confirmed safe point.
- Turn back before fatigue, cold, or battery loss makes the decision for you.
- If you hear a familiar voice from a distant wall, do not follow it unless your guide confirms its location by radio and visual signal.

Sound is not trustworthy here. A voice may travel through ducts or pipes and emerge elsewhere. A dropped tool may echo long after it lands. Alarms may be distorted by the chamber’s geometry. Use visual confirmation and agreed hand signals. Do not improvise verbal commands during a machinery event.

Keep your own orientation markers distinct from the facility’s marks. The facility’s arrows may describe process flow, not human travel.

## Water, pressure, and machinery

Learn the warning sequence before entering. Red and amber lamps may not mean the same thing they would in an ordinary factory. When they activate in sequence, assume a pressure, drainage, or transfer event is imminent.

At the first sign of a cycle—pipe hammering, valve shrieking, a change in fan noise, sudden illumination, or a deep approaching rush—leave the floor and reach a designated high platform or refuge. Do not attempt to outrun water through a channel. It may arrive faster than it looks, and the channels may conceal sudden drops, grilles, or suction points.

Avoid placing yourself between:

- A moving crane and its rails.
- A pressure gate and the area it seals.
- Two operating machines with no clear escape route.
- A tank and a retaining wall.
- A flooded trench and an electrical cabinet.
- A sealed structure and the pumps working around it.

The cranes and conveyors may be automated. A machine carrying an empty container is not idle; it is following a schedule. Never walk beneath suspended loads, even if the crane appears inactive. Never assume a compressor, pump, or fan is safe because it is quiet. It may restart without warning.

The water-management system may be the facility’s central life-support mechanism. If a pump stops, a channel changes direction, or a normally wet area dries out, report it and withdraw. A change in water behavior may indicate not a malfunction, but a deliberate transfer to another level.

## Atmosphere and exposure

The visible cold and mist are not proof of clean air. In enclosed industrial depths, oxygen can be displaced by gases from tanks, drains, corrosion, biological growth, or chemical processes. Check the air continuously, not just at the entrance.

If you smell something strongly, do not interpret that as a reliable warning. Some gases dull the sense of smell, while others can be dangerous at concentrations too low to notice. Leave immediately for:

- Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, drowsiness, or sudden euphoria.
- Burning eyes or throat.
- A metallic taste.
- Unexplained difficulty breathing.
- Loss of coordination.
- Changes in the gas monitor.
- A sudden absence of machinery noise or airflow.

The sudden absence of sound may be more dangerous than the sound itself. It could indicate ventilation failure, power isolation, or an approaching pressure change.

## The deeper chambers

The transparent panels into older spaces should be treated as observation boundaries, not windows. Do not open, break, or lean against them. The fact that the operators can look inside without entering strongly suggests that entry is undesirable or unsafe.

Do not descend farther merely because a lift is available. “Down” may lead to pressure systems, flooded levels, unsealed excavation, or a region where the facility’s operating rules differ. Confirm the destination before boarding. Use elevators only with a known emergency release procedure; a lift that descends beyond mapped levels may not return when called.

If you encounter a chamber whose dimensions, water behavior, or machinery do not make physical sense, do not try to resolve the inconsistency by approaching it. Photograph it from a safe distance, record the time and local conditions, and leave.

## The central structure

Do not approach the low sealed building in the open basin.

The water is being drawn toward it despite the apparent grading, and the pumps are laboring continuously to keep it dry. That indicates either a concealed intake, a pressure differential, a leak into a lower system, or something inside that is affecting the surrounding structure. The impacts from within are the clearest sign that this is not ordinary idle equipment.

Establish a perimeter well outside the pump field. Observe:

- Whether the strikes follow a pattern.
- Whether nearby machinery responds to them.
- Whether water levels change after each impact.
- Whether lights or alarms activate.
- Whether the sounds are transmitted through the floor, walls, or air.
- Whether the structure’s surface flexes, sweats, heats, or ices.

Do not knock back. Do not answer any apparent voice. Do not place instruments directly against the walls. If the facility recognizes the structure as restricted, let its restrictions stand. An ordinary traveler has no reason to make first contact with something that is being continuously drained, sealed, and monitored by an entire underground landscape.

## Psychological preparation

Expect the place to produce false confidence and false urgency.

Long stretches will appear empty and safe, while the real hazard may be a remote gate, a pressure cycle, or a barely visible floor change. Conversely, alarms and sudden mechanical movement may sound catastrophic without requiring immediate danger if you are in the correct refuge. Follow procedures rather than instinct.

The scale will make you feel watched, even if no person is present. Motion-activated lights, self-operating cranes, distant shapes, and voices emerging from wrong directions will encourage you to search the darkness. Resist that impulse. Keep your attention on your route, your instruments, and your group.

Do not linger in darkness merely because it feels sheltered. The darkness may contain pits, active machinery, blind organisms, or water. Do not hurry into light merely because it feels safe; illuminated areas may be working zones.

## Emergency rules

If separated from your group:

1. Stop moving.
2. Turn off unnecessary lights and listen only long enough to identify your last confirmed position.
3. Transmit your location once, then conserve power.
4. Move toward the nearest marked refuge or raised platform, not toward a familiar-sounding voice.
5. Do not cross a channel or enter a machinery room to reunite with others.
6. If water rises, climb first and communicate second.
7. If the air becomes questionable, put on respiratory protection before attempting rescue.
8. If the facility enters an alarm cycle, shelter until the cycle is confirmed complete.

If power fails, assume that pumps, gates, ventilation, lighting, and communications have all become unreliable simultaneously. Do not descend, cross open floors, or enter an elevator during the first blackout. Establish whether water is rising and whether air is moving before choosing an exit.

## The right mindset

Visit as a surveyor, not an explorer seeking the deepest point.

The landscape is unfinished, but it is not waiting for you. It has its own weather, circulation, metabolism, and maintenance rhythms. It will continue manufacturing itself around your presence and may classify you as an obstruction, a leak, a moving load, or simply something to route around.

Stay dry where possible, elevated when water moves, quiet enough to hear changes, and far away from anything that appears to be sealed for a reason. Most importantly, leave while you still have a clear route back. In this place, the safest moment to depart is not when the machines begin to fail. It is while they are still working exactly as expected.
