## Preparation for Visiting the Living Industrial Basin

Treat this location less like a garden and more like a functioning organism built around the remains of a factory. You are not entering an abandoned site that happens to be overgrown. You are entering a slow, wet, distributed machine whose components include roots, walls, water, cables, and probably your own body if you remain long enough.

The main danger is not an immediate attack. It is being gradually incorporated, misled, contaminated, or placed in the wrong location when the buried system changes rhythm.

### Before entering

**Do not go alone.** Take at least one partner who remains outside the boundary with a clear line of communication and a written schedule. The basin’s paths are formed by living pressure and may shift without visibly “moving.” A companion can track your position, monitor elapsed time, and verify that you have actually returned.

Establish a strict maximum visit duration. Do not rely on how long it feels you have been inside. The place’s slow, repetitive activity may distort your sense of time, and all surfaces encourage lingering. A short reconnaissance visit—perhaps twenty to thirty minutes—should be the first expedition.

Mark the boundary from outside using removable, nonmetallic markers. Use bright cloth, chalk, or biodegradable flags placed on nearby ordinary ground, not inside the vegetation. Do not drive stakes into the mat. Do not attach markers to pipes, walls, trunks, roots, or cables. Anything left in contact with the growth may eventually be translated into part of the system.

### Clothing and equipment

Wear:

- Waterproof, knee-high boots with sealed seams and aggressive tread.
- Close-fitting, washable outer clothing.
- Thick gloves with impermeable palms and cuffs.
- Eye protection.
- A respirator suitable for biological aerosols, molds, and fine organic particles.
- A hood or head covering that prevents spores, insects, and falling condensate from entering your hair or collar.
- A hard hat or other head protection; falling branches and heavy leaves may descend very slowly and still be dangerous.
- A buoyant or easily recoverable outer layer if you intend to approach the black-water boundary.

Avoid loose straps, dangling cords, open pockets, exposed jewelry, and untreated leather. Vines and tendrils may catch them. Do not wear clothing made of natural fibers if you can avoid it; the plants may recognize or accept such material more readily than synthetic fabric.

Bring:

- Redundant lights, preferably sealed and battery-powered.
- A physical compass and a survey line.
- A camera with local storage rather than a system-dependent network connection.
- A nonmetallic measuring tape or fiberglass staff.
- A first-aid kit containing wound sealant, antiseptic, antihistamines, and supplies for flushing eyes and skin.
- Clean water and sealed food.
- A personal air-quality monitor if available.
- A dosimeter or electrical-field detector if your technology permits; the green current in the root-like cables should not be treated as harmless.
- A tether or line, but keep it in your own possession and do not tie it to the vegetation.
- Several sealed sample containers only if collecting specimens is authorized. Ideally, collect nothing.

Do not bring unshielded electronics that you cannot afford to lose. The system may induce current in cables, bulbs, metal objects, or nearby devices. Keep communications equipment protected and test it at the boundary. If radio contact becomes unreliable, do not venture farther to restore it.

### Air and water precautions

The air is warm and wet, with vapor released rhythmically from the fleshy towers. Assume that the vapor contains active biological material, chemical irritants, or electrically charged particles. The smell of mint, wet iron, and insulation is not evidence of safety. Keep your respirator on throughout the visit.

Do not drink from seepage, pools, leaf cups, or cracks. Do not wash your face or hands in the water. The clear water may have passed through industrial residues, living filters, or tissues capable of transporting signals. Treat all water as contaminated.

Avoid touching your mouth, eyes, nose, and exposed wounds. Cover every cut before entering. The soft floor may conceal sharp metal, glass, thorns, or roots with biological activity.

After leaving, remove outer clothing before entering your vehicle or lodging. Seal it in washable or disposable bags. Shower thoroughly, including hair and under fingernails. Inspect your skin for tiny roots, fibers, punctures, discoloration, or persistent warmth. Any unexplained swelling, numbness, faint green luminescence, altered pulse, or sensation of movement under the skin warrants immediate medical isolation.

### Moving through the basin

Walk slowly, but not aimlessly. The plants respond to pressure, vibration, water movement, and perhaps electrical activity. Move in the same deliberate manner throughout the visit. Do not run unless there is an immediate emergency.

Test every step with a staff before shifting your full weight. The fibrous floor is not mud, but it may be hollow, floating, or supported by structures that have not been tested under a human load. A springy surface may conceal a pool, a shaft, a machine housing, or a thin membrane over open water.

Stay on the most established routes: broad root bridges, dense stable mats, and visible remnants of old paving. Avoid:

- Narrow cracks where water rises.
- Areas that pulse, twitch, or warm noticeably.
- Root bridges with a hollow sound beneath them.
- Stands of reeds with actively closing valves.
- Vapor towers when their pores begin to open.
- Cables and roots that carry visible light.
- Walls or masonry that creak in response to your movement.
- Any patch where leaves turn toward you rather than toward the light.

The vegetation may not attack you, but it may close around you, redirect your path, or lower suspended branches. If a passage narrows, retreat immediately. Do not force your way through. Cutting or tearing the growth may produce sap, spores, electrical discharge, or a coordinated response from the surrounding plants.

Keep one hand free and maintain visual contact with your route behind you. The interior will not necessarily look different after you turn around. Use your own markers sparingly and photograph fixed objects at regular intervals.

### Interacting with the machinery

Assume that everything in the basin is connected. A rusted pipe may be carrying water, pressure, current, chemical vapor, or biological signals. A glass bulb in a trunk may be an indicator, a sensor, or an organ. A cable disguised as a root may still conduct dangerous voltage. Do not cut, unplug, open, or pull anything.

Do not leave tools, coins, tags, clothing, food wrappers, or samples behind. Even brief contact may begin the slow process of incorporation. If a metal item touches the floor, retrieve it immediately if this can be done without disturbing the vegetation. If it has become difficult to remove, abandon the item rather than wrenching it free.

The deep thudding is not background noise. Record its rhythm from outside and compare it with recordings made inside. Note whether the pace changes near you, near the central clearing, or when you touch structures. If the grinding shudder begins, stop moving, crouch if necessary, and wait until the tremor passes. Do not stand beneath walls, hanging root bridges, or loaded branches during a cycle.

When the system operates, observe the plants’ reactions. Reeds changing interval, vapor towers closing, or leaves orienting toward a common source may indicate a transition. These are not scenic details; they are status lights.

### The central clearing and the control panel

The central clearing should be treated as a restricted control room.

Do not approach it on a first visit unless there is a compelling reason. If you do approach, photograph the panel and the surrounding growth before entering the water. Do not step into the shallow pool without testing it for conductivity, depth, and biological activity. The concrete pedestal may be isolated from the water, or it may be the interface between the buried machinery and the entire basin.

Most importantly: **do not press the movable switch.**

The two fused switches are not evidence that the third is safe. A control that still moves may be a manual override, a maintenance function, a direction reversal, a pressure release, or a command whose consequences unfold over hours or years. The observed response—slower reeds, closing vapor pores, a long inhalation, and machinery turning in the opposite direction—is not a harmless change in ambience. It is a system-wide state transition.

If someone has already pressed it, do not immediately press it again to “restore” the rhythm. Record the original and altered tempos, withdraw from the clearing, and leave the basin along the route you came in. The system may be capable of returning to its prior state, but you should not assume that reversal is symmetrical or safe.

### What to do if the basin moves

The landscape’s movement will probably be gradual enough to seem nonthreatening. That is precisely why it is dangerous. A slowly closing passage can trap you without ever appearing to lunge.

If the floor shifts:

1. Stop touching nearby plants or structures.
2. Mark your current position only if you can do so without delay.
3. Follow the tether or your most recent route marker.
4. Avoid newly exposed cavities and machinery.
5. Do not investigate anything revealed beneath the mat.
6. Leave even if the movement appears to be opening a convenient path.

Never enter a hole or chamber uncovered by a tremor. The basin may contain active pistons, pressure reservoirs, digestive or filtering structures, or areas where the plant-machine interface is more concentrated.

### Biological and psychological considerations

Expect the place to feel attentive. Leaves will orient, sounds will overlap in ways that resemble industrial activity, and the underground beat may begin to resemble a heartbeat. Do not infer intention from every movement, but do not dismiss coordinated responses either. Maintain a written checklist and have your partner ask simple orientation questions over the radio: your name, destination, entry point, and time inside.

Leave immediately if you experience:

- A desire to remain despite your planned exit.
- Difficulty remembering the route.
- The conviction that the plants are communicating instructions.
- Rhythmic movements in your own muscles synchronized with the thudding.
- Sudden warmth, pressure, or vibration beneath your clothing.
- Unusual drowsiness or an inability to hear external sounds.
- The impression that a wall, root, or cable has begun to recognize you.

These may be ordinary environmental effects, exposure symptoms, or something more specific to this universe. You do not need to know which before withdrawing.

### Best overall strategy

Make the first visit observational and external. Map the boundary, record the thudding, inspect the old building and visible pipes from stable ground, sample the air remotely, and determine whether the system’s rhythm changes with daylight, weather, or human presence. Do not touch the living machinery. Do not attempt to repair it. Do not test the switch.

The basin has survived by making every distinction—plant and machine, wall and root, waste and nutrient, structure and organism—gradually disappear. Your safety depends on preserving distinctions of your own: inside and outside, object and body, observation and participation, curiosity and consent.
