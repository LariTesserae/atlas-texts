## Prepare for a wet highland settlement

Treat this as a mountain wetland, not as a normal village in rainy weather. The principal difficulties are constant damp, unstable footing, limited dry storage, dense vegetation, animals moving through inhabited areas, and the fact that the settlement’s infrastructure is alive or semi-alive. You will not be arriving somewhere with clear roads, clean boundaries, or reliably enclosed buildings.

### Before departure

Find out as much as possible about:

- The basin’s elevation and whether visitors commonly suffer altitude sickness.
- Whether humans from your world can tolerate the local air, pollen, fungi, and microorganisms.
- Which animals are dangerous, venomous, parasitic, or simply likely to knock you off a bridge.
- Whether the inhabitants use money, barter, labor exchange, or gifts.
- What foods are safe for outsiders.
- Whether outsiders are permitted to enter freely, or must be escorted.
- Which local plants are medicinal, poisonous, hallucinogenic, or culturally restricted.
- How the settlement handles fire, waste, weapons, and sleeping arrangements.

If your multiverse equipment permits it, arrive with medical screening and vaccinations appropriate for a humid mountain environment. Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics only under competent medical guidance; more important are clean wound care, antifungal treatment, antiparasitic protection, and supplies for dehydration, fever, and respiratory infections.

Do not assume that your world’s ordinary microbes are harmless here, or that this world’s microbes are harmless to you. Avoid exchanging bodily fluids, sharing drinking vessels until invited, and eating uncooked food during your first days.

## Clothing

Your clothing must be designed to remain functional while wet. “Waterproof” alone is not enough: you will sweat, and a sealed outer layer can become wet from inside.

Bring:

- A close-fitting moisture-wicking base layer.
- Several insulating layers that retain warmth when damp, preferably wool, treated plant fiber, or an equivalent local material.
- A durable, breathable rain shell with a hood.
- Waterproof trousers or a wrap that permits climbing and crouching.
- Multiple pairs of socks, stored in genuinely waterproof containers.
- High-ankle boots with aggressive grip and protected seams.
- Lightweight sandals or indoor footwear for drying periods.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity while protecting against bark, thorns, insects, and wet rope.
- A broad-brimmed hat or hood to keep persistent dripping off your neck.
- A spare complete outfit sealed separately from everything else.

Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, polished soles, long coats that can snag on roots, and fabrics that become dangerously heavy when soaked. Bright synthetic colors may attract insects or make you conspicuous; muted colors are safer unless the settlement uses bright cloth as a marker of status or peace.

Your boots will become muddy. This is unavoidable. Bring a small brush or scraping tool, but do not scrub mud and plant matter into every path or water channel. Ask where footwear is removed and where it may be cleaned.

## Packs and equipment

Use a small, waterproof pack with external surfaces that will not catch easily on branches. Every important object should have a secondary waterproof wrapping.

Useful equipment includes:

- A sealed water bottle or collapsible vessel.
- Water purification tablets, a filter, and a metal container for boiling.
- Waterproof matches or a storm lighter, kept in two separate places.
- A headlamp with a red-light option, plus spare batteries in sealed bags.
- A stout walking staff or collapsible pole.
- A compact knife or multitool, carried discreetly.
- Cordage that remains strong when wet.
- A small tarp or emergency shelter.
- A whistle for locating companions in dense vegetation.
- A repair kit for fabric, straps, boots, and waterproof seams.
- Insect repellent that does not harm local plants or animals.
- Fine mesh for sleeping, if the hosts permit you to use it.
- Water-resistant notebooks or recording equipment.
- Sealed containers for food and medication.
- A personal drying line and several absorbent cloths.
- Basic eye protection if you will be working around branches, splashing water, or pounding platforms.

Do not arrive overloaded. Paths are narrow, bridges are flexible, and you will occasionally need both hands. A small waterproof pouch worn against the body is preferable to a large chest or rigid case.

Avoid bringing autonomous drones, loud devices, chemical aerosols, machinery, or anything that produces unexplained light or sound. The inhabitants live in a sound-rich environment and may regard mechanical noise as a threat. A drone could provoke birds, monkeys, dogs, or people, and may be interpreted as a spying device.

## Arrival and first movement

Do not simply walk into the basin alone.

The settlement’s paths are likely obvious only to residents. What looks like a solid bank may be a thin mat over water; what looks like a stream may become a torrent after rain. Wait for a guide or ask visibly before crossing bridges, climbing rooted structures, entering tunnels, or stepping onto platforms.

On arrival:

1. Stop at the edge of habitation.
2. Make your presence known without shouting.
3. Keep your hands visible.
4. Do not touch plants, animals, tools, food, or structures without permission.
5. Accept that you may be inspected for weapons, illness, insects, or dangerous objects.
6. Follow the guide’s exact footsteps until you understand the paths.
7. Ask where visitors may stand, sit, wash, eat, sleep, and relieve themselves.

Do not interpret constant movement as disorder. The settlement is operating according to a dense set of local understandings: which root can bear weight, which water is clean, which animal is tolerated, which bridge is temporary, which wall must not be touched. A resident may interrupt you abruptly because you are about to step into a drainage channel, disturb a nest, or lean on a living support.

Walk slowly. Test every board and stone before committing your weight. Keep one hand free for balance and one eye on the person ahead. Do not step backward without looking.

## Water and sanitation

Water is everywhere, but that does not make it safe.

Assume that all pools, channels, roof runoff, and apparently clear streams are contaminated until a local resident identifies a safe source. Water may contain parasites, animal waste, decaying plant matter, or minerals that are harmless locally but unpleasant or dangerous to you.

Do not:

- Drink from a channel because it looks clean.
- Wash wounds in standing water.
- Swim or wade barefoot.
- Urinate or defecate near paths, pools, bridges, or cultivated areas.
- Pour soap, chemicals, oils, or unfamiliar medicines into drainage water.
- Leave food scraps on the ground.

Ask where washing occurs and whether the settlement has separate bathing, drinking, animal, and waste channels. Use sandals or waterproof footwear around wet ground. Dry your feet carefully every day; prolonged dampness will produce blisters, trench foot, fungal infections, and infected cuts faster than you expect.

Inspect yourself for leeches, ticks, mites, and small clinging organisms whenever you return from vegetation or water. Check socks, boot seams, waistbands, hair, and behind the knees. Do not rip attached organisms away if doing so leaves mouthparts embedded; have a local healer or medically trained companion remove them properly.

## Food and health

Eat what is offered only after determining whether it has been cooked, fermented, smoked, or otherwise prepared for outsiders. A food that is ordinary and safe for the inhabitants may cause severe illness in you.

Start with:

- Thoroughly cooked grains, roots, fish, and meat.
- Hot broths.
- Foods peeled or cooked immediately before eating.
- Small portions until your digestion adjusts.

Be cautious with raw leaves, berries, mushrooms, fermented liquids, honey, unfamiliar insects, and mountain water used in cooking. Avoid eating anything you pick yourself. The settlement may have plants whose toxicity depends on season, preparation, or which part is consumed.

Carry oral rehydration salts. In a cool, wet place you may not feel thirsty while losing fluid through exertion. Carry a thermometer if available, and tell a host quickly if you develop fever, confusion, persistent vomiting, breathing difficulty, or unusual weakness. In this environment, a small infected cut can become a serious problem.

Protect wounds with sealed dressings. Do not walk around with exposed abrasions, especially on hands and feet. If you use a medical disinfectant, ask where the residue may be disposed of; some compounds could damage the settlement’s living infrastructure or contaminate water.

## Fire and shelter

Fire is vital but dangerous here. Do not light one without permission, even outdoors. Living roots, thatch, dried reeds, hanging leaves, and smoke-filled enclosed spaces create a continuous fire risk.

Use only designated hearths. Keep clothing, bedding, packs, and loose cord away from flames. Never leave a fire unattended, and never assume that a wet roof or green wall cannot burn. Smoke may spread horizontally beneath the canopy, so a fire that appears contained can fill nearby rooms.

Your sleeping place may be raised, enclosed by woven walls, or incorporated into a tree or root chamber. Expect:

- Damp bedding.
- Insects and small animals nearby.
- Unfamiliar nighttime sounds.
- Flexible flooring.
- Limited privacy.
- Smoke used to dry materials or repel pests.
- The possibility that the shelter will be altered during your stay.

Keep your belongings suspended or inside sealed containers. Anything placed on the floor may become wet, moldy, inhabited, or incorporated into a repair. Do not hang food near your sleeping place unless instructed; it may attract animals.

Before sleeping, establish:

- The nearest safe route out.
- Where to go if water rises.
- Which sounds indicate ordinary activity and which indicate danger.
- How to summon help.
- Whether doors, curtains, and animal barriers should be closed.
- What to do if a bridge, wall, or roof shifts during the night.

## Animals and plants

Assume every animal has priority on a narrow path unless a resident tells you otherwise. Do not chase, feed, corner, photograph at close range, or make sudden movements toward wildlife.

Dogs or dog-like animals may be guardians rather than pets. Let their handlers introduce you. Avoid reaching over their heads or staring directly into their eyes. If a large animal enters the settlement, do not run unless a local tells you to; running may trigger pursuit or cause you to step off a walkway.

Monkeys, birds, and small mammals will investigate anything bright, edible, loose, or reflective. Keep food and tools secured. Do not leave medicine where an animal can reach it.

The vegetation is structurally important and culturally significant. Never cut, bend, harvest, or climb a plant simply because it appears abundant. Some roots are load-bearing. Some vines are handrails. Some leaves may be cultivated, medicinal, poisonous, or sacred. Ask before touching even apparently wild growth.

Also ask whether you may:

- Stand beneath fruiting trees.
- Remove insects from your clothing.
- Collect specimens.
- Walk through planted areas.
- Use local herbs.
- Enter hollow trunks or root chambers.
- Touch flowers, nests, or carved growth marks.

## Weather and terrain

The basin may change rapidly. A light rain can become a channel crossing a path within minutes. Before traveling, ask which routes remain safe during rain and whether any slopes are prone to mudslides or falling branches.

Be especially cautious during:

- Heavy rain.
- Strong wind in the canopy.
- Sudden clearing after long cloud cover, when runoff may still be rising.
- Fog, when sound and distance become deceptive.
- Night travel.
- Periods when animals enter the basin in unusual numbers.

Do not climb to high platforms, ridges, or tree forks without an experienced guide. Visibility may vanish abruptly, and wet stone or wood can be dangerously slick. If you become separated, do not wander downhill blindly. Stop, use the agreed signal, and wait. Sound carries strangely through wet vegetation, and the apparent direction of a call may be misleading.

## Social conduct

The inhabitants are likely to judge you less by your appearance than by whether you understand that the basin is shared infrastructure.

Useful behavior:

- Offer practical help rather than money alone.
- Carry, tie, hold, sort, mend, or clear when invited.
- Ask before photographing, recording, mapping, or drawing.
- Do not describe the settlement as primitive, dirty, chaotic, or overgrown.
- Do not call the animals pests or the plants scenery.
- Respect work areas, drying racks, pounding platforms, traps, and water-diversion structures.
- Return tools cleaner and drier than you received them.
- Keep your voice moderate; shouting is often reserved for warnings.
- Learn the local signals for danger, permission, and direction.
- Give people space even when the paths are crowded.

A useful first gift would be something durable and nonintrusive: strong needles, waterproof cord, high-quality cutting blades, sealed salt, simple medical dressings, or repair material—provided you have confirmed that such objects are welcome and do not disrupt local production. Do not introduce seeds, animals, pathogens, pesticides, or unfamiliar food plants.

If you are asked to move uphill, leave a bridge, extinguish a lamp, or surrender an object, comply immediately and ask questions afterward. The request may be based on information you cannot perceive: a rising channel, an aggressive animal, a structural weakness, or a plant that must not be disturbed.

## Mental preparation

The settlement will feel crowded even when no one is speaking directly to you. There is no clean division between wild and domestic, house and forest, path and stream, or building and organism. You may feel watched because animals are always watching. You may feel that nothing is dry, quiet, or motionless. That is normal here.

Do not expect the environment to become familiar quickly. Its patterns are learned through repetition: which drip means rainwater and which means a leaking roof, which birdcall is ordinary and which causes everyone to move, which green surface is soft moss and which is a concealed pool.

Prepare to move at the settlement’s pace rather than imposing your own. The place survives through continuous small corrections. A visitor who tries to force straight paths, clear vegetation, exclude animals, or create a perfectly dry private space will cause trouble. A visitor who learns to watch water, listen to the structures, keep belongings secured, and ask before altering anything can become useful.

The correct attitude is not “I am entering a village in a swamp.” It is:

**I am entering a living mountain system that people have learned to share.**

Arrive lightly equipped, medically cautious, observant, and willing to work. Keep warm, keep dry where possible, keep your feet protected, and assume that every surface, sound, animal, and current of water has a function you do not yet understand.
