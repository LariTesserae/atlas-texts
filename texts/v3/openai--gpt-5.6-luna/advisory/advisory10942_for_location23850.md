## Preparing to Visit the Open Rainworks

Treat the Open Rainworks less like a wilderness and more like a **living construction site with weather, wildlife, and unreliable local physics**. The landscape is open and visually beautiful, but openness does not mean safety. There is little cover, little stable ground, and very little that remains unchanged for long.

Your main objectives should be:

1. **Stay on authorized ground.**
2. **Protect hearing, balance, skin, and hydration.**
3. **Never mistake silence, stillness, or apparent emptiness for safety.**
4. **Be prepared for the terrain to change around you.**
5. **Assume that Vey’s infrastructure is observing you continuously.**

---

# Before Departure

## Confirm that you are arriving at the correct Vey

Because the sky sometimes exposes unfamiliar stars and the region may have connections to other worlds, do not rely on a normal spatial coordinate alone. Bring:

- A physical description of Vey’s settlement pattern
- A local contact or recognized host
- A sample of Vey’s permission-path signal, if available
- A means of verifying local time and gravity
- A return beacon that does not depend on local satellites, stars, or electromagnetic navigation

You should not arrive directly in the open plain unless you have an experienced guide. Request a landing or transit point within a maintained part of Vey, preferably near a staffed moisture station or medical structure.

Do not appear in a damp seam, a fresh fracture, a pool, a shadowed depression, or on an unmarked road. A location that looks flat and harmless may be actively forming around you.

## Get local permission in advance

Vey recognizes disturbances in the air and apparently uses them as identity signatures. A stranger walking through town may be detected before they can see a building. Arrange for your arrival pattern to be registered.

Give the settlement:

- Your name and any alternate names
- Your species or physiology
- Your expected body temperature and movement profile
- Whether you emit unusual radiation, sound, scent, or electrical charge
- The number of people and machines arriving with you
- Any large tools, weapons, drones, or autonomous equipment
- Whether you require water, shelter, medical assistance, or translation

Do not assume that “permission” means privacy. Vey archives footstep patterns, body heat, movement hesitation, and probably more than it openly admits. This is not necessarily hostile; it is simply part of how the settlement operates. Travel there with the expectation that your presence will be logged.

If you require confidentiality, discuss it before arrival. Do not attempt to create a blind spot. Vey apparently has none, and an attempt to hide from the infrastructure may be treated as a threat or a geological emergency.

# Clothing and Personal Equipment

## Wear full-body protection

Even on a dry day, parts of your body may suddenly encounter rain, condensation, salt, abrasive dust, or wet vegetation. Wear:

- A waterproof outer layer with sealed seams
- Breathable underlayers for heat management
- Close-fitting gloves
- High boots with soles designed for unstable ground
- Eye protection with side shielding
- A hood or helmet that protects against falling water and airborne mineral fragments
- A face covering or respirator rated for dust, biological particles, and unknown aerosols

Do not wear loose scarves, trailing cords, capes, or anything that can become entangled in vines, pressure bridges, maintenance machinery, or newly forming stone.

A good outer garment should be capable of handling the strange precipitation pattern: a vertical column of rain on one side of your body, horizontal water passing overhead, or a sudden full-basin collapse of suspended water. Carry a rapid-drying insulating layer even in warm weather. The major weather fronts may be brief but violent, and wet clothing will become heavy, cold, and potentially conductive.

## Protect your ears and nervous system

Sound is one of the principal hazards. Bring:

- High-quality hearing protection
- Active noise-canceling equipment with a manual override
- A bone-conduction or visual communication system
- A low-frequency alarm system for ground vibration
- A way to record and identify local warning tones

Do not rely only on noise cancellation. The basin contains subsonic vibration, persistent sounds, pressure phenomena, and tones that may have physical consequences. A device that suppresses all sound could conceal an important warning from the listening trees or the approach of a moving absence.

Use hearing protection selectively. Keep enough of the local sound available to recognize:

- Harmonic warnings from listening trees
- The onset of a weather front
- Sudden unnatural silence
- Changes in the ground’s vibration
- Structural warnings from Vey

Your equipment should distinguish between ordinary noise and meaningful patterns rather than simply making everything quiet.

## Protect against electrical charge

The dark winged organisms gather electrical charge from suspended rain. When they pass in great numbers, your hair may rise and the air may become metallic. Carry:

- Static-dissipating footwear
- Grounding straps suitable for local materials
- Shielded electronics
- A nonflammable outer layer
- Surge protection for medical and navigational devices

Do not raise metal poles, antennae, or conductive tools during a mass flight. Do not stand beneath a visible crack-like line of moving organisms unless a local guide tells you it is safe.

## Bring redundant navigation

Ordinary compasses, GPS, inertial systems, and visual landmarks may all become unreliable. Carry at least three independent systems:

1. A local beacon keyed to Vey’s infrastructure
2. A physical route map showing permission paths
3. A short-range line, marker, or tagged-particle system that can guide you back to a known structure

Do not assume that a route remains where you left it. Permission paths are maintained, but land outside them can rise, close, or shift. Markers placed on unapproved terrain may be swallowed or incorporated into the stone.

Keep your return beacon elevated or attached to your body. Do not leave it on the ground.

# Arrival Procedures

## Pause and observe

Once you arrive, do not immediately walk forward. Spend several minutes taking baseline readings:

- Gravity and local weight
- Temperature in light and shadow
- Ground vibration
- Air pressure
- Electrical charge
- Sound intensity and frequency
- Moisture levels
- Movement of nearby plants
- Location and condition of the nearest permission path

Pay particular attention to directional differences in weight. The variation may be slight, but it could affect jumping, balance, climbing, thrown objects, and the operation of tools.

Test the ground with a probe before placing full weight on any surface that is not marked. A hard-looking black crust may bend. A pale ridge may be freshly formed and structurally hollow. A patch of dust may conceal a developing channel or a living organism.

## Let Vey identify you

When entering the settlement, allow its doors and pathways to react naturally. Do not force open a wall, climb over a structure, disable a drone, or interfere with a door’s recognition process. Doors recognize disturbances in the air rather than ordinary keys, so hurried attempts to bypass one may be interpreted as deliberate intrusion.

Expect voices from roofs, bridges, or distant structures to address you by name. This is likely an ordinary feature of the settlement’s monitoring system. Respond clearly and identify yourself. Do not shout in panic; loud sound may have effects beyond communication.

# Rules for Moving Around the Plain

## Stay on permission paths

Unless accompanied by a local specialist, do not leave the blue-lit routes. The permission paths are not merely roads. They are areas whose surfaces have been instructed to remain stable.

Outside them:

- Ridges may rise overnight
- Ravines may open
- Wetlands may close over people or equipment
- Plants may spread rapidly
- Footprints may harden into permanent hollows
- Tools may become embedded in the mineral surface
- Ground may form around a stationary object

Never stand still for long on unapproved ground. Do not set down bags, cameras, tripods, tents, weapons, or tools outside a designated stable area. If you must stop, remain on a maintained surface or a platform designed for temporary weight.

Travel light. The local people do so because the landscape records and incorporates weight. Keep essential equipment attached to you or on a recognized vehicle. Do not drag heavy objects behind you.

## Do not touch fresh stone

The newest stone is still forming and is the least predictable. Avoid:

- Pale, sharply fractured ridges
- Glossy black surfaces that flex under pressure
- Areas with glowing or wet-looking seams
- Recent scars and channels
- Surfaces around fresh footprints
- Any rock that appears to be growing toward an object

If a tool or piece of equipment begins to sink, do not pull violently. Sudden force may cause the surrounding material to harden or expand. Alert Vey or a local guide.

## Treat shadows as environmental phenomena

Shadows can retain heat after their source has moved, and some vegetation responds to moving shadows. Do not shelter in a shadow merely because it is cooler. Measure it first.

A shadow may be:

- Hotter than direct light
- Associated with dormant vegetation
- A zone of condensation
- A persistent pressure or sound phenomenon
- A place where wildlife waits

Do not chase moving shadows, and do not assume that a shadow corresponds exactly to the object that cast it.

## Do not approach condensation pools casually

Dawn pools contain translucent organisms that remain active after the water disappears. Their biology is unknown. Avoid drinking, bathing, stepping into, or collecting from these basins.

Blue-white salt residues may be chemically or biologically active. Use sealed footwear and gloves near them. If you need water, obtain it from Vey’s collectors or approved conduits.

# Handling the Weather

## Learn the warning sequence

A major weather front announces itself through the listening trees. Before a front:

1. The trees begin to pulse.
2. Winged organisms leave the air.
3. Suspended water begins to fall.
4. The entire basin may become a white, violent roar.
5. Temporary rivers and channels form after the rain.

At the first sign of the trees pulsing, stop exploratory travel and seek a designated shelter. Do not wait to see clouds. The front arrives without them.

## Shelter correctly

A suitable shelter should be:

- Anchored to a maintained foundation
- Above known temporary flood levels
- Away from fresh ridges and channels
- Not directly beneath a listening tree unless that is standard local practice
- Equipped with drainage and pressure relief
- Connected to Vey’s warning and communication systems

Do not shelter beneath exposed machinery, bridges, floating vegetation, or a seemingly solid rock overhang. The storm may drive mud, organisms, debris, and newly forming mineral material across the ground.

During the storm:

- Stay indoors or inside a rated enclosure
- Do not open doors to inspect conditions
- Disconnect nonessential external equipment
- Keep clear of transparent conduits and collectors
- Avoid standing directly against exterior walls
- Monitor vibration and structural alarms
- Do not attempt to cross even a short distance unless local authorities direct you

The rain may last only minutes, but its immediate aftermath is more dangerous than the rainfall itself. Temporary rivers rush toward low areas that did not exist before the storm. Wait for Vey to declare a route stable.

# Wildlife and Biological Hazards

## Grazing animals

The tall, jointed animals with sensory filaments are not necessarily aggressive, but they are unpredictable. Do not approach from the front or attempt to block their path. Their transparency and displacement behavior make ordinary distance judgment useless.

If one becomes transparent:

- Stop moving toward it
- Do not throw anything
- Do not pursue the location where it disappears
- Check your surroundings for the reappearance point
- Let it pass

Keep food and biological samples sealed. Do not imitate their sounds or sensory movements.

## Winged organisms

Mass flights can produce electrical charge and interfere with visibility and communications. Move indoors or beneath a grounded shelter when they gather in large lines. If caught outside:

- Lower conductive equipment
- Avoid isolated high points
- Do not run beneath the swarm
- Keep your arms and antennae close to your body
- Use shielded communications

## Plants

Do not assume that a plant is inert because it is rooted. Some vegetation changes in response to observation, moving shadows, sound, moisture, or body heat.

Avoid:

- Walking through air-rooted vines
- Touching glassy reeds
- Disturbing black moss
- Staring at plants that appear to unfold only while watched
- Standing in dense vegetation after a storm
- Carrying cut specimens into Vey without quarantine

Use a pole or remote probe to test vegetation. Keep all skin covered. If plants withdraw from your shadow, give them room; this may be a defensive behavior or a sign that something else is approaching.

## Moving absences

These are the most important predator hazard. A moving absence is detected less by sight than by the sudden removal of sound. If a region of silence begins moving toward you:

1. Do not enter it.
2. Do not call out into it.
3. Do not assume it is empty.
4. Move toward a maintained path, structure, or group of people.
5. Keep your party together.
6. Avoid sudden loud sounds unless a local guide has identified that as an effective deterrent.

The danger may arrive after the silence, when all surrounding noise returns at once. Do not relax merely because sound has come back. Confirm that the zone has passed and that your group is accounted for.

A portable acoustic monitor should continuously compare local sound with a recent baseline. If all sound disappears in a moving region, treat it as a predator until proven otherwise.

# Sound and Voice Discipline

Sound is not merely information in the Open Rainworks. Sufficiently strong sound may become pressure, leave depressions, or fill channels with water. Therefore:

- Avoid shouting
- Keep speakers and alarms at low output
- Do not test weapons or explosives
- Do not sing unfamiliar songs outdoors
- Do not use high-powered sonic mapping without permission
- Keep children and animals from vocalizing near unstable ground
- Use visual or tactile communications whenever possible

Certain local songs may have hydrological effects. Do not perform music in the open plain unless you know exactly what it does.

If a voice, bell, or tone appears to persist after its source stops, do not approach the lingering sound. Report it and retreat to stable ground.

# Medical and Survival Preparations

Bring supplies for at least twice your expected duration, even if Vey is supporting you. Include:

- Water from a trusted origin
- Electrolytes and sealed food
- Broad-spectrum protective clothing
- Antihistamines and anti-inflammatory medication
- Antimicrobial treatment
- Eye and skin decontamination supplies
- Burn and cold-exposure treatment
- Hearing protection and treatment for acoustic injury
- Static and electrical injury supplies
- A rigid splint and pressure bandages
- Emergency oxygen or respiratory support, if your physiology requires it
- A sealed waste container
- Quarantine bags for contaminated clothing and equipment

Do not drink local water without testing. Do not eat local organisms. Do not assume that Vey’s air is biologically compatible with you merely because it is breathable.

Have a clear response plan for:

- Sudden displacement of a person or animal
- Partial incorporation into the ground
- Loss of hearing
- Unexplained silence
- Electrical charge buildup
- Flooding
- Gravity variation
- Contact with fast-growing vegetation
- Exposure to unknown spores or translucent organisms

Anyone who becomes disoriented, hears a persistent sound, loses time, or finds that their equipment has begun sinking should be removed from the field immediately.

# Social Conduct in Vey

Vey is sparse, monitored, and infrastructural. Its residents may appear distant because the settlement is designed around large spaces and continuous environmental maintenance. Do not interpret the lack of visible people as abandonment.

Observe these courtesies:

- Announce yourself through the settlement’s recognized channels
- Walk only where the town indicates
- Do not obstruct maintenance drones
- Do not touch conduits, collectors, bridges, or path markers
- Ask before recording people or infrastructure
- Assume that all movement is already recorded
- Do not attempt to defeat surveillance
- Keep your voice low and your equipment shielded
- Report any crack, ground movement, or unexplained plant growth immediately

A maintenance drone may be carrying a child, injured animal, medical device, or geological sensor. Do not swat at or obstruct one. If a drone approaches you, remain still and allow it to scan or route around you.

The town may speak your name from a distance. Answer calmly. If you hear your name from several directions at once, contact your host rather than following any one voice.

# Things Not to Bring

Do not bring, unless specifically approved by Vey:

- Heavy vehicles
- Explosives
- Industrial sonic equipment
- Unshielded high-voltage devices
- Autonomous digging machines
- Large quantities of soil, seeds, or biological material
- Unsecured metal poles or antennae
- Open flames
- Permanent stakes
- Objects you cannot afford to lose to the ground

Avoid bringing anything that depends on a fixed horizon, stable gravity, unobstructed radio signals, or ordinary darkness.

# Recommended First Excursion

For a first visit, limit yourself to a short route:

1. Begin inside Vey at a staffed facility.
2. Walk along a permission path to a nearby moisture collector.
3. Observe the plain from an elevated, anchored platform.
4. Visit no fresh ridges, pools, or open wetlands.
5. Remain within immediate reach of shelter.
6. Return before the listening trees begin any unusual harmonic pattern.
7. Review your recordings with a local expert before planning a longer trip.

Do not schedule your first excursion near a predicted weather front. Do not camp outside. Do not attempt to cross the plain between districts alone.

# Mental Preparation

The Open Rainworks will continually undermine ordinary instincts.

A wide empty space will contain hidden predators. A dry plain will hold sudden water. Rain will fall sideways or remain suspended. A living forest may appear as scattered sheets in the distance. A person may become transparent and reappear far away. Silence may be more dangerous than noise. The safest road may be a faint blue line that looks insignificant, while the inviting open ground may be actively growing.

You should prepare to feel watched, exposed, and slightly off-balance. That sensation is not merely psychological; the settlement really is monitoring you, the ground really does vary, and the environment really does respond to sound and weight.

The correct attitude is neither panic nor confidence. It is **measured humility**.

Move slowly. Keep your equipment attached. Listen without surrendering your hearing. Follow marked paths. Respect every warning from the trees, the town, and the animals. When the land begins to change, assume that it has noticed you—and give it room.
