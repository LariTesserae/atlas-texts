## Preparing to Visit

Treat this as a hostile, inhabited industrial ecosystem—not as a city with unusual architecture. The primary dangers are cold, saturation, unstable ground, poor visibility, unfamiliar biology, and accidentally interfering with systems that operate on geological timescales.

### Before departure

**Obtain local authorization and a guide.**  
Do not arrive unannounced. The city has strict rules concerning drainage, sound, migration routes, excavation, construction, and contamination. A visitor should be registered with whichever authority coordinates water, transit, fauna, and foundation-stability systems. Ideally, travel with a local guide or liaison who can interpret:

- warning lights and pressure signals;
- permitted routes;
- animal corridors;
- forbidden sounds and frequencies;
- areas of active geological change;
- districts where walls, floors, or machinery are biologically integrated;
- emergency signals, which may be visual, vibrational, or transmitted through surfaces rather than spoken.

You should also establish what counts as consent when interacting with apparent infrastructure. Some walls may be living filters, some crystals may be communication organs, and some “empty” buildings may be occupied by fauna.

**Arrange a quarantine protocol.**  
Your body, clothing, equipment, microbes, lubricants, and waste may all be ecologically disruptive. The city is wet, dense with organisms, and built around carefully balanced drainage systems. Bring only equipment that can be sterilized and sealed. Expect inspection on arrival and departure. Do not import food, soil, living plants, untreated biological materials, or ordinary lubricants.

Your guide should know how to respond if local organisms attach to you, enter your clothing, or begin nesting in your equipment. Do not kill or remove anything without instruction.

### Physical preparation

**Prepare for persistent cold and complete wetness.**  
This is not merely a rainy place. Water is suspended in the air, condenses on everything, enters machinery, and accumulates in every seam. Ordinary waterproof clothing will eventually become cold, heavy, and unreliable.

Bring:

- a fully sealed, insulated environmental suit;
- redundant waterproof gloves and boots;
- a sealed hood with a clear, anti-fog faceplate;
- electrically insulated outer layers;
- vapor barriers under insulation;
- several changes of dry underclothing in individually sealed containers;
- chemical or electrical hand warmers rated for wet environments;
- tether points and non-slip footwear;
- sealed containers for electronics, documents, medicine, and food.

Your outer clothing should not shed fibers or flakes into channels. Avoid exposed Velcro, loose straps, porous fabrics, absorbent padding, and untreated leather. Every seam should be designed for condensation, not just falling rain.

**Do not assume the atmosphere is breathable.**  
The description gives no guarantee about oxygen content, pressure, particulate load, or biological compatibility. A high basin may have reduced oxygen, accumulated gases, or atmospheric organisms. Bring a supplied-air respirator or closed-cycle breathing system, not merely a filter mask. Carry enough breathing reserve for a delayed evacuation; transit and rescue systems move slowly.

The breathing system should include:

- oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitoring;
- humidity control;
- particulate and aerosol filtration;
- a sealed emergency air reserve;
- a way to prevent condensation from freezing or obstructing valves;
- alarms that use vibration or light, since audible warnings may be confused with local machinery.

Do not remove your respirator simply because locals appear unmasked. Their biology may be radically different.

**Protect hearing and balance.**  
Sound is unusually deceptive here. Water and enclosed structures carry vibration over great distances. Machinery, animal calls, pressure releases, and ground-borne signals may be physically harmful or may trigger local responses.

Use hearing protection that still permits communication—preferably active, low-profile protection with a vibration or visual backup. Avoid loudspeakers, sonic beacons, alarms, recreational music, gunfire, or testing unfamiliar devices. A sound that seems harmless to you may disturb migration herds or be interpreted by the city as a structural or emergency signal.

Because vertical streets and inclined transitways may operate in fog and darkness, bring an inertial navigation aid, a physical tether, and a reliable method of detecting orientation. Do not trust apparent distance: a noise from below may seem nearby, while a nearby animal may be acoustically invisible behind wet structures.

### Mobility and personal safety

**Use only designated surfaces.**  
The city’s roads may float over fields, conceal machinery, or move imperceptibly. Some plates may be held in place by invisible forces; others may be part of a long-term transit system. Do not step onto an unmarked surface, lean against a bridge, or place equipment in a drainage seam without confirmation.

Take special care around:

- grates and channels, where blind animals move;
- inclined transitways;
- vertical cargo shafts;
- transparent floors and roofs;
- wet crystal growths;
- sealed districts;
- areas where a building or neighborhood appears to be migrating;
- ground that gives off a regular pulse, warmth, or vibration.

Wear a tether when moving near drops, shafts, suspended roads, or fog-obscured edges. Keep one hand free for wall contact, but touch surfaces only with a gloved hand after checking that they are not conductive, responsive, or biologically active.

**Expect slow systems and delayed rescue.**  
Nothing may happen quickly enough to save you from a poor decision. A door may take minutes to open. A bridge may move over hours. A rescue operation may require reshaping the surrounding stone over days. Carry your own supplies rather than assuming a nearby shelter can be reached.

At minimum, carry:

- several days of water and sealed food;
- medical supplies suited to cold, immersion, and pressure injuries;
- emergency shelter or a heated survival cocoon;
- redundant lights, including low-intensity lights that will not disturb fauna;
- a passive locator beacon approved by local authorities;
- physical route markers that can be removed without leaving residue;
- a compact repair kit for seals and breathing equipment;
- a way to record time without relying on sunlight.

Keep your group together. Visibility can vanish within a few meters, while sound can make separated travelers seem close.

### Biological precautions

The fauna are not background wildlife. They are part of transport, drainage, communication, construction, and possibly governance.

**Do not approach herds, swarms, or drainage animals without permission.**

- Do not shine bright lights into the swarms.
- Do not brush them off walls or walk through them if another route exists.
- Do not block channels or grates.
- Do not feed, trap, collect, or dissect anything.
- Do not touch the branching conductive crystals.
- Do not imitate animal tones or test whether creatures respond to your voice.
- Do not enter an apparent nest, abandoned building, or “empty” maintenance chamber.

The larger animals may be harmless in the ordinary sense yet essential to the city’s atmospheric or electrical systems. A herd standing motionless may be filtering the air or exchanging signals with the foundation. Give them a wide route and never position yourself between a herd and prevailing wind, water collection structures, or migration corridors.

Assume that all water is contaminated until treated. It may contain unfamiliar organisms, dissolved minerals, conductive particles, or biological signals. Carry your own drinking supply and use sealed collection equipment only if local authorities specifically require samples.

### Light, sound, and communications

Bring several forms of communication:

1. ordinary spoken communication for close range;
2. text or visual signaling for fog and machinery noise;
3. short-range radio if permitted;
4. vibration-based or wired communication for sealed districts;
5. a simple emergency code agreed upon with the guide.

Avoid continuous bright illumination. Use dim, shielded, narrow-beam lights, preferably in colors that local fauna do not respond to. Never point a beam into a tower, water membrane, herd, crystal formation, or opaque wall simply to see what happens.

Marking a route with paint, flares, chemical tracers, or discarded objects may violate local water and migration rules. Use approved temporary markers or digital mapping. Keep an updated map, but do not assume it will remain correct: districts migrate, bridges unfold, channels alter course, and geology may absorb or expose infrastructure.

### Conduct around the city

The central etiquette is **leave every system at least as clean, stable, and unobstructed as you found it**.

- Return or properly process all water.
- Pack out every piece of waste.
- Keep away from drainage channels unless assigned to them.
- Never drill, dig, anchor, or sample the ground casually.
- Never cast a long-term shadow over a marked migration route.
- Never operate equipment that produces persistent vibration.
- Ask before entering any structure, even if it appears abandoned.
- Treat slow movement as intentional; do not “help” a bridge, tower, membrane, or district move.
- If the ground pulses, warms, cracks, or changes sound, stop and report it rather than investigating.

Do not mistake the city’s lack of obvious people for abandonment. Occupancy may be expressed through swarms, animals, pressure signals, water movement, or structural behavior. The city may be watching and responding without using faces, windows, or speech.

### Psychological preparation

The environment will undermine ordinary human instincts.

You may never see the sky or sun. You may not know whether you are traveling uphill, whether a distant sound is close, or whether a wall is architecture or anatomy. The city’s motion will mostly be too slow to notice, yet its consequences may occur without warning. There will be no clean silence, dry surface, open horizon, or truly unoccupied room.

Prepare for:

- sensory confusion caused by fog, reflections, echoes, and pressure;
- chronic damp and cold;
- difficulty sleeping because of continuous vibration and sound;
- anxiety around apparently motionless animals;
- uncertainty about whether systems are mechanical, biological, or geological;
- long delays during transit or emergencies;
- the unsettling awareness that the city may be a participant rather than a backdrop.

Use a strict buddy system and scheduled check-ins. Keep a personal log of location, time, body temperature, breathing status, and exposure to local organisms. Travelers should agree in advance on stop conditions: hypothermia symptoms, disorientation, suit leakage, unexplained skin contact, auditory distress, or any inability to distinguish route markers.

### Recommended first visit

For an initial visit, do not attempt an expedition into the outer basin. Stay in a regulated, inhabited terrace near an authorized arrival facility. Spend the first day acclimating to the atmosphere, sound, and navigation system. Observe the movement of water and fauna before traveling.

A sensible first itinerary would be:

1. medical and biological screening;
2. equipment inspection and local environmental briefing;
3. supervised walk on a fixed, well-maintained terrace;
4. introduction to drainage and migration rules;
5. short transit on a stable route;
6. return before local visibility or pressure conditions deteriorate;
7. decontamination and equipment quarantine.

Most importantly, do not arrive with the attitude that the city is unfinished and therefore available for exploration. It is unfinished in the way a living body, a young mountain, or an evolving ecosystem is unfinished. Its apparent gaps are often active processes. Its empty spaces may be organs, routes, nests, or future foundations.

Visit quietly, remain sealed, stay on negotiated surfaces, and assume that the mountain—and everything built upon it—is still making decisions.
