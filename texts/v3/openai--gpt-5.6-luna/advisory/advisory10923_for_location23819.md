## Prepare for a cold, enclosed, water-conscious settlement

Treat this as a living subterranean community, not as a ruin or wilderness cave. The inhabitants have adapted to a very specific balance of light, condensation, roots, passageways, and slowly descending stone. Your greatest danger is likely not violence but accidentally interfering with one of those systems.

### Before you go

**Find a local guide or intermediary if at all possible.** The entrance is nearly invisible, the interior is difficult to map, and the residents communicate through quiet speech, signs, and wall-tapping. An ordinary surface traveler could easily mistake a household passage for a public stair, or walk into a water channel, root garden, shrine, or structurally important opening.

A guide should be able to tell you:

- When the roof-light reaches the angle that reveals the entrance
- Which seam is the true entrance
- Where visitors may sleep or wait
- How to announce yourself without shouting
- Which stairs must be crossed barefoot
- Which areas are private, sacred, or structurally fragile
- How to respond when seepage begins

Do not rely on modern mapping or a straight-line sense of direction. The place has grown over itself for generations. Marking walls with paint, chalk, knives, or bright fabric would be intrusive and might damage the settlement. Use a removable cord only if the residents approve, and never attach anything to a plant, channel, lintel, or old stair.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Bring clothing suited to a perpetually shaded cellar:

- Several warm layers rather than one heavy coat
- A moisture-resistant outer layer
- Wool or another insulating fabric that remains useful when damp
- Gloves with enough dexterity for climbing and handling vessels
- A hood or close-fitting cap for dripping ceilings
- Spare socks and underclothes sealed in waterproof bags
- Soft, flexible footwear with good grip

The interior is crowded and vertical. Hard boots, backpacks with projecting frames, dangling straps, and metal equipment will catch on vines, awnings, ladders, and people. Use a compact soft pack that can be held close to the body. Keep both hands free where possible.

Bring a pair of easily removable shoes. The oldest stairs are crossed barefoot, and this is likely a rule of reverence rather than a mere practical custom. Wash your feet before doing so, if water can be spared, and follow the guide rather than guessing which stairways qualify.

Useful equipment includes:

- A covered personal water flask
- A small, waterproof lamp with a dim setting
- A second light source kept sealed and unused except in an emergency
- A simple respirator or cloth mask for dust at the entrance
- Waterproof containers for documents and medicines
- A compact first-aid kit
- Blunt, quiet tools rather than sharp or noisy ones
- A way to record notes without scraping, tapping, or illuminating walls excessively

Do not bring open flames unless the inhabitants specifically request them. Smoke could damage plants, obscure the already diffuse light, foul the air, and alarm residents. Your electric light should be used sparingly. The settlement is bright enough to see in, and a harsh beam will mark you as disruptive.

### Water and food

Arrive already hydrated, and carry enough water for the journey in and out. Do not assume that the settlement will offer you a drink immediately. Water is not a commodity there; it is an anticipated event and a communal responsibility.

Never take water from a basin, jar, channel, cistern, or plant without permission. A vessel that appears unattended may belong to the whole settlement or may be positioned for a specific seepage pattern. Do not wash, fill your bottle, or dip your hands into a pool unless invited.

Bring food that is:

- Dry
- Odor-light
- Compact
- Non-crumbing
- Free of seeds, insects, or organisms that could establish themselves there

Avoid bringing fresh fruit, soil, untreated plant material, or live animals between worlds. The hollow is an extremely concentrated ecosystem. A foreign fungus, mite, mold, or seed could be disastrous. Clean your clothing, boots, and equipment before entry. Ideally, arrive with equipment that has been heat-treated or otherwise inspected for pests, while recognizing that too much heat or chemical residue may itself be unacceptable inside.

Do not casually accept food or water from the residents without asking what it is and how it is customarily consumed. Their fruits, mushrooms, insects, and moss-grown foods may be safe for them but not for your biology.

### Entering the hollow

The entrance is revealed by circumstance, not by force. Wait for the correct solar angle and look for the pale vapor. Do not widen the seam. Do not pry at it, hammer it, insert equipment into it, or clear away surrounding stone and thorn unless residents or a guide direct you.

The vapor’s smell is a useful confirmation, but do not lean directly over the opening or inhale deeply. It may contain concentrated moisture, spores, or gases from the hollow. Enter slowly. Keep your body low, and expect the transition to be less like descending into a cavern than slipping into a folded pocket in the earth.

Once inside:

- Pause to let your eyes adjust.
- Keep your voice low.
- Move only where the route is clearly established.
- Keep your shoulders and pack narrow.
- Never stand beneath a low ledge without checking whether it is used as a water collector or growing surface.
- Avoid touching moss, vines, crystals, snails, walls, and hanging vessels unless invited.
- Do not assume a wall is merely architectural; it may contain a cistern, root system, shrine, or old sealed room.

### How to behave among the inhabitants

The settlement’s quiet is social, practical, and possibly sacred. Speak softly enough that someone nearby can hear you, but not someone several rooms away. Do not call across the hollow. Do not clap, whistle, shout, or use loud electronic alerts.

Watch how people communicate. They may use:

- Small hand gestures
- Head movements
- Taps against particular walls
- Signals passed from bridge to bridge
- Deliberately close conversation

Ask before attempting to imitate their tapping codes. A pattern that sounds like a greeting to you may be a water warning, a household summons, or an emergency.

Keep your hands visible when approaching someone. Offer no object by placing it on a wall or ledge without permission; surfaces are heavily used and may be part of the city’s water, food, or shrine systems. Gifts should be practical and clean: good fiber, carefully made needles, a quiet cutting tool, a durable vessel, or useful knowledge. Avoid metal gifts unless you understand their implications. Metal is scarce and precious, and giving a large quantity could distort local relationships.

Do not photograph or brightly illuminate people without asking. In the mineral light, your equipment may be startling. Do not treat their homes, gardens, animals, or construction layers as curiosities for inspection. A room built into a foundation may still be inhabited, remembered, or ritually significant even if its original purpose is unknown.

### Respect the settlement’s rules

The rules should be followed literally:

- **Do not block a light crack.** Do not stand beneath it for long, hang equipment from it, or place a pack where it interrupts illumination.
- **Do not narrow a roof channel.** Keep away from grooves and dripping points unless directed there.
- **Do not cut living growth.** Do not trim vines for passage, pick fruit, scrape moss, or remove roots from clothing without permission.
- **Store water in three places.** If you are given water, ask where your personal reserve should be kept. Do not consolidate everything into one bottle.
- **Cross the oldest stairs barefoot.** Follow the local practice precisely.
- **Stop work at seepage.** If water begins to appear, put down your tools immediately and take instructions. Do not continue walking, eating, unpacking, or exploring while residents are carrying vessels.
- **Preserve necessary emptiness.** Do not leave bags, equipment, sleeping mats, or discarded packaging in niches or unused corners. In this place, “unused” does not mean unclaimed.

Most importantly, do not create a blockage in the central **Listening Way**. Cross it quickly but carefully, preferably after watching others. Do not sit there, camp there, place luggage there, or conduct experiments. If residents kneel and listen to the stone, give them room and become quiet. The channel is likely both infrastructure and communal ritual.

### Health and physical hazards

The cold is steady and can become dangerous because it does not feel dramatic. You may not notice hypothermia developing in a still, enclosed environment. Keep dry layers available, eat regularly, and do not sleep directly on stone.

Expect:

- Dripping water from unexpected points
- Slippery moss and polished limestone
- Narrow ledges and shoulder-height crossings
- Low roofs and protruding beams
- Sudden darkness when a crack’s light changes
- Mold, spores, and unfamiliar insects
- Minor oxygen or air-quality changes in crowded lower spaces
- Claustrophobia and disorientation
- Cold water and deep basins hidden under vegetation

Move slowly. Use three points of contact on ladders and steep stairs. Never climb onto a roof, platform, tree, or wall merely to get a better view. The city has been compacted around old structures; what appears solid may be a former room, a thin clay facing, or a roof channel.

If you have asthma, severe allergies, panic disorder, balance problems, or difficulty tolerating enclosed environments, bring appropriate medication and a companion who knows how to use it. Keep medical supplies sealed but accessible. Do not assume the local residents can treat a modern emergency.

### Sleeping and staying

You will probably be offered a place only after your presence has been assessed. Do not choose a sleeping location yourself. A dry shelf may be a family’s storage area, an airflow route, or a place reserved for collecting condensation.

Sleep in minimal clothing layers, with your belongings secured close to you. Do not hang anything across a passage. Do not use a bright light while others are sleeping or tending nocturnal animals. The distinction between day and night is based on changes in the roof-light, flowers, and activity, so ask how visitors should mark time.

Keep your exit plan clear. If the entrance is difficult to locate from inside, have the guide identify it again before you settle. Do not leave during a period of seepage, roof movement, or communal water collection unless there is an emergency.

### Departure

Leave no foreign material behind:

- No packaging
- No food scraps
- No fibers, batteries, medicine, or disinfectant
- No marked stones
- No cuttings or seeds
- No waste in channels or basins

Before leaving, inspect your clothing and equipment for snails, insects, spores, seeds, and moss fragments. This protects both the hollow and whatever world you return to.

The best visitor will be unobtrusive: warm, quiet, clean, compact, patient, and willing to wait. Do not arrive expecting a tour of an underground marvel. Enter as someone being temporarily admitted into a city whose survival depends on noticing what is small: a bead of water, a new root, a blocked crack, a tremor beneath stone. If you can learn to notice those things too, the inhabitants may regard you not as an intruder, but as a person capable of staying beneath the roof without making it descend faster.
