## Preparation for the Plateau

Treat this as a high, cold, recently disturbed industrial wilderness—not as a scenic hiking destination. It is probably survivable for an ordinary human, but only with careful equipment and a conservative attitude toward anything that appears stable, familiar, or abandoned.

### Before arrival

**Confirm the basics of the local environment if possible:**

- Atmospheric composition and pressure
- Gravity and the behavior of liquids
- Length of day and night
- Temperature range, including overnight temperatures
- Radiation and ultraviolet levels
- Whether local plants, water, molds, and insects are biologically compatible with you
- Whether the industrial system recognizes, tolerates, or reacts to outsiders
- Whether the plateau is subject to sudden geological or spatial shifts

The bright, nearly colorless sky and sharply defined shadows suggest intense ultraviolet exposure and possibly a thin atmosphere. Do not assume that cool air means mild sunlight. Bring high-SPF sunscreen, lip protection, UV-rated goggles, and clothing that covers the skin.

If your arrival method allows it, establish a return marker well away from the infrastructure and preferably on stable ground. Do not place it on a rock shelf, beside a rail line, inside a building, or beneath a transmission cable. The landscape changes slowly, but the local geometry may not be entirely reliable.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing suitable for cold, wet, windy alpine terrain:

- Layered thermal clothing
- A windproof and waterproof outer shell
- Durable trousers, preferably reinforced
- Waterproof insulated boots with aggressive soles
- Gaiters to keep water, mud, moss, and plant fragments out
- Cut-resistant gloves
- A warm hat and spare dry socks
- Eye protection against reflected sunlight and rock splinters
- A helmet, especially near cliffs, machinery, pipes, and old buildings

The stone is freshly fractured and unusually sharp. Ordinary hiking shoes and lightweight fabric will be inadequate. Expect to scrape yourself on basalt, schist, broken concrete, exposed bolts, and the edges of concealed machinery.

Carry at least two independent light sources, preferably with red-light modes, and spare batteries kept warm and dry. The plateau is quiet enough that a flashlight may make you conspicuous over long distances, and many buildings may contain stairwells, shafts, or submerged rooms.

### Travel over the ground

Move slowly. This is not terrain where speed is useful.

Use trekking poles or an ice axe for balance, but do not trust them to hold in moss-covered cracks. Test every shelf before putting your full weight on it. Avoid:

- Outward-leaning shelves
- Rock undercut by water
- Green surfaces that may be moss over a void
- Areas where pipes disappear beneath the ground
- The edges of new channels
- Cliffs with no visible drainage pattern
- Any slope that seems unnaturally clean or geometrically straight

The stone has not weathered into rounded, predictable forms. A fall is likely to produce deep cuts, not merely bruises. Bring a serious first-aid kit containing pressure dressings, clotting gauze, irrigation fluid, antiseptic, blister care, splints, and enough supplies for evacuation delays.

Use a rope for gullies and exposed traverses. A compact harness would be sensible. Do not rely on the apparent age of a surface: young rock may be stronger than old concrete, while old concrete may be hollow, tilted, or internally compromised.

### Water and weather

Water is widespread but not necessarily safe.

Rain and condensation collect in places where contamination may be concentrated: beneath machinery, inside pipes, around pumping stations, and in pools that receive runoff from industrial structures. Do not drink untreated water. Carry a filter, chemical purification tablets, and a means of boiling. A UV purifier may be unreliable if the local water contains unusual organisms or suspended minerals.

Bring more water than you expect to need. The plateau’s small pools and temporary channels may vanish rapidly, and the apparent presence of water can be deceptive. A pool reflecting a different sky may be an optical anomaly, a chemical anomaly, or something worse; do not use it for drinking, bathing, or testing with bare skin.

Weather can change abruptly. The vegetation and the exposed rock suggest repeated wetting, freezing, and drying, even if the air is presently calm. Take shelter before rain begins rather than after. Sudden rain could fill the young gullies quickly, even if no permanent rivers are visible.

Do not shelter:

- Beneath overhanging shelves
- In drainage channels
- Inside unverified pipes
- In buildings with water on the floor
- Near transmission towers
- Beside the rail line
- Under the concrete arches

A proper expedition tent should be pitched on a broad, flat, visibly stable surface, away from channels and machinery. Stake it aggressively; the wind may be intermittent rather than weak.

### Altitude and health

The plateau is high enough that altitude sickness is a genuine concern. Ascend gradually if your arrival method permits it. Watch for:

- Headache
- Nausea
- Unusual fatigue
- Confusion
- Poor coordination
- Shortness of breath at rest
- Difficulty sleeping

Do not interpret fatigue or dizziness as merely atmospheric. The constant low tremor, faint electrical activity, unusual shadows, and possible industrial emissions may complicate symptoms.

Carry oxygen only as an emergency aid, not as a substitute for acclimatization. If anyone develops confusion, inability to walk straight, blue lips, or worsening breathlessness, descend or return immediately.

### Plants, animals, and biological precautions

The vegetation should be treated as unknown alien biology even if it resembles familiar ferns, mosses, grasses, and shrubs.

Do not:

- Eat anything
- Crush plants against bare skin
- Sleep directly on moss
- Drink from leaf condensate
- Burn unfamiliar plant material
- Collect glowing lichen
- Touch the pale jointed “trees” or disturb their roots
- Walk through dense vegetation without eye protection

The plants may contain irritants, toxins, spores, or organisms adapted to the local industrial system. Bring sealed sample containers if collecting is necessary, and use gloves and a respirator rated for biological particles. Keep samples outside living quarters.

The animals are more concerning because their behavior is unclear. The lack of calls and visible predation does not mean the plateau is harmless. The long-legged distant forms may be passive, camouflaged, or simply observing. Do not approach them, pursue them, call to them, or assume that an animal standing motionless is unaware of you.

Avoid traveling alone if possible. Maintain visual contact with companions and use physical line markers in addition to electronic navigation.

### Industrial infrastructure

Assume that every surviving component is still active, even if it looks abandoned.

The pumping stations, pipes, transmission towers, rail cars, and control buildings may operate automatically and may not have human safety standards. Do not open panels, climb towers, cross cables, enter machinery rooms, or place equipment on tracks.

The narrow rail line is especially dangerous. The cars are nearly silent, driverless, and likely to follow schedules that are invisible to you. Stay well clear of the tracks—at least several meters, more near bends and shelves—and never walk along them as a convenient trail. A car may approach without warning, or the line may carry something other than the visible vehicles.

Do not stand beneath the transmission cables. Their sag does not obey ordinary gravity, and the slow-moving radiance may be a traveling energy discharge. Keep clear of towers, grounding structures, exposed conduits, and any cable that emits light, hum, warmth, or vibration. Avoid metal objects during electrical activity, including trekking poles and drones.

The amber-lit pipes should be assumed hazardous. They may carry energy, hot fluid, radiation, or information-like phenomena rather than water. Do not tap them, lean against them, or follow them into the ground.

The buildings require particular caution:

- A room containing ankle-deep clean water may be flooded, pressurized, chemically contaminated, or spatially abnormal.
- A staircase ending in solid rock may indicate a sealed structure—or a non-Euclidean transition.
- Clouded windows may conceal machinery, organisms, or a pressure difference.
- Doors that open by themselves should not be entered immediately.
- Do not close a door behind you until you know it can be reopened.

Mark every entrance and keep a written record of the route. Electronic mapping may fail or quietly accommodate impossible geometry.

### Dealing with the anomalies

The plateau’s unusual features are subtle, which makes them more dangerous than dramatic anomalies. A traveler can become disoriented without realizing that anything is wrong.

Adopt a strict observation protocol:

1. Record the time, location, weather, and direction of travel.
2. Photograph shadows, reflections, water levels, and the position of the sun.
3. Use physical markers as well as GPS or equivalent tools.
4. Check your compass against fixed landmarks, but do not assume the compass is correct.
5. Never navigate solely by a reflection, a shadow, a cable, or a rail line.
6. If two people perceive different skies, distances, or routes, stop traveling and compare notes.
7. If a familiar object appears in two places, do not choose either route until the discrepancy is resolved.
8. If the terrain seems to “correct” itself when you look away, retreat to the last confirmed location.

Avoid prolonged stillness near anomalous pools, towers, arches, or machinery unless you are deliberately conducting an observation. The landscape seems to reveal its irregularities only after sustained attention. A person who becomes absorbed in watching a cable’s radiance or a plant’s movement may lose awareness of weather, companions, or time.

Keep an analog watch, paper maps, pencil, mechanical compass, and physical distance markers. Bring redundant communication devices, but assume that radio transmissions may be delayed, reflected, rerouted, or answered by something that is not your party.

### Energy and shelter

The place may have electricity, but do not count on using it. Bring your own power banks, solar panels, and chemical warmers. The bright sunlight makes solar charging attractive, but the local radiation or electrical fields may damage equipment.

Set up a base camp before exploring. It should have:

- A clear view of nearby approaches
- No direct line beneath cables
- No obvious channels or seepage
- Solid rock or compact ground underfoot
- A visible route back to the arrival marker
- Enough distance from machinery that low-frequency vibration is tolerable

The deep turbine tremor may cause fatigue, nausea, disturbed sleep, or a false sense of bodily motion. If the vibration becomes stronger, rhythmic, or changes frequency, leave the area rather than trying to determine its source.

### Food and waste

Bring all food. Use sealed, low-odor containers and do not leave scraps. The apparent scarcity of birds and insects may mean that the local ecosystem is unusually efficient, chemically constrained, or simply watching.

Pack out everything. Do not bury waste near streams, machinery, or vegetation. Do not introduce microbes, seeds, or insects from your own universe. In a place where infrastructure and biology have already merged, even a small contamination event could have unpredictable consequences.

### Recommended mission limits

For a first visit, keep the expedition short:

- Arrive during the brightest part of the local day.
- Establish a camp and test the air, water, instruments, and local time.
- Explore only a small radius on the first outing.
- Avoid entering underground structures.
- Avoid the rail line and transmission network entirely.
- Return before darkness or heavy weather.
- Maintain a hard turnaround time regardless of what you find.

The plateau does not feel actively hostile. That should not reassure you too much. Its danger is that it is functioning normally according to rules that are only partly shared with your own world. The land, water, plants, and machines have entered into a long, quiet arrangement. An ordinary human visitor is an interruption in that arrangement.

Move carefully, make little contact, disturb nothing that is still working, and leave as soon as the landscape begins to seem more attentive than merely strange.
