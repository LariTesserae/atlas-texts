## Preparing to visit the high country

Treat this place less like a mountain wilderness and more like a stable, exposed environmental anomaly. The obvious dangers—cold, altitude, cliffs, scarce water—are serious, but the stranger dangers are the lack of temporal change, the constant wind, and the possibility that ordinary human equipment and habits will not remain reliable there.

### 1. Arrive in stages if possible

Do not enter directly from a low-altitude world unless you have no alternative. The description suggests genuinely thin air, even if the cold is not extreme. A person may be able to breathe, but still suffer rapidly from hypoxia, headache, confusion, poor coordination, and impaired judgment.

Bring:

- Supplemental oxygen, preferably in several independent containers
- A regulator that works in cold and low pressure
- A pulse oximeter and an analog backup for basic monitoring
- Medication for altitude sickness, if medically appropriate
- A way to descend or evacuate without relying on your own strength
- A partner trained to recognize confusion and hypoxia in you

Acclimatization may not work normally if the place is “settled outside of time.” Your body might not adapt, or it might remain in the same state indefinitely. Assume that the first hour is as dangerous as the first day.

If you have a portal or transport device, establish a low-country staging area before proceeding upward. Keep an open return route. Do not assume that a portal left unattended will remain aligned with the same location or time.

### 2. Dress for wind, not merely temperature

The air is cold but not brutally cold. The wind, however, is continuous and immense. It will strip heat from exposed skin, interfere with balance, fill clothing with dust, and make ordinary speech impossible.

Use a layered system:

- Moisture-wicking base layer
- Insulating middle layer
- Windproof outer shell
- Face covering and insulated hood
- Gloves with reinforced palms
- Gaiters sealed over the tops of your boots
- Tight-fitting eye protection
- Durable, high-ankle boots with excellent grip

Do not rely on a tent as your main shelter. A normal tent is likely to become an airborne object, shred against the rock, or act as a sail. Bring a low-profile shelter that can be anchored to existing cracks or heavy stone, and carry redundant anchors, pitons, webbing, and ground screws if the rock permits them.

Never camp on:

- A narrow ridge
- The lip of a basin
- The underside of a visibly unstable ledge
- A wind tunnel between terraces
- A quartz seam that produces a sustained tone
- A dry-looking gully, since it may be a permanent or intermittent water channel despite the apparent lack of weather

The best shelter will probably be an existing hollow on the lee side of a broad terrace. Even there, expect vibration through the ground.

### 3. Protect your eyes and hearing

The brightness may be more dangerous than the cold. The combination of hard sunlight, white mineral surfaces, ice, and glare from basin floors creates a real risk of snow blindness or retinal injury even without snow.

Bring:

- High-quality glacier or mountaineering goggles
- A spare pair
- Side-shielded sunglasses with strong ultraviolet protection
- A dark face covering or visor for periods of extreme reflection

Do not remove your eye protection just because the sky is clear. The reflected light comes from below and from many directions.

The wind is also a permanent acoustic hazard. In some areas, you will not be able to hear your own voice. Prolonged exposure could cause hearing damage, exhaustion, panic, and communication failure.

Use:

- Sealed hearing protection or active noise-reduction earmuffs
- A hardwired or bone-conduction communication system
- Hand signals agreed upon before arrival
- Visual status boards, flags, or light signals
- A written emergency protocol in case all electronics fail

Do not completely isolate yourself from the environment. You still need to hear warnings from companions, rockfall, animals, and changes in the terrain’s vibration. Use protection that reduces the roar without making you deaf to nearby danger.

In particular, avoid standing close to the “singing” quartz seams. The metallic tone may be physically harmful at close range, and the vibration could interfere with balance, instruments, or implanted medical devices.

### 4. Bring more water than you think you need

Water exists, but not in a form you should casually trust. It is sparse, separated by great distances, and may contain unusual minerals or organisms. The streams do not behave like ordinary streams: they neither swell nor thin, and they never fully freeze. That makes them useful, but also makes them biologically and chemically unfamiliar.

Carry your own supply sufficient for the entire planned visit and an unexpected delay. Bring:

- Insulated containers
- Multiple independent purification methods
- Fine filters for insects and mineral particles
- Chemical treatment and a method of boiling, if fuel is practical
- A mineral and microbial testing kit
- Electrolytes, since dry air and constant wind will increase fluid loss

Do not drink directly from the black pools or seeps. Do not assume that clear water is safe. The permanent streams may contain microorganisms adapted to this world, and “permanent” water could mean that whatever contaminates it also remains indefinitely.

Avoid placing food, clothing, or equipment in contact with wet mineral surfaces until you know whether the condensation is merely water.

### 5. Prepare for a landscape that defeats ordinary navigation

The plateau is visually deceptive. Glare will flatten depth, distant cliffs will appear near, and nearby stones may appear far away. There are no paths, cairns, walls, or persistent human marks. A marker you place may settle into the visual order of the landscape—or may simply become impossible to distinguish from everything else.

Do not rely on:

- Painted arrows
- Stacked stones
- Footprints
- Flagging tape alone
- A compass without testing it
- The position of the sun
- The passage of time
- A route remembered by visual appearance

Use several navigation systems simultaneously:

- Inertial navigation
- GPS or local equivalent, if the world supports it
- A measured rope or survey line for dangerous traverses
- Reflective or active electronic beacons
- A tethered relay beacon at the landing site
- Detailed range measurements and topographic scans
- A physical return line wherever the terrain becomes confusing

Test whether your markers persist. Place a harmless object, observe it, leave the area, and return by instrument rather than memory. If the object has moved, vanished, or visually “belonged” to the terrain, assume that passive marking is useless.

Every expedition should have a strict turnaround rule. Because the light does not progress toward evening, you cannot use darkness or sunset to force yourself home. Time must be imposed by your equipment and mission plan.

### 6. Carry clocks and recorders of several kinds

This place may not merely have unusual weather; it may lack ordinary temporal change. You need to know whether your body, machinery, food, and information are advancing normally.

Carry:

- Mechanical and electronic clocks
- A sealed chemical timer
- A battery with a measurable discharge curve
- A biological sample or perishable reference item, if permitted
- An independent camera or recorder
- A radiation or environmental sensor
- A device that periodically transmits a timestamp to the outside

Photograph the same ridge, pool, and shadow at regular intervals. Record the position of your team, equipment temperatures, battery levels, and pulse rates. If every external feature remains unchanged but your clock continues, you may be in a place where the environment is temporally fixed while visitors are not.

If your clock stops, do not assume time has stopped. If your clock continues but your food does not spoil, do not assume you are safe. Both could indicate that different things are subject to different temporal rules.

Bring food that remains safe under uncertainty, and avoid relying on fermentation, cooking time, chemical reactions, or medical treatments that require predictable elapsed time.

### 7. Plan for a world with little usable fuel

There are no forests, obvious deadwood, or easy building materials. Fire will be difficult to maintain in the wind and may be dangerous around mineral dust, lichen colonies, or unknown gases.

Bring:

- All cooking fuel
- A wind-shielded stove
- Chemical or electric heat sources
- Spare batteries kept warm and insulated
- A means of melting ice without making a fire
- Emergency body warmers

Do not expect to manufacture useful tools on site. There may be stone and bone, but there is little material for handles, shelter, cordage, or durable containers. Bring every specialized item you need, with redundancy.

Also assume that a shelter, repair, or cache may not remain meaningful. The landscape’s apparent resistance to lasting disturbance suggests that abandoned objects could become lost, buried, visually assimilated, or otherwise unavailable later.

### 8. Treat all fissures and animal colonies as hazardous

The life here is abundant but concentrated in cracks and damp stone. That means the areas most attractive to a tired traveler—sheltered crevices, seeps, mossy ledges, overhangs—are also likely to contain the greatest biological density.

Wear gloves when touching rock. Keep sleeves and trouser cuffs sealed. Inspect clothing frequently for:

- Translucent insects
- Flea-sized jumpers
- Lichen fragments
- Fine mineral growths
- Small burrowing animals

Bring a full medical kit with suction, sterilization, antihistamines, broad-spectrum antibiotics, anti-inflammatory medication, and equipment for treating unknown bites or punctures. Do not crush or ingest any organisms. Do not assume that local insects are harmless simply because they are small.

Avoid disturbing colonies. The plateau appears stable because every part of it has a role, and biological disturbance may not disperse or recover normally. Do not collect specimens without a containment plan.

### 9. Give the larger animals space

The wool-coated herds are probably not aggressive, but their calmness should not be mistaken for tameness. They may have no evolutionary experience with humans, or they may respond to threats in ways unfamiliar to us.

Do not approach, feed, surround, or separate them. Move across their path only if you can remain far downwind or well outside their visual field. Their reflective eyes and long whiskers may indicate unusual sensory adaptations.

The catlike predators are a more serious concern. Their camouflage and stillness make them difficult to detect, and the constant wind may conceal movement. Use:

- Thermal imaging
- Wide-angle visual scanning
- Perimeter alarms around camp
- Elevated observation points
- A buddy system when moving between shelter and water

Do not trust the wind to reveal a predator. The description implies that these animals have adapted to remain motionless in a wind that never changes. A place that appears empty may contain a hunter only a few meters away.

Never sleep without a secured perimeter. Never leave food scraps or blood exposed. The plateau’s scavenger systems are efficient, and the fact that carcasses disappear into cracks does not mean the cracks are safe.

### 10. Do not approach the outer rim casually

The far edge is not merely a cliff. It may mark a boundary between worlds, spaces, or physical rules. Beyond it there is glare rather than a visible lower landscape. The wind pours into blankness, and the sensation is one of suspension.

Treat the rim as an absolute exclusion zone unless your mission specifically concerns it.

- Establish a marked setback line well inland.
- Use harnesses and redundant anchors near any overlook.
- Keep loose equipment secured.
- Do not lean, jump, throw objects, or lower instruments over the edge.
- Do not assume that a thrown object will fall normally.
- Do not approach while fatigued, hypoxic, or disoriented by glare.
- Keep one person watching the team rather than the view.

The psychological effect may be powerful. The absence of a visible bottom or continuation can create vertigo, derealization, or an urge to step closer simply to resolve the scene. Build physical barriers and behavioral rules before anyone sees it.

### 11. Maintain psychological discipline

The most dangerous feature may be the absence of change. A traveler expects weather to shift, shadows to lengthen, thirst to become more urgent, animals to migrate, and the world to provide signs of progress. Here, none of those cues may arrive.

People may lose track of:

- How long they have been walking
- Whether they have already visited a location
- Whether a companion has moved
- Whether a stream or ridge has changed
- Whether they are remembering or observing the present

Use a written schedule, frequent verbal or visual check-ins, and strict rest intervals. Do not allow the team to “wait and see” for an environmental sign that may never occur.

Every traveler should carry a card or display stating:

- Name and origin universe
- Mission objective
- Time of entry
- Planned time of return
- Return location
- Conditions requiring immediate evacuation
- Names of companions
- The rule that unchanged scenery does not mean no time has passed

Do not explore alone. A partner is not only protection from animals and falls; they are a reference point for reality.

### 12. Bring a way to leave that does not depend on the landscape

Your exit should be active, redundant, and scheduled. Do not plan to navigate back by retracing your steps. Do not hide a portal device under a stone or assume you can find it later.

Use:

- A continuously transmitting homing beacon
- A physically tethered return route
- A second exit device or emergency extraction signal
- A local power reserve
- A prearranged external countdown
- Automatic extraction if the signal is lost or the traveler stops moving

Before departure, test whether signals cross the plateau, whether electromagnetic devices function normally, and whether a beacon remains in the same place over time. If the location has unusual temporal behavior, an external countdown may not correspond to the traveler’s experience—but it is still safer than relying on subjective time.

No one should be permitted to remain after the last extraction window merely because the sky still looks like midday.

## A sensible minimum expedition kit

At minimum, an ordinary human should arrive with:

- Supplemental oxygen and altitude medication
- Windproof insulated clothing
- Glacier goggles and hearing protection
- High-traction boots, gaiters, gloves, and face protection
- A low-profile anchored shelter
- Several days of water and food
- Independent purification equipment
- Wind-shielded cooking and heating systems
- Mechanical and electronic clocks
- Inertial and satellite or local navigation
- Active beacons and a physical tether
- Thermal imaging and night-independent observation equipment
- Medical and bite-treatment supplies
- Perimeter alarms
- Redundant communication systems
- Harnesses and anchors
- A written schedule and emergency plan
- A guaranteed extraction method

The central rule is: **do not mistake stability for safety**. This plateau does not seem hostile in the ordinary sense. It supplies water, food, shelter forms, and living creatures. But it supplies them in fixed, exact quantities, and it does not adapt to human needs. It is a world that has already reached equilibrium. A visitor is an intrusion into that equilibrium—and should behave like one: lightly, cautiously, with every need supplied in advance, and with a clear route out before setting foot on the first pale shelf.
