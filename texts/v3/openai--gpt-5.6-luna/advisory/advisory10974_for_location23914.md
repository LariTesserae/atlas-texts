## First decision: do not enter casually

This is not a normal flooded cave, pocket dimension, or underwater ruin. It is a slowly rearranging enclosure with uncertain gravity, unreliable distances, nonfunctional acoustics, and a living environment that seems to notice intruders. An ordinary human should treat it as a hazardous extradimensional ecosystem rather than an exploration site.

If the purpose is merely sightseeing, do not go. Observe the basin remotely, preferably from a separate universe or through a probe. If entry is essential, prepare for a short, controlled incursion and assume that recovery may be difficult.

## Physical preparation

### Use a fully enclosed life-support suit

Do not rely on ordinary scuba equipment. The surrounding medium may look and feel like water in some places, but it can also behave like a dense atmosphere, a suspended liquid, or something between the two.

Bring:

- A sealed pressure suit with independent oxygen supply.
- A full-face mask or helmet; exposed eyes, nose, and mouth are especially vulnerable to the suspended water films.
- Redundant closed-circuit breathing systems.
- Thermal protection. The pocket is persistently cold, and the cold may not behave normally once it enters the suit.
- A pressure gauge, but do not assume it is telling the whole truth.
- Manual controls rather than relying entirely on electronic life-support automation.
- A hard external shell or reinforced fabric over the suit. The black stone shelves are sharp and appear freshly formed.
- Gloves and boots with excellent grip, though do not trust grip alone when gravity is undecided.
- A tether attachment at the waist and another at the chest or shoulders.

An ordinary wetsuit would be inadequate. Even a conventional diving suit might fail because there is no dependable surface, bottom, or pressure gradient.

### Prepare for abnormal buoyancy

You may not be able to swim in the usual sense. Water can hang in layers, collect on vertical walls, or allow objects to sink upward. A fall may take hours.

Use adjustable ballast and buoyancy control, but also carry:

- A compact hand-operated winch.
- A reel of strong, low-stretch line.
- Several soft anchors that can grip around stone shelves or thick plant mats.
- A cutting tool for the tether, in case the environment grows around it.
- A body harness that remains secure if “down” changes.

Do not release anything merely to see where it falls. It may drift upward, remain suspended, or take an extremely long time to arrive. A dropped object may also become a misleading landmark if distances later change.

## Navigation and retreat

### Establish the exit before entering

The basin itself may be difficult to relocate from within. Arrange a physical guide line from a stable external anchor, through the entry point, and back to a clearly marked recovery station.

Do not rely on:

- A compass.
- GPS or equivalent positioning.
- Reflections.
- Sound.
- A straight-line estimate of distance.
- A single beacon.

The pocket changes according to attention. A place that was near may be far away once you look elsewhere. The tether may not remain geometrically faithful, so use several independent markers and record them by touch, texture, and sequence as well as by sight.

Useful markers would include large, brightly colored, nonliving tags attached to the tether at regular intervals. Do not scatter loose markers in the habitat; they may become permanent parts of it or be displaced by the pocket’s spatial behavior.

Have the external team maintain a continuous record of:

- Time of entry.
- Tether tension.
- The position of the basin.
- The apparent width of the entrance.
- Any change in water level or surface behavior.
- The number of people inside.

A traveler inside should not attempt to navigate by memory alone. At every junction, stop, study the route deliberately, and leave only after the whole team has confirmed the next segment.

### Maintain visual and bodily contact

The lack of sound is a major hazard. A shouted warning may never reach anyone. Radios, sonar, and ordinary acoustic beacons may produce nothing useful.

Travel in pairs or groups of three, with:

- Continuous visual contact where possible.
- Hand signals agreed upon in advance.
- A physical line connecting every person.
- Light signals as a backup.
- A simple abort signal that can be transmitted through a tug pattern on the line.

Do not separate to investigate an interesting wall, plant, doorway, or reflection. The distance may change as soon as one person stops attending to the others.

## Lighting and observation

Bring diffuse, low-intensity, redundant lights. Narrow beams may become distorted by the layered water, and strong illumination may alter the behavior of the vegetation or make reflections more confusing.

Avoid reflective equipment. Polished metal, visors, screens, and glossy panels could display a non-current version of the pocket. If reflective surfaces are unavoidable, cover them with matte material during navigation.

Do not use reflections as maps. They may show:

- An earlier state of the pocket.
- A possible future state.
- A complete structure that no longer exists.
- A route that cannot be entered.
- A version of the traveler or expedition that is not presently there.

If a reflection shows a larger, clearer, or more inviting passage, assume it is not available.

Cameras may be useful, but do not trust their live image to represent present geometry. Keep an ordinary written or tactile route log as well. If possible, use multiple cameras at different angles and compare them only after returning.

## Biological precautions

The vegetation is the principal life-form and should be treated as an active organism, even though it is not overtly hostile.

Wear an outer layer that resists:

- Root penetration.
- Adhesion.
- Irritating sap or mineral films.
- Slow constriction by vines.
- Contamination entering suit joints.

Do not cut plants unless they are trapping a person. They appear to share tissues, and damage to one patch may provoke a response elsewhere. Avoid standing in dense growth for longer than necessary. The plants lean toward people with “patient interest”; this may be harmless observation, or it may be the beginning of envelopment.

Do not step into cups, hollows, or glowing green patches. Their water may come from sources that are not physically local.

Any translucent strand should be assumed to be alive until proven otherwise. In particular, the long, eyeless creature may resemble a vine. Do not touch, grasp, or follow anything that moves only when no one is looking at it. Keep two people observing suspicious growth if an inspection is necessary.

Do not collect specimens. A plant, shell, mineral seam, or water sample may continue growing after removal, may contain the pocket’s spatial behavior, or may recognize the traveler as a place to grow around.

On return, quarantine the suit, tools, line, and collected environmental residue. Ideally, collect nothing at all.

## Psychological preparation

The environment will attack orientation and expectation more than the body.

Before entry, practice operating while:

- Unable to tell up from down.
- Unable to hear your companions.
- Unable to judge distance accurately.
- Unable to trust a mirror or camera.
- Watching objects move too slowly to seem alive.
- Experiencing a strong sensation of being observed by plants.
- Seeing structures that appear familiar but cannot be reached.

Establish strict rules in advance:

1. No one follows a light, reflection, creature, or apparent voice without group approval.
2. No one removes their helmet or opens a suit seal.
3. No one touches the central clearing.
4. No one crosses a doorway that leads into impossible or non-enterable space.
5. No one turns away from a team member while that person is changing position.
6. Any unexplained change in the tether is an immediate reason to retreat.

The silence may be disorienting. Since impacts and movement make little sound, you may lose the ordinary sense that your body is moving through space. Use touch, breathing rhythm, and deliberate motion to remain grounded.

## The central clearing

The three-foot patch of dark water at the center should be considered a prohibited zone.

It is not simply a pool. Nothing sinks into it, nothing grows in it, and contact causes it to reflect a starless sky that does not exist locally. This strongly suggests a spatial aperture, an observation surface, or a boundary to another region. There is no evidence that it is safe, reciprocal, or capable of returning anything that enters.

Do not:

- Touch its surface.
- Place a probe into it.
- Shine a beam directly into it.
- Look into it for prolonged periods.
- Lean over it while unrestrained.
- Allow a tether or loose equipment to cross it.

Mark its approximate location from a distance and leave it undisturbed. If it appears in a reflection somewhere else, do not assume that apparent location is the real one.

## Recommended entry procedure

1. Observe the basin for several days or longer from outside.
2. Confirm that its position, size, and ripple behavior remain stable.
3. Send in an inert, tethered probe before sending a person.
4. Test whether the probe can be recovered along the same line.
5. Send one suited traveler only as far as the first stable shelf.
6. Wait and monitor for changes in the entrance, tether, and external basin.
7. Enter with a second traveler only after the first has returned safely.
8. Keep the initial expedition short. Do not attempt to reach the center on the first visit.
9. Maintain a fixed retreat schedule rather than waiting until equipment is low or the environment becomes interesting.
10. Leave immediately if the entrance narrows, the tether slackens unexpectedly, the walls begin creaking more loudly, or vegetation starts crossing the route.

Because the pocket changes slowly, “immediately” may not mean hurrying. It means abandoning investigation and following the retreat protocol without distraction.

## What to bring back

Ideally, bring back only observations.

Do not bring stones, plants, shells, water, or fragments of masonry unless a containment specialist has confirmed that the item is inert outside the pocket. The built traces may not be ruins in the conventional sense; they may be temporary arrangements made by the landscape itself. Removing one could alter the structure or cause it to continue forming around the expedition’s equipment.

The safest expedition would leave no permanent object inside except biodegradable or recoverable route markers, and would recover every piece of gear on departure.

## Final mindset

Enter as though the place is not a cave but a living thought that is slowly becoming smaller.

Move slowly. Keep attention deliberate. Trust tactile lines more than visual distance, present geometry more than reflection, and agreed procedures more than curiosity. Do not fight the pocket’s slowness or attempt to force ordinary physics upon it. The environment is not violently hostile; it is simply under no obligation to preserve human orientation, anatomy, or assumptions.

The most important preparation is the willingness to leave without understanding what you saw.
