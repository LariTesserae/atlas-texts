## Preparation for Visiting the High Plateau

Treat this world as a vacuum-adjacent industrial disaster zone, an active geophysical anomaly, and a wilderness with no reliable rescue infrastructure. Do not approach it as a hiking destination. The landscape may look calm for years at a time, but its dangerous events are abrupt, regional, and governed by rules that may change locally.

Your primary objective is not exploration. It is to establish a stable, reversible presence and return alive.

### 1. Bring a sealed life-support system

The atmosphere is breathable only in the most technical sense: cold, thin, dry, and insufficient. A normal human should not expose their lungs to it.

Bring:

- A fully sealed pressure suit with independent oxygen supply.
- At least three times the expected oxygen reserve.
- Carbon-dioxide scrubbing and humidity recovery.
- Backup breathing tanks physically isolated from the main system.
- A pressure bladder with automatic leak detection.
- A rigid or semi-rigid helmet; do not rely on a flexible face covering.
- Heated underlayers and active thermal regulation.
- A means of cooling yourself after exertion or during solar exposure.
- Anti-static and conductive exterior layers.
- A suit designed to resist abrasion by glass, mineral dust, and metal particles.

The atmosphere will not necessarily feel windy before it becomes dangerous. Large pressure fronts can arrive as silent movements of air. Your suit should therefore monitor pressure, electrical potential, magnetic-field changes, dust concentration, and local gravity continuously.

Do not remove your helmet merely because the sky looks clear. The danger is not always visible, and a harmless-looking basin may have an atmosphere locally altered by an approaching front.

### 2. Protect yourself from the dust storms

The storms are the largest immediate hazard.

They contain fine abrasive particles, powdered glass, charged minerals, metallic vapor, and pieces of the crust. A conventional storm shelter is inadequate unless it is sealed, anchored, electrically grounded, and able to resist high-speed particulate impact.

Carry or establish a shelter with:

- A buried or partially buried profile.
- No exposed windows unless they are sacrificial and heavily shielded.
- Double airlocks.
- Replaceable external filters.
- Faraday shielding.
- Independent power.
- A mechanical anchoring system, not merely magnetic or gravitational attachment.
- Internal pressure control.
- A way to detect whether the shelter’s surroundings have become weightless or unstable.
- At least several days of consumables beyond the planned mission.

If a storm is visible, assume it is already closer than it appears. The colors may be beautiful, but the advancing wall can include electrical discharges, high-velocity dust, magnetic disturbances, and sudden changes in pressure and gravity.

Never shelter under a thin overhang, suspended shelf, or apparently stable cornice. The plateau material may remain in place through anomalous forces and then lose that support without warning. A cliff that has stood for millennia can fold, levitate, or disintegrate during a single field event.

### 3. Use hard navigation, not visual navigation

Distances are deceptive. A feature that appears nearby may be hundreds or thousands of miles away, while a structure that appears small may be too large to circumnavigate in a day. Familiar visual cues—haze, atmospheric perspective, cloud movement, scale—will be unreliable.

Bring:

- Multiple inertial navigation systems.
- Stellar navigation, if the stars remain geometrically stable.
- Short-range radar and lidar.
- Ground-penetrating sensors.
- A continuously updated terrain map.
- Redundant clocks and distance standards.
- Physical route markers that can be retrieved and checked.
- A tether or local beacon system for work near shelters and anomalies.
- A navigation log recording every change in gravity, magnetic field, and surface condition.

Do not trust a single compass, gyroscope, orbital map, or satellite position. The local field may alter instruments as well as the environment. Compare independent systems. If they disagree, stop moving until you understand why.

Mark your route with objects that cannot be mistaken for native formations, but do not assume those markers will remain where you left them. Stones may move, ravines may close, and ground may become transparent or reorganize itself. Record the route in time as well as space.

### 4. Assume gravity can change locally

Your body is adapted to one direction and one approximate strength of gravity. This place may offer neither.

Prepare for:

- Sideways gravity.
- Brief weightlessness.
- Increased local weight.
- Drifting or rotating loose material.
- A change in the direction of “down” across a short distance.
- Gravity gradients near structures, ravines, circular scars, and storm boundaries.

Your suit should have handholds, body restraints, deployable anchors, and a means of orienting yourself without relying on the horizon. Carry a propulsion or winch system for recovering from low-gravity areas, but use it cautiously: a small push may become an irreversible departure if the field changes.

Do not jump across gaps. Do not climb beneath floating slabs. Do not stand near an edge while observing an anomaly. Fasten all equipment to your body or pack. Anything dropped may become weightless, transparent, or impossible to retrieve.

Test every new area with a small instrumented probe before entering it yourself. A weighted test mass, laser rangefinder, and tethered drone are more expendable than a traveler.

### 5. Protect against electrical and magnetic events

Lightning travels along the ground and follows ancient seams. The surface may become an enormous conductor. Metals may vaporize or behave unpredictably. Instruments and implants could be affected.

Use:

- Nonmagnetic tools where possible.
- Shielded electronics.
- Optical and mechanical backups.
- Surge protection.
- External grounding cables for your shelter.
- No exposed conductive loops.
- Insulated boots with replaceable soles.
- A suit with a controlled conductive outer layer rather than random isolation.

If the ground begins to glow along cracks, if dust grains start orbiting, or if your equipment reports rapid field changes, leave exposed terrain and seek a grounded shelter. Do not crouch beside a seam, tower, bridge, or isolated needle. These may be preferential paths for current or field concentration.

Assume that old structures are active unless proven otherwise. Their apparent inactivity is not evidence that they are abandoned.

### 6. Prepare for the surface itself to move

The terrain is ancient, but not stable.

A plain may split. A ravine may be cut by dust. A plateau may flex. A surface may become transparent, exposing a lower landscape. A depression may lose or gain support. The cleanest-looking ground may be the most dangerous, especially if it contains no debris to reveal previous movement.

Maintain a minimum distance from:

- Cliff edges.
- Suspended shelves.
- Circular scars.
- Glowing cracks.
- Black plate fields with red gaps.
- Fields of fine powder.
- Moving stone lines.
- Any surface that produces vibration without an identifiable source.
- Any feature whose geometry changes when you look away and back.

Never walk through a newly formed ravine just because its walls appear solid. Its bottom may be inaccessible, gravitationally abnormal, or occupied by a dust current moving upward.

Do not camp in a depression merely because it offers protection from wind. It may collect charged dust, become a pressure sink, or experience altered gravity.

### 7. Bring food and water; do not depend on the environment

There are no rivers or lakes. Mineral crusts and salt-like deposits are not water sources. The local vegetation is not safe to eat, and even its chemistry may be incompatible with terrestrial biology.

Bring:

- All drinking water.
- A generous reserve for cooling and emergency decontamination.
- All food.
- Closed waste storage.
- Chemical and biological sampling equipment.
- A strict rule against touching exposed faceplates, filters, or food containers with dusty gloves.

Even if the organisms are not aggressive, they may exchange gases, minerals, charge, or information in ways a human immune system cannot handle. The low, mineralized growths and crystalline “forests” should be regarded as hazardous materials until analyzed.

Do not approach the animals merely because they appear passive. Their reflective communication may be a warning, territorial display, or coordination behavior that you cannot perceive. Observe them from a distance through shielded optics. Do not imitate their signals.

### 8. Treat ancient technology as an active system

The remnants are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They may still be performing functions, maintaining fields, storing locations, altering terrain, or interacting with structures that are no longer visible.

Do not:

- Enter a black needle.
- Stand inside a ring of blue lights.
- Cross the bridge during a field disturbance.
- Touch self-repairing walls.
- Interrupt moving stone sequences.
- Remove, pocket, or reposition artifacts.
- Assume an empty room is unoccupied or inactive.
- Use an old mechanism as a power source.
- Rely on a sealed window to protect you from whatever is outside.

Before approaching any construction, establish a remote observation period. Monitor it across changes in light, pressure, magnetic field, and storm activity. Look for repetition, synchronization, responses to motion, and changes in nearby terrain.

Carry no object across an anomaly unless you are prepared for it to age, lose mass, become transparent, or acquire a different physical history. Keep samples in isolated, labeled containers. Store nothing important in a shelter whose internal time or material aging may differ from the outside.

If a chamber contains an ordinary table, lamp, or domestic arrangement, do not assume it is safe because it is familiar. Familiarity may be part of the mechanism.

### 9. Establish strict expedition rules

A competent team should use a buddy system, but not ordinary verbal communication alone. Sound is weak and unreliable. Equip each traveler with:

- Light-based signaling.
- Vibration or haptic communication.
- Short-range encrypted radio.
- A directional laser communication link for clear conditions.
- Automatic distress beacons.
- Vital-sign telemetry.
- A dead-man return signal.

Set hard abort conditions before arrival. For example:

- Any unexplained gravity change.
- Loss of two independent navigation systems.
- A rise in dust charge or particle velocity.
- Visible movement in a cliff or plateau.
- New illumination from a dormant structure.
- Ground transparency.
- Instrument disagreement beyond a fixed threshold.
- A storm front appearing within the defined travel radius.
- Any team member reporting altered time, memory, orientation, or equipment age.

Do not renegotiate these conditions while standing in the anomaly. This environment will reward curiosity with precisely the wrong kind of evidence.

### 10. Plan a return route before landing

You need an extraction point that is not dependent on the local terrain remaining unchanged. Ideally, maintain an orbital or interdimensional retrieval capability with:

- Autonomous return capability.
- Multiple landing or pickup windows.
- Independent reference coordinates.
- A beacon outside the local field anomaly.
- Remote confirmation that the traveler is physically coherent before retrieval.
- A quarantine procedure.

Do not assume the place you arrived is still the same place. The surface may shift, fold, or acquire a different geometry while remaining apparently continuous. Keep a temporal record and a reference sample from your origin environment, though even these may be affected by local physics.

A stranded traveler should not wander toward distant lights. A flare on the horizon may be a storm, a machine, a breach, or an event occurring far below the crust. It may take days to reach and seconds to disappear.

### 11. Prepare psychologically

The most difficult part may be the absence of ordinary scale.

You may see the same cliff for days. You may walk across a plain with no living presence, no weather sound, and no sign that your movement matters. Footsteps may arrive in your bones before your ears. A storm may cross the horizon in absolute silence. The sky will remain clear while the ground beneath you rearranges itself.

This can produce derealization, false confidence, panic, or the conviction that distant objects are moving when they are not. Use scheduled check-ins, instrument review, and deliberate orientation routines. Do not make important decisions solely from perception. Require confirmation from at least two independent sensors and another team member.

Maintain a normal sleep cycle inside a controlled shelter. Avoid prolonged exposure to the horizon and to repetitive terrain. Keep written records. If your sense of time, age, bodily weight, or personal identity begins to feel unstable, return immediately.

### 12. What to wear and carry, in practical terms

A minimum field outfit would include:

- Sealed pressure and thermal suit.
- Hard helmet with filtered optics.
- Electrostatic and radiation monitoring.
- Redundant oxygen and carbon-dioxide systems.
- Abrasion-resistant outer shell.
- Nonmagnetic tools.
- Tether, anchors, winch, and controlled propulsion.
- Ground-pressure and gravity probe.
- Shielded communication and lighting.
- Dust sampling and filtration kit.
- Emergency shelter with independent power.
- At least several days of food and water.
- Mechanical navigation backup.
- Remote drones.
- Retrieval beacon.
- Quarantine containers.
- A physical notebook and non-electronic timepiece.

Your first expedition should be short, local, and mostly robotic. The safest human visit would consist of landing near a preselected stable shelter, deploying sensors, observing one weather cycle from behind protection, and leaving without touching anything.

### Final attitude

Do not expect the plateau to attack you. Expect it to continue doing what it has always done.

It will erode, flex, conduct, drift, expose, bury, and rearrange itself without regard to your presence. The greatest danger is not a creature or a single storm, but the assumption that a familiar-looking landscape obeys familiar rules.

Stay sealed. Stay anchored. Keep independent measurements. Remain near a functioning shelter. Treat every apparent constant as provisional.

And when the plateau looks dead, remember that this is only what it looks like from far away.
