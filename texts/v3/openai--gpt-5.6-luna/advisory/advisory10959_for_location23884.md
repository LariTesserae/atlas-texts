## Preparing to Visit the Stillworks

Treat the Stillworks less as a country and more as an operating industrial organism. You are not visiting a ruined factory. You are entering a manufacturing process whose purpose has been forgotten, but whose procedures continue.

Your main objectives should be:

1. **Remain recognizably human.**
2. **Avoid being incorporated into the works.**
3. **Keep track of where and when you are.**
4. **Do not interfere with slow machinery or animal schedules.**
5. **Leave before the Stillworks assigns you a function.**

Do not assume that a familiar object, map, doorway, puddle, or unit of time will retain its ordinary meaning.

---

## Before Departure

### Bring a guide if at all possible

An ordinary traveler should not enter alone. Hire or accompany someone from an inhabited section of the Stillworks, preferably someone who has crossed the basin recently rather than an official guide who merely knows the old routes.

Ask your guide:

- Which doors are solid **today**?
- Which staircases presently lead downward?
- Where is the nearest reliable habitation?
- Which water is safe to drink?
- Which animals are currently moving between levels?
- Which areas are under active production?
- What is the present condensation schedule?
- Which local names should not be spoken near machinery?
- Where can a person wait without becoming part of the structure?

Do not rely on a single guide’s memory. The country changes by accretion, and a route can remain physically present while no longer leading to the same destination.

### Establish an external return arrangement

Your departure point should record more than the time you leave. It should record:

- Your appearance and clothing.
- The number of people in your party.
- Your intended duration.
- A description of your equipment.
- A physical sample of something you carried in.
- A promise to retrieve you if you do not return by an agreed interval.

The Stillworks may not obey your clocks, so establish several return conditions: “after twelve hours,” “when the third signal arrives,” and “if no message is received after three local dawns,” for example. A fixed date alone is insufficient.

If you can do so, leave a counterpart or observer outside who can compare your returned body and equipment with the original inventory. The Stillworks may return you wearing the same clothes while changing what those clothes are for.

### Prepare for physical cold

Bring clothing for persistent industrial cold, but avoid anything too bulky or loose.

Recommended:

- Insulated underlayers.
- Waterproof outerwear.
- Gloves with separate fingers for handling cables, latches, and tools.
- A close-fitting hood or cap.
- Eye protection.
- A respirator or high-quality particulate mask.
- Waterproof boots with flexible soles and good grip.
- Knee and elbow protection.
- Several dry pairs of socks.
- A way to dry clothing without open flame.

The cold is not necessarily dangerous because of low temperature. It is dangerous because it is constant, damp, and deceptive. You may be chilled for hours without realizing how cold you have become. Condensation can appear on the inside of clothing, under equipment straps, and in sealed containers.

Do not bring fur or loose fabric that can catch on moving machinery, birds, or the growing edges of new construction.

## Equipment

### Carry as little as possible

Anything left behind may become part of the Stillworks. A dropped knife may be found later as a wall bracket. A pack left beside a bed may become a cabinet. A person who abandons equipment should assume it is gone permanently and may be structurally significant.

Use one compact, closely attached pack. Avoid wheeled luggage.

Your essential kit should include:

- A mechanical compass, though it may behave inconsistently.
- Several independent watches or timers.
- A notebook and pencils.
- A non-electronic map board or foldable drafting sheet.
- A whistle or low-tech signaling device.
- A headlamp with spare batteries.
- Chemical light sources.
- Water in sealed, clearly marked containers.
- High-energy food in small portions.
- Basic first-aid supplies.
- Spare gloves and socks.
- Cable ties, cord, and soft straps.
- A small mirror.
- Salt.
- Copper wire.
- A few smooth, warmable stones.
- A simple hand tool, such as pliers or a screwdriver.
- Ear protection, though do not wear it continuously.
- A personal identification card or token that you can keep on your body.

Salt and copper wire are commonly left as offerings at junctions. You should not assume they are magical, but the local inhabitants consider them appropriate acknowledgments of the country’s processes. Warm stones are useful as gifts, markers, or social signals.

Do not bring a large quantity of batteries, magnets, exposed electronics, or complex autonomous devices. The Stillworks may classify them, attract them, or incorporate them into an unknown circuit. Use simple, replaceable equipment whenever possible.

### Use multiple kinds of markers

Ordinary paint marks and reflective tape are unreliable. New construction may cover them, and old surfaces may move relative to them.

Use markers that record your passage in different ways:

- A written entry in your notebook.
- A small amount of chalk or wax.
- A tied cord at eye level.
- A sketch of surrounding pipes and angles.
- A timed sound, if safe.
- A description based on what is visible from a specific position.

Never depend on one marker. If you tie cord to a railing, the railing may later become a moving component or shift to another level. If you mark a wall, make sure you also record what the wall was facing when you marked it.

Do not leave valuable markers behind. Marking something is not the same as abandoning it.

## Rules for Staying Human

### Keep moving, but do not hurry

The Stillworks has a strong preference for slow, purposeful movement. Standing motionless is dangerous; running is also dangerous.

If you remain in one place too long, the country may begin to interpret you as equipment. The change may begin subtly:

- Your outline seems more angular.
- Your joints feel fitted rather than flexible.
- You start thinking in terms of indicators, cycles, and access points.
- You feel an urge to wait for a signal.
- Other people stop looking directly at you.
- You notice that your shadow contains vents or hinges.

This does not mean you should constantly pace. Walk, pause briefly, examine, then continue. If you must rest, change posture regularly and have a companion speak to you. Do not sleep on bare concrete, steps, rails, or beside an unmarked machine.

Sleep only in an inhabited room after asking whether the room is currently used for habitation.

### Never leave anything unattended

Do not put down:

- Your pack.
- Your shoes.
- Your tools.
- Your food.
- A person.
- A sleeping animal.
- A cup of water.
- A map.

If you must set something down, keep one hand or one strap connected to it. If you lose contact with it, do not immediately try to retrieve it. Wait and observe. The object may already have become part of the structure, and pulling it free may damage a load-bearing or conceptual component.

### Do not lean against unfamiliar surfaces

A wall may be a door. A door may be a machine face. A warm pipe may be an animal habitat. A floor that appears solid may be a temporary cover over a shaft.

Before leaning, sitting, or placing weight on anything, tap it lightly and watch for changes. Do not place your hand on moving, warm, wet, or newly manufactured surfaces.

If a surface feels warm despite the surrounding cold, move away. In the Stillworks, warmth often means that something is alive, active, or not yet finished.

### Keep speaking to your companions

Sound behaves strangely, but silence is worse. Travelers should maintain quiet, ordinary conversation. Periodically state:

- Your name.
- Your current location as you understand it.
- The last place you passed.
- The direction you believe you are traveling.
- The number of people in the group.

Do not shout unless necessary. A footstep or spoken phrase may return from a different direction and confuse the party. If you hear your own voice answering before you have finished speaking, stop and wait. Do not follow the reply.

Do not imitate machinery, animal calls, sirens, or the distant impact sounds. The inhabitants may interpret an imitation as a scheduled signal.

## Navigation

### Do not trust distance

A visible tower may take hours to reach, or may remain the same apparent distance indefinitely. A staircase may lead to a higher level after descending. A corridor may open onto the sky without being outdoors.

Navigate by **sequence**, not by geometry.

Record a route as:

> three white tanks, a low pipe crossing, six lamps, a wet square, a red door, a stair with missing fourth step

rather than:

> go east for two kilometers, then turn left.

Use the route’s order of events. When the same feature appears twice, assume it is not repetition until proven otherwise.

### Do not insist on going toward a destination

The safest path may appear unproductive. If a bridge seems to align only after waiting, wait. If a corridor appears to go nowhere, it may be the correct corridor. If a route becomes increasingly direct and convenient, be suspicious; the Stillworks may be drawing you into an active process.

Never force a door. Never climb a barrier simply because you can see your destination beyond it. Never descend because “down” seems safer. Ask a local inhabitant, observe animal traffic, or wait for the structure to reveal its current arrangement.

### Use water carefully

The gutters and channels are not reliable reflections of the world.

Do not drink water from:

- A gutter that reflects a place you have not visited.
- A pool that shows a person standing in it.
- Water flowing uphill.
- Water that remains perfectly still while machinery operates nearby.
- Any reservoir in which you cannot see your own face.

If your reflection is delayed, distorted, or absent, step away immediately. If the water reflects a member of your party who is not physically present, leave the area without discussing what you saw.

Real drinking water should be brought in. Treat all local water as industrially contaminated, biologically uncertain, or spatially significant.

### Respect weather boundaries

Do not cross the edge of a localized rain square, mist bank, frost tier, or upward-falling curtain without a local guide. Such boundaries may not merely separate weather; they may separate operating conditions, levels of reality, or different stages of the same structure.

If rain begins only around you, do not move to escape it in a straight line. Walk back along your previous path. If rain falls upward beneath a bridge, do not stand underneath to watch. If water hangs motionless in the air, do not touch it.

## Animals and Living Systems

Treat the fauna as organized residents, not wildlife.

### Birds

Do not approach nesting birds, especially in conveyor hoods and overhead machinery. Their shadows may make sound, and the sound may attract attention from systems that cannot see you.

If birds suddenly stop moving, stop moving too. This often indicates a distant cycle completion or an approaching structural adjustment.

### Pale mammals

The jointed pale mammals are likely harmless if given room. Do not block service tunnels or crossings. If a group waits at a gate, stand aside. They may be responding to a signal you cannot hear.

Transparent eyelids are not a sign that they are asleep.

### Antlered animals

Never stand between an antlered herd and an inactive gate. When they pass through, they may flatten into a dark plane. Anything occupying the threshold with them may be carried through, compressed, or reassigned.

### Reservoir creatures

Do not approach black reservoirs, even if the water appears calm. If a smooth back with lit windows surfaces, do not signal it, illuminate it, or attempt to count the windows. Leave quietly.

### Insects

Keep switch cabinets closed. Do not brush sparks from beetles onto your clothing or equipment. Check packs and sleeves before entering habitation. The bright beetles may be useful to local residents, but they are not suitable souvenirs.

## Machinery and Construction

Assume every machine is still active, even if its last visible motion occurred years ago.

Maintain a generous distance from:

- Presses.
- Furnaces.
- Conveyor belts.
- Cylindrical vessels.
- Rails.
- Unidentified cables.
- New concrete, tile, metal, or glass.
- Any mechanism that seems to be waiting.

Never climb on a machine because it is not moving. A belt that advances one meter every few hours can still carry you away while you sleep. A piston that descends once every few months can crush a shelter that seemed safe for weeks.

Do not count cycles aloud. Do not attempt to speed them up. Do not repair, oil, unplug, label, or “finish” anything.

If a new structure appears near your route, leave the area. Freshly manufactured surfaces are particularly dangerous. They may be extruding from below, and their edges may continue expanding. Bright metal and clean white tile are warning signs, not signs of safety.

## Local People and Etiquette

The inhabitants may look human, but do not presume shared customs.

Approach slowly and keep your hands visible. Offer a small length of copper wire, salt, or a warm stone rather than food or money. Ask permission before entering a room, crossing a threshold, or touching a map.

Do not ask what the Stillworks used to make. The question is likely meaningless, insulting, or operationally dangerous.

Do not ask inhabitants whether they are “real,” “alive,” or “workers.” Ask instead:

- “What is this place doing now?”
- “Is this room for sleeping?”
- “Which way is stable?”
- “What should not be touched?”
- “How long may I wait here?”
- “What does the next signal mean?”

Do not accept a job, duty, badge, uniform, tool, or assigned room unless you intend to remain. A local’s casual instruction may be a genuine employment offer. Once assigned a function, departure may become extremely difficult.

If someone tells you to stand in a particular place, ask how long. Never agree to “wait until called.”

## Psychological Preparation

The Stillworks will make you doubt basic categories:

- Inside and outside.
- Above and below.
- Old and new.
- Living and mechanical.
- Near and far.
- Weather and architecture.
- Memory and signage.

Before entering, agree with your party on several facts that you will not revise casually:

- Your name.
- The names of the others.
- Your origin universe.
- The purpose of the visit.
- The fact that you are a visitor.
- The fact that you will leave.

Repeat these facts at intervals, but do not let the Stillworks replace all conversation with recitation. You are trying to preserve identity, not become a litany.

Be especially cautious if you begin to experience satisfaction from repetition, waiting, sorting, alignment, or maintenance. These impulses may be natural responses to the environment—or early evidence that you are being assigned a function.

## Signs That You Should Leave Immediately

Withdraw toward habitation or your agreed extraction point if:

- Your shadow points in a direction different from everyone else’s.
- Your watch advances only when nobody is looking.
- A tool fits your hand too perfectly.
- Your clothing develops seams, vents, or access panels.
- A door opens only after you stop wanting to leave.
- You hear a siren that no one else acknowledges.
- Animals begin waiting for you.
- Your footprints become bolt holes, drains, or sockets.
- A local person calls you by a job title.
- You find a room containing an exact copy of your equipment.
- You discover a structure that appears to have been built specifically around your body.
- You have stood still long enough to forget why you stopped.

Do not investigate these signs. Do not take photographs. Do not go back for a companion’s abandoned object. Leave.

If a member of your party begins to acquire mechanical features or speaks in terms of cycles and tolerances, keep them walking and speaking. Do not restrain them against a wall or tie them to a pipe. If they become immobile, seek local help immediately.

## Departure Procedure

Begin preparing to leave before you think you are ready. Do not wait until the journey feels complete. Nothing in the Stillworks is likely to produce a natural stopping point.

Before departure:

1. Return all borrowed tools and objects.
2. Remove every marker you personally placed, if it remains removable.
3. Verify the count and identity of your party.
4. Compare your current clothing and equipment with your original inventory.
5. Check that no one is carrying an item they do not remember acquiring.
6. Avoid newly appearing structures on the return route.
7. Use the same route back only if it is still recognized by a local guide.
8. Cross thresholds together, not one at a time.
9. Do not look back after the final boundary unless instructed.

Once outside, remain under observation. Some changes may not become visible immediately. Watch for:

- A tendency to stand facing one direction.
- Dreams of conveyor systems.
- Missing or extra joints.
- Unexplained indicator lights.
- New knowledge of industrial classifications.
- A need to leave offerings at intersections.
- Inability to tolerate natural soil.
- Discomfort when nothing is moving.

If any of these occur, do not return to the Stillworks for clarification. The country is very good at making a visitor believe that returning would complete a process.

The safest attitude is respectful incompleteness. You are not there to understand the Stillworks, repair it, map it, or discover its original purpose. Walk slowly. Keep hold of what is yours. Remain in motion. Let the machines continue their work without your assistance.
