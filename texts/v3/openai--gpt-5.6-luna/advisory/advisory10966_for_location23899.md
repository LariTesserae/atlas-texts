## Prepare as if visiting a flooded, active industrial facility—not a wilderness

This basin is survivable for an ordinary human, but only with discipline. Its greatest dangers are cumulative: contaminated water, unstable ground, heat and humidity, poor visibility, industrial machinery, tidal flooding, and animals concentrated around food and warmth. Nothing needs to be supernatural to kill you here.

### Before departure

**Arrange legal access and a local escort.**  
The district is still maintained, even if much of it looks abandoned. Fences, gates, pump stations, restricted yards, and apparently empty buildings may all be active. A local guide should know:

- Which roads and walkways are load-bearing
- Which channels carry hot or chemically contaminated discharge
- The pump and sluice schedules
- Tide tables and storm warnings
- Emergency shelters and radio channels
- Which structures are vacant and which only appear vacant
- Where workers are permitted to walk

Do not rely on visual inspection. A dry road may conceal a collapsed culvert; a silent building may contain pressurized equipment.

**Confirm atmospheric compatibility.**  
The air is probably breathable for a human, but industrial haze, biological aerosols, vapor from treatment ponds, and accidental releases are real possibilities. Bring a portable analyzer or at minimum a reliable warning monitor for:

- Oxygen deficiency
- Hydrogen sulfide and other sewer gases
- Chlorine or ammonia
- Volatile hydrocarbons
- Carbon monoxide
- Fine particulates
- Unusual heat or radiation, if your destination’s industry is unknown

If your multiverse technology permits it, take a short atmospheric sample before entering. Do not assume that a familiar smell means a familiar concentration. Your sense of smell is not a safety instrument.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing for persistent warm rain, mud, abrasion, and chemical splash.

**Recommended clothing:**

- Lightweight, breathable base layers that dry quickly
- A hooded waterproof outer shell
- Chemical-resistant waterproof over-trousers
- Tall impermeable boots with aggressive soles
- Chemical-resistant gloves worn over thinner work gloves
- Eye protection with a seal or close-fitting side shields
- A hard hat
- Hearing protection, especially near pumps and machinery
- A properly fitted respirator compatible with the contaminants identified by your monitor

Ordinary sneakers, sandals, denim, and porous hiking clothing are poor choices. Water will enter from below, rain will enter from above, and mud will work its way into every seam.

Carry spare socks, gloves, and a complete dry layer in sealed bags. Keep your phone, map, medical supplies, and identity documents in waterproof cases. Use equipment with lanyards; dropped objects may vanish into black water, drains, or mud.

A headlamp is essential even during the day. Bring two, with spare batteries, and preferably a low-glare red or amber mode. The district is full of weak lamps and reflective water; a powerful light can destroy your night vision and attract insects.

### Water, food, and contamination control

Do not drink from any open water, drainage channel, condensation line, or apparently clean tap unless it has been explicitly certified. The water may contain industrial chemicals, salt, sewage, heavy metals, pathogens, parasites, and concentrated biological growth. Even washing your hands in it may be unsafe.

Bring:

- More drinking water than you expect to need
- Sealed, high-energy food
- Oral rehydration salts
- Hand sanitizer and disposable wipes
- A way to rinse boots and equipment
- Separate bags for contaminated clothing
- A simple field shower or clean-water wash kit

Keep food sealed at all times. The district is crowded with animals accustomed to finding nourishment around human infrastructure. Open food will attract insects, birds, crabs, rodents if present, and larger scavengers. Do not feed anything.

Assume all mud and standing water are contaminated. Avoid touching your face. Do not eat, smoke, or adjust contact lenses while wearing field gloves. At the end of the visit, remove outer garments in a controlled area, clean your boots and tools, and wash thoroughly with clean water. Do not bring wet gear directly into your sleeping or transport compartment.

### Heat, rain, and physical strain

The climate is not cold, but it is dangerous in a slower way. Humidity prevents sweat from cooling you, while concrete, black water, tanks, and machinery radiate heat. Rain may make you feel cooler without meaningfully reducing your core temperature.

Plan short walking intervals with scheduled rests. Drink before you feel thirsty. Watch for:

- Headache
- Nausea
- Confusion
- Unusual fatigue
- Muscle cramps
- Loss of coordination
- Chills despite the heat

These may indicate heat illness or chemical exposure. Do not try to “walk it off.”

Bring a waterproof shelter or identify one in advance. Rain can rapidly fill low spaces and turn ordinary paths into channels. Never shelter beneath discharge pipes, under corroded gantries, beside tanks, or in low culverts.

### Movement through the basin

Stay on elevated walkways and designated embankment roads whenever possible. The ground is deceptive. Water may hide:

- Open drains
- Broken pavement
- Deep wheel ruts
- Uncovered sumps
- Collapsed service tunnels
- Soft silt
- Submerged rail cuts
- Mud that will hold a boot and pull you down

Use a walking staff or probing pole, but do not use it as permission to enter the water. A pole may find the bottom while missing a hidden current, chemical layer, animal, or sharp metal obstruction.

Never walk alone. Maintain visual or radio contact with your group. Mark your route, but do not rely solely on painted arrows or handheld navigation; metal structures, tanks, and weather may interfere with signals. Carry a paper map showing elevations, drains, culverts, service roads, flood gates, and safe high ground.

Cross bridges and catwalks one person at a time if they are visibly corroded. Avoid leaning against railings. Do not climb machinery, tanks, cranes, pipework, or warehouse roofs for a better view. A surface may support birds but not a human.

### Tides, pumps, and water movement

Learn the tide and pump schedule before entering. These are not background details; they determine whether a route exists.

A channel that is shallow at one hour may become a fast-moving, chest-deep flow later. A sluice gate may open automatically. A pump starting nearby can create suction, turbulence, or sudden changes in water level. Stay well away from:

- Intake screens
- Outfall pipes
- Sluice gates
- Pump sumps
- Culvert mouths
- Narrow channels during rising tide
- Edges undercut by flowing water

Never wade after dark, regardless of how shallow the water appears. Do not stand beneath an outlet or walk close to the lip of a settling pond. Heat, pressure, chemical concentration, and depth can all vary invisibly.

If you fall in, do not immediately attempt to climb back onto the nearest corroded structure. Move away from active machinery and current if possible, call for help, and use the group’s recovery plan. A fall into contaminated water should be treated as a medical incident even if you feel fine afterward.

### Industrial hazards

The old infrastructure is still doing work, though slowly and intermittently. Treat every pipe, cable, tank, valve, and electrical enclosure as active unless a qualified local worker has isolated it.

Be alert for:

- Unmarked hot surfaces
- Steam or chemical discharge
- Pressurized lines
- Automatic machinery
- Energized cables
- Arc flash around substations
- Falling rust, concrete, or bird-nesting material
- Sudden generator or pump startup
- Slippery oil films
- Confined spaces with unbreathable air

Do not enter tanks, tunnels, pipe interiors, basements, drains, or sealed sheds. These are classic confined-space hazards: the air may be oxygen-poor or toxic, and a rescuer without equipment may become a second casualty.

Keep clear of overhead loads and cranes even when they appear abandoned. Wind can move suspended or partially detached material. Also avoid standing under bird-heavy roofs and gantries; accumulated droppings can be slippery, corrosive, infectious, and unexpectedly heavy when wet.

### Animals

The animals are abundant but not necessarily aggressive. Their danger comes from density, disease, surprise, and the fact that you will be entering feeding and nesting areas.

**Water animals:**  
Do not put hands into channels, drains, warm outflows, or gaps beneath pallets. Eels, catfish, crabs, and other animals may bite or clamp onto skin, and some may carry unfamiliar parasites or toxins. Wear gloves, but do not treat gloves as armor.

**Insects:**  
Use repellent on exposed skin and wear a head net in areas of heavy swarms. Keep cuffs, collars, and gloves closed. Mosquitoes and flies may be disease vectors even if the local animals seem healthy. Avoid bright lights near insect clouds when possible.

**Birds:**  
Do not approach nests, eggs, injured birds, or dense roosts. A mass takeoff can cause falls, collisions, or panic on narrow walkways. Droppings can contaminate eyes, mouth, clothing, and open wounds. Keep goggles on around roosting structures.

**Frogs, snails, and microbial films:**  
Do not handle them casually. Their harmless appearance says little about parasites or local biochemistry. Do not touch dark films, slime, sediment, or floating scum.

The deeper ponds may contain animals larger than the visible fish and crabs. Do not assume that a surface disturbance is merely a fish strike.

### Health preparation

Before departure, obtain vaccinations and prophylaxis appropriate to the biological hazards your medical team can identify. Bring:

- Waterproof first-aid supplies
- Antiseptic and wound dressings
- Treatment for burns and chemical splashes
- Antihistamines
- Insect-bite treatment
- Fever and pain medication
- Rehydration salts
- Any personal medication in redundant sealed containers
- An emergency exposure card explaining what contaminants may be involved

Cover every cut before entering. If contaminated water enters a wound, rinse immediately with clean water and seek medical evaluation. Flush exposed eyes for a prolonged period using clean sterile fluid. Do not neutralize chemicals with improvised substances.

Afterward, monitor for fever, rash, vomiting, respiratory irritation, unusual fatigue, diarrhea, jaundice, or wounds that swell or discolor. Some exposures will not announce themselves immediately.

### Best time and route

Go during daylight, in stable weather, at a time when the tide and pump schedule leave generous margins. Avoid storms, heavy rain, fog, and periods of extreme heat. The dim conditions can make daytime resemble evening; carry reliable timekeeping rather than judging by the light.

A sensible first visit would be:

1. Begin at a staffed gate or maintenance station.
2. Take an elevated route along the basin perimeter.
3. Visit one controlled industrial sector with a guide.
4. Observe the ponds and tidal flats from a secured platform.
5. Return before dusk, well before the tide or pump cycle changes.
6. Decontaminate at the exit.

Do not make the first expedition a crossing of the entire basin. Distances will look shorter than they are, and the empty roads encourage overconfidence.

### Psychological preparation

The place will not feel like a normal city or a normal wetland. It is quiet, but not peaceful. It is inhabited, but not socially occupied. Long stretches may contain no people while being full of machinery, birds, insects, and unseen movement. The light will flatten distance, and reflections in black water may make routes and edges difficult to read.

Expect your attention to be strained by:

- Constant low machinery noise
- Sudden animal eruptions
- The absence of vegetation
- Repetitive concrete and pipework
- Weak, uneven lighting
- The sense that water is present beneath almost everything

Do not let the emptiness make you careless. Here, “nothing is happening” often means a pump is about to start, the tide is turning, or an animal mass is moving out of sight.

### Essential rule set

If you remember only a few rules, remember these:

- Never enter without permission and a local guide.
- Never trust a dry-looking surface.
- Never drink or casually touch the water.
- Never wade after dark.
- Never enter a confined space.
- Never stand beneath pipes, outlets, cranes, or bird-heavy structures.
- Keep food sealed.
- Stay on elevated routes.
- Watch the tide and pump schedule.
- Treat every wound or water exposure seriously.
- Leave before fatigue, darkness, or weather turns a manageable hazard into a rescue operation.

With sealed clothing, clean water, atmospheric monitoring, route discipline, and respect for the machinery, an ordinary traveler could experience the basin safely. The correct attitude is neither panic nor adventure-tourist curiosity. Move through it like a careful maintenance worker: assume the landscape is active, assume the water is hazardous, and assume that every apparently abandoned structure has a reason you have not yet discovered.
