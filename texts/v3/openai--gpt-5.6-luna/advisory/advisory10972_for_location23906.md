Treat this as an extreme-altitude expedition to an unstable mineral environment, not as a scenic mountain stop. The notch is small, but it offers almost none of the ordinary protections that make a mountain habitable: no vegetation, no fuel, no dependable running water, no soft ground, and very little room to retreat from weather.

## Before departure

### Confirm that your body can tolerate the world

Do not assume that “mountain air” means merely cold, thin air. Establish:

- atmospheric composition and pressure;
- oxygen percentage and whether the pressure is low enough to cause rapid hypoxia;
- gravity;
- ultraviolet and other radiation levels;
- whether the air contains mineral aerosols, corrosive vapors, or spores;
- whether the world’s moisture freezes at familiar temperatures;
- whether the clouds below are ordinary clouds or something chemically or electrically hazardous.

Bring an independent atmosphere monitor. A human may become confused, euphoric, clumsy, or unconscious from hypoxia before recognizing the danger. Carry oxygen or a pressure-capable breathing system even if initial readings appear tolerable. A small emergency hyperbaric or pressure shelter would be worthwhile if the destination is accessible enough to justify it.

If your multiverse travel method can fail or be disrupted by altitude, pressure, electromagnetic activity, or local geology, establish the return point below the notch rather than inside it. The floor is constantly shifting, and the open entrance is exposed to wind. Do not make the only way home depend on a device resting on a mineral shelf that may crack or slide.

### Plan for a short visit

The location does not support casual habitation. Prepare for hours to a few days, not indefinite residence, unless you bring a complete life-support station.

Bring:

- sealed drinking water and high-calorie food;
- a way to melt or purify collected water, though collected water should be considered contaminated until analyzed;
- a pressure-rated, insulated tent or rigid shelter;
- noncombustion heat, preferably a chemical, electric, or closed-loop system;
- spare batteries kept inside insulation;
- redundant communication and navigation equipment;
- a tether system and climbing rope;
- a helmet, eye protection, gloves, and a full-face mask;
- an emergency extraction plan.

There is no usable firewood and probably no practical fire. A flame would be unreliable in thin air and dangerous inside the old shelter. Smoke and heat may also alter the local condensation cycle or damage the mineral formations.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for continuous exposure, not for the brief intervals when sun warms a dark rock.

Use a layered system with:

- a moisture-wicking inner layer;
- thick insulating layers;
- a windproof outer shell;
- insulated, waterproof boots with rigid soles;
- overmitts or gloves that allow careful handling of rope and tools;
- a balaclava or face covering;
- sealed goggles or mountaineering glasses with strong UV protection;
- a hood and neck protection.

The sunlight will be unusually intense because of altitude, reflected glare, and the pale mineral surfaces. Snow blindness is possible even though the ground is not snow. Use eye protection from the moment you arrive. Apply high-grade skin protection to any exposed skin; the cold will mask the sensation of burning.

Avoid bare contact with the formations. They may be ice-like, glass-like, chemically active, or merely extremely sharp. Wear cut-resistant gloves and do not brace yourself against a blade or frost cluster unless you have tested its strength. Some formations grow, collapse, and release delayed heat or light; their behavior is not familiar enough to trust.

Carry a small personal shelter or emergency bivouac even if you expect to use the old stone structure. The existing shelter is a historical artifact, not a safe base. Its rawhide ties may be brittle, its ice wedges may shift, and its roof could fail under a minor vibration or thermal change.

## Approach and arrival

Use a route with a secure anchor system. The entrance is open to an immense drop and exposed to wind passing over the notch. Wind may be relatively calm inside while still capable of throwing loose fragments across the opening. Keep everyone clipped to a rope or fixed line near the entrance and on the higher shelves.

Arrive during the driest, most stable part of the local cycle if possible. The formations thicken during damp periods, and condensation may frost exposed equipment almost immediately. Pressure changes associated with clouds below may trigger new water pulses, so weather beneath the mountain matters as much as weather at the notch itself.

On arrival:

1. Stop and assess the air, wind, and ground.
2. Establish a marked safe zone near the most stable wall.
3. Anchor equipment above the floor rather than leaving it in channels or basins.
4. Identify at least two retreat routes.
5. Photograph or map the terrain before walking through it.
6. Keep a continuous line of communication with someone outside.

Do not rush toward the old shelter, the central basin, or the higher ladder. The apparently obvious paths are likely the ones most affected by repeated human use and mineral growth.

## Moving through the notch

The ground is a collection of shelves, shallow basins, ridges, seams, and hard translucent crusts. Ordinary hiking boots will not be enough. Use mountaineering footwear, traction devices, and a short safety tether. Test every foothold before committing weight.

Particular care is needed around:

- black seams, which may be channels or cracks;
- smooth pale crusts, which may be thin covers over water;
- ledges with visible condensation;
- frost blades that appear load-bearing but are hollow or brittle;
- the central basin;
- the base of cliff walls, where fallen formations may accumulate.

Walk slowly and keep three points of contact whenever possible. Turn sideways through crowded passages rather than forcing a pack between the formations. Keep packs narrow and protect straps from snagging on mineral blades.

Wear a helmet at all times. The place is subject to slow movement punctuated by sudden fracture. A ridge that has shifted for centuries may fail during your visit. The deep vibrations are not necessarily earthquakes, but they are sufficient warning that the shelves are under stress.

Do not stand beneath a dense formation during warming or condensation. Even if the blades do not melt, they may shed pieces, slide, or be incorporated into a pressure release. Treat every unfamiliar growth as a fragile overhead hazard.

## Water and food

Bring enough water that you never need to drink from the notch.

The local water may have passed through mineral structures, absorbed salts, or carried particles from the cliff. The condensation bowls and rock-cut basins are evidence of collection, not evidence of safety. If you must sample it, use sealed containers and test it for:

- dissolved metals;
- salts;
- acidity or alkalinity;
- unknown suspended particles;
- biological contamination, if biology is possible there;
- effects on your own physiology.

Do not drink from the central basin merely because its film looks clean. The water may be a chemical phase or a concentrated mineral solution. Do not put your tongue on the formations or collect melting frost with bare hands.

Eat before becoming cold or exhausted. In thin air, people often mistake hypoxia and exposure for ordinary fatigue. Carry food that can be eaten without cooking and keep it inside your clothing so it does not freeze solid.

## Camp and shelter

Place camp on a broad, dry, visibly stable shelf away from:

- active condensation grooves;
- the mouths of cracks;
- the central basin;
- the base of overhanging frost;
- the entrance wind;
- any area where deep vibrations are strongest.

The old shelter may be used as a windbreak or observation point, but do not sleep inside it without inspecting the roof, walls, floor, and anchors. Do not remove its tools, cloth strips, bowls, rods, or stone plates. They may be culturally important, and some may be part of a functioning measuring or water-collection system.

Keep all equipment insulated and off the floor. Condensation will frost anything exposed, and sunlight may wet equipment only to have the water drawn into a seam or freeze again moments later. Protect cameras, batteries, medical supplies, and dimensional instruments in sealed cases with desiccant and thermal insulation.

Use low-intensity lighting. The delayed blue gleams from the formations may be harmless, but bright lamps, heat sources, or electrical equipment could provoke an unknown response. If you need illumination, use shielded lights directed at the ground rather than flooding the walls.

## Sampling and scientific caution

Although nothing here hunts, feeds, or reproduces in a familiar way, the notch behaves like an active system. Its formations grow, fracture, migrate, and reabsorb material. That is enough reason to treat it as an ecology, even if it is purely mineral.

Do not:

- break formations without permission or a scientific necessity;
- divert channels;
- warm a wall to accelerate condensation;
- pour water onto the floor;
- place metal objects in the central basin;
- hammer shelves to test their strength;
- remove large samples;
- touch formations with bare skin.

If sampling is authorized, take only tiny pieces from already-fallen material. Record their exact location, temperature, light exposure, moisture conditions, and time since arrival. Seal samples in separate containers. Some of the delayed gleaming may indicate stored energy, internal stress, or a chemical reaction rather than simple optical behavior.

Avoid using metal tools near the central basin until its response is understood. The branching water patterns may be a harmless surface phenomenon, but they may also be a sensing, signaling, or pressure-release process. Watch from a distance first. Do not interrupt it.

## Health and emergency precautions

Monitor every traveler for:

- headache, confusion, poor coordination, or unusual calmness;
- numbness or loss of dexterity;
- rapid breathing or chest discomfort;
- frostbite;
- eye pain or visual halos;
- dizziness near the edge or after looking into the glare;
- coughing from mineral dust;
- skin irritation after contact with water or formations.

Use a buddy system. A hypoxic or cold-impaired person may insist they are fine while becoming incapable of returning. Check one another’s gloves, faces, and speech regularly.

Set hard turnaround rules based on time, oxygen reserve, weather, and battery life. Do not stay to observe a rare water pulse, a beautiful evening glow, or a vibration that “may become interesting.” The notch changes on geological timescales, but the human body fails on ordinary ones.

If a crack opens, a shelf shifts, or a deep vibration intensifies, move toward the established retreat route without stopping to collect equipment. Leave caches only if they are expendable. Never shelter in a narrow basin during an unknown ground movement.

## Respect for the place

The strongest preparation is psychological. This is not an empty location merely because it has no animals or visible people. It is occupied by weather, pressure, mineral growth, and a long record of human use. It may have been a workplace, shrine, laboratory, observatory, or seasonal refuge. The bowls, ladder, frame, tools, cloth, channels, and shelter all imply a culture that understood the notch better than an outsider will.

Enter quietly and alter as little as possible. Do not leave food, plastic, biological material, waste, or warm objects behind. Do not fill footprints, polish surfaces, or “improve” the paths. Mark your route with removable, noninvasive indicators rather than paint, stakes, or drilled anchors.

The ideal visit would be brief, tethered, well-documented, and almost invisible afterward. You should leave with your water still mostly full, your samples mostly absent, your shelter untouched, and a clear memory that the notch is not a place to conquer. It is a small, severe machine made of mountain, moisture, light, and time.
