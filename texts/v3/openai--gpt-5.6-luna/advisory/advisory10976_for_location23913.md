## First principle: do not arrive as a diver

An ordinary human should not attempt to visit this place with normal scuba equipment, a wetsuit, or a simple breathing mask. The location is not a submerged ruin that can be explored from a nearby shore. It is an entire drowned continental landscape, with potentially enormous depth, no usable landmarks, no reliable surface light, and long stretches of cold water above and below.

Prepare as though you are visiting an undersea archaeological site the size of a country, in a world where rescue is unavailable.

Your minimum viable expedition is a **self-contained deep-water habitat or pressure-rated exploration suit**, supported by a return-capable transport craft or a stable arrival station.

---

# 1. Establish exactly where and when you are arriving

Before crossing into the location, determine:

- The depth of your arrival point
- The water pressure at that depth
- Whether the water is fresh, marine, chemically unusual, or stratified
- Whether the sea has a true surface above you
- The temperature profile from arrival depth to the ruins
- The strength and direction of currents
- Whether the water contains dissolved gases or suspended particulates dangerous to breathe or corrosive to equipment
- Whether your arrival will displace water violently or produce a pressure wave

The landscape sounds geologically stable, but “stable” here means stable over centuries. It does not mean safe at human scale. A portal or vehicle appearing inside a drowned hall could collapse a ceiling, release silt, or trap the expedition in a sealed chamber.

If possible, arrive in open water above an unobstructed stretch of former road or plain, not directly inside a structure. If the destination coordinate is uncertain, choose a location with:

- A broad, level floor
- Few overhanging slabs
- No visible root curtains
- No obvious channels or gates
- Sufficient clearance for a vehicle to retreat in every direction

You should also leave a permanent, mechanically independent return beacon at the arrival point. Do not rely on radio navigation, satellite positioning, visible markers, or a single powered device. The water will absorb and scatter light, distort sound, and interfere with any ordinary sense of distance.

---

# 2. Use a pressure vessel, not ordinary diving equipment

The best option is a **rigid atmospheric diving suit** or a small submersible with a one-atmosphere cabin. This allows you to work at depth without exposing your body to the ambient pressure.

A flexible diving suit can work only if you already know the depth and have professional mixed-gas support. For an ordinary traveler, it introduces too many failure modes:

- Nitrogen narcosis
- Oxygen toxicity
- Carbon dioxide buildup
- Decompression sickness
- Gas supply exhaustion
- Difficulty controlling buoyancy among vegetation and ruins
- Inability to perform a rapid but safe ascent
- Crushing or suit failure in unexpectedly deep water

Your primary vessel should have:

- A rigid pressure hull
- Redundant oxygen supplies
- Carbon dioxide scrubbers
- Independent electrical systems
- Mechanical emergency controls
- External cameras and sonar
- A manipulator arm
- A tether or reel
- A locator beacon
- An emergency ascent or retreat system
- Enough power for several times the planned mission duration

Do not plan around the assumption that “the ruins are not very deep.” The description gives a horizontal scale, not a trustworthy vertical scale. A low country may still lie under kilometers of water.

If you must leave the submersible, use a rigid suit with its own emergency reserve. Carry a second breathing and power system inside or attached to the suit. A single leak, snag, or failed seal should not become an immediate death sentence.

---

# 3. Prepare for cold that is constant rather than dramatic

The cold here is not merely uncomfortable. It is persistent and penetrating. The water, stone, vegetation, and old structures all appear to have reached the same deep temperature. There is little surface warmth, no sunlight, and no warm rock to retreat to.

Use:

- Active thermal regulation if available
- An electrically heated underlayer
- Multiple insulated glove layers
- Heated visor and suit seals
- Backup batteries kept warm inside the pressure system
- Strict limits on excursion time

Cold impairs judgment, hand coordination, and the ability to solve simple equipment problems. It also makes small leaks much more dangerous. A suit that is technically survivable for an hour in temperate water may become disabling here.

Do not enter a long flooded hall or vegetation-covered district merely because your instruments say the temperature is survivable. You need enough reserve to return while cold, tired, and possibly partially blinded by silt.

---

# 4. Bring your own light, and assume light will not behave normally

Do not expect to see a distant surface, the shape of a city, or even the full height of a nearby structure. The water is described as clear only in a degraded, old-glass sense. Suspended material, vegetation, silt, and darkness will reduce visibility rapidly.

Carry several independent lighting systems:

- Wide floodlights for orientation
- Narrow-beam lights for inscriptions, joints, and openings
- Red or infrared illumination for instrument use, if your cameras support it
- Chemical or passive emergency markers
- At least one light source requiring no external power

Use low-intensity lighting whenever practical. Strong beams will reveal only a bright cone and make the surrounding darkness seem deeper. They may also cause you to mistake a nearby wall or plant canopy for a distant landscape.

Place physical or acoustic markers along every route. Ideally use markers that:

- Do not float away
- Do not snag vegetation
- Can be detected by sonar
- Are visible in total darkness
- Do not permanently damage the ruins

A laser line may help in clear water but should not be your only guide. A line, reel, or fiber-optic tether is much more trustworthy. The streets and causeways form grids, but the grid will not necessarily help you. Many routes lead into silt, root masses, blocked channels, or flooded chambers.

---

# 5. Navigate by instrument and sound, not by sight

The landscape has no horizon and no obvious vertical hierarchy. You may not be able to tell whether you are:

- Looking across a plaza
- Looking down a road
- Looking into a shallow basin
- Facing the wall of a flooded building
- Drifting beneath a ceiling of vegetation
- Traveling parallel to a causeway

Bring:

- Multibeam sonar
- Short-range imaging sonar
- Inertial navigation
- Depth and pressure sensors
- A magnetic compass, though its reliability should be tested
- An odometer or dead-reckoning system
- A tether reel
- Acoustic transponders
- A physical map board or local offline display

Do not trust ordinary sonar interpretation immediately. The drowned construction contains slabs, cavities, flooded halls, channels, terraces, and overgrown surfaces. A sonar return may be a wall, a root curtain, a suspended sheet of leaves, or the underside of a collapsed structure.

Sound behaves strangely in the described environment. Long galleries may return weak tones, channels may amplify minor machinery, and distances may be difficult to judge because echoes come from several surfaces. Keep transmissions short and coded. Continuous active sonar may overwhelm your ability to interpret the environment, while passive listening is likely to be more useful than usual.

Before entering an unfamiliar district, stop and listen. The absence of animal life means that an unexpected sound is more likely to be:

- Your own vehicle
- A current through a structure
- A shifting gate
- A collapsing slab
- A pressure event
- A mechanical fault
- A sound carried from a much greater distance than it seems

---

# 6. Treat the vegetation as a structural hazard, not as harmless scenery

The plants do not attack, flee, or display animal-like behavior. That does not make them safe.

Their growth is dense, ancient, and integrated with the ruins. It may:

- Hide holes, stairwells, drainage shafts, and sudden drops
- Wrap around propellers, thrusters, or articulated limbs
- Block channels and create pressure differences
- Conceal unstable masonry
- Release clouds of silt when disturbed
- Obscure your tether
- Seal a passage that appears open
- Make a flat road into a soft, snagging mat

Never use a propeller-driven vehicle at high power through dense vegetation. Prefer shrouded thrusters, vectored water jets, or a slow crawler with protected systems. Carry cutting tools, but use them sparingly. The plants are the main living inheritance of the location, and cutting them can destabilize the sediment or destroy evidence.

Do not grasp vegetation to steady yourself. It may be rooted in a hollow or attached to a loose slab. Do not enter beneath a hanging canopy until sonar has shown that the ceiling is clear. Pale root curtains may conceal a drop into a chamber or a narrow passage that can trap a suit.

The vegetation also has biological risks. Since there are no animals, pollinators, grazers, or scavengers, the plants may possess unfamiliar chemical defenses or accumulate compounds in their tissues. Do not touch bare skin to them. Do not taste, ingest, or bring wet plant samples into the cabin without containment and sterilization.

---

# 7. Assume the masonry is stronger than it looks—and less stable than it appears

The construction was made for permanence, but the place has been altered by water, roots, silt, and immense time. A wall that looks solid may be a thin surviving skin over a void. A road may be the crown of a buried avenue. A broad terrace may be a roof that has held itself together only because it is evenly loaded.

Maintain generous clearance from:

- Overhanging slabs
- Root-filled cracks
- Suspended ceilings
- Blocked gates
- Deeply silted basins
- Long unsupported walls
- Narrow stone bridges
- Areas where water is moving through masonry

Do not hammer on structures, drill into them, anchor a vessel to them, or use thrusters to clear vegetation. The landscape is quiet enough that a small vibration may travel a long way through stone and water. A distant failure could send silt through multiple districts, cut visibility to zero, or redirect an old channel.

If excavation or sampling is necessary, first create a complete digital and photographic record. The site is not just a collection of ruins; its importance lies in the continuity of the built terrain. Removing one gate, slab, root mass, or inscription may damage the only remaining evidence of how districts connected.

---

# 8. Prepare for total biological silence

The absence of animals will affect your judgment more than you expect. There will be no fish scattering from your lights, no insects, no distant calls, no movement except water and plants. You may begin to imagine intention in every shift of leaves or every returning echo.

Use a strict crew protocol:

- Maintain verbal contact at scheduled intervals
- Announce every change in direction
- Confirm instrument readings aloud
- Use visible or acoustic partner markers
- Never allow a member to explore alone
- Check that apparent motion is not merely current-driven vegetation
- Take regular psychological breaks inside the vessel

The silence may also make your own noises feel enormous. Breathing, pump operation, suit joints, and sonar pings can become the dominant events in the environment. This can produce tunnel vision and a false sense that the landscape is responding to you.

It is not necessary to interpret the place as haunted or sentient. But you should respect the psychological effect of entering a dead civilization where nothing is watching, nothing is fleeing, and nothing is waiting in the ordinary biological sense. The world’s stillness is likely to be more disorienting than its darkness.

---

# 9. Bring all food, water, medicine, and waste capacity

There is no indication that anything here is safe to drink or eat. Even if the water is chemically clean, it is cold, ancient, and in contact with decaying plant matter, mineral surfaces, silt, and unknown biological compounds.

Carry:

- All drinking water
- All food
- Electrolytes
- Anti-nausea medication
- Cold-injury treatment
- Eye and respiratory protection
- Broad-spectrum emergency medical supplies
- Sample containers with airtight secondary containment
- Waste storage

Do not assume the lack of animals means the water is sterile. Plant-derived microorganisms, dormant spores, and chemical residues may still exist. Keep the exploration vessel’s internal atmosphere isolated from the exterior water. Decontaminate suits and equipment before bringing them into a habitat.

If returning to your home universe is possible, quarantine all samples. The plants have had vast time to evolve in isolation, and their reproduction is explicitly slow and non-animal-dependent. A fragment, runner, spore, or seed introduced elsewhere could behave in ways you cannot predict.

---

# 10. Plan every excursion around retreat, not discovery

The principal danger is not getting lost in a spectacular abyss. It is becoming gradually committed to a route that is difficult to reverse.

For each excursion, establish:

- A maximum duration
- A maximum distance from the vessel
- A maximum depth change
- A power reserve for return
- A point beyond which you will not proceed
- A lost-contact procedure
- A silt-cloud procedure
- An entanglement procedure
- A medical evacuation procedure
- A precise abort signal

Use the rule that half your available resources are for going out and half are for returning. In practice, reserve more than half. Cold, current, navigation errors, plant entanglement, and inspection stops will all consume time.

Do not follow a road “for just another few minutes.” The roads run for miles, cross at measured intervals, and may continue into districts that look identical. A straight route is not necessarily a safe route. The original builders may have designed it for people in air, not for a traveler moving through a flooded and overgrown grid.

---

# 11. Be prepared for a sudden loss of visibility

A minor disturbance could lift centuries of silt from a terrace or basin. A current could move a sheet of leaves into your lights. A damaged wall could release sediment. Your visual range may fall from several meters to nothing in seconds.

If visibility reaches zero:

1. Stop propulsion.
2. Maintain neutral buoyancy or place the vehicle on a known stable surface.
3. Switch to sonar and tether navigation.
4. Avoid thruster bursts.
5. Do not separate from the guide line.
6. Wait for suspended material to settle if your power and atmosphere permit.
7. Retreat along the recorded route rather than improvising.

Every suit should have a tactile tether system that can be followed by hand. Every vehicle should be able to return using an inertial record and acoustic beacon even when cameras show only blackness.

---

# 12. Do not depend on reaching the surface

The description implies a sea extending above the drowned country, but the surface may be unimaginably far away, permanently dark, or difficult to locate. A direct ascent may be impossible, dangerous, or incompatible with your pressure system.

Your actual escape route should be:

- The return portal
- The arrival station
- A sealed submersible
- A pre-positioned habitat
- A second independent multiverse exit

If a portal is involved, make sure it remains usable from both sides and cannot be blocked by water movement, vegetation, or pressure changes. Mark its exact spatial position physically and acoustically. A portal opening in a dark underwater hall may be impossible to relocate after a short excursion.

If your transport craft is not itself pressure-rated, do not assume the portal will protect it. Water may enter from all sides immediately, and any trapped air pocket could implode or shift violently.

---

# 13. Visit first as a surveyor, not an archaeologist

On the first trip, your purpose should be to understand the environment:

- Map the floor
- Measure temperature and pressure
- Identify currents
- Locate stable surfaces
- Determine whether the ruins are continuous
- Record plant distribution
- Test acoustic behavior
- Find safe landing or parking sites
- Establish a network of beacons

Do not enter the deepest halls first. Do not begin by looking for a central monument. There may be no center, and the landscape’s significance lies in its horizontal continuity. A causeway, basin, drainage channel, or repeated threshold may tell you more than an isolated surviving building.

The most valuable observations may be small:

- The way paired channels align
- The standard dimensions of steps
- The angle at which walls turn
- The relationship between old planted squares and current vegetation
- Which outlets remain open
- Where silt accumulates
- Whether the same construction materials recur across enormous distances

This is a world of repetition and maintenance. Its remaining meaning is distributed through the grid.

---

# 14. Keep technology modest and non-invasive

The place is not full of functioning machines, active defenses, or hidden technological traps. Its technology is exhausted and practical. Do not bring equipment optimized for combat, extraction, or aggressive excavation unless there is a specific reason.

Prefer:

- Low-thrust propulsion
- Passive sensors
- Soft-contact sampling tools
- Non-destructive imaging
- Reversible anchors
- Low-frequency communications
- Modular repair equipment
- Quiet power systems

Avoid:

- Explosives
- High-powered cutting lasers
- Unshielded drilling
- Powerful sonar used continuously at close range
- Open chemical discharge
- Hot exhaust directed at vegetation or masonry
- Heavy vehicles that rest directly on fragile structures

The ruins have survived by being massive, but survival is not proof that they can tolerate modern equipment.

---

# 15. Prepare psychologically for a world with no dramatic revelation

Do not expect a palace, a surviving population, a final machine, or a single ruin that explains everything. The location has been flattened into continuity. Its grandeur is not vertical and not concentrated.

The experience will likely be:

- Slow
- Repetitive
- Quiet
- Claustrophobic despite the vastness
- Difficult to photograph faithfully
- More exhausting than frightening
- Full of structures that are both ordinary and impossibly extensive

You may travel for hours through roads, planted squares, reservoirs, walls, and halls without encountering a “main attraction.” The emotional force will come from realizing that every plain slab and submerged channel belonged to a coordinated human landscape, and that the landscape now functions without humans, animals, or visible purpose.

Let the silence remain silence. Do not manufacture danger to make the visit feel meaningful. The place is already extraordinary: an entire civilization reduced to a navigable seabed, still obeying the geometry of its builders while water and plants complete the work.

---

## Recommended minimum expedition

For a cautious first visit, take:

- One one-atmosphere submersible
- One rigid atmospheric suit
- Two independent power systems
- Redundant oxygen and carbon dioxide management
- Heated suit and cabin
- Multibeam and imaging sonar
- Multiple navigation beacons
- A physical guide tether
- Wide and narrow lights with passive backups
- Protected thrusters or a low-speed crawler
- Entanglement cutters
- Non-destructive sampling tools
- Waterproof cameras and mapping systems
- Emergency medical supplies
- Full food and water reserves
- Decontamination equipment
- At least two independent return methods
- A crew trained to abort without debate

Arrive above an open, level district. Descend slowly. Record before touching. Move at walking speed. Keep the return route physically marked. Treat every dark opening as deep, every ceiling as unstable, every plant mat as concealing a void, and every sound as acoustically misleading until confirmed.

Most importantly, bring enough capability to leave while the place still feels uneventful. The drowned country does not need to attack you. It only needs to remain vast, cold, dark, and patient while a human traveler drifts a little too far from the way back.
