## Prepare as for a hot, unstable, contaminated lava chamber—not as for a garden or a ruin.

The chamber may look quiet and habitable, but it is an active geological and mechanical system. Its dangers are subtle: bad air rather than immediate flames, unstable footing rather than a visible collapse, heat stress rather than an obvious inferno, and an ecology that may be harmless—or entirely incompatible with human biology.

### Before entering

Bring:

- A properly fitted respirator or, preferably, a closed-circuit or supplied-air breathing system.
- A portable gas monitor capable of detecting oxygen deficiency, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, methane, and other volcanic or industrial gases.
- Heat-resistant boots with rigid soles and good traction.
- Gloves suitable for both hot metal and sharp volcanic glass.
- A helmet with face and eye protection. The basalt fractures are fresh and sharp, and falling mineral crust or loosened stone is possible.
- Full-body protective clothing, preferably sealed at the cuffs and neck. Treat the green dust, moss, and condensation as biologically unknown.
- Several independent lights. Do not rely on the plants’ glow; it is too weak for safe footing and may disappear if the plants are disturbed.
- A thermal imager or infrared thermometer. Several surfaces that look merely black may be hot enough to burn through clothing.
- A climbing line, anchors, and a way to mark your route. The chamber is small, but small caves become confusing in darkness, especially if an entrance is narrow or partially obscured.
- A hard case for instruments and samples. Do not place equipment directly on the floor or near the pools.
- A medical kit for burns, inhalation injuries, eye contamination, dehydration, and lacerations.
- Redundant communications and a timed extraction plan. Assume radio signals may not pass through the basalt.
- A reserve of drinking water and electrolytes. Do not depend on the chamber’s water.

Carry a local air supply even if the first gas reading looks normal. The air is described as damp in pockets, dry elsewhere, and faintly metallic. That suggests stratification, mineral vapors, or intermittent release from the cracks and machinery. Conditions may change when the pump cycles or when you disturb a sealed fissure.

If possible, send in a remotely operated probe first. It should measure:

- Oxygen concentration at floor, waist, and ceiling height.
- Temperature gradients around the floor cracks and pump.
- Airflow before, during, and after a pumping cycle.
- The structural condition of the ceiling and the lifted slabs.
- Whether the pump is connected to anything beyond the chamber.
- Any radiation or unusual electromagnetic output from the machinery or glowing seam.

### Do not assume the vegetation makes the air safe

The plants may photosynthesize, but their light output does not prove that they produce usable oxygen. They may use an unfamiliar chemistry, survive on mineral energy, or be partly synthetic. The lack of insects and other visible animals is not reassuring. It may mean the environment is sterile, chemically hostile, or governed by organisms too small to see.

Avoid:

- Breathing uncovered near the green dust.
- Brushing against ferns or ceiling vines.
- Shaking the plants.
- Walking through moss mats.
- Collecting open samples with bare hands.
- Bringing food into contact with the local growth.

The fine green reproductive dust should be treated as an airborne biological hazard. It could be harmless pollen, an irritant, a toxin, a spore, or something capable of colonizing human tissue. Keep your respirator on continuously. If you need to collect a sample, use a sealed sampler and change or decontaminate your outer clothing before leaving.

### Treat every surface as unstable

The basalt is fresh and fractured. Its sharpness is not merely an inconvenience; it indicates that the chamber is young or still changing. The shelves and tilted plates may shift under weight, especially near the orange-lit seam and the lifted slab.

Move slowly and test every foothold. Avoid:

- The edges of the fresh cracks.
- The raised slab.
- Any thin crust over an orange glow.
- Areas where water has collected in floor channels.
- The narrow space beneath low sections of ceiling.
- The sealed fissure containing the rail.
- The unfinished doorway, unless its stability has been inspected.

The orange seam is not safe merely because there is no visible lava. A hot rock seam can conduct enough heat to cause severe burns, weaken boots, ignite materials, or expand suddenly when cooled by water. Maintain a generous distance and do not pour water on it.

Wear a helmet even in the quietest part of the chamber. A low-frequency vibration, pump start, or minor geological movement could loosen material overhead.

### Keep clear of the pumping station

The machine is probably the most dangerous object in the chamber because its behavior is regular enough to invite interference.

Observe it for several full cycles before approaching. Record the timing of the motor, the three clicks, valve movement, pipe temperature, vapor release, and changes in the air. Do not touch the controls, cables, gauge, cylinder, pipes, or mineral deposits around them.

The machine may be:

- Maintaining the chamber’s humidity.
- Preventing water accumulation in a deeper void.
- Cooling or venting a geothermal pocket.
- Part of a larger automated installation.
- Operating under a program that responds to human presence.
- Keeping something sealed or contained.

The clouded gauge may be broken—or may be indicating a condition that cannot be read from outside. The pipes may be hot, pressurized, chemically contaminated, or carrying water that is not water in the ordinary biological sense.

Stay away during the motor’s engagement unless there is a compelling reason to study it. A person standing beside the station could be injured by a sudden valve release, a ruptured line, or a hidden mechanical movement. Never block a vent, cap a pipe, disconnect a cable, or “repair” anything.

Most importantly, do not shut the pump off. The ecology may depend on its cycle, but the pump may also be preventing a buildup of heat, gas, or water. If it stops on its own, withdraw rather than attempting maintenance.

### Do not drink from the pools

The pools are visually inviting but should be considered toxic until analyzed. The water has passed through hot rock, mineral seams, condensation surfaces, pipes, and a closed ecological cycle. It may contain:

- Heavy metals.
- Volcanic acids or dissolved gases.
- Industrial contamination.
- Unfamiliar microorganisms.
- Concentrated biological compounds from the plants.
- Substances that appear harmless but react dangerously with human tissue.

Do not touch your face after handling anything near a pool. Avoid kneeling beside one, since heavier gases may collect in the hollows. Do not lean over the water for long periods without monitoring the air at floor level.

If you take a sample, use a sealed container and assume it may corrode ordinary materials or begin changing once removed from the chamber.

### Minimize your effect on the ecology

The chamber is not thriving in the ordinary sense; it is balanced. Its living areas are sparse, isolated, and precisely arranged around moisture and heat. A careless visitor could disrupt it very easily.

Do not:

- Trample a green island to reach another.
- Touch the hanging vines or their roots.
- Move stones around a pool.
- Fill channels with equipment or soil.
- Add outside water.
- Spill fuel, disinfectant, food, blood, or human waste.
- Introduce insects, seeds, fungi, or microbes.
- Remove more than the smallest authorized samples.
- Use bright heat-producing lamps for extended periods near the plants.

The plants may have no obvious animal life because they have never encountered it. An ordinary human could be as destructive here as an invasive species.

Use clean outer coverings and, if practical, enter through an airlock or temporary containment tent. On departure, decontaminate boots, clothing, tools, and respirator surfaces. Keep all samples double-sealed and isolated from your vehicle, shelter, and other worlds until examined.

### Expect the chamber to be psychologically disorienting

There is no ordinary cave ambience here. The green radiance will reveal just enough to suggest movement without actually showing much. The pump’s low vibration may be felt in the body more than heard. The repeated clicks will become a timekeeper. The lack of animals, wind, and decay will make every human sound seem intrusive.

Use a buddy system and maintain verbal contact. A person who becomes fascinated by the still pools, machinery, or glowing seam may unconsciously approach a hazard. Set a strict time limit. Warm, damp air and low oxygen can impair judgment before the visitor recognizes the problem.

Do not remain alone in the chamber to “watch what happens.” Nothing changes quickly, and that is precisely how a dangerous stay becomes an unplanned overnight one.

### Establish exit triggers

Leave immediately if:

- Oxygen drops or any unexpected gas appears.
- The pump changes its timing, tone, or number of clicks.
- The orange seam brightens, cracks widen, or the floor shifts.
- Water begins collecting outside its established hollows.
- The ceiling sheds fragments.
- The air becomes suddenly hotter, wetter, metallic, or difficult to breathe.
- The plants release visible dust.
- The machinery activates continuously or stops after an unusual sound.
- Any member of the team experiences headache, nausea, confusion, coughing, eye pain, tingling, or unexplained fatigue.

Mark the route before exploring. Keep a safety line near the entrance and never let the entire party move into a confined recess at once. Leave someone with communications and a clear view of the return path if the chamber’s geometry permits it.

### The right mindset

Enter as a field researcher entering a sealed volcanic laboratory, not as a tourist entering a hidden grotto.

The chamber is neither abandoned nor naturally self-sufficient. It is a maintained system whose machinery, water cycle, plants, heat, and stone have settled into a fragile arrangement. It may continue functioning for centuries, but that does not mean it is robust. A single broken pipe, contaminated pool, blocked vent, or shifted slab could alter everything.

Observe from a distance. Touch as little as possible. Bring your own air, water, light, heat protection, and way out. Above all, leave the pump, the pools, the glowing seam, and the quiet balance of the chamber exactly as you found them.
