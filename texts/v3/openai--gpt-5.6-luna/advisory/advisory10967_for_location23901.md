## Prepare for the city as for a flooded industrial ecosystem, not an ordinary metropolis.

You are not visiting a place with a “downtown” and an edge. The whole lowland is urbanized, and the infrastructure extends beneath, above, and beyond what looks like the city. You may arrive at what appears to be an outer district and still be hundreds of kilometers from anything resembling open country. Plan for a long, complicated transit through connected construction.

### Before entering

Obtain local guidance on:

- **Pressure-cycle schedules**, especially near atmospheric engines.
- **Flood-gate and sluice-gate operations.**
- **Current animal lanes and migration routes.**
- **Which levels are open to unregistered visitors.**
- **Water and air-quality alerts.**
- **The most recent major structural shifts.**
- **The local meaning of light colors and warning patterns.**

Do not rely entirely on maps. The city changes its roads, buildings, bridges, water channels, and pedestrian routes. A map may be accurate in the same way an old river map is accurate: it tells you where the landscape used to prefer being.

Carry both a functioning electronic guide and a printed or physically encoded emergency plan. Expect communications to fail in rain, vibration, pressure events, and acoustically hostile districts.

If you have any choice of landing site, choose a **sealed, elevated, acoustically protected civic facility** with multiple exits and a known water-pressure rating. Do not materialize or disembark in an apparently empty paved area. It may be an animal lane, a service roof, a removable flood surface, or the top of a submerged structure.

## Clothing and equipment

Bring clothing designed for persistent warm wetness rather than occasional rain:

- A sealed outer shell with hood and face protection.
- Waterproof gloves and boots with excellent grip.
- Clothing that remains insulating when wet.
- Several changes of underclothes in sealed bags.
- Eye protection against mineral-laden rain and reflected light.
- A respirator or filtration mask rated for fine mineral dust, industrial vapor, biological particulates, and aerosols.
- Ear protection capable of reducing sustained industrial noise without completely eliminating warning signals.
- A secondary vibration or light-based alert device, since ordinary alarms may be inaudible.
- Waterproof bags for all electronics, documents, medicine, and food.
- A headlamp or body light with multiple visible colors and a low-intensity mode.
- A physical tether or handline for crossing slippery walkways and unstable service areas.
- A compact pressure gauge, if your equipment can use one.
- A first-aid kit containing antiseptic, sealant dressings, anti-inflammatory medicine, and treatment for eye and respiratory irritation.
- Food and clean water for at least a day beyond your expected travel time.

Use hard-wearing footwear. There is likely to be no dry, clean ground for long periods, and many surfaces will be coated with mineral deposits, biological films, oil, or condensation. Smooth fashion soles are dangerous.

Do not wear loose scarves, dangling equipment, open packs, exposed jewelry, or anything that can catch on cables or be seized by a passing animal. Bright colors may have infrastructural meanings; ask before wearing a garment that broadcasts a local warning color.

## Breathing and water

The atmosphere may be oxygen-compatible for a human, but that does not make it healthy to breathe unfiltered. Rain, vapor, mineral dust, industrial exhaust, animal aerosols, and chemically altered water are ubiquitous.

Do not drink rainwater. Do not drink from street channels, reservoirs, decorative basins, or apparently clean public taps until the water has been certified. Rain has crossed the exposed interior and may contain concentrated salts, iron, silicates, or other substances your body cannot handle.

Keep your mouth closed during pressure releases. Protect your eyes. If you develop a metallic taste, tingling, coughing, unusual fatigue, or a burning sensation in your throat, leave the area and seek a medical station. Local residents may treat such symptoms casually because they are accustomed to them. You should not.

Bring enough of your own medications for the entire visit plus delays. Assume local pharmacies may store drugs in forms, doses, or biological standards incompatible with your physiology.

## Noise and communication

Learn local nonverbal signals before entering crowded districts. Speech is unreliable at street level. You will need some combination of:

- Hand signs.
- Directional light signals.
- Short-range text or projected messages.
- A bone-conduction or skull-contact communication device.
- Sealed acoustic booths for important conversations.

Do not shout continuously. It will not help and may interfere with local warnings. In many districts, people may interpret your raised voice as distress, aggression, or an attempt to override a directed signal.

Carry a written card or display stating:

- Your name and origin.
- That you are a visiting outsider.
- Your medical requirements.
- Your inability to interpret local light codes.
- Whether you are authorized to handle machinery or animals.
- The location of your lodging or escort.

Arrange a local guide who knows the current infrastructure. An ordinary tourist should not navigate alone below street level, near engine districts, or during major weather and pressure changes.

## Animals

Treat the city’s animals as inhabitants, not pests.

They occupy walls, ducts, bridges, drains, roof spaces, and warm machinery. Many may be harmless; many may be engineered for functions you do not recognize; some may be intelligent enough to learn your routines. Do not feed them, corner them, touch them, photograph them at close range, or block their routes.

The most important rule is that **an apparently vacant space may be occupied**. A wall may contain a nest. A dry corridor may be dry because it is draining around a large body. A pile of wet material may be a dormant organism. A warm metal panel may be a heat exchanger—or the protected surface of something alive.

Give way to animal traffic, particularly:

- Flocks or swarms moving in coordinated patterns.
- Six-legged cargo beasts.
- Any animal escorted by barriers or signal lights.
- Creatures traveling in channels beneath roads.
- Large bodies moving through floodwater.

If a crowd suddenly moves aside, do the same without asking why. If animals flatten themselves against the ground, walls, or bridge supports, follow their example and get under stable cover. They may sense a pressure event before human systems announce it.

Do not assume small creatures cannot harm you. Some may carry unfamiliar microbes, discharge electricity, inject digestive fluids, or react defensively to your heat and chemical signature. Keep exposed food sealed.

## Movement through the city

Stay on marked pedestrian routes, but do not assume markings are permanent. Watch the lighting, pressure indicators, and local behavior around you.

Never:

- Stand beneath an unmarked ceiling during a pressure change.
- Lean against an unverified wall.
- Step over cables or pipes unless instructed.
- Cross a bridge during a structural adjustment.
- Enter a flooded passage merely because the water is shallow.
- Walk in a channel used by cargo animals.
- Climb onto machinery for a better view.
- Enter an old tunnel without a guide and a return marker.
- Assume a door is abandoned because it is sealed.
- Follow a dry route that no one else is using.

The city is layered horizontally, so route planning must include vertical transit. An apparently short trip may require crossing several independent roads, drainage levels, animal routes, service decks, and pressure compartments. Allow large delays.

Mark your route physically as well as electronically. Water, moving structures, and municipal reconfiguration may defeat ordinary navigation. Keep a companion informed of your location, and establish a rule for what happens if you are separated.

## Atmospheric engines and pressure events

Avoid the atmospheric-engine districts unless they are the specific purpose of your visit. They are likely the city’s most dangerous public environments.

If you must enter one, remain behind designated barriers and wear full eye, respiratory, and hearing protection. Secure everything that can be lifted by a blast. A pressure cycle may:

- Make the air visibly buckle.
- Lift water from the street.
- Reverse or redirect rain.
- Cause suspended roads and towers to swing.
- Drive animals into walls, ducts, or crowds.
- Release hot vapor, mineral dust, and ozone.
- Make metal surfaces unstable or dangerously hot.

When a cycle begins, do not run unless directed. Get low, brace yourself, protect your face, and follow local shelter behavior. Do not stand near grates, open channels, hanging signs, bridge anchors, or unsealed doors.

If a district’s lamps turn amber, violet, or another unfamiliar color, stop and ask a local what it means. Do not assume the color indicates a simple traffic signal. It may represent water pressure, structural stress, animal movement, disease danger, or an event the civic systems do not understand.

## The rule about silence

Learn this before anything else:

> When the lights go entirely dark, do not move until the sound returns.

This is not ordinary power failure. It is a recognized citywide condition, associated with synchronized engines and deep pressure. During it:

- Stop where you are.
- Do not enter a doorway or cross a road.
- Do not touch machinery.
- Do not call out.
- Do not follow moving shadows or unfamiliar lights.
- Stay away from open water and foundation edges.
- Keep your body low and still if possible.
- Wait for authorized instructions or for the city’s sound to resume.

The silence itself may last only minutes, but it is the time when the city’s usual mechanical and animal activity ceases and something moves beneath the foundations. Even if locals appear calm, imitate their stillness. Do not attempt to investigate the underground movement.

When sound returns, remain sheltered for a little while. The reactivation may be abrupt: pumps, animals, transit systems, rain, and pressure mechanisms can all resume simultaneously.

## Health and psychological preparation

This city will be difficult for an ordinary human nervous system. Prepare for:

- Constant wetness.
- No natural quiet.
- No empty horizon.
- Repeated reflections and visual glare.
- Confined, crowded air.
- Animals occupying every layer of the environment.
- The sensation that the ground is moving.
- Unfamiliar warning systems.
- Long periods without seeing anything genuinely natural or green.

Bring a sleep hood, filtered ear protection, and a private sealed room if possible. Confirm that your lodging has stable air filtration, drainage, emergency lighting, and no unexplained warm surfaces.

You may experience distress from the absence of vegetation. That response is reasonable. Do not wander into restricted “garden” or fog terraces seeking real plants; they are likely water, thermal, ceremonial, or technical installations.

## Social expectations

The inhabitants will probably regard your fear of water, animals, noise, and infrastructure as understandable but inconvenient. Be respectful of their competence. They have learned to read pressure, vibration, lamp colors, animal behavior, and structural sounds in ways you cannot.

Ask before:

- Photographing people or animals.
- Crossing a work lane.
- Entering a raised platform.
- Touching a wall, rail, cable, or hatch.
- Using a public signal color.
- Passing through a crowd during a pressure warning.
- Taking shelter in a service alcove.

Do not call the city dirty, diseased, or unnatural. Its inhabitants may understand those descriptions technically, but the city is also their maintained habitat, and its wetness, machinery, and fauna are not evidence of neglect.

## The right mindset

Do not visit expecting scenery, landmarks, or a clean division between nature and civilization. The city is a continuous managed organism—or something close enough that the distinction may not matter. Its danger comes less from individual hazards than from interacting systems: rain changes water pressure; water affects foundations; foundations carry animals; animals alter traffic; engines alter weather; weather changes visibility and sound.

Move slowly. Keep your equipment sealed. Watch what locals watch. If a route feels empty, ask why. If a wall feels warm, leave it alone. If every animal in the area suddenly changes direction, follow them. And if the entire city becomes silent, become still with it.
