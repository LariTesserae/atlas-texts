## Prepare as if you are visiting a working mine, refinery, wetland, and animal migration route at the same time

This is not a wilderness in the usual sense. It is an old industrial ecosystem that has absorbed its machinery, and it is dangerous mainly because every system is large, continuous, and indifferent to one person. You should not arrive casually, wander off a marked route, or assume that an apparently empty street is actually empty.

### Before arrival

Arrange, if possible, for:

- **A local guide or city-issued escort.** The useful person is not merely a tour guide, but someone who understands pump schedules, animal routes, pressure hazards, flood behavior, and which “roads” are actually drainage channels or service corridors.
- **A current map.** Old maps may be misleading. Buildings, channels, animal paths, and rail lines are likely to have been rerouted around leaks, nests, collapses, and migrations. Ask specifically for:
  - active industrial zones;
  - closed foundations and abandoned warm sites;
  - current migration roads;
  - safe elevated walkways;
  - shelters during vapor or weather events;
  - potable-water points;
  - emergency beacons and staffed stations.
- **Permission to enter industrial districts.** Some areas may be open to pedestrians, but pumps, pressure mains, furnaces, settling towers, and rail cuts should be treated as restricted machinery, not as scenery.
- **A biological and chemical briefing.** You need to know whether the air, water, animals, and surface organisms are safe for human exposure. The city’s residents may have long-established tolerances that an ordinary human does not.
- **A reliable route back to your arrival point.** Do not assume that a portal, vehicle, or transit connection will remain accessible through a migration, flood, power interruption, or weather shift.

If you can bring equipment through the crossing, bring duplicates. The city is wet enough to defeat a single piece of electronics, and its infrastructure is old enough that a local repair may involve parts you cannot identify.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing for **constant dampness, industrial residue, and poor footing**.

Recommended:

- Waterproof outer shell with sealed seams.
- Moisture-wicking inner layers; avoid cotton.
- Waterproof, high-ankle boots with exceptional grip on smooth stone and metal.
- Gloves with both traction and chemical resistance.
- Eye protection, preferably sealed or close-fitting.
- A hood or helmet with a brim. Drips from pipes and bridge undersides are unpredictable.
- Hard hat if entering any industrial or construction zone.
- Respirator or at least a high-quality particulate mask, subject to local air testing.
- Ear protection that still permits warning signals and communication.
- Reflective markings or a bright light; dark clothing will disappear in vapor and industrial shadow.
- Several changes of socks and gloves.
- Corrosion-resistant containers and sealed bags for clothing, documents, and equipment.

Use a harness or safety line near open reservoirs, escarpment edges, sluices, and elevated walkways if the local authorities permit it. The wet surfaces may not look especially slippery until you put your full weight on them.

Carry:

- Two independent lights, preferably one headlamp and one hand lamp.
- A waterproof local map and a compass. Do not rely entirely on satellite navigation or unfamiliar electronic positioning.
- A whistle or other loud signaling device.
- A short-range radio if local frequencies are available.
- A personal locator beacon.
- A first-aid kit including treatment for cuts contaminated by industrial water.
- Water and sealed food from outside the local water system.
- A small towel or absorbent cloth for lenses, hands, and equipment.
- A thermometer and basic air-quality monitor if available.
- A sealed waste bag. Do not leave biological material, food, or packaging behind.

Keep your footwear and outer clothing separate from your sleeping or transport equipment. The mineral residue and biological material in the streets may continue reacting with fabric and metal long after they appear dry.

## Air, water, and exposure

Do not assume that breathable air is safe air.

The vapor may contain:

- Aerosolized mineral salts.
- Industrial gases or furnace exhaust.
- Microorganisms adapted to the reservoirs and runoff.
- Fine particles from crushed filter media.
- Irritating or toxic compounds released when pressure systems vent.

Spend your first hours in a monitored, ventilated location. Test the air at street level, under bridges, near tanks, and in low channels; concentrations may differ substantially. Vapor is not necessarily poisonous, but it is dense enough to conceal dangerous gases and to reduce visibility before anyone notices a change.

Do not drink:

- Street runoff.
- Water from basins or reservoirs.
- Condensation from pipes or walls.
- Clear water unless it comes from a designated potable source.
- Water offered by animals or collected from the plateau.

“Clean” and “safe to drink” are likely different categories here. The city works specifically to separate water into salts, metals, organics, and other residues. A clear pool could contain dissolved metals, active organisms, or a chemical balance that is harmless to local life and dangerous to you.

Cover any cuts immediately. Waterproof bandages are preferable. Avoid touching your face while outdoors, and wash with approved water before eating. At the end of each day, inspect your skin, boots, and clothing for clinging organisms, unusual residue, bites, or translucent films.

## Moving through the city

Walk only on marked pedestrian routes or with a guide. A flat, dark surface may be:

- A road.
- A roof.
- A covered channel.
- A maintenance hatch.
- A thin bridge over a reservoir.
- A section of old industrial flooring weakened by years of seepage.

Do not step over, climb on, or lean against pipes unless they are explicitly designated for public use. A pipe that looks inactive may carry hot water, pressure, corrosive slurry, or a fluid whose function is not obvious. Do not stand beneath valves, vents, tanks, or suspended loads.

Expect sudden transitions:

- Warm air into cold dripping water.
- Clear visibility into vapor at arm’s length.
- Quiet pavement into a vibrating foundation.
- An apparently vacant street into a controlled migration corridor.
- Dry clothing into mineral-stained wetness within minutes.

Keep one hand free and do not walk while staring at maps or devices. The city is likely to have low walls, channels, rails, hoses, and irregular repairs at foot level. Mark your route physically or electronically, but verify landmarks by sound, light patterns, and elevation as well as appearance. Vapor can make two districts look identical.

The industrial noise will interfere with ordinary conversation and can conceal approaching vehicles or animals. Establish hand signals with your guide. If a warning bell, horn, or repeating light begins, stop and ask what it means rather than continuing toward the source.

## The animals

Treat every animal as a wild animal, even if it appears slow, familiar, or indifferent.

Their apparent lack of aggression should not reassure you. They are adapted to wet stone, close quarters, and machinery. Their bodies may contain defensive structures, corrosive secretions, parasites, or anatomy that makes “approaching from behind” meaningless. Some may react to vibration, warmth, light, or pressure rather than to sight.

Rules:

- Do not touch, feed, photograph with flash, block, or separate an animal.
- Do not stand between an animal and water, warmth, a wall, or another member of its group.
- Do not enter drainage channels or warm foundations because they appear empty.
- Do not crouch near small organisms; size is not a reliable indicator of danger.
- Keep food sealed and avoid carrying strong scents.
- Ask before using lights around reservoirs or migration paths.
- If workers lower gates, lower yours too.

During a migration, get above the route or leave the district entirely. Do not attempt to cross the stream. A migration that appears to move slowly may still exert enormous combined force, and the animals may not be able to see a human at ground level. Stay behind barriers and remain there until local personnel reopen the route. Do not assume that the last animal marks the end; stragglers, young, predators, and smaller associated organisms may follow.

After a migration, avoid the route until it has been inspected. The animals may leave behind slippery sediment, shell fragments, contaminated fluids, damaged paving, and changes to drainage.

## Weather and terrain

The plateau is broad but not forgiving. Its weather operates over long periods, which may make it seem stable until a bank of vapor or rain reaches the basin and changes conditions for hours.

Prepare for:

- Persistent rain and condensation.
- Reduced visibility.
- Sudden surface flooding.
- Water flowing through old channels beneath streets.
- Slippery basalt and mineral shelves.
- Cold exposure from repeated drenching despite generally warm air.
- Wind and exposure near the rim.
- Falling water, loose stone, and unstable old construction.

Stay well away from escarpment edges unless the route is engineered and guarded. The terraces may look solid because they have endured for centuries, but erosion is active and water is continuously finding weaknesses. Never shelter in a cavity, old foundation, or honeycombed rock chamber without confirming that it is stable and free of animals or industrial drainage.

At night, use the city’s lights as orientation, but do not mistake illumination for safety. Dark spaces beneath structures are likely to be active animal habitat or service infrastructure. The reflected light from wet bodies and surfaces may also make distance difficult to judge.

## Health and psychological preparation

The sound will be more exhausting than it first appears. The city does not roar dramatically; it produces a continuous pressure of pumps, vents, rails, furnaces, and vibration. After several hours, you may become irritable, disoriented, or unable to distinguish nearby sounds. Schedule quiet recovery periods in a sealed, structurally sound room. Sleep with hearing protection if local alarms permit it.

Expect sensory confusion:

- Vapor hides the scale of the basin.
- The sun follows a slow cycle but does not determine activity.
- Warmth comes from machinery as much as from weather.
- The ground may vibrate without visible movement.
- The “empty” landscape is alive at nearly every scale.
- The city’s age makes repairs look like original construction.

Do not interpret the place as abandoned simply because it is quiet in one district. Much of its work happens out of sight, and a silent section may be temporarily shut down rather than safe.

Bring an ordinary medical kit, but obtain local advice for animal contact, water exposure, inhalation, and skin contamination. If cross-world microbes are a possibility, use strict quarantine procedures on arrival and departure. Clean your boots, equipment, clothing, and any samples under controlled conditions. Do not export living organisms, mineral residue, water, eggs, larvae, spores, or biological films without explicit permission.

## A sensible first visit

Your first visit should be deliberately uneventful:

1. Arrive in a dry, monitored facility.
2. Confirm gravity, air composition, radiation, temperature, and local time cycle.
3. Remain indoors long enough to observe weather and industrial schedules.
4. Take a guided walk on elevated public routes.
5. Visit the observation district or another high, controlled viewpoint before entering the basin floor.
6. Learn the alarm signals and migration markings.
7. Sample no water and approach no animals.
8. Return before visibility deteriorates.
9. Inspect yourself and your equipment for residue or attached organisms.
10. Only then consider entering the industrial middle or outer animal roads.

The right attitude is not fear so much as humility. The city is not waiting to attack you, and its inhabitants may be perfectly accustomed to its dangers. But it is a place where water, machinery, animals, stone, and time have become one continuous system. You are the temporary, fragile component. Move slowly, stay on the systems designed for people, and assume that every apparently inert surface has a history and possibly a function.
