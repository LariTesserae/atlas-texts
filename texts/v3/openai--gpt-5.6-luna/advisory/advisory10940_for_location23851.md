## Preparing to Visit the Basin Settlement

Treat this as a visit to a living geological system, not merely a remote mountain town. The settlement is inhabited by people, but it is also inhabited by water, stone, weather, and animals. Your safety depends less on resisting the environment than on noticing what it is trying to do and getting out of its way.

### Before departure

Arrive with a local intermediary if at all possible. A resident, former resident, or traveler familiar with the basin is worth more than any amount of equipment. You will need someone who can interpret:

- the wall-signs and district markings;
- the local knock-and-tap signals;
- the difference between an ordinary crack and a “thinking” crack;
- the sounds that indicate a street, stair, or building is about to shift;
- which animals are merely passing through and which are claiming a space;
- the current condition of the cleft and the routes beyond the town.

Do not rely on a map made more than a short time ago. Bring a waterproof notebook, grease pencil, chalk, and a system of notes based on landmarks and functions rather than street names. Record where water is flowing, which passages are dry, and which buildings appear to be leaning together. Expect these observations to become obsolete.

If your multiverse technology permits it, establish a return anchor outside the basin, on stable ground above the cloud line. Do not place your only extraction point inside the settlement. The mountain may move it, bury it, redirect it, or incorporate it into something else.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for cold, constant damp, glare, and sudden changes in footing.

Bring:

- layered insulating clothing that remains warm when wet;
- a waterproof outer shell with sealed seams;
- gloves with good grip, preferably in two pairs;
- waterproof boots with flexible soles and strong ankle support;
- spare socks sealed in waterproof bags;
- a hood or close-fitting hat that will not obscure your hearing;
- protective eyewear or tinted goggles for the intense reflected light from snow, water, and black stone;
- lip balm, skin protection, and treatment for cold injury;
- a small towel or absorbent cloth;
- a mask or scarf for mineral dust if a wall opens or the mountain shifts.

Do not wear loose trailing fabric, dangling ornaments, or decorative straps. Narrow passages, moving masonry, dense roof animals, and sudden water flow make such things dangerous.

Carry a short rope, but do not use it casually. A rope may be useful for crossing a wet stair or securing yourself during a shift, but tying yourself to a building, bridge, gutter, or animal shelter could make you part of the wrong movement. Carry a whistle only as an emergency signal; normal communication is quiet, and an unnecessary loud noise may alarm both people and animals.

A walking staff is useful, especially one that can test depth and firmness. Do not hammer it into cracks. Touch the stone lightly. The inhabitants may interpret repeated probing as interference.

Bring only sealed food and a small supply of clean water for your own needs. Do not assume you may drink from channels, gutters, or the reservoir. Water is socially and practically precious, and an outsider who contaminates a water path will be treated very seriously.

### Health precautions

The altitude, cold, brilliant reflected light, damp air, and unfamiliar minerals may all affect you. Spend the first hours resting rather than exploring. Watch for:

- headache, confusion, nausea, or unusual breathlessness from altitude;
- numb fingers and toes;
- dizziness caused by glare or reflected light;
- skin irritation from mineral-rich water or masonry dust;
- infections from cuts exposed to standing water;
- sleep disruption caused by the settlement’s continual low noises and subtle movements.

Keep wounds covered. Do not put your hands into channels, wall cavities, roof nests, or pools merely to see what is inside. There are living things beneath the water, and the town’s animals may regard your intrusion as either an invitation or an injury.

If you have a strong fear response to enclosed spaces, unstable architecture, or animals appearing where they should not be, prepare yourself in advance. The settlement’s changes are not dramatic in the usual way. A wall may move gradually while you are looking at it. A stair may acquire steps. A passage may become longer without any visible breaking. This is psychologically more unsettling than a conventional collapse.

### Entering the town

Wait to be received. Do not stride directly toward the center or choose a route by apparent convenience. At the edge of the settlement, observe the flow of water and people before taking a step.

The inhabitants will likely notice you before you see them. Keep your hands visible. Move slowly. Do not speak loudly. If you are offered a place to stand, stand there; it may be a location selected for its structural stability.

Ask permission before:

- crossing a bridge;
- entering a house or work court;
- touching a carved sign;
- approaching the reservoir;
- photographing or copying wall marks;
- feeding, touching, or following an animal;
- inspecting a crack, drain, or newly formed passage.

A gift is appropriate, but choose carefully. Useful, durable items are better than luxuries: good thread, needles, sealed medicines, blades, oil, waterproof containers, or materials that can repair clothing. Do not bring large quantities of food, metal, artificial light, or brightly colored manufactured objects unless your host specifically requests them. The people may have no use for them, or may consider them disruptive.

### How to move through the settlement

Assume that every path is temporary.

Walk with one hand free and keep your attention on:

- changes in floor angle;
- water beginning to flow against its previous direction;
- fresh gaps between stones;
- new dampness in a wall or roof;
- unusual animal movement;
- sounds traveling through masonry;
- steps that seem subtly different on a return journey.

Count steps only as a personal reference. Do not trust the count to remain correct.

Keep to raised plankways when water enters the streets, but do not block them. If residents move aside, move aside with them. If everyone stops, stop. If someone begins removing stones from a wall, do not help until instructed; they may be relieving pressure in a carefully understood way.

Never wedge a door, brace a wall, dam a channel, fill a crack, or prop up a stair without local direction. These would seem like sensible emergency measures in another place. Here they may prevent a necessary shift and transfer the force into a house, bridge, person, or water system.

If a surface begins to tilt, lower your center of gravity and move toward open space only if the locals are doing so. Do not run downhill. Do not shelter under an overhang unless directed. During a change, the safest place may be beside a moving structure rather than beneath it, but only residents will know which side is yielding and which side is bearing.

Keep your belongings in a compact pack. Never set equipment down in a gutter, channel, stairwell, or doorway. Anything left unattended may be washed away, buried, or treated as an obstruction.

### Water etiquette

Water is the settlement’s circulatory system. It is not scenery.

Do not:

- wash in a public channel;
- spit into a gutter;
- dispose of food, ash, medicine, or waste in flowing water;
- step across a narrow watercourse unless invited;
- plug a leak because it appears inconvenient;
- take water from a cistern without permission;
- touch the reservoir.

Ask where to wash, where to relieve yourself, and where to fill your containers. The residents will have systems for each. Follow them precisely.

If water begins emerging from a wall, do not crowd around it. Someone will determine whether it should be collected, redirected, or left alone. Warm water in particular may indicate a major internal change.

### Animals

The animals are neither domestic stock nor ordinary wildlife. Assume that they understand the town better than you do.

Do not make prolonged eye contact with the ash-colored birds, even if you cannot see eyes. Do not reach beneath roof huddles. Do not step between a furred wall creature and the masonry it has chosen. Do not attempt to catch the white things in the channels or follow the translucent ones across a roof.

If an animal appears in an impossible location, do not announce it, corner it, or try to return it to where you think it belongs. Leave a clear route away from you. The local rule is sound: if it has arrived, it may need to leave by a route you cannot perceive.

You may be tolerated if you behave as part of the background. Sudden gestures, loud calls, bright lights, and attempts to touch animals will make you conspicuous.

### Communication and social conduct

Speak softly and let pauses remain. The people may communicate through taps, wall knocks, hand signs, and changes in water flow. Do not imitate these signals until you know what they mean. An innocent knock may be a warning, an invitation, a structural report, or a message sent through an entire block.

When someone tells you to wait, they may be listening to the building rather than being indecisive. When they tell you to move, do so immediately and without argument.

Do not describe the settlement as primitive, backward, silent, or abandoned. It is highly organized, simply organized around different necessities. Its inhabitants possess detailed knowledge of their environment, though much of that knowledge is embodied in habit rather than written explanation.

Expect hospitality to be practical rather than expressive. A dry place to sit, a marked stone, a cup of water, or a warning tap may be a considerable kindness.

### During a disturbance

You should assume a disturbance is always possible, but not treat every change as an emergency. Watch the residents. Their behavior is your best classification system.

Signs that a change may be developing include:

- water beads appearing on dry stone;
- gutters ticking in a new rhythm;
- animals leaving roofs or clustering in unusual places;
- a low sound beneath the paving;
- a door or stair becoming difficult to open or close;
- shadows shifting without a corresponding change in light;
- a wall seam turning blue;
- residents quietly removing household objects or placing ropes beside thresholds.

When these occur:

1. Move away from narrow passages and the downhill side of leaning structures.
2. Keep clear of gutters, reservoirs, and newly wet masonry.
3. Retrieve your pack only if it is within immediate reach.
4. Follow the nearest resident rather than your original route.
5. Do not attempt to save, stabilize, or hold anything in place.
6. If told to cross a moving section, do so promptly and without stopping halfway.
7. Once clear, remain where directed until the town’s people begin moving again.

Do not assume that a quiet change is a safe change. A building sliding three body-lengths while remaining intact may injure someone who believes there has been no collapse.

### The cleft and the outer basin

Do not visit the bright cleft alone. Consider it a separate expedition, not a scenic walk beyond town.

The air there is colder, visibility may be distorted by suspended water, and the route may be changing more rapidly than the inhabited basin. Bring a guide, rope, spare warmth, and a fixed return time—but understand that time and distance may not behave consistently near the opening.

Do not pass the handprint wall without permission. Do not add your own mark merely for the sake of participation. If you are invited to leave a print, ask which hand, what substance, and whether the mark should be human, gloved, wet, or mineral-coated. The many kinds of prints suggest that the wall is not simply commemorative. It may be a record, a boundary, a greeting, or a means by which the basin recognizes travelers.

Do not enter the cleft because it appears open. A passage that has just formed may be unfinished, hungry, or connected to a place that does not return people in a consistent condition.

### Departure

Arrange your departure before you begin exploring, and tell at least one local person where and when you intend to leave. Reconfirm the route shortly beforehand. Your arrival path may no longer exist.

Leave no equipment behind unless you deliberately offer it and it is accepted. Do not abandon markers, food wrappers, powered devices, or artificial structures. The settlement incorporates unattended objects into its systems, and your harmless convenience could become a blocked channel or an unexplained disturbance.

Before crossing out of the basin, check yourself for wetness, parasites, mineral residue, and animals concealed in your pack or clothing. Check your equipment twice. More importantly, verify that your return anchor still points to the same universe, date, and physical location. A shifting mountain may not be the only thing that has moved.

Most of all, leave without trying to understand everything. The inhabitants have built a life around listening for changes rather than conquering them. Your safest posture is attentive, quiet, and provisional: step where others step, drink only where permitted, let the stone move, and make yourself easy for the mountain to forget.
