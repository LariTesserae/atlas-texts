## Preparation for visiting

Treat this as a cold, wet, high-altitude ruin in a region with almost no reliable shelter or rescue—not as a normal archaeological sightseeing trip. The principal dangers are exposure, disorientation, unstable masonry, hidden water, and the cumulative effects of walking for hours on steep, slick ground.

### Before arrival

- **Find out the elevation and season.** Even moderate altitude can cause headache, nausea, poor sleep, and impaired judgment if you arrive directly from low country. If possible, spend a day or two acclimatizing at a lower shelf of the plateau.
- **Arrive with a return plan.** The city is large enough to swallow a careless traveler, and fog can make familiar streets unrecognizable. Establish a fixed departure time, a fallback shelter, and several marked routes back to the arrival point.
- **Carry redundant navigation.**
  - A waterproof map and compass.
  - A location beacon or radio if technology functions in this universe.
  - Bright, removable trail markers that will not damage the ruins.
  - A written route plan in a waterproof container.
  
  Do not rely exclusively on visibility, landmarks, or electronic mapping. Whole districts may disappear under fog, and the boundary between road, terrace, roof, and slope may be difficult to recognize.

- **Bring enough food and water for at least twice the expected duration.** The settlement’s channels and cisterns may contain runoff, silt, animal contamination, or microorganisms adapted to the local environment. Carry a filter, chemical treatment, and a way to boil water, but regard boiling as a backup rather than a reason to drink from every visible channel.
- **Ask whether fire is permitted or practical.** The city contains old timber, dry interior dust, and many confined spaces. Although the exterior is wet, a fire in a shaft, cellar, or courtyard could produce smoke, carbon monoxide, or an uncontrollable structural fire. Bring an insulated stove only if you understand how to ventilate it safely; do not count on finding dry fuel.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for prolonged cold rain and wind, not for the temperature shown at your point of arrival.

- Waterproof shell jacket and trousers.
- Warm insulating layers, preferably synthetic or wool rather than cotton.
- Several spare pairs of socks in sealed bags.
- Waterproof gloves, with a thinner pair underneath for dexterity.
- Warm hat and a hood that does not obstruct peripheral vision.
- Sturdy waterproof boots with aggressive soles and ankle support.
- Gaiters to keep water, mud, and marsh plants out of the boots.
- Trekking poles or a staff. These are particularly valuable on slick paving, submerged steps, and uncertain ground.
- Sunglasses and sunscreen. The sky may be gray, but high-altitude ultraviolet exposure can still be severe when the cloud breaks.
- Headlamp with spare batteries, even if you expect to return in daylight. Fog and cloud may turn afternoon into darkness, and many interiors will be entirely lightless.
- Eye protection for wind-driven sleet and grit.

Carry everything in waterproof bags inside a pack. Assume that every pocket, map, match, and battery will eventually be exposed to damp.

### Medical and survival supplies

Bring a compact but serious first-aid kit:

- Blister treatment and spare laces.
- Bandages for cuts and pressure injuries.
- Pain relief and any personal medication.
- Rehydration salts and high-energy food.
- A thermometer if available.
- Emergency bivouac or insulated shelter.
- Heat packs, though they should not replace dry clothing and shelter.
- Water purification.
- A whistle and signal mirror.
- A repair kit for clothing, pack, poles, and footwear.

Know the symptoms of **hypothermia**: persistent shivering, clumsiness, confusion, unusual tiredness, and irrational decisions. At this site, exhaustion and dampness may become dangerous before the weather feels dramatically cold. A person who has stopped shivering is not necessarily improving; they may be deteriorating.

### How to move through the city

Assume that every apparently solid surface is only conditionally solid.

- Stay on broad, visible roads and terraces where possible.
- Do not walk along the edges of roofs, parapets, retaining walls, or collapsed buildings.
- Test paving before committing your weight. Water may conceal a missing slab, a shaft, or a deep gap.
- Avoid entering structures with sagging roofs, cracked arches, bulging walls, or floors covered by standing water.
- Never stand directly below a visibly saturated slope, leaning wall, or roofline.
- Do not climb old towers merely for the view. Their upper masonry is likely to be more exposed to frost and wind, and their floors may be missing.
- Treat every lower level as a potential drainage route. Water may rise quickly after rain, and a dry passage can become a channel.
- Do not cross marshes by stepping from stone to stone unless you can see the stones clearly and confirm their stability. The pale-looking margins may be deep, soft, and cold enough to immobilize someone.
- Keep three points of contact on wet steps and steep switchbacks.
- Use poles to probe mud, shallow water, and vegetation-covered gaps.

The city’s quietness will make hazards easy to underestimate. A collapsing wall or saturated bank may make very little sound until it moves. Water can conceal the beginning of a failure. If you feel masonry tremble or hear a muffled internal slide, move away from the slope and toward open, stable ground.

### Shelter

Do not assume that an old house is safe shelter merely because it has four walls.

Before using a building, inspect it from outside:

- Is the roof sagging?
- Are walls leaning or separated at the corners?
- Are arches cracked?
- Is water entering through several levels?
- Are doorframes distorted?
- Is the floor hidden beneath silt, moss, or water?
- Do birds or animals avoid the interior?

Prefer a low, intact structure with a sound roof, good drainage, and more than one exit. Avoid basements, enclosed cisterns, deep courtyards, and rooms with no ventilation. Sleep above the floor on an insulated groundsheet or pad. Keep boots and clothing inside the sleeping area so they do not freeze or become waterlogged. Do not block an entrance with equipment; fog and water may force a rapid departure.

If no building is trustworthy, use a modern emergency shelter in an open but protected location, well away from walls, ravines, marsh edges, and falling masonry. The ideal camp is sheltered from wind but not positioned beneath anything that can collapse.

### Water, food, and sanitation

The abundance of water will be deceptive. It will be everywhere, but not necessarily drinkable.

Collect from the cleanest available source—preferably rain caught directly in a clean container, or a flowing channel above the settlement rather than pooled water within it. Avoid water downstream of burial grounds, animal tracks, marshes, collapsed buildings, and old industrial-looking or mineral-stained areas. Filter and disinfect it.

Keep food sealed. Sparse wildlife does not mean absence of scavengers, parasites, or disease. Do not eat local plants, amphibians, or animals without substantial knowledge of the world’s biology. The disciplined vegetation may be harmless, poisonous, or nutritionally useless.

Use sanitation well away from watercourses, cisterns, courtyards, and occupied shelter. The city’s channels are part of a functioning drainage system; contaminating one may spread waste through several districts.

### Weather and timing

Plan each day around the weather, not around a list of ruins.

A clear morning may be followed by a basin-wide rainstorm or a wall of fog. Fog will erase distance, flatten slopes, and make openings and drop-offs difficult to distinguish. In sleet or freezing rain, avoid high terraces and exposed ridges. Ice may form in channels and on polished road centers even when surrounding ground looks merely wet.

Begin returning well before the expected loss of light. The city’s upper districts may appear close but require long switchbacks to reach, and descending wet stairs in darkness is more dangerous than climbing them. If fog arrives, stop moving until you can determine where you are. Wandering in search of a landmark is likely to make the situation worse.

### What the visit will feel like

Prepare psychologically for a place with almost no social cues. There will be no traffic, voices, smoke, or ordinary signs of habitation to reassure you. Small sounds—water under a grate, a bird striking the air above a courtyard, a distant slide of earth—will seem disproportionately important. The city will feel occupied by weather rather than by people.

Do not interpret the silence as safety. The settlement is continuously changing. A passage that was dry on arrival may be slick or flooded later; a wall that appeared permanent may be one season from failure. You are visiting a city that has not been abandoned so much as absorbed, slowly and methodically, by the plateau.

The safest attitude is cautious respect: travel in pairs or a well-equipped group, leave a route record, avoid unnecessary entry or climbing, take nothing from the structures, and be willing to turn back while conditions still seem merely unpleasant. The location will not reward bravado. It rewards dry layers, conservative decisions, good navigation, and enough time to let the weather pass.
