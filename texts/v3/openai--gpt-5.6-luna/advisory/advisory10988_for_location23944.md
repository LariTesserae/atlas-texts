Treat this as an expedition into a warm, pressurized ocean—not as a wet terrestrial mountain range. An ordinary human should not arrive here wearing a diving suit and carrying camping equipment. The minimum safe approach is a small, pressure-rated submersible or a rigid, pressure-balanced exploration suit with life support, propulsion, mapping equipment, and a reliable extraction system.

## Before entering

### Determine what “surface” means

The ocean’s upper boundary may be far above the highest ridges, but the traveler must establish:

- Whether there is a true air-water interface
- How deep the ridges are below it
- The pressure at the intended arrival point
- The temperature gradient from the upper water to the vents
- Whether the water is chemically compatible with human equipment
- Whether the unusual buoyancy behavior affects gases, bubbles, and objects

A ridge described as “hundreds of meters below the surface” could impose pressures of dozens of atmospheres. If the water column is deeper than it appears, the pressure could be substantially greater. Do not assume that a normal atmospheric diving profile will apply.

A pressure sensor should be deployed before a person enters. If a portal or vehicle opens directly into the water, it should open inside a pressure vessel or unmanned probe, not around an unprotected human.

### Use a rigid pressure habitat

A normal flexible diving suit is inappropriate. The expedition should use one of:

1. **A small submersible**, preferably the primary method.
2. **A rigid atmospheric suit**, designed to maintain approximately one atmosphere around the occupant.
3. **A pressure-balanced suit**, only if the traveler has thoroughly tested the local water chemistry and can tolerate the associated breathing-gas and decompression complications.

The submersible should have:

- A pressure hull with generous safety margins
- Multiple independent seals and electrical systems
- Emergency ballast release and positive buoyancy
- Mechanical arms rather than direct human contact
- External lights and cameras
- Sonar or other nonvisual mapping equipment
- A tether or navigation beacon leading back to the arrival point
- Enough power and life support for a rescue delay lasting weeks, not hours

Do not rely on ordinary buoyancy assumptions. The setting already contains bubbles that do not rise in the expected way, and the pressure fronts may produce unfamiliar currents. Use active propulsion and controllable ballast rather than depending on a simple inflatable lift bag.

### Prepare for decompression and gas hazards

If anyone must leave the pressure vessel, establish:

- A controlled airlock
- A staged decompression system
- A habitat or chamber capable of matching the local pressure
- Redundant breathing-gas supplies
- A way to recover a lost or unconscious explorer

No one should make repeated free-diving excursions. The warm water may make the depth feel comfortable while concealing severe decompression risk. Any air pocket in clothing, equipment, lungs, or machinery is potentially dangerous. Never assume that bubbles will rise normally or that a bubble can serve as a safe emergency breathing source.

## Personal equipment

The traveler should wear or occupy equipment providing:

- A closed-cycle oxygen supply
- Carbon dioxide removal
- Humidity and temperature control
- Protection against high external pressure
- Full-body isolation from the water
- Redundant lights
- A physical tether or tracking transponder
- A compass alternative, since local geology may distort magnetic navigation
- A wrist or helmet display showing pressure, depth, temperature, remaining life support, and distance from the vehicle
- A panic beacon with both acoustic and electromagnetic signaling
- A cutting tool for vines, fronds, cables, or entangling growth
- A way to mark a route without releasing pollutants or permanent objects

Bring no open flame. No unprotected electronic device should be assumed safe around hot mineral plumes or chemically reactive water.

Thermal protection should be adjustable. The general environment is warm, but moving water can still remove heat rapidly. Near vents, the danger reverses: localized plumes may overheat the suit, damage seals, or cause burns through apparently clear water. A temperature sensor should be mounted ahead of the traveler and on the vehicle, not merely inside the cabin.

## Choose the landing site carefully

The best first landing site would be:

- On a broad, stable terrace
- Well away from cliff bases
- Away from leaning walls and balanced boulders
- Away from vents, mineral crusts, and visibly active fissures
- Outside dense vegetation
- In clear water with good visibility
- Far from the edge of a trench or sudden drop
- Up-current or up-gradient from any contaminated plume, if a current can be determined

Do not shelter under an overhang. The cliffs are young and continuously shedding material. A shelter beneath one could be crushed by a slow fall that gives no useful warning. A seemingly stable ledge may be held together by friction, root nets, or mineral growth and may fail when touched.

Establish a remote base rather than sleeping in the open. The base should be a pressure-rated, self-contained habitat anchored to known stable rock or suspended independently of the slopes. Keep the submersible ready for immediate departure; do not bury it in sediment or attach it to living growth.

## Assume the water is biologically and chemically dangerous

The vegetation is not familiar enough to treat as harmless. It may:

- Release toxins or corrosive compounds
- Alter the local chemistry when damaged
- Detect pressure or vibration
- Grow into equipment
- Produce spores, filaments, or microscopic reproductive particles
- Consume oxygen or release gases
- Be part of a large connected organism

Do not touch the plants, collect samples with bare equipment, or let plant matter enter filters and propellers. The root nets may be structurally important, and cutting one could destabilize a slope or trigger a local collapse.

The warm mineral water around vents deserves the same caution as an extraterrestrial chemical spill. It may contain sulfides, acids, dissolved metals, unusual gases, or organisms adapted to chemistry lethal to humans. Sample remotely first. Use sealed sample containers and an external decontamination compartment.

Do not assume green growth produces breathable oxygen. It may use a different energy system, photosynthesize inefficiently at depth, or consume oxygen at night or under sediment. The traveler must remain on stored or recycled oxygen at all times.

All samples and equipment should undergo quarantine before returning to the home universe. The environment is biologically dense despite its sparse visible animal life, and its organisms may spread through water, spores, dormant tissue, or mineral fragments.

## Navigation and observation

Human vision will be unreliable. Sunlight becomes green, bronze, and eventually deep blue, while silt can reduce visibility to a few dozen meters. Use:

- Sonar for terrain and moving animals
- Structured light or lidar for nearby ledges and vegetation
- Inertial navigation
- Multiple fixed acoustic beacons
- A continuously recorded depth and pressure map
- Redundant route markers

Do not navigate by the sun alone. The water may thicken with sediment, pressure fronts may alter visibility, and a broad shadow from a submerged mountain can make direction difficult to judge.

Map the terrain before approaching it. The ridges are angular, unstable, and full of blind valleys. A route that looks open may end in a vertical drop, a silt-filled trench, or a forest dense enough to trap a vehicle.

Avoid rapid movement through tall vegetation. Something large may be using the plants as cover, and the absence of visible animals does not indicate the absence of nearby life. Since the fauna is rare, any large moving shadow should be treated as a significant encounter rather than as harmless scenery.

## Pay attention to pressure fronts and ground movement

This world has no ordinary storms, but pressure changes can act like weather. Warning signs may include:

- Forests bending in a common direction
- Dust lifting from terraces
- Visibility changing without an obvious current
- A low pressure tone
- Repeated clicks or small shifts in nearby rock
- Mineral plumes changing direction
- Sudden plant movement far ahead of the vehicle

If vegetation begins to lean, retreat to open water or the pressure habitat. Do not wait to feel the current directly. The plants may register changes long before a human or vehicle does.

Never park below an unstable shelf. The characteristic event here may not be a dramatic avalanche but a single stone falling at the wrong time, followed by a delayed collapse. Keep a clear route in all directions and avoid placing the habitat where a sediment fan could bury its access point.

## Understand the sound environment

The water will not be silent. The traveler may hear:

- Continuous venting
- Pressure flowing around cliffs
- Plants rubbing against one another
- Stones settling
- Distant shelves cracking
- Possible low-frequency signals from large organisms or geological processes

Do not interpret every sound as an animal, but do not dismiss broad tones as harmless background noise. Record them and compare their timing with observed ground movement. Acoustic equipment may be more useful than radio, especially inside deep valleys.

The traveler should communicate with the vehicle by both wired tether and acoustic backup. Spoken communication through an ordinary mask or helmet may be confusing because sound transmission through the water and suit will differ radically from air.

## Daily operating rules

A sensible expedition protocol would be:

1. Send unmanned probes first.
2. Measure pressure, temperature, chemistry, visibility, currents, and biology.
3. Establish a beacon network from the arrival point.
4. Enter in the submersible, not in an exposed suit.
5. Remain near open water during the first survey.
6. Avoid vents, cliff bases, dense growth, trenches, and unexplained shadows.
7. Take no samples until remote observation shows that sampling is safe.
8. Never allow both the explorer and the rescue vehicle to be out of contact simultaneously.
9. Maintain at least one full reserve of power, oxygen, and navigation capability for an unplanned return.
10. End the mission immediately if pressure behavior, water chemistry, or local physics begins deviating from the initial survey.

## Psychological preparation

The place may be more unsettling than a conventional alien wilderness. It is bright enough to resemble an ordinary landscape, yet there is no air, no sky, no dry ground, and no reliable horizon. The plants move slowly, the animals are nearly absent, and geological change is visible as a continuous background process.

The traveler should expect:

- Distorted distance and scale
- Difficulty judging vertical direction
- Long periods with no obvious motion
- Sudden, remote geological sounds
- The feeling of being watched despite seeing almost nothing
- A landscape that looks stable but is constantly settling

Do not mistake quietness for safety. The dominant dangers are pressure, unstable young rock, chemical exposure, entanglement, loss of orientation, and isolation—not predation.

The safest mindset is that of a deep-ocean geologist entering an active, living construction site. Admire the suspended ocean, the green-gold light, and the forests growing beneath cliffs, but remain inside a sealed vehicle, keep an escape route open, and treat every apparently beautiful surface as potentially unstable, alive, or chemically hostile.
