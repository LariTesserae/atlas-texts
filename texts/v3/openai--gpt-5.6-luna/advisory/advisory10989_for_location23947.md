## Prepare as if visiting a hot mine, a wet factory, and a newly forming cave at the same time

This is not a sightseeing cavern. It is an active excavation and industrial settlement whose floor, walls, drainage, and support structures are all still changing. An ordinary human traveler should arrive with authorization, a guide, and equipment for heat, water, unstable rock, machinery, poor visibility, contaminated surfaces, and animal contact.

### Before entering

- **Obtain a local briefing and escort.** Do not enter independently. The quarter has elevators, rails, pumps, conveyors, active drilling, shallow shafts, temporary bridges, and areas that may look like streets but are actually work zones.
- **Learn the evacuation routes.** Identify:
  - the nearest surface access or elevator,
  - refuge rooms,
  - emergency telephones or radio stations,
  - areas that flood first,
  - routes that remain usable if power fails.
- **Confirm that the pumps are operating.** Their failure may not produce an immediate disaster, but water could accumulate, redirect through the settlement, undermine foundations, and make passages impassable.
- **Arrange a strict check-in schedule.** Visibility and navigation are poor, and the environment is visually repetitive. Carry a beacon or locator that works underground rather than relying only on ordinary radio.
- **Verify the local atmosphere.** Even if the air is breathable, have someone test oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, methane or other flammable gases, hydrogen sulfide, drilling dust, and industrial vapors. Warm, damp air can feel breathable while still being dangerous.
- **Check for cross-universe biological incompatibility.** Do not assume the local microbes, molds, parasites, or animal diseases are harmless to you. If possible, use a clean-room procedure on arrival and departure.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing chosen for heat and abrasion rather than ordinary outdoor comfort:

- Lightweight, moisture-wicking base layers.
- Durable long sleeves and trousers to protect against rust, sharp rock, insects, and contaminated mud.
- A waterproof outer layer only where needed; a fully sealed suit may trap heat and moisture and cause overheating.
- A properly fitted **industrial helmet** with chin retention. Falling grains of rock are common, and the raw ceiling may shed larger fragments.
- Impact-rated eye protection, preferably with indirect ventilation to resist mist and splashing.
- Hearing protection that still allows you to hear alarms and instructions. A combination of earplugs and electronic earmuffs may be appropriate near drilling or compressors.
- Waterproof, steel-toed or otherwise protective boots with aggressive, slip-resistant soles. They should support the ankle and be comfortable when continuously wet.
- Chemical-resistant gloves with a good grip. Carry a second dry pair.
- A headlamp plus at least two backup lights. Hooded lamps are too weak for safe travel, and the mist will reduce visibility.
- A personal gas monitor, temperature monitor, and preferably a small particulate monitor.
- A compact first-aid kit containing wound dressings, antiseptic, blister treatment, eye-wash, and supplies for minor burns and abrasions.
- Hydration equipment with electrolyte solution. Do not rely on local water unless it has been tested and treated.
- A mask or respirator appropriate to the measured hazards. A simple dust mask is not a universal solution; use local industrial guidance, especially during drilling or around mineral deposits.

Do not bring loose scarves, dangling straps, jewelry, or anything that can catch in belts, chains, sprockets, elevators, or ventilation equipment.

### Heat and humidity

The heat is described as moderate but continuous, which may be more dangerous than a brief high-temperature exposure. The damp air will prevent sweat from evaporating effectively. You may feel clammy rather than visibly drenched while still losing water and salts.

- Acclimatize before visiting if possible.
- Drink regularly rather than waiting until thirsty.
- Use electrolytes, not only plain water.
- Take frequent rests in the coolest approved areas.
- Watch for headache, nausea, confusion, weakness, cramps, unusual irritability, or failure to sweat. Treat these as heat illness, not as something to push through.
- Do not enter after alcohol, sedatives, or anything else that impairs judgment.
- Keep a dry set of clothes outside the quarter or in a sealed locker. Everything inside will gradually become damp, dirty, and mineral-coated.

Fans will not necessarily make the air cool; they may only move hot vapor around. Avoid standing directly over warm exhaust vents, even if they seem to offer a clear patch of air.

### Movement and navigation

Move slowly and stay on marked walkways.

- Assume every wet surface is slippery.
- Treat rails, conveyor supports, pipes, cables, and temporary bridges as operational infrastructure, not convenient handholds or paths.
- Never walk beneath freshly cut rock, unsupported ceiling, suspended loads, or active conveyor routes.
- Do not cross a bridge, shaft edge, or loading area without explicit confirmation that it is open.
- Keep well away from puddles around electrical equipment.
- Do not climb over machinery or enter rooms marked as empty, unfinished, or under survey.
- Mark your route with approved signs or digital tracking rather than improvised chalk or tape.
- Keep one hand free when using ladders or narrow walkways.
- Expect visibility to deteriorate suddenly when a vehicle, fan, or group of bats disturbs the mist.

The quarter has a strong sense of incompleteness. A passage may look permanent but have been built only recently, and an apparently solid wall may be fresh excavation awaiting support. If you see new cracks, falling grains, a change in water flow, unusual creaking, or a support that appears displaced, retreat and report it.

### Water, rock, and machinery

Assume that all water is contaminated until tested. It may contain industrial residue, concentrated minerals, animal waste, larvae, or organisms unfamiliar to your biology. Keep it out of your mouth, eyes, wounds, and clothing openings.

Do not touch:

- pump intakes or drainage screens,
- mineral crusts,
- exposed cables,
- corroded bolts,
- fresh rock faces,
- unidentified pipes,
- standing water near machinery,
- animals caught in screens,
- abandoned tools or containers.

The rock is especially deceptive. Freshly cut surfaces may look firm and clean but can still be under internal stress. The ceiling has not “settled” in the normal sense. Loose material can fall without a dramatic warning.

Stay outside machinery exclusion zones. Ask before approaching any moving belt, elevator, compressor, pump, drilling rig, or loading vehicle. The machinery is slow enough to invite carelessness but powerful enough to make a small mistake fatal.

### Animals

The animals are abundant, accustomed to the industrial habitat, and not necessarily aggressive. They are still a significant health and safety concern.

- Do not handle, feed, photograph at close range, or corner them.
- Avoid blocking their routes, especially around vents, drains, and roosting areas.
- Do not shine bright lights directly into bat clusters.
- Keep food sealed; human food may attract animals into work areas.
- Wear gloves and cover all cuts.
- Do not lean over drainage channels or put hands through grates.
- Treat every bite, scratch, splash, or contact with droppings as a medical incident.
- Avoid breathing dust from bat guano or dried drainage material. A respirator and eye protection are particularly important in roost areas.
- Report animals trapped in pumps or screens rather than attempting rescue yourself.

The slow, passive appearance of the wildlife should not reassure you too much. A frightened animal in a confined industrial space can cause a fall, jam equipment, or contaminate an injury.

### Health precautions

Before the visit:

- Have current vaccinations and any locally recommended prophylaxis.
- Tell the local medical staff about allergies, asthma, heat sensitivity, claustrophobia, ear problems, and immune conditions.
- Avoid entering with open wounds, compromised skin, or respiratory illness.
- If the settlement has a clinic, locate it before beginning the tour.

Afterward:

- Wash thoroughly with treated water.
- Disinfect boots, gloves, tools, and clothing before bringing them into another environment.
- Inspect ears, eyes, nose, skin folds, and small cuts for grit, fungal growth, or embedded mineral particles.
- Seek medical attention for fever, persistent cough, breathing difficulty, unusual rashes, swelling, gastrointestinal symptoms, or any animal bite or scratch.
- Quarantine biological samples unless the local authorities have a clear containment procedure. Do not bring moss, insects, water, mud, fish, guano, or “harmless” stones back across universes.

### Psychological preparation

The place will probably feel more unsettling than frightening at first. It is not a grand natural cavern but an unfinished settlement: yellow lamps in vapor, machinery beating somewhere out of sight, dripping water, fresh scars in rock, and animals occupying every neglected surface. The darkness will seem populated rather than empty.

Expect:

- A persistent sense of being underground even in brightly lit areas.
- Difficulty judging distance because mist turns lamps into halos.
- Sudden movements from bats, insects, amphibians, or passing equipment.
- Repetitive noise that makes it hard to locate individual sounds.
- Disorientation caused by short passages, identical industrial rooms, and layered repairs.
- A gradual loss of visual confidence as every wet surface begins to resemble every other one.

Use a buddy system. Keep verbal contact in noisy areas, and do not wander toward a light, sound, animal movement, or apparent opening without confirming what it is.

### Recommended itinerary

For a first visit, limit yourself to the maintained quarter:

1. Arrival and air-quality check.
2. Brief inspection of the canteen, clinic, and control office.
3. Guided route through the main workshop and warehouse.
4. Observation from a protected platform near the pumping station.
5. A short, authorized look at the boundary works.
6. Return before fatigue or heat stress becomes significant.

Do not make the outer survey lamps, the steaming basin, the raw excavation faces, or the deeper elevator shafts your first destination. They are precisely the places where the settlement’s temporary nature is most dangerous.

### The essential rule

Treat the quarter as a living worksite, not as a ruin and not as a tame cave. Water is slowly redesigning it, heat is working through it, animals are claiming it, and excavation is still making it larger. If a surface looks unused, it may be waiting for machinery, drainage, support, or wildlife. If a place looks dry, it may be dry only for the moment.

The safest visitor is quiet, well-equipped, escorted, hydrated, and willing to turn back early.
