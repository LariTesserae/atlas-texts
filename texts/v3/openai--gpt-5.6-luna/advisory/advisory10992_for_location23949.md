## Before departure

Treat this as a **hot, high-altitude wetland with geothermal and possibly artificial hazards**, not as a normal mountain landscape.

Do not assume that “ordinary daylight” means a breathable atmosphere. Bring a compact atmospheric analyzer capable of checking:

- Oxygen and carbon-dioxide concentration
- Methane and sulfur compounds
- Volatile organic toxins
- Pressure and humidity
- Sudden changes near springs, caves, vents, and depressions

Carry a lightweight emergency respirator with cartridges suitable for sulfur gases and methane-adjacent contamination, but do not rely on it as permanent breathing equipment. If the analyzer gives uncertain readings, leave. The absence of a strong smell is not reassuring; the description specifically suggests at least one gas with no familiar odor.

You should also obtain a biological and environmental quarantine kit. The local plants, water, fungi, and microbes may be harmless to native organisms but dangerous to a human immune system—or vice versa. Bring sealed sample containers, disposable gloves, a waste bag system, and enough food and water that you never need to consume local material.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for **heat, saturation, insects, and rough footing** simultaneously.

Bring:

- Loose, breathable, moisture-wicking clothing
- A broad-brimmed hat or hood
- Clothing that covers the arms and legs despite the heat
- Waterproof but ventilated outer layers for constant dripping and mist
- Quick-drying socks and several changes of footwear
- High-traction boots with broad soles for peat, moss, wet rock, and porous ground
- Gaiters to keep insects, water, and mud out
- Eye protection against mist, spores, and flying insects
- Thin gloves with good grip
- A head net or equivalent insect protection
- Antifungal and antiseptic supplies
- Blister treatment, rehydration salts, cooling packs, and heat-exhaustion medication
- A personal cooling system if available: evaporative neck wrap, phase-change vest, or compact powered fan

Humidity will prevent sweat from cooling you effectively. You may feel wet without realizing that you are overheating. Schedule frequent rests in shade, monitor one another for confusion and loss of coordination, and do not wait for thirst before drinking. Use electrolytes rather than consuming unlimited plain water.

The altitude and cloud exposure may also increase ultraviolet exposure even when the sky is cloudy. Use sunscreen and protect your eyes.

## Water and food

The water is visually inviting but should be considered **unsafe by default**.

Warm springs, glowing cracks, hidden aquifers, and channels near vents may contain:

- Dissolved sulfur or methane
- Heavy minerals
- Extreme microbial populations
- Pressure or temperature changes beneath apparently calm surfaces
- Biological agents adapted to the local environment

Bring all drinking water from your origin point, preferably in redundant containers. A normal filter is not enough. Even boiling may fail to remove minerals, gases, spores, or unusual chemistry. If resupply is unavoidable, use chemical and physical testing before treatment, and consider the water for emergency cooling only after testing it for skin-irritating compounds.

Do not eat fruit, flowers, seeds, insects, or anything that resembles a familiar food. Do not taste the translucent vines or touch the vapor-releasing cushions with bare skin. Their water may be highly concentrated with minerals or biologically active compounds.

## Navigation and route choice

Do not enter the valleys casually. The most attractive routes are likely to be the most saturated, unstable, and biologically dense.

Travel on exposed rock, established hard ground, or the visible upper slopes where possible. Avoid:

- Moss carpets that tremble underfoot
- Floating meadows and peat mats
- The edges of hidden channels
- Waterfalls and mist-filled clefts
- Black rocks that appear to be weeping
- Areas where vegetation is unusually black or grows toward a vent
- Depressions where gas could collect
- Unmapped caves and cracks
- Any ground that produces warmth, vibration, or a hollow response

Use poles or a probing staff, but do not probe deep into pools or cracks. A lightweight drone, lidar scanner, thermal camera, and ground-penetrating or vibration-sensing instrument would be more useful than ordinary binoculars. Visual identification is unreliable here: some animals imitate moss, basalt, reeds, or vertical vegetation, and some ground may be a thin living mat over moving water.

Mark your route with non-polluting, recoverable markers and maintain a constantly updated map. Do not depend on the clouds, sun angle, or distant ridges for orientation; the cloud rivers and fog may erase every landmark within minutes. Carry redundant navigation systems, including inertial navigation and short-range beacons. Keep a beacon at the landing or extraction point, because the terrain may change subtly while you are away.

Travel in a team of at least three. The wet air carries sound far but also distorts it with echoes, waterfalls, and returning animal calls. Do not assume that a voice or distress sound is coming from the apparent direction.

## Fauna

Assume every motionless object could be alive.

Before sitting, leaning against a tree, placing a pack on the ground, or reaching into leaf litter, inspect the area with thermal, motion, or polarized imaging if available. Wait before stepping into reeds or shallow water. Watch for:

- Moss that closes or shifts
- Basalt that changes position
- Tall reed-like shapes that do not move in the wind
- Tracks that stop abruptly
- Water disturbance without an obvious source
- Animals watching from shade or from above

The local animals do not sound frantic, but patient predators are more dangerous to a human than visibly aggressive ones. Avoid approaching any creature, even apparently herbivorous or amphibious ones. Do not run unless necessary; running may trigger pursuit, and wet ground makes falls likely.

Use sealed food containers and keep waste secured. Food odors may attract animals that are otherwise indifferent to you. At night, do not shine lights directly into animals’ eyes unless defending yourself; the buried systems and fluorescent watercourses may provide enough ambient marking to navigate without announcing your position everywhere.

## Geothermal and atmospheric hazards

The hottest gullies deserve the greatest caution. Sulfur, methane, hot vapor, and the unidentified cold gas may occur together or in changing concentrations. A clear, cool-looking depression can be more dangerous than a visibly steaming one.

Stay uphill and upwind of vents when possible. Never camp:

- In a gully
- Beside a spring
- Under a waterfall
- Near a cave or sealed shaft
- On the downwind side of volcanic peaks
- Near black vegetation or unusual animal absence

If animals suddenly leave an area, insects stop calling, or your analyzer changes even slightly, retreat immediately. Do not investigate the cause.

The heat also makes flash floods and slope failures particularly deceptive. Constant dripping can saturate soil without a visible storm. Clouds may spill over the escarpments and produce rain above you while the sky at your position remains bright. Avoid channels, waterfalls, and low basins when weather is changing. Camp on a stable, gently sloping shelf well above the obvious watercourses, with a clear route to higher ground.

## The old technology

Treat all structures as active, not ruined.

The bridge may be self-adjusting, but that does not make it safe for an unrecognized human. Before crossing:

1. Scan it for load limits, electrical or electromagnetic activity, and wet or condensed areas.
2. Cross one person at a time.
3. Follow the illuminated footing lines rather than improvising.
4. Keep packs secured and do not stop in the middle.
5. Maintain a tether if the span is fogged or visibility is poor.
6. Do not attach equipment, anchors, flags, or cables to it.

Do not enter the station, touch the embedded instruments, approach the antenna during a storm, or attempt to open the access shaft. The sealed membrane opens only for certain animals, which is a strong indication that it recognizes biological credentials or performs some form of environmental screening. Trying to bypass it could trigger defense mechanisms, contamination, or a change in the local water system.

The pillars, instruments, and buried geometric arcs should be photographed and surveyed from a distance. Do not excavate them, place metal nearby, pour water on them, or use them as camp fixtures. The technology has incorporated itself into the ecology; even a seemingly minor disturbance could redirect water, heat, or pressure over a very large area.

## The underground vibration

The taut-cable sound and the deep mechanical vibration are probably normal, but “normal” does not mean harmless.

Carry a vibration logger and note whether the sound changes with:

- Time of day
- Weather
- Water level
- Proximity to the geometric arcs
- Animal behavior
- Ground temperature

Avoid camping directly over strong vibration. If pools begin forming perfect rings, if fur or loose fibers stand up, or if the sound intensifies sharply, move away from the source and toward open, stable ground. Do not assume that the mechanism is communicating with you, but do assume that it can sense or respond to disturbances.

## Camp and night travel

Night will not be quiet. Continuous water noise, distant calls, echoes, and the deep machinery may impair sleep and make alarms difficult to hear. Bring active noise protection that still permits emergency signals, and use visual, vibration, or radio alarms in addition to audible ones.

Use a raised, enclosed shelter rather than a ground tent. It should be waterproof, insect-resistant, and capable of ventilation without admitting constant mist. Elevate packs and food, but do not hang anything from living trees or artificial structures. Keep the shelter away from water, vents, animal paths, and cliff edges.

Avoid night travel except for emergencies. The fluorescent aquifer markings may be beautiful but could draw you toward chemically dangerous water, unstable ground, or active machinery. If travel is unavoidable, use thermal and lidar navigation, stay with the group, and maintain a marked retreat route.

## Psychological preparation

The greatest danger may be complacency. The landscape feels ancient, calm, and unhurried. That atmosphere will encourage you to sit down, wander farther, touch things, and assume that any danger would announce itself dramatically.

It probably will not.

A slope can move by only a few centimeters and still open a fissure. A patient animal can remain still until you are within reach. A warm pool can be poisonous without bubbling. A machine can alter a river slowly enough that you notice only after your route has disappeared.

Go slowly, but not carelessly. Establish fixed check-in times, a maximum excursion distance, and clear abort conditions before arrival. Leave before exhaustion, before fog fills the lower ground, and before weather or machinery changes make the route uncertain.

The correct attitude is not fear. It is respect for a place in which geology, biology, weather, and technology have been operating together for a very long time—and in which a human visitor is the least adapted thing present.
