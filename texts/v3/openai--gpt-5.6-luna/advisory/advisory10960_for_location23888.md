Prepare for this as a **cold, high-altitude, submerged settlement**, not as an ordinary lakeside town. A person who simply swims down from the surface would be in serious danger before reaching the first inhabited structure.

## Before departure

### Acclimatize to the mountain

The lake lies above the tree line, so the surface air may already be thin, cold, and weather-prone. Spend time at altitude before attempting descent. Bring equipment for:

- Hypoxia and altitude sickness
- Sudden cloud, snow, and wind
- Very low surface temperatures
- Limited rescue access
- A long return journey if the lake becomes inaccessible

Do not assume that because the city is underwater, altitude will cease to matter. Your descent begins in thin mountain air, and any surface-support equipment must function there.

### Establish the water’s chemistry and biology

Have the lake tested before you enter. The water may be fresh, but it is not necessarily safe for human lungs, eyes, skin, or digestion. Check for:

- Unusual dissolved gases
- Toxic minerals from the dark stone basin
- Microorganisms adapted to the plant structures
- Irritating or invasive spores
- Animal venoms or defensive secretions
- Water chemistry incompatible with your equipment

Bring sealed food and water. Do not drink from the lake or eat anything growing in it until the residents have explicitly identified it as safe.

## Bring proper diving equipment

### Use a closed breathing system

Ordinary swimming gear is inadequate. You need either:

1. A small pressure-capable submersible that can reach the settlement without damaging the plant structures, or  
2. A full saturation-diving setup with a closed-circuit rebreather, redundant oxygen supply, and controlled ascent procedures.

A simple scuba tank may not be enough. The city is spread through the lake rather than sitting immediately beneath the surface, and the depth may be deceptive. The water continues below the inhabited basin into a much deeper, darker region. You should not descend toward that lower slope merely because the city remains visible above you.

Carry at least two independent breathing systems. Every system should have:

- Redundant regulators
- A manual emergency air supply
- Pressure and oxygen monitoring
- A way to locate your guide if visibility fails
- A means of communicating without shouting

Do not remove your breathing apparatus inside a chamber merely because it appears to contain air. Some plant chambers may hold stale, oxygen-poor, or chemically unusual gas. Others may be under pressure. Treat every enclosed space as an untested environment until the residents have verified it.

### Protect against cold

The water is cold throughout the settlement, and the inhabitants are adapted to conserving heat. Wear a properly fitted dry suit or equivalent insulating suit, with:

- Thick hood
- Insulated gloves
- Thermal boots
- Layered undergarments
- Face and neck protection
- A way to warm yourself during long stops

Do not plan on moving continuously to stay warm. Residents move slowly and may expect conversations, demonstrations, or waiting periods. Your thermal protection must work while you are stationary.

Have a heated refuge available at the surface or in a support vessel. Hypothermia will be a greater danger than many of the animals.

### Control buoyancy carefully

The city is built from living stalks, floating leaves, roots, and fragile woven bridges. Avoid bulky, unstable equipment that can snag on vegetation. Use a buoyancy system with very fine control, and carry a secure tether.

A loose fin kick, dangling hose, or sudden ascent could:

- Tear a roof leaf
- Break a young stem
- Unravel a bridge
- Stir silt into a household or spawning hollow
- Drive you into a root structure
- Startle a resident or animal

Use soft, rounded equipment wherever possible. Avoid exposed metal edges, hooks, cutting tools, or anything that can gouge living material.

## Plan for decompression and rescue

The inhabitants may be comfortable with the depth, but you are not. Arrange a complete decompression plan before entering the water. That plan should include:

- Maximum depth and bottom time
- Controlled ascent rates
- Decompression stops
- Surface oxygen
- A recompression chamber or equivalent medical facility
- A way to retrieve you if you become unconscious
- A hard turnaround time

Do not allow a guide’s confidence to replace your physiology. The residents may move through the settlement without considering pressure, but they may have different bodies, different lungs, or different habits of staged acclimatization.

If your equipment permits, remain at a depth-compatible pressure rather than repeatedly traveling between the surface and the city. Repeated ascents and descents will be especially dangerous.

## Enter only with a local guide

The settlement is deliberately difficult for outsiders to read. The routes are marked by details such as:

- Braided vine lines
- Hanging shell clusters
- Stone basins
- Particular leaf shapes
- Gaps in the vegetation
- Root bridges that appear to be part of the lake floor

Without a guide, you could pass through a neighborhood and fail to realize it was inhabited. Worse, you might follow an attractive but dead-end passage into a current, a spawning hollow, or an unprotected deep-water slope.

Travel in a small group. Keep your guide in sight and maintain a fixed order of movement. Agree beforehand on signs for:

- Stop
- Follow
- Return
- Animal nearby
- Structure damaged
- Breathing emergency
- Lost contact
- Surface immediately

Sound carries strangely through the water. The city is full of vibration—falls, roots, hollow stalks, shells, fish, and current—so ordinary speech may be useless. Rely on agreed hand signals, low-power lights, or a translated signaling device.

## Bring subdued lighting and navigation aids

There is daylight above, but it becomes diffuse and dim through the water. Deeper among the roots, colors disappear and shadows become nearly black.

Bring:

- A dimmable lamp with a wide beam
- A red or low-impact navigation mode
- Redundant position markers
- A short-range mapping system
- A physical line for emergency return
- A waterproof timepiece and depth gauge

Avoid flashing lights or strong beams into homes, flowering groves, or animal shelters. The residents may regard intense artificial light as aggressive, disorienting, or biologically harmful.

Do not rely solely on electronic navigation. The city’s channels may change, and living structures may interfere with signals. Your route should be marked in a reversible way, or you should remain attached to the local route line.

## Observe the city’s laws as ecological laws

These are not merely customs. The settlement depends on the living architecture remaining healthy. Assume that every structural plant is part of somebody’s home, bridge, food system, or family responsibility.

You must not:

- Cut, break, scrape, or sample a living stalk
- Tie anything to a plant without permission
- Touch roots or foundations casually
- Enter a flowering structure
- Block a channel
- Disturb silt around old foundations
- Collect shells, fish, flowers, tools, or stone
- Feed or chase animals
- Fish in a tended spawning hollow
- Walk across a bridge without knowing whether it is mature
- Open a stem, leaf chamber, or air pocket
- Remove a tool or relic from the central court

Bring no saws, knives, drills, anchors, or construction materials unless specifically requested. Even a small cut may cause a plant to bleed or alter an entire neighborhood over years.

If you damage anything, report it immediately. Do not conceal it or attempt an improvised repair. Their repairs are patient, biological, and seasonal; the correct response may be to dismantle a structure before decay spreads.

## Dress and behave for the inhabitants

Their clothing is designed for insulation and movement in water, not for dry-land conventions. Do not treat their appearance or the open, partially submerged houses as primitive or indecent. Their architecture has developed around water so thoroughly that “inside” and “outside” have different meanings.

Move slowly. Sudden movement may frighten animals and can damage living structures. Do not stand in a passage or channel merely to look around. If you need to stop, ask where to hold position.

Expect long pauses. A new arch, a widened root, or an abandoned room may represent decades of work. Do not ask why the city has not been expanded, modernized, or made denser. Its residents may understand growth as maintenance rather than construction.

## Bring appropriate gifts

Useful gifts would be things that do not impose a new technology or ecological burden:

- Carefully made waterproof containers
- Medical supplies, if approved
- Noninvasive measuring tools
- Durable fiber, bone, or shell-working materials
- Records or images from other worlds
- Knowledge exchanged through demonstration rather than extraction

Avoid offering metal tools, seeds, living organisms, chemical substances, engines, explosives, or brightly colored synthetic materials. Their culture uses little metal by choice or necessity, and introducing a foreign material into their biological infrastructure could be disruptive.

Do not take souvenirs. The suspended tools in the central court are not decorations; they are history.

## Be prepared for the animals

Most animals will probably avoid you, but you should not assume that unfamiliar behavior means hostility.

Particular cautions:

- Silver schools may obscure your guide or entangle equipment.
- The long-bodied grazers may be harmless but large enough to crush a bridge or pin a diver.
- The broad-winged descending creatures may create strong currents as they rise.
- Blind scavengers can lift sudden clouds of silt and may emerge from beneath foundations.
- Fish striking walls may panic if pursued.
- Animals around flowering groves may be territorial or essential pollinators.

Never shine a light directly into an animal’s eyes, corner anything in a channel, or use your hands to investigate a silt cloud. If the water suddenly clouds beneath you, stop moving and let the guide identify the cause.

## Expect sensory disorientation

The city will not feel quiet. The falls above will be a continuous low vibration through your body. Hollow stalks will knock together. Leaves will clap in currents. Pebbles will move along the lake floor. Some sounds will seem to come from every direction.

You may also lose your sense of orientation. The surface is a distant bright blur, the bottom is patterned with roots and ruins, and many structures float or lean. Keep a depth reference and a route line. Do not instinctively swim toward a light unless you know whether it is the surface, a lamp, or an opening between leaves.

The city is sparse enough that several seconds of empty water can separate one living structure from the next. Do not interpret emptiness as abandonment. A dark patch beneath a leaf may be a room, a nursery, or a route used by residents who can see you more clearly than you can see them.

## A sensible first visit

Your first expedition should be short and conservative:

1. Acclimatize at the mountain lake.
2. Meet the residents at the surface or through a protected submersible.
3. Allow them to inspect your equipment.
4. Enter with a guide along one established route.
5. Visit only a public structure, preferably the central court.
6. Remain in a shallow, well-known section of the settlement.
7. Do not enter the deep outer lake.
8. Make no physical contact with the architecture without invitation.
9. Leave before you become cold, tired, or fascinated enough to ignore procedure.
10. Undergo a full medical and equipment inspection after ascent.

The most important preparation is psychological: do not arrive expecting to explore a ruin. This is a living city whose streets, roofs, walls, and boundaries are all in various stages of growth. You are not entering an underwater park. You are entering an organism-supported community in a hostile environment, where patience is a building material and careless curiosity can become structural damage.
