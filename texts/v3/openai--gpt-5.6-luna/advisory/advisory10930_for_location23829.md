## What to expect

Prepare for a place that is neither conventionally flooded nor conventionally dry. Water occupies streets, buildings, shafts, and the air above them, but its behavior is inconsistent. It may flow uphill, hang motionless, rise from a pool, or carry objects sideways. Ordinary assumptions about gravity, drainage, weather, and falling objects will be unreliable.

The safest mindset is: **you are entering a living construction site built on unstable geology, inside a partially suspended body of water.** Move slowly, attach yourself to things, and never assume that a surface is where it appears to be.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Wear clothing that remains functional when wet and does not snag easily:

- Close-fitting, quick-drying base layers.
- A short waterproof outer layer or waxed oversmock.
- Flexible footwear with excellent grip on wet basalt, wood, and plant-covered surfaces.
- Gloves with good tactile control.
- Eye protection against mineral spray, splintered stone, and drifting vegetation.
- A hood or close-fitting head covering; upward-falling rain and suspended droplets are normal.
- Avoid loose scarves, trailing coats, long straps, and dangling ornaments.

The water is warm, so cold-weather immersion gear is unnecessary unless you intend to explore exposed ridges or mineral flats. The greater risks are abrasion, infection, entanglement, and losing your belongings to abnormal currents.

Bring several changes of dry clothing and keep them in genuinely sealed containers. “Waterproof” bags may not be sufficient if water can approach from below, above, or sideways. Use waxed cloth, sealed ceramic, treated leather, or layered containers with independent closures.

## Personal flotation and attachment

Do not rely on ordinary swimming ability. The water is cloudy, vertically extensive, and may not obey normal buoyancy.

Bring:

- A broad, low-profile flotation harness.
- A retractable safety line or sturdy waist tether.
- Several soft, non-cutting loops for attaching yourself to rails, bridges, or guide ropes.
- A compact cutting tool in case aquatic plants or flexible walkways entangle you.
- A clearly marked personal buoy or float.

Use the tether selectively. Attach to fixed, load-bearing structures—not plants, temporary bridges, hanging platforms, or anything that appears to be growing out of the stone. A line that is safe in ordinary water may become dangerous if the water begins moving sideways or upward. Keep slack in it, as the inhabitants do.

Do not carry heavy emergency weights. A stone, metal tool, or weighted belt may descend, drift, or later rise unpredictably. You want to be able to release everything quickly.

## Breathing and water safety

Even if the exposed streets have breathable air, carry redundant breathing equipment for short periods:

- A sealed respirator or simple diving mask.
- A compact air reserve.
- A mouth and nose covering against spores, algae, mineral dust, and plant fibers.

Do not assume that every body of water contains the same atmosphere. The suspended ceiling may be water with a different pressure, chemistry, or gas content than the lower channels. Treat each transition—street to flooded room, flooded room to overhead surface, warm pool to mineral vent—as a separate environment.

Bring your own drinking water. The local water may be warm and apparently clean but still contain unusual minerals, algae, parasites, or dissolved substances. Use sealed rations until local food and water have been tested. Do not drink from cisterns, vents, roof gardens, or open channels without permission and treatment.

A compact filtration and sterilization kit is essential, though ordinary sterilization may not neutralize local biology. Inhabitants should be able to tell you which waters are safe.

## Navigation

Do not navigate by distance. Navigate by **height, landmarks, and human infrastructure**.

Learn the local levels before leaving your lodging:

- Lower Salt
- Middle Gardens
- Black Rise
- Roofwater
- Any district-specific names for ridges, vents, channels, and safe crossings

Ask for a guide. The city’s routes can change subtly over the course of a season, and a road that looks continuous may become a channel, stair, shaft, or dead end. Carry a physical route board, knotted cord, or waterproof map rather than relying exclusively on instruments. Compasses may be disturbed by the geological activity and imported ironwork; electronic equipment, if you possess it, may fail in mineral-rich water or under the suspended sea.

Mark your own path only with removable, biodegradable markers. Do not paint walls, cut living plants, or drive stakes into the ground. The people are accustomed to provisional construction, but they will not appreciate a visitor damaging a wall or confusing a local route.

The bells indicate elevation and district relationships. Listen to them before attempting to interpret them. A bell heard from underwater, through a bridge, or across a flooded court may seem to come from the wrong direction.

## Movement

Move at the speed of the city.

Test every surface before committing your weight. Fresh basalt can be sharp, hot, porous, or hollow beneath a thin crust. Mineral deposits may look solid while remaining fragile. Wooden walkways may be designed to flex and should not be braced or “corrected” by a visitor.

On stairs, use the handrail but do not pull hard against it. A stair may gain or lose a step, or tilt upward, without anyone treating the event as an emergency.

When crossing water:

1. Secure loose equipment.
2. Check for plant growth and submerged openings.
3. Ask whether the water is flowing, rising, or merely appearing to flow.
4. Keep one hand or tether connected to a known structure.
5. Cross in pairs if possible.
6. Do not jump.

Jumping is particularly dangerous. A thrown or falling object may rise, stop, drift sideways, or enter the water above. The same may happen to you.

Do not stand beneath a suspended surface, roof garden, or apparently motionless stream without asking what normally happens there. Water that has remained in place for years may begin moving without warning, and a harmless upward drip can become a vertical current.

## Geological hazards

The ground is still forming. This means hazards may be slow enough to ignore but powerful enough to destroy a structure.

Watch for:

- New black stone appearing in floors or plazas.
- Hairline cracks that widen toward vents.
- Warm walls or rapidly drying surfaces.
- Tilting doorframes and bridges.
- Vibrations accompanied by falling mineral flakes.
- Sudden changes in water level or direction.
- Steam, bubbling, or dark liquid emerging from fresh rock.

Do not shelter in low plazas, against raw cliff faces, or beside a new vent. If the inhabitants begin quietly moving boards, tools, or household goods uphill, follow them without waiting for an alarm. Their lack of panic does not mean the event is harmless; it means they have learned that frantic movement is usually more dangerous than gradual relocation.

Never block a newly forming opening merely because it is inconvenient. A hole that is covered today may be the pressure release preventing a larger rupture tomorrow.

## Plants and animals

The vegetation is not necessarily hostile, but it is active, dense, and integrated with the city.

Avoid touching:

- Broad translucent leaves unless invited.
- Roots that click, tighten, or retract.
- Floating mats beneath the upper water.
- Fruit from unfamiliar trees.
- Vines crossing doors, ladders, or bridges.

Some plants may be cultivated food, medicine, building material, or structural support. Others may be poisonous, adhesive, abrasive, or capable of closing around small animals. Ask before cutting, moving, or harvesting anything. Even apparently abandoned gardens may belong to a household or district.

The local animals are few and quiet. Do not pursue the silver creatures under stairs or approach the amphibious birds on rooftops. Their silence is not evidence that they are tame. If a large shadow passes across the suspended water, get under a solid structure and wait. Do not signal, throw objects, or attempt to attract it.

Bring your own medical kit, including antiseptic, wound dressings, anti-inflammatory medication, and treatment for fungal or waterborne infection. Basalt cuts and plant abrasions are likely to become contaminated quickly.

## Light, fire, and technology

Expect dim, green-gold illumination and long periods when the city darkens without an obvious cause. Bring protected lamps, but use them sparingly and ask where flame is permitted. Oil lamps are common, which suggests that fire is possible, but waxed cloth, dry fibers, roof gardens, and enclosed air pockets may make fire unusually dangerous.

Use lamps with:

- Clay or metal shields.
- Secure handles.
- Low heat.
- A way to extinguish them instantly.

Do not use bright flashes casually. Light may travel through the water in strange ways and could disturb animals, reveal private interiors, or be interpreted as a signal.

If bringing electrical or advanced equipment, seal it completely and expect interference from heat, minerals, moisture, and unstable local physics. Do not assume that a device with a pressure rating or waterproof housing has been tested against water flowing uphill.

## Food, shelter, and supplies

Arrange lodging in an elevated, inhabited structure—not an isolated tower, roof, or abandoned upper floor. The best shelter will have:

- Multiple exits at different heights.
- A dry storage chamber.
- Reliable access to fresh water.
- A roof or platform that can be reached if the lower levels flood.
- Flexible rather than rigid construction.
- Local guide ropes and visible elevation markers.

Keep your supplies divided between several containers and several elevations. Do not store everything in one pack or one room. A sudden shift may make the lower level inaccessible while leaving the upper level untouched.

Carry compact food that requires little cooking. Local cooking facilities may be on rafts, in wet kitchens, or near unstable vents. Rations should be individually sealed and attached to you with breakaway loops.

## Social preparation

The residents are likely to regard the city’s incompleteness as ordinary. Do not describe it as ruined, malformed, or unfinished in a contemptuous way. Their buildings are provisional because the world is provisional; this is competence, not failure.

Before traveling, find a local sponsor or guide and learn:

- Which bridges are private.
- Which gardens may be harvested.
- Which bells indicate danger rather than time.
- Which waters are drinkable.
- Which surfaces are sacred, dangerous, or newly forming.
- How visitors are expected to announce themselves.
- What objects must not be carried into homes or markets.

Offer practical assistance rather than imposing unfamiliar solutions. Do not “improve” their flexible bridges by making them rigid. Do not drain a flooded room, straighten a tilted wall, seal a vent, or remove a plant without asking. Their structures are designed to accommodate change; your intervention may eliminate the movement that keeps them safe.

Speak softly. Sound carries badly and unpredictably through the water, and people may hear your voice from a different direction or level. If someone calls your name, do not immediately follow the voice. Confirm their location visually or through a local signal.

## Emergency rules

Memorize a few simple rules before arrival:

1. **If the water changes direction, stop moving.**
2. **If stone begins emerging, move uphill and away from the freshest surface.**
3. **If a bridge tightens, twists, or loses slack, leave it immediately.**
4. **If plants begin moving together, get out of the channel.**
5. **If the city darkens suddenly, stay where you are until you can identify a local guide or light.**
6. **If an object falls upward, do not look directly beneath it.**
7. **If a local person begins calmly dismantling something, help or get out of the way.**
8. **Never enter the suspended sea alone.**

The most important emergency supply is a dependable relationship with someone who knows the city. In this place, maps become obsolete, but experienced inhabitants can recognize the difference between a harmless rearrangement and the beginning of a district-wide change.

## The right mental preparation

Expect disorientation. Verticality is the defining feature: above may be another street, below may be a roof, and a doorway may lead not into a room but into water, a garden, a stairwell, or open depth. Your sense of “inside,” “outside,” “up,” and “down” will repeatedly fail.

Do not rush to restore familiar categories. Let the city teach you its own.

The safest traveler will be patient, lightly equipped, visibly respectful, and willing to abandon a route without resentment. This is not a place to conquer, survey rapidly, or force into a stable shape. It is a place to pass through while everything—stone, water, plants, buildings, and roads—is still deciding what it will become.
