## Overall assessment

Treat this as a **live, recently excavated geothermal-industrial site**, not as a ruin. It is structurally unstable, thermally dangerous, electrically wet, and still performing automated operations without supervision. Its most serious hazards may not be visible: oxygen displacement, toxic or corrosive gases, pressure releases, rock movement, and machinery that reacts to changing water levels.

An ordinary human should not enter alone, casually, or without assuming that the atmosphere is unsafe.

## Before entering

### Establish whether entry is possible at all

You should have:

- A reason to enter that justifies the risk.
- At least two other people outside the chamber, able to retrieve you.
- A continuous communication link with the surface or a safe staging area.
- A prearranged extraction time. If you do not report by that time, the team acts rather than waiting.
- A map of the three exits, with the understanding that the exits may change or become blocked.
- A way to mark your route physically. Electronic mapping may fail in dense metal, heat, or electrical interference.

Do not assume that the chamber’s continued operation means that it is safe. It may be operating unattended because its original operators died, evacuated, or ceased to be relevant.

### Test the atmosphere

Bring professional confined-space equipment:

- Oxygen meter.
- Carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide detectors.
- Flammable-gas detector.
- Carbon dioxide detector.
- Instruments for steam, ammonia, chlorine, or other industrial gases if the machinery’s purpose is unknown.
- A calibrated temperature and humidity monitor.

A normal respirator is not enough if the oxygen level is low or an unknown gas is present. Bring supplied-air equipment or self-contained breathing apparatus, with enough duration for the planned visit and an emergency retreat.

The chamber’s heat, dampness, and hidden water make oxygen displacement especially plausible. Steam and carbon dioxide may collect in low places. Hydrogen sulfide could be present even if the smell is weak or absent; do not rely on your nose.

### Bring equipment suited to heat and water

Wear:

- A helmet with a face shield or sealed goggles.
- Flame-resistant, nonmelting outer clothing.
- Insulating gloves, but not gloves so bulky that you cannot operate valves or climb.
- Waterproof, electrically insulating boots with aggressive soles.
- Knee and elbow protection for crawlspaces and rough rock.
- A harness and fall-arrest line where ladders, open grates, or the extraction column make a fall possible.
- Hearing protection that still allows radio communication and alarms to be heard.
- A cooling vest or other heat-management system, if compatible with the protective gear.

Carry water and electrolyte replacement, not merely drinking water. In this environment, dehydration and heat illness may develop before you realize how much you are sweating. Do not count the surrounding water as safe to drink, regardless of how clear it looks.

Use equipment rated for hot, wet industrial environments. Ordinary cameras, phones, battery packs, and flashlights may fail or become ignition hazards.

### Prepare for equipment loss

Carry redundant:

- Lamps, preferably helmet-mounted and separately hand-held.
- Radios, with a wired or physical backup.
- Route markers.
- Gas detectors.
- Batteries kept in sealed, heat-rated containers.
- A mechanical watch or timer.
- A compact first-aid and burn kit.
- A cutting tool, pry bar, and escape line.
- A low-tech whistle or impact alarm, though sound may be poorly transmitted through the machinery.

Keep electronics away from dripping condensate and do not place batteries on hot metal. Moisture and mineral deposits can create conductive paths where none are obvious.

## On entry

### Spend time observing before moving

At the threshold, stop and record:

- Air readings at floor level, chest level, and near the ceiling.
- Temperature and humidity.
- The timing of pump cycles, lamp dimming, water surges, and pressure-line shudders.
- Which pipes, grates, tanks, and walkways are visibly moving or vibrating.
- The direction of air movement.
- The locations of wet floors, fresh rubble, and active leaks.

Watch the room through at least one complete operating cycle if possible. The chamber has rhythms. Learn when the reservoir fills, when the lamps dim, when vapor increases, and when the machinery changes configuration. Do not begin exploring during a pressure or water event merely because the room seems quiet.

### Assume every surface can be unstable

The fresh excavation is still settling. A crack that advances only fractions of an inch is still an active warning. Avoid standing:

- Beneath the spreading ceiling fracture.
- Beside the cracked concrete facing.
- Under suspended cable bundles or pipe racks.
- On grates over unknown voids.
- Against sealed pressure lines.
- Near the base of the extraction column.
- On freshly fallen rubble, which may be supporting something above it.

Test each walkway before committing your weight. Look for bright new metal, flexing welds, mineral crusts hiding corrosion, and bolts that have shifted. Keep one hand free for balance and never use hot pipes as handholds.

Do not lean over the central opening. The ladder around the extraction column may be hot, slippery, inadequately secured, or exposed to upward steam and pressure. The amber light inside the column is not evidence that its interior is accessible.

### Keep away from active systems

Do not:

- Turn pressure wheels.
- Open valves.
- Reset electrical panels.
- Disconnect cables.
- Remove inspection covers.
- Block vents or drains.
- Move numbered rubble.
- Operate the crane.
- Touch the translucent stalks or lichen.
- Stand beneath a dripping flange.
- Put hands into warm water or cracks to determine their depth.

The chamber is a network of compensating systems. A small intervention could redirect hot water, disable cooling, release pressure, or cause a cascade through machinery that is currently balancing itself.

If a control must be touched, document its position first and change only one thing at a time. In most cases, the correct action is to leave it alone.

### Treat the containers as hazardous

The dates and frost patterns suggest that the containers may hold materials with unusual thermal behavior or active refrigeration. One has apparently been opened and resealed from the inside. That is an extreme warning.

Maintain distance from all containers. Do not:

- Open one.
- Tap one to hear what is inside.
- Lean against one.
- Trust its temperature.
- Assume an apparently empty container is harmless.
- Release a seal because it looks damaged.

Mark the opened container and withdraw. If it moves, vents, warms rapidly, frosts over, or produces sound, do not investigate at close range.

### Do not trust the water

The clear flooded tunnel is not necessarily safe. It may conceal:

- A sudden rise in level.
- Electrified equipment or conductive mineral deposits.
- A drop, sump, or broken floor.
- Extreme temperature changes.
- Gas pockets.
- A passage that has been sealed by newly formed stone.

Measure depth and temperature from the edge, and use a probe or line rather than entering immediately. Since the tunnel ends at smooth new stone, it is probably not a useful route unless it provides an emergency retreat or contains information.

The lower stairwell is more dangerous. Intensifying heat, wet iron odor, and a sweet mineral smell suggest hot gases, geothermal fluids, or dissolved compounds. Do not descend without a supplied-air system, thermal protection, a fixed lifeline, and a confirmed return path. The stairwell may lead toward pressure rather than toward a larger safe chamber.

The upper hatch is the most promising route but should not be forced. Its cold condensation and pale hinges imply a different environment beyond it, possibly a pressure boundary. It may open only from the other side or respond to pressure. Do not hammer, drill, heat, or pry it until you know whether it is retaining gas, water, or structural load. If it opens unexpectedly, retreat from the doorway and allow the pressure to equalize while monitoring the air.

## While inside

Use a buddy system and maintain constant accountability. Nobody should disappear around the extraction column, enter a crawlspace, or descend the stairs without an explicit check-in.

Keep the visit short. The chamber’s heat and humidity will defeat cooling through evaporation, and protective clothing will make the problem worse. Watch for:

- Headache, confusion, nausea, unusual calmness, or loss of coordination.
- Rapid breathing or a racing pulse.
- Cramps and weakness.
- Blurred vision.
- Difficulty judging distances.
- A sudden absence of sound.
- Gas alarms, even if they stop immediately.

The “almost silent” intervals are especially important. If the lamps dim and the room’s sound seems muffled, leave the lower areas and move toward the safest exit. That may indicate the reservoir filling, altered ventilation, or a change in pressure. Do not interpret it as a convenient quiet period for exploration.

Keep clear of the center during water-rise events. The extraction column, floor opening, and surrounding ring are likely where pressure and flow are concentrated. If water begins appearing through cracks, if the ceiling fracture changes, or if a pressure line shudders, stop, turn around, and withdraw.

## Emergency rules

Leave immediately if:

- Any gas reading is abnormal.
- Oxygen falls outside safe limits.
- The floor begins to tremble continuously.
- Fresh stone falls from above.
- Water rises faster than expected.
- A pump or fan stops.
- A lamp sequence changes unexpectedly.
- A container vents, shifts, or changes temperature.
- The upper hatch moves.
- A machine begins reconfiguring in a way you did not observe during the initial cycle.
- Communication with the outside is lost.

Do not wait to confirm the cause. The chamber has few open spaces, so a minor failure can become an impassable route quickly.

If a person collapses, do not rush in after them. A victim may be unconscious from gas exposure, heat, or oxygen deficiency, and a rescuer without breathing equipment may become the second victim. Use a retrieval line or supplied-air rescue procedure.

If steam or hot water erupts, get low only if the air is known to be breathable; otherwise move toward the tested exit while staying out of channels and depressions. Protect the face and eyes, and do not run across wet metal. Electrical and slip hazards will worsen during a flood.

## After leaving

Quarantine clothing, tools, water samples, and mineral fragments. Do not bring them directly into another habitat. The lichen-like growths and pressure-responsive stalks may be harmless, chemically active, infectious, or not biologically alive at all. Fresh deposits may also carry corrosive or radioactive minerals.

Decontaminate boots and equipment, record all atmospheric readings and machine cycles, and inspect for burns or chemical irritation after cooling down. Heat injury can continue to develop after leaving.

The safest expedition plan is therefore:

1. Establish remote monitoring and rescue capability.
2. Test the atmosphere before crossing the threshold.
3. Observe several cycles without touching anything.
4. Survey only the stable upper walkways.
5. Avoid the lower stair, extraction opening, containers, and active fractures.
6. Approach the upper hatch only after studying its pressure behavior.
7. Leave at the first unexplained change.

The chamber is not waiting to be explored. It is still being formed, and its machinery is still trying to control that formation. A visitor should behave less like an adventurer in a ruin and more like a hazardous-environment inspector entering a reactor that has forgotten its operators.
