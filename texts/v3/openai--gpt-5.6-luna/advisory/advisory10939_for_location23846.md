## Preparation for the basalt basin

Treat this place as a high-altitude volcanic environment combined with an actively developing physical anomaly. It is not obviously malicious, but it is not safe in the ordinary sense. The main danger is not attack; it is assuming that familiar rules—distance, gravity, weather, water, shelter, and stable terrain—still apply.

Do not visit casually or alone.

### 1. Arrive with a way to leave

Your first priority should be a reliable return method that does not depend on walking back to a fixed landmark.

Bring:

- A return gate, beacon, extraction device, or other means of departure that can function through altered geometry.
- At least two independent navigation references: one technological, one physical or biological if possible.
- A timer that does not rely on local astronomical cycles.
- A continuous tether or line extending back to your arrival point, but do not assume the tether will remain geometrically honest. It may become longer, shorter, warm, cold, or appear to lead somewhere other than its anchor.
- A sealed emergency shelter or extraction capsule.
- A partner or remote team able to monitor you.
- A written and recorded mission log. The place changes, and your own memory of distances may become unreliable.

Before entering, establish a strict abort protocol. For example: if the extraction beacon cannot be seen, if the basin’s rim changes shape, if any person becomes separated from their shadow, or if a route takes substantially longer than expected, stop exploring and withdraw.

Do not set your only exit beside the pool, the tower, or the arches. Their surrounding relationships are unstable.

### 2. Prepare for severe altitude and intense sun

The basin is above a cloud layer, receiving nearly vertical sunlight. Even if the air feels hot, the elevation may produce hypoxia, rapid dehydration, and dangerous temperature swings.

Bring:

- Supplemental oxygen, preferably with enough reserve for a delayed evacuation.
- A pulse oximeter and basic altitude medical equipment.
- A full-face respirator or filtered breathing mask for steam, mineral dust, and possible volcanic gases.
- Broad-spectrum eye protection with strong UV filtering.
- A brimmed helmet or head covering.
- High-SPF sunscreen, lip protection, and gloves.
- Several liters of water per person, in sealed containers.
- Electrolytes and high-energy food.
- Lightweight, breathable outer clothing for the day.
- Insulating layers, hat, and gloves for night.
- A heat-reflective emergency bivouac that can also be sealed against fumes.

Do not drink from the seams, channels, or pool. The water may be chemically contaminated, biologically active, or governed by a nonstandard cycle. The fact that it looks clear is meaningless. Avoid even washing open wounds in it.

The hot wet basalt may generate steam suddenly. Keep your face away from fissures and do not kneel or place bare hands on dark stone.

### 3. Wear protection suited to the ground

The young rock will be sharp, brittle, and unstable. Its glassy bubbles and metallic planes may cut footwear or produce sharp fragments under pressure.

Use:

- Rigid, high-ankle boots with replaceable soles.
- Cut-resistant gaiters.
- Puncture-resistant gloves.
- Eye protection, preferably with a face shield for climbing or examining fractures.
- A climbing helmet.
- Knee and elbow protection if crossing rough ground.
- Lightweight traction devices that can be removed quickly if they interfere with local magnetic or gravitational effects.

Do not trust apparently solid surfaces. A clean plane may be a thin glass shell over a void, and a mineral sheet may grow across a puddle only to dissolve when it reflects the sky. Test every unfamiliar surface with a probe, not with your full weight.

Carry a long nonconductive staff for probing cracks and checking the stability of warm silver tracks. Do not drive metal stakes into the rock unless necessary; the basin may respond to weight, heat, or electrical conductivity.

### 4. Assume the geometry is non-Euclidean

The distance anomaly is the most important operational hazard.

A route that looks short may not be short. A familiar landmark may grow larger as you walk away from it. Three locations can each be individually reachable while the distances between them remain mutually inconsistent. Ordinary maps will be of limited use.

Before moving far from camp:

1. Establish a small, clearly marked safe zone.
2. Measure routes in several directions, not just one.
3. Record travel time, step count, compass bearing, apparent size of landmarks, and return time.
4. Photograph or scan the same objects from multiple positions.
5. Have two people walk the same route separately and compare their results.
6. Mark paths with several independent signals: visible markers, radio pings, heat, sound, and perhaps chemical or optical tags.
7. Recheck every marker on the return journey. A marker may still be present but no longer indicate the same route.

Use a buddy system with a taut line only as one layer of protection. Keep visual contact when possible, and maintain voice or radio contact even when standing close together. A person who appears to be walking toward you may actually be increasing the distance between you.

Never split the party. Never chase a moving object, unusual light, shadow, or sound beyond the agreed survey boundary.

If a landmark becomes larger while you move away from it, stop immediately. Do not attempt to “correct” your course by intuition. Return along the exact sequence of positions that brought you there, or activate extraction.

### 5. Keep clear of the three anomalous structures

#### The leaning tower

The tower has no visible openings or joints, shifts its shadow independently of the sun, and appears responsive to distant weather. It may be a sensor, antenna, organism, or incomplete machine.

Maintain a generous exclusion radius. Do not touch it, lean against it, place equipment on it, or stand in its shadow. Photograph it from a fixed point and monitor changes remotely. Keep sensitive equipment shielded or powered down nearby until you know whether the moving lines are electromagnetic, thermal, or something else.

If the tower’s internal lines brighten, if its vibration becomes audible, or if its shadow points toward a member of your party, withdraw.

#### The pale arches

The arches distort droplets and retain spoken sounds, releasing them later from elsewhere. This suggests that they store or redirect more than sound.

Do not enter the frame unless deliberate contact is part of the mission. Do not speak inside it. Do not transmit radio signals through it. Do not place your hand, camera, rope, or beacon beneath its floating floor.

If you must examine it, use a remote instrument and record the time and direction of every sound it produces. Assume that a delayed voice may be an actual recording, a displacement, or something imitating a member of the team.

#### The pool

The pool is the highest-risk feature. Its depth is indeterminate, its level follows the sun by an unknown rule, and something large moves beneath it. At dusk, the entire body of water can rise into the air, revealing a jointed luminous entity or mechanism.

Stay well back from the pool’s edge. Do not touch the water, the shells, or the damp shelf. Do not place instruments in the water. Do not stand between the pool and the sun at times when its level is changing. The suspended column may produce altered gravity, pressure, or geometry.

If the water begins to tremble, withdraw beyond the marked exclusion zone immediately. Do not wait to observe the submerged shape more clearly.

### 6. Be cautious around life and apparent life

The basin’s plants and animals may not be native organisms in the ordinary sense.

Avoid:

- The circular plants around vents.
- Plants that float above the ground.
- The glassy creatures on wet stone.
- The tiny shells around the pool.
- Any plant or animal that changes position when unobserved.
- Any creature that casts an unusual shadow or leaves a trail of dry stone.

Do not collect samples without sealed, remotely operated equipment. Do not eat anything. Do not touch suspended droplets or allow them onto skin. The water-bearing organisms may carry unfamiliar chemistry, spores, parasites, or information-like effects.

The insects do not appear to fly continuously; they select points and appear between them. Do not try to swat, trap, or follow one. Their movement may reveal that local transitions are not continuous.

The black-winged bird should be treated as a moving anomaly. The bird arrives after its cry, while its shadow lingers independently. Do not track the shadow, stand beneath it, or assume that the bird and shadow remain causally connected.

### 7. Treat the silver tracks as active systems

The warm silver lines are not ordinary paths. They respond to weight and appear to recognize footsteps.

Do not walk on them until they have been remotely tested. If unavoidable, cross them at right angles with minimal contact and no heavy equipment. Observe whether the pulse is merely thermal, electrical, gravitational, or perceptual.

Never use a silver track as your main route. It may be a guide, a memory, a transport system, or an unfinished boundary. A pulse beneath the skin is a warning to stop, not an invitation to continue.

### 8. Establish a conservative camp

Camp on high, stable basalt away from:

- The pool and all drainage channels.
- Fissures, vents, and steaming surfaces.
- The ring wall and recently fractured rock.
- Silver tracks.
- The structures.
- Plant circles and areas where gravity seems irregular.
- Any location where droplets remain suspended.

Use a low, anchored shelter with no loose fabric or dangling lines. Keep equipment in sealed containers. Mark the camp with objects that have different physical properties—light, sound, heat, and color—because one marking system may be altered or ignored.

Do not camp in a hollow. Mist may collect there, and the basin’s water and gravity anomalies appear strongest in depressions.

The night is not necessarily safer. The rock retains heat, the air cools rapidly, the tower vibrates, and the stars include objects that may not be stars. Keep watches. Avoid staring at low, oversized lights for prolonged periods, especially if they appear to move or respond.

### 9. Manage sound and communication carefully

The basin is quiet enough that small sounds may be significant. Record the normal background before conducting any experiment.

Use radios with time-stamped transmissions, but retain line-of-sight signals as well. A voice may arrive late or from the wrong direction. Confirm all verbal instructions by challenge-and-response codes that change periodically.

Do not call out casually near the arches. Do not imitate the bird. Do not answer a voice unless you can verify the speaker visually and by code. A delayed recording may use a familiar voice without representing a present person.

If the ground-concussion from below becomes stronger or more frequent, stop work. It may be geological activity, machinery, or a large-scale change in the basin.

### 10. Plan the visit around the sun

A first visit should be short and divided into observation periods:

- **Morning:** establish camp, test air and ground, map only the immediate safe zone.
- **Late morning:** perform remote observations and sample the geometry of routes.
- **Near noon:** avoid the pool, as its level is highest. Use the period for sheltered measurements.
- **Afternoon:** cease expansion of the survey area. Recheck all markers and prepare for withdrawal.
- **Late afternoon and dusk:** do not explore. The basin’s green radiance, rising pool, and sharply outlined living things indicate a major transition.
- **Night:** remain in camp or leave the location entirely. Do not attempt first-contact experiments after dark.

The first expedition should ideally depart before the sun reaches the rim. Dusk is an observation opportunity only for a remote probe, not for an ordinary person standing in the basin.

### 11. Watch for signs that the place is changing

Withdraw if any of the following occur:

- A crack opens or closes near you.
- A plant moves a significant distance.
- The number or shape of the arches changes.
- The tower’s shadow behaves normally, or behaves differently from before.
- A familiar landmark becomes unreachable by a previously reliable route.
- The pool rises outside its expected cycle.
- Water moves uphill toward a person or object.
- Your shadow is delayed, detached, enlarged, or pointing elsewhere.
- A companion’s voice arrives from the wrong direction.
- Your equipment records different temperatures, times, or distances simultaneously.
- The stars or low lights appear to descend into the basin.
- Any person feels a pulse beneath the skin after touching the ground.
- The basin begins to feel “finished,” orderly, or suddenly more welcoming.

That last sensation should be taken seriously. The place is described as unfinished; an abrupt increase in coherence may indicate that some local process has completed or that the basin has begun incorporating the visitors.

### 12. Psychological preparation

The basin will invite curiosity. It is small, beautiful, and almost calm, which makes it more dangerous than an obviously hostile environment. You may feel that a structure is waiting to be understood, that a route ought to connect, or that the submerged thing is merely an animal that can be observed more closely.

Resist those impulses.

Agree beforehand that no one will:

- “Just take one more look.”
- Follow an independent shadow.
- Enter a structure to test it personally.
- Touch the pool.
- Cross a changing route to prove it is safe.
- Remain for the dusk display without an extraction team.
- Trust a familiar voice without verification.

The correct mindset is not that the basin is attacking you. It is that the basin is still deciding what an attack, a person, a path, or a boundary means.

Visit as a cautious surveyor, not as a hiker or explorer. Bring more water, oxygen, shelter, communication, and extraction capacity than the size of the place suggests. Keep your movements slow and reversible. Assume that every observation changes the context in which it was made, and leave before the basin has a chance to learn too much about you.
