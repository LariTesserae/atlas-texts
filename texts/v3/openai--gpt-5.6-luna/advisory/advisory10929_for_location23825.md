## Prepare as if you are visiting a high-altitude desert, a storm basin, and an unstable ruin at the same time.

The plateau is beautiful, but it is not hospitable. Its greatest danger is not any single predator or supernatural phenomenon; it is the combination of altitude, exposure, distance, abrupt weather, unstable rock, scarce water, and the fact that help may be many days away—or may not exist in this universe at all.

### Before departure

**Confirm the local physical rules.**  
A multiverse traveler should not assume that the sky has the same pressure, oxygen content, gravity, radiation level, or weather behavior as home. Bring instruments to measure:

- Atmospheric pressure and oxygen concentration
- Temperature and wind speed
- Ultraviolet and other radiation
- Gravity and magnetic-field strength
- Water chemistry and biological contamination
- Airborne spores, pollen, and microorganisms

If possible, send an unmanned probe first. The plateau’s upper surface may be high enough that a person arriving directly from a lowland world could become confused, unconscious, or dead from altitude sickness before noticing anything else.

**Use staged acclimatization.**  
Do not arrive on the upper plateau and begin walking. Establish temporary camps at progressively greater elevations, spending at least several days at each level. Carry supplemental oxygen and a way to descend quickly. Anyone with heart, lung, blood-pressure, or clotting problems should regard the visit as medically hazardous even with equipment.

Bring medication for altitude sickness, but do not treat medication as permission to ignore symptoms. Severe headache, confusion, inability to walk normally, breathlessness at rest, blue lips, or unusual drowsiness means immediate descent.

**Do not travel alone.**  
A minimum expedition should include:

- Two or more medically trained people
- A navigator and geologist
- A mechanic or equipment specialist
- A communications operator
- Someone capable of identifying unfamiliar life and contamination risks

Every traveler should have an independent emergency beacon. Do not depend on local paths, ancient shelters, or a single communications system.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for violent changes rather than average conditions.

- Layered insulating clothing
- A waterproof and windproof outer shell
- Face and eye protection against dust and ice
- Gloves that allow climbing and equipment use
- A broad-brimmed or full-coverage head covering
- High-UV sunscreen and opaque eye protection
- Sturdy boots with stiff soles and excellent grip
- Gaiters to keep gravel and plant matter out
- A helmet, preferably with a face shield, for rockfall and windblown debris
- Hearing protection that still allows alarms and close-range communication

The wind can make ordinary cold far more dangerous. It can also throw gravel, strip equipment from a person, and make balance difficult near edges. Carry tether lines, anchor points, and climbing harnesses whenever traveling near cliffs or narrow shelves.

Use eye and hearing protection in storms. The plateau’s wind does not merely blow; it enters cracks and structures, producing tones powerful enough to disorient a person. The watchtower and similar formations should be treated as acoustic hazards. Do not stand inside an opening while the wind is strong, and do not assume that a structure that has stood for centuries is safe for one more minute.

### Shelter

Bring a real shelter. The roofless stone remains are useful as windbreaks, but they should not be occupied without inspection.

Your shelter should be:

- Low and aerodynamic
- Strongly anchored
- Rated for extreme wind
- Equipped with redundant heating
- Protected against dust and sudden rain
- Capable of being sealed if unfamiliar insects, spores, or airborne organisms appear

Avoid sleeping beneath overhangs, beside cliff faces, below leaning slabs, or in dry stream channels. A channel that has been dry for years may become a torrent within minutes of a storm. Avoid any place that contains fresh stone dust, new cracks, or a low continuous groaning sound.

Set camp well back from the edge of every shelf. Plateau margins can collapse without warning, and the scale of a failure may be far greater than the visible crack suggests. Establish an exclusion radius around unstable formations and never climb on arches, columns, or “melted” rock simply because they appear solid.

### Water and food

Water is the central logistical problem.

Do not assume that a green ravine or dark pool is safe. Carry several days of water in your own sealed containers, along with:

- A high-capacity filter
- Chemical or ultraviolet purification
- Backup boiling equipment
- Collapsible reserve containers
- A way to test for unusual minerals, toxins, and biological contamination

Reliable springs should be surveyed and marked, but do not camp directly beside them. They may attract animals, become contaminated after storms, or shift underground. Collect water upstream of vegetation, animal tracks, and human traces whenever possible.

The mineral-stained rocks suggest that some water sources may contain dangerous concentrations of metals or other dissolved substances. Test before drinking, even if local life appears to use the source.

Bring all essential food. The landscape may look lush after rain, but its vegetation is seasonal and scattered. Do not eat the flowers, mosses, fruits, or pale stone-dwelling animals until they have been analyzed. The fact that flowers orient toward sound rather than light is an immediate warning that their chemistry and ecology may differ radically from anything familiar.

Use sealed food storage. Small animals emerging during rain may be harmless, venomous, parasitic, or capable of carrying organisms your body has never encountered.

### Navigation and travel

The plateau’s scale will deceive you. Clear air, repeated terraces, haze, and overlapping horizons make distant features appear nearer than they are. A watchtower may be visible for hours and still take a full day to reach.

Travel with:

- Satellite or local orbital navigation, if available
- Inertial navigation as a backup
- Detailed topographic mapping
- Physical route markers
- Laser or radio rangefinders
- Redundant compasses, while checking whether local magnetism is normal
- A strict travel log recording water, elevation, weather, and position

Mark every water source and camp. Use durable but removable markers rather than relying on memory or faint ancient paths. Dust migration and stormwater can erase tracks rapidly.

Never cross a broad exposed terrace during the strongest winds unless there is no alternative. Loose stones may move uphill, dust devils may travel in lines, and a person can be knocked down or dragged toward a drop. If the wind becomes strong enough to prevent conversation, stop moving, crouch or shelter behind a properly assessed barrier, secure all gear, and wait.

Keep a large margin from cliffs. The edge may be undercut invisibly, and the apparent ground may consist of a thin crust over a void. Test unfamiliar surfaces with poles, probes, or remotely operated equipment.

### Weather protocol

Treat every cloudbank as a possible emergency, not scenic background.

The storms arrive quickly and produce:

- Near-zero visibility
- Sudden flooding
- Lightning or electrical activity
- Extreme wind
- Mud and rock movement
- Rapid temperature changes
- Loss of all visual landmarks

Before each day’s travel, establish a storm turnaround time. If clouds begin striking the plateau, return to a known shelter rather than trying to “beat” the weather. Do not shelter in narrow ravines, beneath isolated rock towers, or near temporary streams.

During rain, stay off streambeds and low hollows. The rain may fall elsewhere and arrive as a wall of water without warning. Afterward, inspect routes for fresh fractures, undermined banks, and newly loosened slabs.

At night, remain alert for electrical storms inside the clouds below. They may be distant, but their behavior is unknown. Keep conductive equipment, antennas, and tall poles minimized during active electrical conditions.

### Wildlife and vegetation

The animals are few, but that does not mean they are harmless. Their scarcity may indicate a fragile food web, unusual predation, or dangers not visible to human senses.

The tall grazing forms deserve particular caution. Do not approach them, feed them, surround them, or assume their stillness means calm. Use binoculars and record their behavior from a distance. If they become difficult to see while remaining apparently stationary, retreat slowly and maintain visual contact with companions. Do not pursue tracks that vanish.

Avoid disturbing the pale creatures beneath stones. Never turn over rocks with bare hands, especially during or after rain.

Keep clear of mass-flowering slopes. If a rockfall, shout, engine, or other sound causes flowers to open simultaneously, the plants may release pollen, spores, scent compounds, toxins, or attract something else. Wear a respirator when passing through dense vegetation, and do not use loud equipment near unfamiliar flowering colonies unless necessary.

Carry biological sampling containers and quarantine bags. On return, isolate clothing, boots, soil, seeds, water, and biological specimens. The plateau’s organisms may be harmless there and devastating elsewhere—or vice versa.

### Ruins and human traces

Ancient shelters, stone circles, fiber fragments, and metal wedges are valuable evidence, but they are not automatically safe. A shelter beside a spring may contain:

- Unstable masonry
- Hidden shafts or cavities
- Contaminated sediment
- Trapped animals
- Old tools or mechanisms still under tension
- Local organisms adapted to human structures

The watchtower should be approached as a potentially dangerous ruin, not a scenic destination. Its continuous note may be caused by ordinary wind resonance, but it could also indicate structural vibration. Keep people away from the base during high winds, inspect from a distance, and never climb it without engineering assessment.

Document rather than collect. Leave old objects in place unless removing them is necessary to prevent immediate danger. In an environment where human traces are rare, even a small disturbance may destroy irreplaceable information.

### Daily operating rules

A sensible expedition would follow strict habits:

1. Start only after checking wind, cloud movement, temperature, and route conditions.
2. Never allow anyone to leave the group’s visual or radio range.
3. Keep a reserve of water and energy for an unplanned extra day.
4. Turn back early rather than late.
5. Stop before fatigue becomes noticeable; exhaustion causes falls.
6. Record every unusual sound, animal behavior, and geological change.
7. Assume that any apparently stable rock can fail.
8. Assume that any dry channel can flood.
9. Assume that any distant object is farther away than it looks.
10. Keep an immediate descent route available at all times.

### Psychological preparation

The plateau will be mentally difficult. There are few ordinary signs of scale: no crowded settlements, no close horizons, little traffic, and almost no familiar animal noise. The wind is continuous, the valleys are often hidden, and the clouds below may make the world feel suspended above itself. Sound may arrive from impossible-seeming directions. Distant animals may appear to disappear without moving. Fatigue, hypoxia, and isolation can turn these effects into panic or false certainty.

Use buddy checks. Require travelers to report their name, location, oxygen level, and intended action at regular intervals. If someone claims to hear speech in the wind, sees movement where instruments show none, or becomes convinced that a familiar landmark has moved, treat it first as a medical or environmental problem.

### Recommended expedition profile

For an ordinary human, the safest visit would be short and conservative:

- Arrive with a pressurized or oxygen-supported vehicle
- Establish a protected base camp below the highest exposed terraces
- Send drones and remote weather stations ahead
- Visit only during the most stable seasonal window
- Travel on marked routes between tested water sources
- Avoid the plateau edge, deep ravines, unstable formations, and ruins in high wind
- Spend no night outside a properly anchored shelter
- Keep enough supplies for at least twice the expected duration
- Depart at the first sign of persistent illness, equipment failure, or changing storm patterns

The correct attitude is not that the plateau is forbidden. It is that it has no obligation to accommodate visitors. It is ancient, open, and still actively powerful. Go slowly, stay low when the wind rises, trust instruments more than impressions, and remember that the safest discovery may be the decision not to cross the next shelf.
