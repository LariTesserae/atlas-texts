## Preparation for Visiting the Unfinished Plateau

Treat this location less like a landscape and more like a **slow geological-industrial process that happens to have walkable surfaces**. It is survivable for a human only with preparation, and even then the main risks are not ordinary heat or terrain. The pocket alters matter, perspective, gravity, moisture, and possibly the meaning of “inside.”

### 1. Do not arrive with an open suit

The atmosphere may be breathable in the conventional sense, but that does not make it safe. It is hot, extremely dry, mineral-laden, and likely contains ozone, reactive vapors, fine ceramic dust, and industrial byproducts.

Wear:

- A fully sealed, positive-pressure environmental suit.
- Heat-reflective outer layers, preferably ceramic or metallic rather than fabric.
- A respirator system only as a backup; do not rely on local air.
- Replaceable particulate filters for the return transition.
- Ozone and corrosive-gas sensors.
- A cooling system designed for **radiant and conductive heat**, not merely hot air.
- Gloves and boots with rigid, nonporous soles.
- A face shield or helmet visor with ultraviolet and infrared protection.

Do not bring exposed organic clothing, leather, paper, untreated wood, food, or absorbent materials. Moisture entering the chamber is not likely to remain water. Sweat may vanish, crust over as mineral, flash into heat, or become incorporated into the terrain. Keep your suit’s humidity entirely internal.

Your suit should have redundant moisture reclamation. If the cooling system leaks, the leaked coolant may not behave like coolant for long.

### 2. Bring more water than you expect to need—but keep it sealed

You cannot safely drink local water because there may be no water in the location at all, and any water container exposed to the environment may be altered.

Carry water in:

- Double-walled, pressure-rated containers.
- Sealed reservoirs inside the suit or pack.
- Flexible bladders only if they are protected from heat and puncture.
- Small emergency capsules distributed across your body rather than one large tank.

Do not open a water container outdoors except inside a sealed suit compartment. Avoid pouring, washing, spitting, or urinating. Bodily fluids could be translated into mineral deposits or heat just as readily as imported moisture.

Bring electrolytes, but use them sparingly. The chamber may interfere with ordinary metabolism through heat stress, radiation exposure, or subtle pressure effects. A medical monitor should track hydration, core temperature, blood chemistry, and tissue mineralization.

### 3. Assume the ground is active

The terrain is not merely rough. It is being manufactured. A surface that looks stable may be in the middle of a geological or industrial cycle.

Bring:

- Rigid boots with wide soles.
- Retractable climbing spikes, but use them only after testing the surface.
- A compact probe capable of measuring temperature gradients and subsurface voids.
- A load-distributing platform or folding walkway for crossing soft-looking mineral.
- Nonmagnetic tools, since black metallic sand and electrical gradients may interfere with them.
- A laser or acoustic rangefinder, but never trust one measurement.
- A physical staff or probe for checking whether a ridge is solid, hollow, or merely behaving as solid.

Avoid standing on:

- Freshly smooth “poured” surfaces.
- Geometric ripples that appear too regular.
- Pale slag or incandescent seams.
- Circular apertures, newly formed basins, or surfaces that have changed since your last glance.
- Any ground that feels warm through the boots, even if the ambient air seems tolerable.

The safest routes will probably be older dark basalt shelves with visible, mature vegetation nearby—though even those may slowly shift their thermal boundaries.

### 4. Protect yourself from the plants

The vegetation is not predatory, but it is not harmless. Its roots react to heat, pressure, and electrical differences. Your body, suit, batteries, and cooling system may appear to it as an attractive environmental gradient.

Do not touch the plants. Do not step over exposed root webs. Do not place equipment against them. Avoid standing between a plant colony and a hot seam.

Particular caution is warranted around:

- Hollow stalks with luminous interiors.
- Copper-green fronds.
- Pale tendrils suspended in air.
- Plants growing beside incandescent mineral.
- Any colony that moves when you are not looking directly at it.
- Dark crystal flakes or sparks released during pressure changes.

The reproductive material may not be biologically dangerous in a conventional sense, but it could attach to your suit, follow electrical fields, or seed your equipment. Carry an external decontamination layer that can be discarded before re-entry into normal space.

If a plant is cut or damaged, do not attempt to treat it as a wounded organism. It may root along the cut, transform into terrain, or become reflective stone. More importantly, its response may alter the shelf beneath you.

### 5. Bring a physical tether and a physical return marker

Electronic navigation will be unreliable. Distances, perspective, and even the apparent shape of the chamber can change without the terrain visibly moving.

Use several independent forms of navigation:

- A continuous physical tether from the entry point.
- Knotted or textured markers that can be recognized by touch.
- Numbered physical tags made from inert ceramic or glass.
- A mechanical stopwatch alongside digital timekeeping.
- A compass only as a curiosity; magnetic and electrical fields may make it useless.
- A mapping system that records repeated observations rather than assuming a stable geometry.
- A personal return beacon that emits a simple, constant signal rather than relying on coordinates.

Do not mark the ground by cutting plants, scratching crystal, pouring paint, or placing water. A mark may be incorporated into the manufacturing process or transformed into part of the landscape.

The tether should not be allowed to touch plants or cross active seams. Keep it slightly elevated where possible, with breakaway sections so that a shifting shelf cannot drag you into a new cavity. A single tether is not enough: use a second independent line if you intend to climb toward the summit.

### 6. Establish strict orientation rules

The chamber will offer you false evidence. A nearby stone may contain an apparent horizon. A slope may seem to join itself. A distant shelf may preserve its distance while you walk toward it. Shadows may move before their objects.

Before entering, agree on simple rules:

- Believe touch and repeated physical contact more than sight.
- Believe the tether more than perspective.
- Believe a clock only when several clocks agree.
- Never walk toward a feature merely because it appears closer.
- Never use a reflection as a map.
- Never assume “up” is the direction your body currently prefers.
- If two observers disagree about the position of an object, stop moving.

Take regular sensory breaks. Close the visor, look at a fixed instrument panel, and describe your position aloud using only measurable facts: “left boot on basalt, tether under tension, temperature rising.” Do not narrate what the chamber “must” be doing. Its inconsistencies may become more dangerous when treated as a solvable visual puzzle.

### 7. Prepare for weak, shifting, and contradictory gravity

Gravity is weak but not absent, and its direction may combine downward pull with attraction toward the unfinished central mass. Loose grains may roll uphill and then reverse their apparent history.

Equip yourself with:

- A body harness.
- Adjustable ankle and waist restraints.
- Magnetic or mechanical anchoring systems, though test both before relying on them.
- A low center-of-gravity pack.
- Handholds that can be deployed without drilling into the terrain.
- A method of securing yourself during tremors or relay events.

Do not jump to test gravity. A jump may become a prolonged drift, a sideways fall, or a movement whose endpoint is not where your eyes predict. Keep at least one point of contact with the terrain when near an edge.

Edges are especially dangerous because the pocket’s size is not consistent. A drop that looks shallow may have no trustworthy bottom, while a seemingly distant surface may be physically adjacent.

### 8. Monitor the machinery

The machinery beneath the plateau is slow, but “slow” does not mean weak. A relay click, granular hiss, or change in the hum may indicate an active manufacturing cycle.

Carry vibration, acoustic, and electromagnetic monitors. When any of them changes sharply:

1. Stop walking.
2. Lower your profile.
3. Move away from seams and newly smooth surfaces.
4. Secure yourself to the tether.
5. Keep your hands off the ground.
6. Wait until the sound and vibration have fully faded.
7. Recheck the map, suit, and local terrain before proceeding.

Do not attempt to locate the machinery. The sound may seem to come from below, behind, or everywhere at once. Excavating, drilling, or opening a wall could turn your expedition into a new manufacturing input.

If the chamber produces a clean vibration with no obvious cause, assume the terrain has changed even if you cannot see how.

### 9. Treat the summit plant as a restricted zone

The lone plant at the highest shelf is the most significant feature in the chamber and the least suitable for casual approach.

Its crown crosses the bright boundary without visibly opening it. That suggests the boundary is not a conventional wall and that the plant may exist partly outside the pocket, partly inside it, or in more than one state at once. Its proximity to an incandescent seam also makes it likely to be electrically, thermally, or structurally important.

Do not touch it. Do not pass beneath its crown. Do not shine intense light at it, heat it, cut it, sample it, or attach instruments to it.

If you must approach, do so only with:

- A full-body tether system.
- A remote probe.
- A strict time limit.
- An observer outside the pocket or at the entry point.
- A prearranged abort signal.
- No loose equipment.
- No open samples or biological material.

Do not assume that the summit is actually the highest point. It may be the location the chamber permits you to perceive as highest.

### 10. Prepare for temporal and observational instability

The location’s timing is unreliable. “A few minutes” may not correspond cleanly to outside time, and slow processes may become perceptually compressed or expanded.

Carry multiple clocks:

- A sealed mechanical watch.
- A radiation-independent timer.
- A short-duration countdown beacon synchronized to the outside.
- A recorder with continuous timestamping.

Set a maximum mission duration before entry. Do not extend it because the place “seems calm.” Calmness is its normal state, including during active changes.

Use a buddy system, but do not rely solely on visual confirmation. A person’s shadow may continue moving after they stop. A reflection may show an impossible position. Maintain voice contact and physical line contact. If either becomes unreliable, return immediately.

You should also prepare psychologically for a place that refuses a complete explanation. Attempts to comprehend the whole chamber may produce quiet contradictions rather than obvious danger. That can encourage repeated checking, wandering, and fixation. Decide in advance that an incomplete map is an acceptable result.

### 11. Bring nothing you cannot afford to lose to transformation

Samples are dangerous to collect. The location’s rules suggest that organic material, moisture, heat, and cut surfaces may be translated into new terrain or mineral forms.

If sampling is necessary:

- Use remote manipulators.
- Place samples in sealed, inert ceramic capsules.
- Never carry living plants, soil, liquid, or loose crystals inside the suit.
- Keep samples isolated from your power system and cooling system.
- Assume anything removed from the chamber may continue changing after extraction.
- Quarantine the entire sample container on return.

Do not collect reproductive sparks or flakes. They are likely to follow invisible lines of force and may not remain inert outside the chamber.

### 12. Plan the exit before entry

The exit point may no longer look the same when you return. The aperture could become a basin, the floor could rise around it, or the chamber could present a different route back while preserving the same apparent intimacy.

At the entry boundary, install:

- A mechanically anchored return frame.
- A visible and tactile marker.
- A continuous beacon.
- A pressure and temperature buffer.
- A decontamination stage.
- A secondary retrieval line controlled from outside.

Before crossing back, inspect yourself and your equipment for:

- Mineral crusts.
- New roots or filaments.
- Unexplained heat.
- Altered shadows.
- Missing or duplicated objects.
- Clocks that disagree.
- Equipment that casts a delayed shadow.
- Moisture that has changed color, temperature, or phase.

Do not let anyone who has become separated from their shadow, reflection, voice, or physical tether return immediately. Isolate them and verify continuity outside the pocket.

### Recommended mission profile

For a first visit, do not attempt to cross the whole plateau. Enter only far enough to establish environmental readings and confirm that the tether behaves normally. Remain near a mature basalt shelf. Observe the plants and machinery without touching anything. Wait through at least one relay event, if the timing permits, and compare your instruments afterward.

The safest visitor is one who regards the place as **unfinished infrastructure**, not wilderness. It is not hostile in the ordinary sense. It does not hunt, pursue, or attack. But it may incorporate your heat, moisture, pressure, shadow, tools, or body into its ongoing construction without recognizing a distinction between visitor and raw material.

Go lightly. Stay anchored. Keep dry. Trust physical contact over sight. Never assume the chamber is smaller, more stable, or more singular than it appears.
