## Prepare as if visiting a living machine, not an underground ruin

This cistern-city is inhabited, maintained, and densely used. You are not entering a monument where you may wander at will. You are entering someone’s water system, food supply, workplace, livestock enclosure, drainage network, and home—simultaneously.

Your first preparation should be mental: do not expect open space, silence, clean air, straight corridors, or any distinction between “public,” “industrial,” and “domestic” areas. The city has no unused background. Every ledge, shaft, channel, grate, recess, and wall joint probably has a purpose.

## Clothing

Wear clothing suited to constant warmth, dampness, and abrasion.

- Loose but close-fitting garments that do not snag on ropes, ladders, hooks, grates, or projecting stone.
- Tough trousers or wrapped leg coverings.
- A long-sleeved shirt or tunic, preferably made of quick-drying material.
- A short, close-fitting outer layer rather than a trailing cloak.
- Soft, quiet footwear with excellent grip.
- Gloves for rough stone, ropes, hot vessels, and animal handling.
- A head covering or hood to protect you from condensation, dripping water, dust, insects, and falling grit.
- Eye protection if available; grit and dirty droplets are likely to come from above.
- A cloth or scarf for your mouth and nose, though do not assume it makes smoke or disease harmless.

Avoid dangling jewelry, loose belts, long scarves, wide sleeves, open sandals, and anything precious enough to make you hesitate before climbing or crawling. Your clothing will become damp and smoky. Bring a spare layer in a sealed waterproof wrapping.

The floor is not merely wet; it is worn smooth in places and coated with grit in others. Expect sudden changes from dry stone to slick moss, shallow water, mud-like masonry dust, and open channels.

## Footwear and movement

Treat every unmarked dark line as a possible drain, channel, shaft, or gap until proven otherwise.

Walk slowly. Keep one hand available for balance. Do not step backward without looking. Do not jump over channels unless a local person tells you to; the far side may be weak, occupied, or covering a concealed opening. Never place your hand into a crevice, drainage grate, wall niche, or pile of refuse. Rats, insects, fish, and defensive animals will all have reasons to object.

When descending steps, keep your weight low and your feet angled carefully. The stairs are rounded by centuries of use, and the central grooves may be full of water. When passing beneath a low vault, stoop early. Do not wait until the ceiling forces you down.

The city’s inhabitants will be much better at moving through it than you are. Follow a designated guide and imitate their pace. They know which stones rock, which channels carry waste, which ladders are sound, and which apparently empty spaces belong to someone.

## Bring very little, but bring the right things

A useful visitor’s kit would include:

- A sealed container for drinking water.
- Small waterproof food rations.
- A warm, dry spare garment.
- A wrapped blanket or sleeping cloth if staying overnight.
- A simple lamp or reserve light, but use it only with permission.
- A way to mark your belongings, preferably with a local sign rather than a bright modern tag.
- Basic bandages and disinfectant.
- Insect protection.
- A small knife or multitool, if tools are culturally acceptable and you can explain it.
- Ear protection for sleeping, but do not wear it while moving through the city or during an alarm.
- A cord or strap for securing your own items, not for tying anything to city infrastructure.
- A sealed bag for waste and contaminated clothing.

Do not carry exposed electronics unless you know they tolerate condensation and mineral dust. Moisture will enter them. Fine grit will enter them. Metal objects may be valuable, suspicious, or forbidden. Strong batteries, flashing lights, drones, cameras, and recording devices could be interpreted as dangerous tools, theft devices, or magical attempts to inspect hidden passages.

Do not bring a tent. There will be nowhere appropriate to pitch one, and it will obstruct circulation. If you need privacy, ask for a designated sleeping shelf or guest chamber.

## Water, food, and bodily needs

Do not drink from a channel simply because it is clear. The city has several classes of water, and the distinction may not be visible to you. Some channels carry drinking water; others carry washing water, animal runoff, industrial waste, or sewage. Ask before touching any basin, trough, grate, jar, or cistern.

Likewise, do not wash your hands, boots, tools, or clothing in a channel unless instructed. You might contaminate a drinking route or disrupt a carefully managed flow.

Carry enough safe water to get through your first day. The inhabitants may be generous, but a stranger who arrives consuming stored water without contribution will be a burden.

Eat cautiously at first. Local food may be safe for the inhabitants but unfamiliar to your body, particularly:

- Cultivated fungi.
- Blind or semi-blind fish from flood channels.
- Fermented grain.
- Animal products raised in dense enclosures.
- Foods preserved in resin, smoke, brine, or mineral-rich water.

Do not sample refuse, insect products, fungus, or channel life out of curiosity. Ask what is food, what is medicine, what is bait, and what is only a processing ingredient.

Determine where visitors may relieve themselves before you need to. The city is organized around flow, and an inappropriate act of waste could be a serious insult or a public health emergency.

## Respiratory and health precautions

The air is breathable, but not necessarily healthy for an unadapted visitor. It is warm, humid, smoky, and full of biological material. You may encounter:

- Charcoal smoke.
- Mold and fungal spores.
- Animal dander.
- Dust from worked stone.
- Sewage gases.
- Insect particles.
- Dense condensation.
- Poorly ventilated pockets of air.

Do not enter a low chamber alone, especially one with a weak flame, no flame, or an unusual smell. A flame that gutters may indicate poor air, not merely a draft. Sudden headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, unusual fatigue, or breathlessness means leave for a better-ventilated area immediately.

Do not sleep near braziers, smoke shafts, animal pens, drain mouths, or a chamber where people are burning unfamiliar fuel. Keep any personal lamp shielded and low. Open flame is a practical necessity here, but it is also a danger to reeds, ropes, cloth, fungus beds, stored grain, and accumulated waste.

Before departure, obtain vaccinations and broad-spectrum medical advice appropriate to your own universe’s biology. If cross-universal disease transmission is possible, use a quarantine protocol. The inhabitants may have defenses against local organisms that you do not, while your microbes may be equally dangerous to them. Cover open wounds, avoid touching your face, and wash with a clean supply rather than drainage water.

## Floods and water events

The greatest immediate danger is probably not the inhabitants or the animals, but a change in water behavior.

A sudden infiltration can become a torrent within minutes. The city’s channels are designed to carry water, but you may not know which routes are active, which grates can be removed, or where the overflow exits. Ask your hosts:

- How is an incoming flood announced?
- Which chambers are evacuated first?
- Which routes must never be blocked?
- Where are the high platforms?
- What sound indicates a gate opening?
- What should a stranger do if separated from the guide?

At the first warning, move upward or toward the designated refuge, not necessarily toward the nearest visible dry space. A chamber that seems safe may be a settling basin or a dead-end drain. Never shelter inside a channel, beneath a bridge, or against a barred opening. Keep your hands free and do not attempt to rescue luggage during a rapid rise.

The fish arrival may be a major communal event rather than an emergency. Still, remain out of the way. Children and experienced fishers may enter water that is dangerous to you, and their shouting may obscure warnings.

## Animals and pests

Assume every dark recess is occupied.

The goats and pigs may be accustomed to humans but are confined, stressed, and surrounded by obstacles. Do not approach young animals, breeding animals, or animals cornered near water. Do not feed anything without permission. A harmless-looking goat may knock you into a channel; a pig may become aggressive around food; burrowing fowl may bite or startle you from beneath a floor.

Rats are not merely vermin. They are part of the city’s ecology and may be trapped, tolerated, hunted, or protected in particular places. Do not set traps, chase them, or interfere with them.

Bats should not be touched. Avoid loud noises near nesting areas. Their presence may be tied to local rules, air circulation, or ritual timing.

Inspect your sleeping place for spiders, beetles, rodents, and dampness. Shake out clothing and bedding before wearing or using them. Keep food sealed and never leave crumbs near a walkway or sleeping platform.

## Navigation

You will become lost quickly if you rely on visual intuition. The city is compact but vertically and acoustically confusing. The same passage may appear to return you to a previous location while actually crossing at another level. Echoes will make voices and footsteps unreliable.

Use a local guide. Ideally, have at least two guides who know different parts of the city: one familiar with water infrastructure and one familiar with ordinary residential circulation.

Before setting out, learn:

- The local words for clean water, waste water, flood, smoke, fire, animal, stranger, stop, danger, and permission.
- The markings for safe passage and restricted passages.
- How doors and workspaces are claimed.
- Which wall symbols indicate maintenance conditions.
- How to identify an active channel.
- Where to wait if you lose your group.
- What sounds mean alarm, work, ritual, or ordinary noise.

Do not make your own trail marks on walls. A chalk line, scratch, paint mark, or tied cord may be mistaken for a maintenance instruction or a warning. If you need navigation aids, use a guide-approved method and remove it afterward.

Avoid relying on shouting. Sound behaves strangely here. A distant voice may be close; a nearby voice may seem to come from behind you. If you hear your own footsteps following you, obey the local rule: stop and wait. Do not run toward or away from the sound until a resident explains what is happening.

## Social conduct

The central etiquette is: **do not obstruct flow**.

This includes physical, social, and informational flow. Do not stand in a walkway while admiring the architecture. Do not block a stair, grate, ladder, cistern, smoke shaft, door, or workbench. Do not place bags on shelves or ledges without asking; those surfaces are probably in use.

Ask before:

- Crossing a channel.
- Touching a jar, tool, animal, rope, grate, or wall marking.
- Entering a chamber.
- Taking photographs or making drawings.
- Lighting a flame.
- Speaking into a shaft or drain.
- Eating or drinking from communal stores.
- Sleeping on a platform.
- Joining a fish harvest or maintenance task.

Keep your voice controlled. Loudness has practical consequences here. It can disturb bats, interfere with warnings, trigger conflict, or carry private conversations through masonry. Do not sing unless invited, and never sing in a drainage passage.

Do not call the city primitive, filthy, ruined, or unnatural. Its complexity is expressed through maintenance rather than spaciousness or ornament. A channel repaired for centuries may be more culturally significant than a statue. Treat workers, water keepers, animal tenders, and repairers as important authorities.

If you bring gifts, bring useful, durable things: clean cloth, needles, good cord, sealed medical supplies, corrosion-resistant tools, or knowledge that can be safely shared. Avoid giving objects that cannot be repaired locally or that encourage dependence. Ask first; a gift that interferes with their systems may be a liability.

## Respect for restrictions

Certain areas will be restricted because they contain:

- Drinking-water sources.
- Fragile vaults.
- Flood controls.
- Sacred channels.
- Food stores.
- Breeding animals.
- Smoke-management systems.
- Sealed chambers.
- Unstable or contaminated masonry.

Do not test a sealed doorway that exhales warm air. The inhabitants regard the city’s habits cautiously for good reason. Do not open gates, clear blockages, follow unexplained drafts, or investigate internal knocks. Curiosity could flood a sleeping tier, release bad air, collapse a passage, or disturb something the residents have chosen to leave undisturbed.

The city’s laws about sound and doors are likely not superstition in the ordinary sense. They are accumulated engineering, ecology, and social memory. Follow them even when their logic is not immediately apparent.

## Mental preparation

The city will challenge several ordinary expectations:

- There may be no true darkness, only different kinds of illumination.
- There may be no true silence, and silence may be a warning.
- “Down” may mean cleaner water in one direction and deadly waste in another.
- A wall may be a home, a drain boundary, a support, or a reservoir.
- A place that looks abandoned may be under observation or scheduled for use.
- Warmth may indicate habitation, machinery, rot, or dangerous air.
- Water may be precious in one channel and hazardous in the next.
- The inhabitants may regard leaks, fungi, bats, fish, and repair materials as parts of one civic body.

Expect your sense of scale to fail. The city is small in area but dense in function. It may feel claustrophobic at first, then strangely expansive as you learn that sound, water, smoke, and people are traveling through layers you cannot see.

A good visitor should become unobtrusive: quiet, clean, alert, and useful. Learn where to place your feet. Learn which surfaces are alive with purpose. Keep your possessions dry, your waste contained, your hands out of the channels, and your curiosity subordinate to local safety.

Most importantly, do not arrive as an explorer demanding access. Arrive as a temporary participant in a system that has survived by noticing everything. Offer to carry, patch, sort, clean, or watch. If you can help maintain the flow without interrupting it, the cistern-city may eventually accept your presence.
