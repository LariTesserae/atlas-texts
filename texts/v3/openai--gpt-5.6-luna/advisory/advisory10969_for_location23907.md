## General assessment

Treat this as a **wet, low-visibility, environmentally active anomaly**, not as an ordinary marsh. It is probably survivable for a healthy human, but only with deliberate preparation. The main dangers are not predators or extreme weather. They are:

- drowning in water whose depth and geometry cannot be trusted;
- losing orientation because sound, distance, and sequence are unreliable;
- exposure, hypothermia, and infection;
- contact with unknown biological or mineral systems;
- interference with electronics, navigation, and return equipment;
- gradual alteration or “registration” by the landscape;
- being separated from a companion or from the departure point.

Do not visit casually. An ordinary traveler should plan for a short reconnaissance excursion, not camping or exploration without a prepared extraction capability.

---

## Before departure

### 1. Establish a strict mission profile

Set a maximum time in advance. The landscape appears stable, but it is not ordinary-stable; recurring anomalies may be periodic, and the region may incorporate visitors over time.

Decide beforehand:

- maximum duration outside;
- maximum distance from the arrival point;
- conditions that trigger immediate return;
- what constitutes a lost-party emergency;
- who or what has authority to abort the mission.

Do not extend the visit because “nothing has happened yet.” The absence of an event may simply mean that you have not reached the relevant phase of the local cycle.

Use a short initial window—perhaps less than an hour—and return before fatigue, dampness, or perceptual confusion becomes significant.

### 2. Bring a way home that does not depend on local geography

Your return mechanism should not rely solely on:

- GPS or equivalent positioning;
- radio triangulation;
- visible landmarks;
- synchronized clocks;
- a signal traveling normally through air or water;
- a device that must remain exposed.

Bring several independent means of confirming the departure point:

- a hardened, sealed return beacon;
- a second beacon physically separated from the first;
- a passive, mechanical marker;
- a prearranged extraction time;
- an external observer who can trigger retrieval without receiving a signal;
- a tether or route line back to the arrival zone.

The beacons should be shielded against moisture and possible electromagnetic or conceptual interference. Test them in multiple realities or anomalous environments before trusting them here.

Do not place all navigation equipment in one bag. If the land is capable of altering fields or calculations, a single failure may be systemic rather than accidental.

### 3. Record a baseline of yourself

Before entry, photograph and document:

- your clothing and equipment;
- the exact arrangement of pockets and straps;
- your physical condition;
- the current time on several independent clocks;
- the state of your shadow, if it behaves normally;
- the wording of your mission and return instructions.

This may sound excessive, but the landscape appears to retain patterns of visitors and may alter the apparent timing or arrangement of objects. A written baseline gives you something to compare against.

Carry a simple card with your name, origin, destination, companions, objective, and abort conditions. Do not rely exclusively on memory. The place contains memory-responsive behavior: moss changes when someone remembers an unknown place, and the terrain seems to revise itself around visitors.

## Clothing and personal protection

### 1. Stay warm and dry

The cool dampness is more dangerous than the temperature suggests. Constant wetness, low light, and exertion can produce hypothermia without dramatic cold.

Wear:

- a waterproof outer shell;
- a breathable insulating layer;
- synthetic or wool base layers;
- waterproof gloves with removable liners;
- sealed eye protection;
- a hood that does not obstruct peripheral vision;
- waterproof gaiters;
- tall waterproof boots with aggressive tread;
- a spare complete set of dry clothing in a double-sealed bag.

Avoid cotton. Assume that every exposed surface will become wet, including equipment inside a seemingly protected pack.

Use boots with ankle support, but do not assume they will protect you from sinking. Add a harness or belt attachment for a safety line.

### 2. Protect skin and lungs

The water, mist, plants, minerals, and airborne organisms are unknown. Wear:

- a properly fitted respirator with replaceable filters;
- sealed goggles or a full-face mask;
- nitrile or similar inner gloves under durable outer gloves;
- skin-covering clothing;
- a hood or neck seal;
- a small supply of clean water for rinsing eyes and wounds.

The respirator may become necessary even if the air smells clean. Mist can contain biological particles or mineral aerosols. Replace filters after the excursion whether or not they appear clogged.

Carry wound-sealing supplies and broad-spectrum medical provisions, but do not assume ordinary antibiotics will cover local organisms. Any puncture, bite, or contact with unknown fluid should be treated as a serious exposure.

### 3. Carry flotation, but use it cautiously

A compact buoyancy aid is worthwhile, especially one that can be inflated manually. However, do not treat it as permission to enter the water. Water depth and connectivity may not behave normally; flotation could hold you on a surface while some other part of your body is pulled through a local discontinuity.

Use flotation as an emergency measure only. Never swim for exploration.

## Equipment

### Navigation and communication

Carry redundant, mostly passive navigation tools:

- a physical survey line on a reel;
- bright, durable route tags;
- a mechanical compass and several electronic compasses;
- a map board and waterproof pencil;
- a measuring cord;
- a stopwatch or mechanical timer;
- visual signal panels;
- short-range radios in waterproof housings;
- simple whistles or impact alarms.

Do not rely on sound for navigation. The marsh separates apparent sound from actual distance. Footsteps may arrive before their makers, a nearby stream may sound distant, and a repeated voice may remain at a fixed apparent range.

Use a **line-of-sight and line-of-contact system** instead:

- remain physically tethered in poor visibility;
- maintain hand signals;
- use lamps with distinct colors or coded flashes;
- establish a check-in every few minutes;
- never pursue a voice, splash, animal shape, or light source without a line attached.

A route should be marked both on the ground and in your written record, but do not assume markers will remain unchanged. Recheck them on the return journey. If reeds begin bending toward your route or pools retain body-shaped distortions, regard that as evidence that the landscape has noticed you—not as a safe navigation aid.

### Lighting

Bring several types of light:

- ordinary waterproof lamps;
- lamps with red or infrared modes;
- chemical light markers;
- a mechanical or non-electronic backup.

The dim green radiance and illuminated stone veins may interfere with visual judgment. Do not use local glow as a landmark until you have confirmed that it remains fixed relative to you.

Keep one light aimed downward and one available for scanning. A headlamp alone may make the mist appear close and compress depth.

### Measuring and recording

Carry equipment to record conditions without touching them:

- time-lapse cameras;
- audio recorders;
- simple water-depth probes;
- laser or optical rangefinders, cross-checked against a cord;
- atmospheric sensors;
- dosimeters if available;
- sample containers for soil, water, vegetation, and air.

Do not make the sample-collection team the lead team. The safest first visit is observational. Every sample is a possible biological, chemical, informational, or dimensional contaminant.

Use physical notes as well as digital records. Electronics may record contradictory timings or images. Preserve the contradiction rather than “correcting” it.

## Choosing a route

### Prefer ridges, but verify them

The visible low ridges are described as comparatively reliable. Start and finish on exposed basalt shelves or raised ground. Avoid assuming that a dry-looking surface is solid; some apparently firm areas may be mineral crusts over water.

Probe every unfamiliar patch before stepping fully onto it. Use a long staff, but do not thrust it into glowing seams, pale films, or water displaying reflections inconsistent with the sky.

### Do not trust width or apparent depth

A broad basin may be shallow in one approach and effectively bottomless in another. A narrow channel may contain disproportionate depth. Approach water obliquely, from solid ground, and observe it for several minutes before crossing.

Avoid any pool that:

- reflects a different weather system;
- shows the pale subsurface grid;
- drains upward;
- brightens in response to thunder;
- produces sound without visible disturbance;
- contains ripples moving toward you;
- appears to have no bottom;
- mirrors your body in a delayed or altered way.

If a crossing is unavoidable, use a tether anchored on both sides, probe continuously, and send only one person at a time. In practice, an ordinary visitor should turn back rather than cross an anomalous pool.

### Treat floating vegetation as provisional ground

Floating mats may be safer than water, but they flex and shift. Spread weight gradually. Do not jump between mats. Keep a pole, tether, and flotation available.

Pale films and upright sheets of water are not safe surfaces merely because they appear solid. They may be surface-tension structures or temporary boundaries. Do not touch them.

## Conduct in the marsh

### Travel slowly and quietly

Fast movement creates avoidable mistakes. The land seems to respond to disturbance, and a small amphibious creature can cause surrounding stones to crack. Loudness may also make the environment acoustically worse.

Use a three-person pattern if possible:

1. a lead observer who probes ground and watches vegetation;
2. a central traveler carrying the main equipment;
3. a rear observer who monitors the route and checks for changes.

The group should remain connected by line in mist or heavy rain. If the line slackens unexpectedly, stop and count heads. Do not assume that a familiar voice or approaching footsteps represent a missing companion.

### Do not follow apparent animals

The antlered figure, underwater creature, winged presence, and other glimpsed fauna should be treated as hazards or perceptual phenomena. Do not approach, feed, photograph at close range, or attempt to rescue anything.

If an animal approaches:

- stop moving;
- avoid eye contact if it appears deliberate or intelligent;
- keep the group together;
- do not block its route;
- retreat along the prepared line if it comes within a few meters.

A lack of visible wildlife is not reassuring. The fauna may be hidden, out of phase with ordinary perception, or capable of affecting the environment without exposing itself.

### Do not answer the distant voice

The repeating voice is especially dangerous as a navigation lure. Do not call back, imitate it, acknowledge it, or attempt to determine its language. Mark the direction, note the time, and leave if it becomes clearer or begins using recognizable words.

Similarly, ignore footsteps that arrive before you make them. They are not proof of another traveler and may be a predictive or recursive event.

### Avoid memory experiments

Do not deliberately think of unknown locations to observe changes in moss. Do not test whether the land remembers you by repeating names, imagining structures, or describing places aloud. Such experiments may alter local processes or establish a stronger connection between you and the terrain.

Keep conversation practical and limited. Avoid discussing personal memories, home coordinates, or details of the departure world where the ground can hear or record you.

## Weather and shelter

The weather arrives in broad bands, so designate retreat points before entering exposed flats. A suitable temporary shelter should be:

- on stable elevated rock;
- away from root curtains and pools;
- outside the reach of upward-falling condensation;
- not directly beneath trees;
- not over visible grid lines or glowing stone veins;
- within sight of the route line.

Do not shelter beneath low twisted trees. Their roots move in still water, and trees may split in impossible directions.

A tent should have a fully waterproof floor and sealed seams, but it is not a guarantee of safety. Do not camp unless extraction has failed and you have no alternative. The longer you remain, the more likely the local system is to incorporate your presence into its equilibrium.

During heavy rain:

- stop crossing water;
- keep off exposed flats if the subsurface grid brightens;
- do not stand near vertical sheets of water;
- secure all loose equipment;
- monitor the sky and ground separately;
- return as soon as the route remains legible.

If the entire marsh produces a sound like an exhalation, stop and wait. Do not continue during the event. If mist begins forming a ceiling or the apparent weather changes inside a pool, retreat immediately.

## Food, water, and contamination

Bring all food and drinking water. Do not drink local water, even after boiling or filtration. It may be chemically contaminated, biologically active, or connected to systems that are not physically local.

Do not eat local plants, fungi, or animals. Do not even taste a sample.

At departure:

- remain in a designated decontamination area;
- seal all clothing and equipment;
- inspect boots and seams for organisms, roots, mineral films, and unexplained moisture;
- rinse with an approved decontaminant;
- quarantine samples and clothing;
- undergo medical and psychological observation;
- compare your baseline photographs, shadow, voice, handwriting, and memory against pre-entry records.

Any object that is warmer inside than outside, casts a detached shadow, changes weight, or appears to retain light should be quarantined rather than carried home casually.

## Signs to turn back immediately

Abort the visit if any of the following occur:

- the route markers move, grow, or point toward you;
- your shadow separates, merges with another, or remains behind;
- a pool reflects a sky or landscape not present overhead;
- your footsteps arrive before you take the steps;
- a companion’s voice comes from two directions;
- a familiar person or place appears in the mist;
- your equipment clocks disagree by more than the pre-established tolerance;
- the rain stops but surfaces continue accumulating water;
- the same event repeats at the wrong interval;
- the landscape begins making room for you;
- you feel that a feature is “expecting” you;
- a line, beacon, or instrument returns a result that is physically impossible;
- any member of the group cannot recall the purpose of the visit.

The last condition is especially important. If someone forgets why they came, do not argue with them in the marsh. Attach them to the return line and evacuate.

## Psychological preparation

The place will probably feel calm rather than hostile. That may be more dangerous than obvious aggression. Its strangeness is worn smooth by repetition; impossible events may feel ordinary after a while.

Prepare travelers to experience:

- false familiarity;
- distorted distance and timing;
- voices that sound meaningful;
- the impression of being watched without visible observers;
- visual afterimages in pools and shadows;
- memory associations that do not belong to them;
- an urge to keep walking because the next ridge appears close;
- the sensation that the landscape is adapting to their presence.

Use regular verbal check-ins with factual questions: name, date, group count, objective, direction of return. Do not use emotionally loaded memory prompts.

The correct attitude is neither panic nor wonder-driven exploration. Be polite, observant, and temporary. The marsh does not need to attack you to become part of you—or to make you part of it.

## Recommended minimum expedition

For an ordinary human traveler, the minimum responsible visit would consist of:

- three or more people;
- a pretested multiversal extraction system;
- sealed protective clothing and respirators;
- redundant lights, clocks, navigation tools, and communications;
- a continuous physical route line;
- all food and water imported;
- no swimming, sampling, or overnight stay;
- a strict time limit;
- an external abort authority;
- complete decontamination and observation afterward.

The safest preparation is to assume that the wetland is not merely a landscape containing an old machine. It may be an old machine that has become a landscape. Its water, weather, plants, sounds, and geological anomalies are probably components of one persistent operation. Visit only long enough to observe that operation without allowing it time to learn you in detail.
