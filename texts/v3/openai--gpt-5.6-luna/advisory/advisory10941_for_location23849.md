Treat this as a **small geothermal cavern with unknown air chemistry**, not as a harmless empty field. Its main dangers are not predators or sudden weather, but heat, poor ventilation, darkness, unstable crust, and the possibility that the atmosphere is unsuitable for human life.

## Before departure

### Establish your return method first

The apparent lack of an entrance is one of the most important facts. Do not assume the fractured cleft is usable as an exit, and do not enter unless you can reliably return by your own means.

Bring at least two independent ways to leave or summon help:

- A fixed, continuously powered return beacon or portal anchor.
- A personal locator that continues transmitting in darkness and through rock, if your technology permits it.
- A physical tether to the arrival point if the portal will remain open.
- A countdown or remote extraction system rather than a manually operated one.
- Enough power and air for your intended stay plus a generous reserve.

Because sound dies quickly and radio signals may be blocked by basalt, do not rely on shouting, echoes, or ordinary short-range communication. Establish communications while still at the arrival point and test them at several distances.

If arriving through a portal, mark the exact arrival area with nonflammable lights, reflective markers, or a beacon that can be found without line of sight. A normal flashlight beam may be absorbed almost immediately by the darkness and rough stone.

### Send instruments before yourself

If possible, deploy a small probe or remote sensor first. It should measure:

- Oxygen concentration and pressure.
- Carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur compounds, methane, hydrogen sulfide, and other volcanic gases.
- Temperature at several heights and directly above the floor.
- Humidity and particulate concentration.
- Radiation, especially radon or geothermal radiation.
- Ground stability and crust thickness.
- Magnetic and electrical interference.
- Whether the apparent darkness is ordinary absence of light or an unusual light-absorbing phenomenon.

The description suggests a dry, stagnant chamber. That may mean the air is breathable, but it may equally mean that heavier gases have accumulated in low areas. Do not infer safety from the presence of plants. Their biology may be entirely unlike yours, and they may tolerate atmospheres that kill humans.

## Clothing and life support

Use a sealed or semi-sealed exploration suit with its own air supply. At minimum, carry:

- A respirator with chemical and particulate filtration.
- A positive-pressure hood or full-face mask.
- An independent oxygen supply.
- A carbon-dioxide scrubber.
- Cooling capability, preferably active rather than merely insulating.
- Gloves that protect against hot rock and mineral dust.
- Sturdy boots with heat-resistant, puncture-resistant soles.
- Eye protection against airborne dust and the possibility of crust fragments.
- A helmet with mounted illumination, sensors, and communications.

The air is described as dry and close, with the smell of hot stone, iron, and chalk. Smell is not a reliable safety test. Volcanic gases can be dangerous at concentrations below the point where an ordinary person recognizes them, and some gases rapidly fatigue or disable the sense of smell.

Do not wear loose, absorbent clothing. Mineral dust will enter fabric, equipment joints, and respiratory gear. Use smooth outer surfaces that can be cleaned before returning through the portal.

The heat may feel tolerable at first because it is steady rather than radiant or gusting. That can be deceptive. In a dry, enclosed space, you may lose little heat through evaporation if the air is stagnant, while exertion causes your body temperature to rise gradually. Set a strict time limit and monitor core temperature, pulse, and hydration.

## Lighting and orientation

Bring several kinds of light:

- A broad, low-intensity lamp for close work.
- A narrow beam for examining cracks and walls.
- Independent backup lamps.
- Infrared or other nonvisible imaging.
- Markers that remain visible to your instruments rather than only to human eyes.

Do not use flame, flares, or anything that consumes oxygen. Besides the unknown atmosphere, combustible gases cannot be ruled out.

The darkness will impair depth perception and distance judgment. The chamber is small, but without a horizon the mind may lose its sense of scale and direction. Lay down a marked route as you move. Use a measured survey rather than memory. Keep one hand free for a mapping device or tether, and do not wander away from the arrival point merely because the chamber appears to have nowhere else to go.

Expect the cleft to be visually misleading. It may look like an entrance while being too narrow, unstable, or filled with a denser gas. Examine it remotely before approaching.

## Footwear and movement

The black floor is old basalt, but its surface is slightly rippled and broken by thin mineral crusts. Walk slowly and place each foot deliberately.

Avoid:

- Stepping on pale crust unless its strength has been tested.
- Putting weight near wall bases or around dark seams.
- Touching plants or inserting tools into cracks.
- Crossing the chamber quickly, since a fall in a confined cavern can become a medical emergency with no rescue access.

The porcelain-like cracking sound from the crust is a warning that it is brittle, not necessarily that it is shallow. A broken patch could expose a hotter layer, a gas pocket, or a sharp-edged depression. Use a probe or scanning pole. Carry a compact fall or extraction harness even though the terrain is nearly level; an ankle injury here would be far more serious than it would be outdoors.

The chamber’s rough walls absorb sound. Maintain visual or electronic contact with your team. A companion only a short distance away might not hear you call.

## Heat and geology

Assume the rock is hotter below the surface than it appears. Do not sit or lie directly on the basalt. Carry an insulating pad, and do not leave equipment resting on the floor for long without checking its temperature.

Look for signs of active geothermal danger:

- Rising temperature near particular cracks.
- Fresh mineral deposits.
- Discoloration or melting around seams.
- Localized gas readings.
- Vibrations or deep settling sounds.
- New fractures appearing in walls or ceiling.
- Any change in the steady warmth into pulses or drafts.

The broad roof is “close enough to feel present,” so inspect it before spending time in the center. Rounded, settled walls are reassuring, but they do not prove long-term stability. Keep away from areas with flaking material, fresh-looking fractures, or accumulations directly beneath ceiling irregularities.

Do not drill, hammer, cut, or generate substantial vibration. The chamber has reached an old balance; there is no reason to test how stable that balance is.

## Biological and contamination precautions

The sparse plants should be treated as unknown extremophile organisms, not as harmless grass. Do not touch, taste, burn, or collect them casually. Their powdery coating may be protective, reproductive, or chemically active. It could also become an inhalable irritant.

If samples are necessary:

- Use sealed sample containers.
- Collect only a tiny amount.
- Take environmental controls from bare mineral dust as well.
- Avoid uprooting living growth.
- Decontaminate all equipment before returning.
- Keep the samples isolated from your own atmosphere and biology.

There are no visible insects or animals, which lowers the likelihood of an immediate biological attack but does not make the ecosystem sterile. Microorganisms may inhabit the mineral crust, roots, or air. Use biological containment procedures on the way out, especially if your universe has vulnerable ecosystems.

Do not introduce water, food, organic waste, fires, or ordinary soil. Water could react with hot minerals, mobilize toxic compounds, or irreversibly alter the site. Human presence alone may be a major ecological disturbance in a place where almost nothing moves.

## Psychological preparation

The location will probably feel more confining than its physical dimensions suggest. There is no horizon, no weather, almost no sound, and very little visual information. After several minutes, your brain may begin manufacturing scale, movement, or direction. You may feel watched even though nothing is present, or may experience an impulse to leave immediately without knowing why.

Prepare for:

- Loss of normal depth and distance perception.
- A strong sense of isolation.
- Anxiety caused by the ceiling and lack of visible exit.
- Difficulty judging how long you have been there.
- Overconfidence after discovering that nothing attacks you.
- Fatigue from concentrating on every step and instrument reading.

Use a short, preplanned visit. Check in at fixed intervals. Do not extend the mission simply because “nothing is happening.” In this place, nothing happening is its defining condition, and that can make a person careless.

## A sensible visit profile

For an ordinary human, the safest expedition would be brief:

1. Send an environmental probe.
2. Establish and test the return beacon.
3. Enter in sealed protective gear.
4. Remain close to the marked arrival point.
5. Survey the floor, walls, ceiling, and cleft without touching them.
6. Record atmospheric and thermal readings.
7. Photograph or scan the plants and mineral deposits.
8. Leave before cooling, air, power, or attention becomes limiting.
9. Decontaminate equipment and inspect yourself before reentering your home environment.

Do not plan to camp there. There is no practical reason to remain overnight, and the chamber offers no reliable shelter from its own hazards. If the portal fails, the environment may be survivable for a while, but survival would depend entirely on your air, cooling, power, and rescue systems.

The place is not threatening in the usual sense. It is old, quiet, and apparently devoid of anything that wants to harm you. That is precisely why you should approach it with discipline: its dangers are passive, concealed, and indifferent. Prepare as though you were entering a sealed volcanic pocket, disturb as little as possible, and keep the way home more important than anything you might find there.
