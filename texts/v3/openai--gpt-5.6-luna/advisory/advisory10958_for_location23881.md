Treat this as a **high-altitude, geothermal, actively forming wilderness**, not as ordinary alpine terrain. The plateau is beautiful, but it is wet, cold, unstable, poorly mapped, and capable of killing a well-equipped traveler without ever becoming dramatically violent.

## Before departure

### Verify the atmosphere

Do not assume that “air” means breathable air.

Bring instruments to measure:

- Oxygen concentration and atmospheric pressure
- Carbon dioxide accumulation
- Hydrogen sulfide and sulfur dioxide
- Carbon monoxide and other volcanic gases
- Temperature, humidity, and wind speed
- Background radiation, if your multiverse technology permits such testing

The vents may release gases that are invisible and odorless at dangerous concentrations. Carbon dioxide can collect in hollows, ravines, caves, and around low-lying seepages. Hydrogen sulfide may smell like rotten eggs at first, but the sense of smell becomes unreliable after exposure. A personal gas alarm is essential; a handkerchief or ordinary mask is not protection.

Carry a dependable respirator or supplied-air mask, and preferably a compact oxygen reserve. Even if the atmosphere is chemically safe, the elevation may be high enough to cause altitude sickness, confusion, poor judgment, and rapid exhaustion.

### Plan for isolation

There are no roads, structures, established trails, or dependable landmarks. Mist can make a nearby escarpment vanish and make a distant cliff appear close. Sound will not travel reliably.

Bring:

- Two independent navigation systems
- A local topographic and geological map, if one can be made
- A beacon that can call your departure point or another traveler
- A physical compass and non-electronic backup
- A location transmitter that works through cloud, rock, and geothermal interference
- Enough power for several times the expected duration
- A prearranged extraction time and an automatic distress signal

Assume that a companion may become invisible within minutes in the mist. Use tether lines or fixed spacing in dangerous areas. Do not rely on shouting.

### Study the geology

The plateau is not merely mountainous; it is still rising and cracking. Before landing, identify:

- Stable shelves of old, dense rock
- Active faults and vent lines
- Areas of fresh fracture
- Likely rockfall paths
- Drainage routes and temporary flood channels
- Sheltered but ventilated campsites
- Elevated ground that is not itself a thin mineral crust

A landing zone that looks flat may be a slab poised to tilt, a hollow over a cavity, or a geothermal crust concealing hot water. If possible, arrive on a broad, established shelf rather than near the highest ridge or a vent field.

## Clothing and equipment

Dress for **cold rain, wind, spray, and freezing temperatures at the same time**.

Bring:

- A waterproof, windproof outer shell
- Several insulating layers, including synthetic or wool insulation
- Waterproof gloves with spare pairs
- A hood that seals around the face
- Eye protection against horizontal rain and mineral spray
- Moisture-wicking base layers
- Fully waterproof boots
- Gaiters to keep slush and gravel out
- Thick spare socks, stored in sealed bags
- Ice traction, but not aggressive climbing hardware alone
- A helmet with rockfall protection
- Trekking poles or a geological staff
- A climbing harness and rope for necessary cliff crossings
- A broad, stable load-bearing pack rather than a tall, top-heavy one

The ground will alternately be slick, loose, sharp, hollow, and hot. Use footwear with a broad sole and adaptable traction. Test every surface before committing your weight. Never jump from one slab to another; the impact may destabilize the slab, and the apparent gap may conceal a fissure.

Carry a compact shelter capable of resisting strong wind and wet snow, but do not assume a tent is sufficient protection near vents. Bring an insulating ground mat; the rock can be dangerously cold in one place and heat-conductive in another.

You will need a stove or other reliable heat source. There is no wood, and the vegetation is too sparse to provide meaningful fuel. Store fuel securely and never operate combustion equipment in an enclosed shelter.

## Arrival and first hours

Do not begin exploring immediately.

Spend the first period:

1. Confirming the atmosphere at ground level and in nearby hollows
2. Checking for altitude symptoms
3. Testing the stability of the landing area
4. Establishing communication and a return beacon
5. Marking the route with durable, removable markers
6. Watching the weather and vent behavior
7. Locating a safe water source
8. Choosing an emergency shelter site

Altitude sickness may begin as headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, dizziness, poor coordination, or irritability. Do not interpret confusion as spiritual insight or local enchantment; it may simply be hypoxia. Descend or use supplemental oxygen at the first serious symptoms. Do not allow an impaired person to continue upward or cross unstable ground.

The plateau’s weak, diffuse light will make distances and slopes difficult to judge. Wear UV protection even when the sun is hidden by cloud; altitude and reflected light from wet stone and cloud can still cause burns and eye injury.

## Choosing a camp

Select a broad, visibly solid shelf with:

- No fresh cracks
- No loose debris above it
- No active seepage beneath it
- No vent, fault, or steaming ground nearby
- Good drainage
- Some wind protection
- Enough elevation to avoid runoff and sudden water release
- Adequate ventilation

Do not camp:

- In a ravine
- In a shallow basin
- Beside a tarn or seepage
- On yellow, green, or chalk-white mineral deposits
- On a crust surrounding a vent
- Beneath a clean cliff face
- In a place where mist consistently pools
- On ground that feels warm, vibrates, or produces a hollow sound
- Directly over animal burrows

A ravine may look sheltered but can collect carbon dioxide, steam, freezing fog, or sudden water. A warm patch is not a blessing; it may be the roof of a geothermal channel.

Set up camp with a clear route to higher, stable ground. Keep boots, outer clothing, oxygen, light, and communication gear within reach while sleeping. Rock movement and vent releases may occur at night when visibility is worst.

## Water and food

There is abundant water, but very little of it should be considered safe.

Avoid:

- Water near mineral deposits
- Water with sulfur odor
- Warm water from fissures
- Cloudy gray slush
- Pools with colored crusts
- Water downstream from vents
- Water collected from unknown seepages

The water may contain sulfur compounds, heavy metals, dissolved gases, or organisms unlike anything in your biology. Carry your own supply for the first several days. If local water must be used, filter it, chemically treat it with agents known to function in that world, and boil it if boiling does not concentrate dangerous minerals. Ideally, use a distillation or purification system capable of removing dissolved metals and geothermal contaminants.

Bring high-calorie, ready-to-eat food. Cold and altitude increase energy expenditure, and cooking may be difficult in rain and wind. Keep food sealed; the burrowing animals may be attracted to warmth, scent, or unfamiliar materials rather than merely edible food.

## Moving across the plateau

Travel slowly and deliberately. The greatest danger is not a dramatic cliff but a surface that is almost stable.

Use a staff to test:

- Fresh gravel
- Pale mineral crusts
- Thin snow
- Slabs spanning cracks
- Ground around seepages
- The edges of tarns
- Areas where vegetation grows in unnaturally regular patches

Watch for:

- Fine cracks opening or closing
- New damp patches
- Steam appearing where none was before
- Scree moving without wind
- Regular tremors
- A low vibration underfoot
- Sudden changes in vent rhythm
- Water disappearing into new fissures
- Animals leaving an area abruptly

If the ground begins to tremble, move away from cliffs, overhangs, vents, and loose slopes. Do not run blindly through mist. Crouch if necessary, protect your head, and wait for the immediate movement to stop before relocating to a broad, stable area.

Keep well back from cliff edges. The clean faces are young and may fail without the gradual warning found in older mountains. Never stand below a fresh fracture, even if the rock appears dry and quiet. Wear a helmet whenever traveling below cliffs.

## Vents, steam, and geothermal hazards

Treat every vent as an industrial pressure system.

Stay far from:

- Active steam columns
- Crusted ground
- Yellow sulfur terraces
- Green-stained rock
- Areas that hiss, throb, or pulse
- Pools that are warm despite freezing air
- Fissures with moving vapor

Steam can scald at a distance, especially when wind shifts. Its edges may appear harmless while the central plume is lethal. A sudden pressure release can throw hot water, stones, or corrosive vapor well beyond the visible opening.

Never lean over a fissure or place a hand on warm rock. Do not enter caves or tunnels. The burrowing animals may tolerate gases and heat that humans cannot, and their tunnels could connect to unstable cavities or active geothermal channels.

If you detect sulfur, dizziness, headache, metallic taste, eye irritation, or unexplained breathlessness, leave immediately—preferably upwind and toward higher, open ground. Do not wait to identify the gas.

## Wildlife

The animals are likely to be more cautious than aggressive, but they are adapted to this place and you are not.

Give the mountain grazers a wide berth. Their broad hooves and sure footing will not help you if they panic, and a herd may move across unstable ground without warning. Do not approach an injured animal; others may remain nearby, and its behavior may be unpredictable.

Do not disturb bird nests or collect eggs. The white birds may blend into cloud and snow, making nests easy to step on. Keep food and bright equipment secured from smaller burrowing creatures.

Pay attention to animal behavior. A sudden absence of birds, grazers turning away from a shelf, or burrowers emerging and fleeing may warn of gas, vibration, steam, or a coming rock movement. The larger scavengers may also reveal where animals have died, which could indicate a poisoned water source or a dangerous vent rather than merely a food opportunity.

## Weather and visibility

The weather may change without a dramatic storm front. Fine rain can become wet snow; mist can thicken until the next shelf is invisible; a brief shaft of sunlight can create severe glare and then vanish.

Set strict turnaround times. Never continue into a new ravine late in the day simply because the weather has temporarily cleared. Mark every junction and record bearings. In mist, avoid wandering toward apparent “open sky”; it may be the edge of a cliff.

Use colored or reflective markers that remain visible in gray conditions, but retrieve them when leaving if they do not belong in the environment. Do not depend on tracks: water, frost, mineral dust, and shifting gravel will erase them quickly.

## Health precautions

Bring treatment for:

- Hypothermia
- Frostbite
- Altitude illness
- Cuts from sharp basalt and quartz
- Burns and scalds
- Eye irritation from mineral dust
- Respiratory exposure
- Sprains and fractures
- Severe dehydration

Keep wounds clean and covered. The wet cold will slow healing, while rough rock will produce frequent small injuries. Change damp clothing before resting. Protect hands and feet especially carefully; numbness is dangerous because it can conceal burns from warm rock or frostbite from slush.

Work in pairs for all difficult crossings. One traveler should remain able to summon help, operate the beacon, and assist the injured person. A minor ankle injury can become fatal here because the plateau offers no natural shelter and no easy descent.

## What to bring back

Assume that every sample may be chemically active, biologically novel, or unstable under different pressure and temperature. Use sealed, labeled containers with secondary containment. Do not carry live moss, animals, water, or vent deposits in an open pack. Mineral crusts may continue reacting, release gas, or contaminate equipment.

Most importantly, do not confuse the plateau’s emptiness with safety. It is empty because its processes are widely spaced, not because they are weak. The land is still deciding where cliffs, rivers, basins, and solid ground will be. Travel as though the entire surface were a construction site operated by pressure, cold, water, and heat—with no foreman, no warning lights, and no obligation to remain still while you cross it.
