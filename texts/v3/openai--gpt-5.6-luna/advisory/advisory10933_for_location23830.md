## First decision: do not go unprotected

An ordinary human should treat this location as an extreme deep-ocean environment combined with an unstable geological site. Do not attempt to visit in ordinary diving equipment, a submarine with a normal view-port, or a suit designed merely for vacuum or shallow water. The pressure, temperature, darkness, currents, suspended sediment, and vent chemistry are all independently dangerous. Together, they leave almost no margin for equipment failure.

If the multiverse traveler can observe the location remotely, that is strongly preferable. Send a probe first. If the destination is reachable only in person, arrive in a pressure vessel rather than directly in a suit.

## Establish where you are going to appear

The most important preparation is accurate spatial placement.

There is no useful surface, horizon, or obvious “up.” A careless arrival could place you:

- inside the seabed,
- inside a mineral chimney,
- in a vent plume,
- beneath a collapsing shelf,
- directly in a dense sediment cloud,
- or in a current strong enough to carry you into rock before you can react.

Your arrival point should be a verified pocket of open water, preferably several meters above the floor and well away from vents, shelves, chimneys, and obvious sediment slopes. Do not appear directly over an apparently empty patch unless remote scans confirm that it is not a thin crust over a cavity or unstable deposit.

The vessel should be able to move immediately in all directions. Do not rely on passive buoyancy. In a world with no clear surface, buoyancy may be weak, misleading, or locally defeated by temperature and mineral-density gradients. Use controlled thrusters and an inertial navigation system.

You should also arrange a return point before departure. A simple “go back to where I came from” mechanism may fail if the arrival location is moving, if the transition is affected by pressure, or if your return gate opens inside a current or sediment plume. Ideally, maintain a remote anchor or a beacon in a stable external frame.

## Use a pressure vessel, not a conventional diving suit

The pressure appears to be substantial and possibly increasing with depth, but the absence of a visible surface makes the pressure gradient impossible to judge by sight. Your vessel should therefore be rated for considerably greater pressure than the initial survey suggests, with multiple independent pressure hulls or compartments.

Essential features include:

- a fully rigid pressure hull;
- no large transparent windows;
- external cameras and acoustic sensors instead of direct viewing;
- redundant seals and life-support systems;
- automatic isolation of a breached compartment;
- a pressure monitor inside and outside the vessel;
- emergency ballast and propulsion;
- a means of cutting free from entanglement;
- a hardened exterior resistant to mineral abrasion;
- protection against sudden pressure waves.

If you must leave the vessel, wear a rigid articulated suit with its own pressure shell. A flexible suit would be vulnerable to compression, puncture, and the abrasive force of suspended sediment. Gloves and joints need special attention: the bottom is full of sharp shale plates, mineral needles, shell fragments, and unseen organisms.

Do not assume that a suit designed for outer space is adequate. A vacuum suit is built to keep pressure in; this location may apply crushing pressure from outside. The suit must be pressure-balanced or structurally reinforced for the ambient conditions.

## Prepare for absolute darkness

Carry several independent lighting systems, but do not expect them to illuminate the landscape in a useful way. The suspended silt will scatter light back into your face, and the water may turn a beam into a glowing wall of particles. Strong lights may also attract organisms or make you visible to animals that are otherwise unaware of you.

Use:

- narrow-beam lights for close work;
- low-power red or infrared systems if the local organisms respond to visible light;
- strobing or coded locator beacons;
- external lights placed away from the camera line to reduce backscatter;
- passive acoustic and lidar-like mapping if available.

Do not navigate by sight alone. The terrain is low, repetitive, and easily hidden. A ridge can be a shelf, a vent crust, or the edge of a collapsing cavity. A dark patch may be open water, a wall, or merely a region where the silt has thickened.

Navigation should combine inertial measurement, sonar, pressure readings, magnetic mapping if useful, and a physical tether or series of beacons. Lay a route as you go. Never assume you can find your way back through a landscape in which currents can rearrange the floor and visibility can disappear in seconds.

## Expect the water itself to move around you

The most dangerous feature may not be the pressure but the currents. They are not ordinary streams. They arrive as basin-wide shudders, carry sediment sideways, reverse direction, fold around shelves, and may change without warning.

Prepare the vessel for:

- sudden lateral acceleration;
- rapidly changing current direction;
- loss of visibility;
- abrasive sediment impacts;
- collisions with loose stone and mineral stalks;
- being pressed against the floor;
- being drawn toward a trench or vent field.

Stay well above the seabed during the first survey. The bottom is not a safe place to land. It is an active transport surface: stones move, silt slides, vents exhale, and apparently solid shelves can collapse.

A tether should not simply trail behind you. It may snag on chimneys, wrap around mineral needles, become buried in silt, or transmit dangerous forces during a current reversal. Use a controlled, buoyed or actively managed line with breakaway sections and a known retrieval system.

If the current begins to reverse, do not fight it near the floor. Move vertically or laterally into open water if possible, reduce speed, and preserve power for stabilization. A current that seems mild at first may become a thick, opaque sediment storm within moments.

## Protect life support from the environment

The vent fields may contain hot, acidic, alkaline, saline, metallic, or otherwise chemically aggressive fluids. Even if the water around them is cold, the plume can corrode equipment, foul filters, poison scrubbers, and damage exposed sensors.

Bring:

- replaceable external filters;
- chemical sensors;
- corrosion-resistant housings;
- independent oxygen and carbon-dioxide systems;
- emergency breathing reserves;
- sealed waste handling;
- decontamination capability;
- sample containers that can be isolated from the cabin.

Do not draw surrounding water into the cabin’s life-support system without analysis. The water may contain dissolved metals, sulfur compounds, toxic gases, microbial aerosols, or unfamiliar biochemistry. A leak in an external cooling loop could become a poisoning event.

The cold will also be a serious problem. The vents are not a source of safe warmth; their chemistry may be dangerous, their temperatures uneven, and their plumes turbulent. Use an internal thermal system and avoid depending on environmental heat exchange near active vents.

## Keep your biology sealed

The local organisms are sparse but highly specialized. That does not make them harmless. Their chemistry may be incompatible with human tissue, and their microbial films may be capable of colonizing equipment, clothing, or wounds.

Wear a completely sealed suit and avoid physical contact with:

- translucent fronds;
- bacterial films;
- vent crusts;
- sediment mats;
- dead stalk forests;
- animal remains;
- exposed seams in the mud.

A creature that seems slow or blind may detect pressure changes more accurately than you can see it. Do not approach fauna merely because it does not flee. In this environment, stillness may be a defensive behavior, an ambush posture, or simply the local equivalent of waiting.

Use sterilized sampling tools, and maintain separate “clean” and “dirty” compartments. Anything taken outside should remain quarantined until it has been examined. Do not bring living material directly into the crew cabin.

On return, undergo full decontamination and medical observation. The relevant danger may not appear immediately.

## Do not trust the floor

The floor is ancient, but “ancient” does not mean stable. It is a layered mass of compressed biological remains, mineral deposits, old cavities, and active chemical structures. It can fail with little warning.

Before approaching the bottom, scan for:

- hollow-sounding sediment;
- cracks and tilted shelves;
- active plume intersections;
- areas where silt is slowly streaming downhill;
- mineral forests leaning in one direction;
- fresh fractures;
- zones with unusual pressure or temperature;
- clouds emerging from beneath apparently solid ground.

Never stand beneath a shelf or mineral overhang. Never anchor to a vent chimney. Never enter a trench whose depth or continuation cannot be mapped. A collapse may begin as a tiny shift and then release an entire section of the basin.

If a collapse begins, visibility may vanish instantly. The safest response is to withdraw into open water, maintain orientation by instruments rather than vision, and avoid ascending or descending blindly. Falling debris and pressure waves may extend much farther than the visible collapse.

## Use acoustics carefully

Sound is likely to be more useful than sight, but it is also a source of danger. It travels far in water, and local organisms may rely on pressure and vibration rather than vision. Loud active sonar, cutting tools, or propulsion bursts could announce your presence across a very large region.

Use low-power, intermittent pulses where possible. Record the background vibration before making loud emissions. This will help distinguish natural basin movement from responses to your equipment.

Communication with the surface or departure point should not depend on ordinary radio. Use a combination of:

- acoustic modems;
- tethered fiber or optical cable where practical;
- timed beacon signals;
- inertial records;
- autonomous repeaters.

Assume that communication can be interrupted by sediment, mineral structures, current shear, or a collapse.

## Plan the mission around withdrawal, not exploration

The location invites prolonged observation because almost everything changes slowly. That is deceptive. A traveler can spend hours studying a patch of floor and fail to notice that the current, pressure, or route home has changed.

Set strict limits in advance:

- maximum time outside the vessel;
- minimum reserve power;
- minimum oxygen reserve;
- maximum allowable pressure differential;
- maximum distance from the return beacon;
- conditions that trigger immediate evacuation;
- a mandatory check-in schedule.

Abort the mission if:

- the water becomes opaque;
- current direction changes repeatedly;
- the vessel begins losing altitude toward the floor;
- unfamiliar sounds or pressure pulses appear;
- sensors show a chemical plume;
- the return beacon becomes unreliable;
- the terrain begins shedding silt or stone;
- any part of the vessel’s outer shell is damaged.

Do not remain to watch a collapse. Do not enter a vent field for warmth or curiosity. Do not follow a large animal into darkness. Do not descend simply because the bottom appears close; the apparent distance will be unreliable.

## Prepare psychologically

The most difficult part may be the lack of ordinary environmental cues. There is no sky, no horizon, no visible surface, no meaningful distance, and perhaps no reliable sense of ascent. The darkness is not empty. It is crowded with motion that you cannot see.

You should expect:

- disorientation;
- time distortion;
- anxiety caused by unseen movement;
- claustrophobia despite the vastness;
- fixation on distant sounds;
- difficulty judging whether the vessel is moving;
- panic when lights reveal only suspended particles.

Train for instrument-only navigation and practice remaining calm when every visual reference disappears. Keep the cabin brightly enough lit for instrument reading but dark enough that external cameras remain useful. Use artificial orientation cues: a stable cabin “down,” a continuously displayed heading, and spoken status reports.

Do not send a solitary traveler. At minimum, use a crewed or remotely supervised vessel with an autonomous return mode. The environment is too indifferent to human error for a single person to troubleshoot every failure while disoriented.

## Recommended visit profile

The safest ordinary-human visit would be:

1. A remote probe arrives first and maps pressure, current, chemistry, and biological activity.
2. A hardened, unmanned relay beacon is placed in a verified stable region.
3. A pressure-rated vessel arrives in open water well above the seabed.
4. The crew remains inside the vessel for the first visit.
5. Observation is performed with passive sensors and low-power lights.
6. A short, tethered excursion is made only if the bottom is confirmed stable.
7. No vents, organisms, or sediment layers are disturbed.
8. The vessel withdraws before the next major current reversal.
9. The return transition occurs from a known, open-water coordinate, not from the floor.
10. The traveler and vessel undergo quarantine and inspection afterward.

The proper attitude is not that you are visiting an underwater landscape. You are entering a moving geological body whose water fills every cavity and whose ground is still being made. There is no safe shore, no empty distance, and no reliable shelter. Prepare to be sealed, tethered, over-equipped, conservatively timed, and ready to leave at the first sign that the basin has noticed you.
