## Preparing to Visit the High Country

Treat this destination less like a wilderness and more like a stable but hostile physical anomaly. It is not dangerous because it attacks visitors. It is dangerous because ordinary assumptions about air, distance, time, matter, identity, and persistence will gradually stop being reliable.

Go only with a defined purpose, a fixed return procedure, and an agreed maximum duration. Do not visit merely to wander.

### 1. Prepare your body for the atmosphere

The air is breathable in the broad sense, but it is thin, extremely dry, and possibly chemically unusual.

Bring:

- A sealed or semi-sealed breathing mask with supplemental oxygen.
- A pressure or altitude suit if your home universe normally has dense air.
- Humidification for inhaled air.
- Eye protection; the air and mineral dust may dry or abrade the eyes.
- Lip balm, skin sealant, and gloves.
- A thermally insulating underlayer. The cold is not severe, but prolonged exposure will drain heat.
- A personal oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitor.
- A medical monitor capable of detecting hypoxia, dehydration, and altered pulse or cognition.

Do not trust your own judgment as an indicator of oxygen deprivation. The landscape is so quiet and motionless that confusion may feel like calm. Establish alarms that force you to return or shelter before you feel impaired.

Do not drink from the basins, lick the crusts, or consume any plant or animal matter. The mineral deposits are not ordinary salt, and their bitterness and electrical taste suggest that they may contain biologically or physically active compounds. Carry all water and food in sealed containers. Bring more water than an ordinary high-altitude expedition would require; the dryness will make you lose moisture without obvious sweating.

### 2. Bring equipment that does not depend on the landscape

Footprints may not accumulate. Marks may not remain. Distances may not behave consistently. Navigation must therefore be based on equipment carried by the expedition, not on visible terrain.

Bring at least:

- Redundant inertial navigation units.
- Independent clocks using different mechanisms.
- A magnetic compass, though do not assume magnetism will behave normally.
- Laser rangefinders and ordinary measuring tape.
- Radio beacons or low-power transponders placed along the route.
- A tether between traveling companions.
- A continuous body-camera recording all directions, especially behind you.
- A written log maintained by multiple travelers.
- A device that records local light levels, pressure, temperature, and radiation.
- A return beacon that is not physically attached to the departure point alone.

Use multiple navigation systems at once. If the inertial unit says you have traveled three kilometers but the horizon says you have crossed no ground, believe neither without comparison.

Do not use a single landmark as your sole reference. Looking away may multiply it. Looking back may replace it with another version. Mark all landmarks with active beacons and record them from several angles. Assume that a perfect visual match is not necessarily the same object.

### 3. Establish strict travel rules

Travel in a group of at least three. Two people are too easily trapped in mutual agreement about what they are seeing. Every traveler should carry an independent clock and write down observations without consulting the others.

Adopt rules such as:

1. No one travels out of tether range.
2. No one follows a creature, shadow, distant ridge, light, voice, or apparent shortcut.
3. No one turns back alone.
4. No one changes direction because the horizon appears nearer.
5. No one accepts an apparent return to camp without checking beacon codes and the written log.
6. Every member must periodically state their name, departure time, destination, and the number of companions.
7. If one person reports seeing an extra companion, stop and compare visual, thermal, and radio records before approaching anyone.

The landscape may permit a ridge to be reached in a few steps from one direction and hours from another. This is not necessarily a shortcut. It may be a change in adjacency rather than movement through space. Keep a fixed route and do not experiment with “wrong” directions unless that is the explicit purpose of the mission.

### 4. Do not trust your eyes, especially shadows

The shadows are not reliable descriptions of their owners. Extra limbs, mouths, antlers, or accompanying bodies may be projections, alternate forms, or something that merely shares the shadow.

Never use a shadow to identify a person or animal.

At intervals, illuminate yourself from several directions and compare the results. Keep your own light sources controlled. A shadow that contains an additional figure is not automatically an attack, but it is a reason to leave the area. Do not touch a shadow, step deliberately into an unusual one, or align yourself with a creature whose shadow appears to be waiting for you.

Use polarized lenses, thermal imaging, and depth sensors where possible. If an object is visible but has no thermal or depth signature, treat it as visually present but materially uncertain. Do not lean against it, sleep beneath it, or place equipment on it.

### 5. Treat the animals as environmental hazards, not wildlife

The animals do not appear conventionally aggressive, but their lack of response is not reassurance. They may overlap physically, fail to react to contact, or occupy more than one position. A body that is harmless in one state may be dangerous to disturb in another.

Maintain a clear corridor around yourself. Move slowly and announce your approach with light or a low mechanical signal rather than touch. Do not feed, ride, mark, dissect, or collect any creature. Do not step between a visible animal and its shadow.

Avoid resting in dense herds or among the clustered forms on slopes. Their bodies may be warm, cold, solid, transparent, or intermittently absent. If a creature begins matching your position—especially one that remains on the same ridge, then appears beside you without visibly approaching—do not confront it and do not attempt to outrun it. Note its location, stop looking directly at it, and withdraw along your marked route while keeping companions and instruments between it and you.

If the animal remains in the same apparent place from multiple directions, assume it is not a normal organism. Do not bring it back through the departure point.

### 6. Avoid the features that invite investigation

Several things should be treated as prohibited unless the expedition exists specifically to study them.

#### The seven-rung ladder

Do not descend it.

It does not lead underground in the ordinary sense. It produces an orientation reversal and an apparent emergence from below despite being shorter than a person. This may be a harmless spatial loop, or it may exchange travelers with another layer of the region. The identical cairns and shadows suggest that local arrangement around it is not trustworthy.

If you must examine it, do so remotely. Do not touch the rungs, place equipment on them, or send an animal or drone down unless you are prepared for the possibility that it returns from a different location, time, or condition.

#### The dark seam in the sky

Do not attempt to widen, reach, photograph through at close range, or pass beneath it while experimenting. It may be a window, a scar, or a boundary between versions of the plateau. The apparent scent and coolness of rain are not evidence that rain is actually present.

Do not follow any change in the sky’s appearance. Return to your established route before the seam vanishes.

#### The sealed hut or shrine

Do not open it. Its materials have resisted the same erosion that simplified the rest of the country, which makes it one of the few objects there that has not reached the local equilibrium. A sealed structure may be preserving something—or preventing it from entering the landscape.

Record it from a distance. Do not remove fibers, plates, bones, or stones.

#### The old wall and cairns

They may be useful as reference points, but do not rebuild them, rearrange them, or add your own stones. Identical stones and identical shadows suggest that repetition and placement may have unusual consequences.

### 7. Plan around the dimmings

There is no true night, but periods of dim light bring out additional organisms or reveal forms normally hidden. These periods should be treated as a change in hazard level.

Before entering, establish a dimming protocol:

- Stop travel when light levels begin to fall.
- Gather the party and perform a full head count using names, heat signatures, and radio responses.
- Retract all route markers or switch them to a coded pulse.
- Stay inside a sealed, independently powered shelter.
- Do not investigate newly visible forms.
- Do not answer signals, movements, or apparent voices from beyond the shelter.
- Do not assume that something that was outside during the dimming is gone when the light returns.

The additional animals may not be arriving. They may always be present and merely becoming visible. This distinction is not useful in the field. Treat the result the same way.

A rigid shelter is preferable to a tent if practical, but do not use the ancient structures for cover. Bring your own habitat with a lockable entrance, controlled atmosphere, external cameras, and a way to verify that the interior has not changed. Every person should sleep tethered to the shelter’s central frame, not merely to another traveler.

### 8. Keep time by several definitions

Time in the high country may pass normally for the body while failing to produce normal changes in the environment. A herd may move one step in centuries, and shadows may not track the sun correctly. Do not use environmental change to estimate time.

Record:

- Local elapsed time.
- Subjective time, according to each traveler.
- Biological time, through pulse, sleep cycles, and metabolic monitors.
- Equipment time, from independent clocks.
- Communication delay with the departure point.

Set a maximum stay based on your own clocks, not on daylight, hunger, weather, or visible progress. A person who says, “We have only been here a short while,” may be correct in one sense and disastrously wrong in another.

Avoid sleeping unless the expedition is designed for it. Dreaming, memory consolidation, and identity may be unusually vulnerable in a place where bodies and positions can persist without ordinary change. If sleep is necessary, maintain continuous monitoring and wake travelers in staggered shifts.

### 9. Bring nothing home casually

Before returning, inspect every person and object for:

- Unidentified dust or residue.
- Mineral crust.
- Fibers, plates, skins, or animal matter.
- Shadows that do not correspond to the body.
- Additional reflections or camera images.
- A second version of any tool, sample, or traveler.
- Changes in clock synchronization.
- Unexplained gaps in recorded footage.
- Objects that remain warm, cold, wet, or animate without a source.

No biological sample should be imported without sealed containment and a quarantine period in a sacrificial intermediate universe or isolated laboratory. Do not bring an apparently dead creature back. In the high country, death does not remove presence, and a corpse may already be functioning as habitat.

Perform the return in stages. Establish a quarantine station on the departure side. Recount the travelers before crossing, immediately after crossing, and again after a delay. Compare spoken memories independently rather than allowing the group to synchronize its account first.

Be especially suspicious if the group returns with exactly the expected number of people but one member’s shadow contains another person.

### 10. Prepare psychologically

The greatest danger is not panic. It is adaptation.

The high country will make impossible conditions feel ordinary. After several hours, you may stop noticing the silence. After several days, you may accept that the same animal is on every ridge. After enough time, you may find the absence of change comforting and begin to resent the idea of leaving.

Before departure, write down what normal change means to you: hunger, aging, weather, decay, movement, consequences, and distance. Carry the statement with you. Read it at regular intervals.

Do not remain long enough to become accustomed to the country’s equilibrium. The place does not need to kill you. It only needs to persuade you that returning to a world where things grow old, fall apart, and move from one place to another is unnecessary.

The proper attitude is cautious respect. The high country is not an empty plateau, and it is not necessarily hostile. It is a completed environment—one in which life, stone, shadow, and distance have settled into final forms. Enter with oxygen, redundant instruments, a tether, a return deadline, and the willingness to leave without understanding what you saw.
