## Preparation for the Limestone City

Treat this as an inhabited, enclosed environment rather than an archaeological site. It is not abandoned, and it is not a place in which ordinary assumptions about gravity, sound, water, time, or ownership are reliable.

Your priorities should be:

1. **Remain physically dry and warm enough.**
2. **Preserve your sense of direction and time.**
3. **Avoid interfering with the city’s maintenance systems.**
4. **Make your presence legible to the inhabitants without being intrusive.**
5. **Maintain a dependable route or method of return.**

### Before entering

Do not enter alone. Use at least a pair, preferably three travelers: one explorer, one observer, and one person responsible for the route and timing. The city’s sound behavior makes ordinary verbal communication unreliable. Agree beforehand on visual hand signals, physical tugs on a line, and scheduled check-ins.

Do not assume that the apparent entrance will remain available once crossed. Prepare a return protocol that does not depend solely on radio, shouted instructions, or visible landmarks.

Bring:

- A **waterproof, low-profile suit** with sealed seams.
- Warm underlayers. The city is not freezing, but prolonged wetness and immobility will produce dangerous cooling.
- Gloves with good grip and enough dexterity to handle ropes, jars, and stone fittings.
- Soft-soled boots with excellent traction. Avoid hard heels; they will make you loud and may provoke unwanted echoes.
- A close-fitting helmet. The ceilings, bridges, chains, ledges, and low arches are all hazards.
- Eye protection against constant dripping.
- A compact breathing mask or respirator, and ideally an oxygen/CO₂ monitor. The air is probably breathable, but the chamber is enclosed and the warm-air shafts are unexplained.
- Waterproof lights, with several independent power systems. Use dim, adjustable illumination rather than a powerful beam.
- A mechanical timer or clock sealed against moisture. Do not rely exclusively on electronics.
- A physical tether or route line, but use it sparingly and never fasten it to inhabited structures without permission.
- Nonmetallic or corrosion-resistant tools.
- Sealed food that requires no cooking.
- A small emergency heat source, but do not plan to use flame inside the city.
- Medical supplies for cuts, slips, exposure, and respiratory irritation.
- A supply of clean water from your own reality. Do not depend on local water for drinking.
- Quiet markers that can be removed afterward. Avoid paint, chalk, cutting marks, or anything that changes the stone.

Avoid loose straps, dangling equipment, hard cases, exposed electronics, noisy buckles, and anything that could catch on ladders or bridges. The city is too compressed for ordinary expedition gear. Keep your pack narrow and close to your body.

### Do not make the entrance your first objective

The entrance is located by following a dry knocking beneath the general sound of the city. This is a poor task for an exhausted or disoriented visitor. Before attempting to enter, spend some time listening from the threshold, if possible. Record the sound pattern mechanically and electronically. Determine whether the knocking remains consistent, whether it changes when you move, and whether your companions hear the same thing.

If the sound seems to move when you approach it, stop. Do not chase it blindly.

The black-water arch should be treated as a boundary, not as an ordinary tunnel. Before crossing, secure your return line, mark the point in a reversible way, and confirm that all members of your party have agreed on the crossing. Do not enter while carrying anything you would be unable to abandon. There may be no conventional “back” once the passage is behind you.

### Expect extreme compression

The city is small enough that ordinary walking will often be impossible. You may be climbing while someone else is passing beneath you, with a drain at your feet and a roof or bridge within reach of your head. Assume every surface is being used by someone or something, even if it appears empty.

Move slowly. Test every step and handhold before committing your weight. Do not lean against walls casually; some of the walls contain channels, dwellings, glowing seams, or hollow passages. Do not stand beneath a water-catching cup, suspended chain, or overhanging balcony without first checking its condition.

The city is maintained, but that does not make it safe for a visitor. Its inhabitants know where the weak steps, hidden drains, and shifting routes are. You do not.

### Keep yourself dry, but do not try to dry the city

The water is constant and likely not entirely ordinary. It tastes of chalk and iron, and its movement does not always follow gravity. Do not drink from channels, bowls, cisterns, or dripping seams unless a resident unmistakably offers it and you have determined that doing so is culturally and biologically safe.

Do not block, dam, divert, or “improve” a channel. A channel that appears to run through a dwelling may be part of a larger balance. Do not place equipment on the floor without checking whether water enters from beneath. A vessel may fill from below, and its weight may change depending on whether it is carried uphill or downhill.

Use your own sealed water and food. Keep them protected from the damp. Store no important paper, fabric, medicine, or battery outside waterproof containers.

Never attempt to dry yourself with a fire. Flame is poorly suited to the atmosphere, and smoke would be a serious intrusion in a compact inhabited chamber.

### Protect your hearing and your sense of causality

The city will be louder than its activity suggests. The rain-like sound is continuous, while impacts and voices may arrive from unexpected locations. A hammer strike can sound nearby when it is not. A footstep may be repeated by several surfaces. A voice spoken against stone may emerge elsewhere minutes or hours later.

Use hearing protection, but not so much that you lose warning sounds. Active noise cancellation may be dangerous if it alters your perception of the central wheel or the dry knocking. Prefer passive, adjustable protection.

Do not shout to locate companions. Loud noises do not behave normally and may become a uniform presence throughout the city. Do not use explosive markers, alarms, whistles, or sonic instruments.

Keep communication visual whenever possible. If you must speak, speak softly into open air, not directly against a wall. Never whisper confidential information into limestone. It may be heard in another dwelling long after you have forgotten saying it.

The central wheel will be audible everywhere. Do not use its apparent loudness to navigate. It is not a useful distance indicator. Its rhythm is more like a condition of the city than a sound produced at one particular point.

### Treat the mineral glow with caution

The pale seams are not ordinary lamps. Their brightness varies with water, and touched areas retain blurred impressions of hands and tools. Do not touch them casually, and do not repeatedly press your hands against them to create a record.

Wear gloves. If you must examine a seam, photograph or measure it without making contact. Do not scrape, chip, collect, or illuminate it with intense light. It may be an archive of contact, a living mineral process, or a mechanism whose effects you do not understand.

Do not assume that your visual record is complete. In the darker parts, a face or figure may be present without being visible to you. Use a slow, diffuse light and look for reflected water, movement, and shadows rather than attempting to flood the entire chamber with brightness.

### Make your presence nonthreatening

The inhabitants may not be surprised by visitors, but that does not mean they welcome disruption. Their culture appears organized around continuity, repair, and proper placement.

On first encounter:

- Stop moving.
- Lower your hands and tools.
- Do not aim a light directly at a face.
- Do not reach toward a person, niche, jar, arrangement of stones, or curtain.
- Allow the resident to decide whether to approach.
- Offer a simple repeated gesture of harmlessness, such as placing your empty hands visibly on your chest and then beside you.
- Wait for imitation or another clear response before proceeding.

Do not assume that silence means consent. Do not assume that an expressionless face means hostility. Their communication may be based on placement, rhythm, maintenance, and water flow rather than speech.

Carry a few objects that can serve as neutral gifts or trade items, but choose them carefully. A clean ceramic bead, a smooth stone from your own world, or a durable hand tool might be suitable. Do not offer food, living matter, fabric, metal that may corrode, or anything technologically conspicuous. Never take an object in exchange unless its removal is explicitly indicated.

The most respectful contribution may be a small act of maintenance: lifting a fallen item, holding a rope, clearing a loose obstruction under instruction. Do not repair, reroute, sharpen, or rebuild anything on your own. In this city, a “helpful” alteration could damage a balance that has been maintained for centuries.

### Avoid the central mechanism

The wheel and bronze-cup chain are the city’s most important landmark and its most obvious temptation. Do not touch the wheel, cups, paddles, chain, basin, or its supports. Do not stop it for an experiment. Do not speed it up, add water, remove a cup, or place an instrument in the mechanism.

Its purpose may be symbolic, hydraulic, temporal, or social rather than mechanical. The fact that its motion appears useless is not evidence that it is unimportant. The inhabitants organize themselves around its rhythm. Even a brief interruption could be interpreted as an attack.

Stay outside its working space. If you need to cross near it, wait for a resident to indicate where to step.

### Keep away from the drainage system

The blind, jointed creatures should not be touched, collected, fed, crushed, or transported out of the city. Their biological status is uncertain, and their role may be more important than it appears.

If one enters the inhabited area, watch from a distance. Do not interfere with the household ritual surrounding it. The appropriate response is observation, not specimen collection.

Keep your hands out of grates and channels. Small animals are not the only thing that may be moving there. Sound traveling through the drains could also make activity seem nearer or farther than it is.

### Maintain a route without marking the city

Ordinary maps will be unreliable because the city is vertically layered and acoustically nonlocal. Make a three-part record:

1. **Physical position:** ledges, bridge height, water direction, ladder count.
2. **Sound pattern:** wheel rhythm, distinctive drip clusters, chain clicks, tool strikes.
3. **Human confirmation:** which resident, dwelling, or maintained feature indicated the route.

Do not trust a single landmark. The same sound may seem to come from every direction, and a familiar passage may appear different when approached from another level.

Use a retractable guide line only where it will not obstruct residents. Never hammer anchors into the limestone. Never tie to a drainage chain, bridge rail, cup, curtain, tool, or structural repair. If the line becomes snagged, abandon it rather than damaging the city.

Before leaving a location, ensure that every member of your party can identify the route by more than sight.

### Be careful with touch and personal traces

Touch has unusual persistence here. Handprints can remain in mineral seams for minutes, and long-term contact has accumulated into visible records. Assume that your contact may be remembered by the stone.

Wear gloves throughout the visit. Avoid leaning, gripping, or resting against glowing surfaces. Do not leave blood, hair, fibers, food, waste, or manufactured residue behind. Remove every marker and piece of equipment you brought in, unless deliberately given away.

Do not touch the inhabitants unless they clearly initiate contact and maintain it. A handshake may be an enormous and durable statement in a place where touch becomes part of the walls.

### Prepare psychologically for stillness

The most dangerous feature may be the city’s lack of change. You will see labor without progress, repair without improvement, and water without variation. Your own body will tire, hunger, shiver, and age while the surroundings seem unaffected. This can produce a powerful sense that you, rather than the city, are the temporary or unreal element.

Use strict visit limits. Set an external duration before entering and obey it. Do not extend the visit because “nothing has happened.” In a place where voices can arrive late and the wheel never stops, subjective time may become unreliable.

Take regular physical checks:

- Are you still warm?
- Are your fingers functioning?
- Are you oriented?
- Do you remember when you last drank or ate?
- Are you hearing a companion’s current voice or an old one?
- Is your equipment actually becoming wet, lighter, heavier, or displaced?

If anyone begins following a delayed voice, repeating a route without recognizing it, staring at the wheel, or treating an old impression in the wall as a living person, begin withdrawal immediately.

### Leaving

Do not wait for the city to become quiet. It probably will not.

Do not assume the exit is marked by a visible change. The dry knocking is the relevant guide, but it may be masked by fatigue, water noise, or delayed sound. Use the recording and your agreed search pattern rather than wandering toward the first apparent repetition.

Before approaching the black-water arch:

- Account for every person.
- Account for every item.
- Confirm that no one has entered a side passage or dwelling.
- Confirm that no line, tool, or marker remains attached to the city.
- Say farewell or make a visible acknowledgment to any inhabitants present.
- Do not take city water, mineral, clay, bone, copper, animals, or touch-marked material with you.

If a resident attempts to stop you, do not force passage. Pause and determine whether they are warning you, requesting an item, or merely observing. If the black water changes level, weight, sound, or apparent depth, postpone crossing until the change is understood or until the residents indicate that it is safe.

Once outside, quarantine your clothing, equipment, water containers, and recordings. Inspect for pale drainage creatures, mineral growth, unexplained moisture, delayed sounds, and objects that were not in your possession before entry. Do not immediately return to the surface settlement or your home reality if the dry knocking can still be heard.

The city is not hostile in the ordinary sense. It is more dangerous than hostile: it is complete. It has no need for you, no obvious fear of you, and no reason to adapt to your expectations. Visit as a temporary disturbance in a system that has learned to preserve itself. Move quietly, touch little, repair nothing without instruction, and leave before the place begins to feel more familiar than the world above.
