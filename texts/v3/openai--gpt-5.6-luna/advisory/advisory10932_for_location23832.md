## Prepare as though you are entering a working industrial installation, not a bath or natural cavern

The reservoir is compact, bright, and beautiful, but it is also a flooded machine with sharp masonry, moving water, manual mechanisms, and no forgiving margin for mistakes. An ordinary human traveler should not enter casually or alone.

### Before entering

Arrange a guide who understands the waterworks and can tell you:

- Which entrance and stairway are currently safe.
- Where the current is strongest.
- Which gates are open and which must not be operated together.
- Where the safe, dangerous, and pressure-vessel water marks are.
- Which galleries have air and which portions are completely flooded.
- How to signal an emergency when speech becomes useless.
- How to reach the exit if the water level changes.

Do not assume the reservoir is open to visitors merely because it is accessible. Someone may be relying on the machinery to supply water, drain a settlement, irrigate fields, or keep foundations from flooding. A curious traveler opening a gate could injure workers or damage the entire installation.

Visit when the machinery is running at its lowest ordinary flow, if the operators have such a period. Avoid arriving during maintenance, heavy rain, construction, or a scheduled filling or draining cycle.

### Breathing and swimming equipment

Your first concern is that the entrance takes you below the waterline immediately. Bring equipment appropriate to the deepest section you intend to enter:

- A reliable breathing apparatus, not merely the expectation that there will be air above the water.
- A mask or goggles that seal well.
- A secure tether or guide line, attached to you in a way that can be released quickly if it snags.
- Buoyancy control; loose flotation may be more dangerous than helpful in strong current.
- A knife or cutting tool for emergency release from rope, reeds, or harness lines.
- A second air supply if you will be fully submerged or entering any vertical well.

Do not rely on the shafts of sunlight for ventilation. They may provide some air, but they may also be narrow, distant, blocked, or connected only to the upper structure. The reservoir contains lamps and workers, so breathable air probably exists in at least some galleries, but that does not guarantee safe air everywhere. The machinery, flames, damp plaster, and enclosed geometry may produce poor circulation. Carry an air-quality monitor if your technology permits it, and avoid putting your face into any stagnant recess.

If your equipment is technologically conspicuous, arrange permission and explain its purpose. Do not allow unfamiliar hoses, tanks, or lights to obstruct walkways, catch on the paddle wheel, or interfere with the lift-bucket chain.

### Clothing and personal gear

Wear close-fitting, hard-wearing clothing rather than robes, scarves, or anything with dangling straps. The current, the wheel, the chain, and the rough masonry all create snag hazards.

Bring:

- Water shoes or boots with firm soles and good grip.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity.
- A helmet, preferably with a low-profile light.
- Eye protection against spray and falling plaster.
- A waterproof pouch for identification, medication, and communication tools.
- A simple whistle or mechanical signaling device, provided its sound can be distinguished from machinery.
- A waterproof slate, marked cards, or agreed hand signals for communication.

The water is warm enough to feel comfortable at first, which makes it easy to underestimate exposure. You may spend a long time wet, exerting yourself against a current, and then stand in a draft beneath the shafts. Bring dry clothing and a way to warm yourself after leaving. Avoid oils, perfumes, dyes, or lotions that could contaminate the water or leave residue on plaster and tile.

Do not drink the reservoir water. Its clarity is not proof of safety. It is a working water system, possibly carrying mineral deposits, construction dust, human contamination, algae, or substances used in the upper settlement.

### Movement inside

Move slowly and keep one hand available for a wall, rail, or guide line. The pale brick terraces may look shallow, but water will distort their apparent depth and the bright reflections will make edges difficult to judge. A step that appears to be one foot below you may be several feet away, and a sharp ledge may be hidden by glare.

Expect:

- Sudden lateral pushes near the sluice.
- Downward or upward pulls at channels and wells.
- Slippery glazed tile.
- Loose plaster falling from arches.
- Sharp replacement blocks and unfinished mortar.
- Rope, chains, reeds, and floating frames crossing the water.
- Deep shadows beneath galleries despite the general brightness.
- Changes in water level while you are still inside.

Stay out of the central current unless the guide specifically directs you there. Never approach the paddle wheel, lift-bucket chain, sluice mouth, grate, or gate mechanism merely to inspect it. A crude machine can be more dangerous than a sophisticated one because it has fewer guards, less predictable movement, and great force concentrated in exposed parts.

Keep clear of the water-management marks on the pillars. If the water reaches the dangerous level, leave immediately; do not wait to see whether it rises further. The pressure-vessel mark is not a warning for visitors to contemplate. It indicates a condition under which walls, arches, gates, or foundations may be carrying forces they were not intended to bear.

### Communication and emergencies

Speech will be unreliable. Before entering, establish unmistakable signals for:

- Stop.
- Follow me.
- Return to the exit.
- I am out of air.
- I am caught.
- The level is rising.
- Machinery has stopped or changed.
- Someone is injured.

Use a buddy system, with one person watching the route and another watching the machinery and water level. Nobody should enter a flooded stairwell or well merely because another person disappeared from sight. In the blue depth and repeated echoes, distance and direction will be deceptive.

Keep the route back to the entrance in mind rather than relying entirely on memory. The bright shafts, reflections, and repeating masonry may make every gallery look similar. A fixed line is safer than trying to navigate by the strongest light. Do not tie yourself to a permanent structure unless the guide approves it; a line can become a hazard if a gate opens or a frame moves.

If the current becomes stronger, retreat immediately. Do not attempt to cross it by force. Get low, face the flow, hold a secure point, and follow the guide’s route to calmer water. If caught in a channel, release anything nonessential before trying to free yourself. Your breathing apparatus and guide line matter more than tools or belongings.

### Respect the workers and the installation

This is not a ruin. It is newly built, maintained, and inhabited by people doing difficult work. Treat every object as functional.

Do not:

- Touch water-level gates.
- Move wedges, ropes, hooks, jars, tools, or buckets.
- Scrape algae from bricks.
- Pick reeds or disturb their baskets.
- Lean on fresh plaster or replacement masonry.
- Put foreign objects into channels or grates.
- Add food, offerings, dyes, chemicals, or “helpful” repairs.
- Light a lamp near spray or move a lamp closer to the water.
- Block a ledge, stair, shaft, or tool platform.
- Photograph or illuminate workers without permission.

If you bring electric lights, keep them dim enough that they do not blind people working in the water or obscure the established lamps. If you bring a camera, protect it from reflections and avoid using flash; the sudden glare could interfere with signals and make the already confusing surfaces harder to read.

Expect the residents to regard you less as a tourist than as a potential obstruction, hazard, or source of contamination. Wear whatever protective or modest clothing the local workers use, accept instruction without argument, and offer practical compensation for a guide’s time. If there is no common language, learn the local words for *stop*, *danger*, *gate*, *current*, *air*, and *exit* before arrival.

### Sensory and psychological preparation

The reservoir will feel more exposed than a dark underground chamber. Sun shafts, white plaster, flame, and moving water make everything visible, yet the roar will prevent easy conversation and the blue distance will hide depth. Prepare for:

- Continuous noise that makes thought and speech tiring.
- Echoes that seem like additional people or machinery.
- Vertigo from looking down through clear water.
- Claustrophobia when the ceiling and water surface converge.
- Disorientation caused by repeated steps, pillars, and reflections.
- A false sense of safety caused by the warm water and inviting light.

Use short visits at first. Do not remain simply because the place is comfortable. Warm water can dull your awareness of fatigue, and fascination is a real hazard in a chamber designed to draw the eye toward moving water and light.

### A sensible minimum plan

For a first visit, go with at least two trained local workers and one companion. Enter only the shallow, calm, well-ventilated galleries. Keep a secured guide line to the entrance. Carry redundant air, lights, and communication equipment. Set a fixed time limit. Leave before anyone is tired, cold, short of air, or tempted to explore “just one more” stairwell.

The right attitude is respectful caution. The reservoir is not haunted, wild, or deliberately hostile. Its danger comes from ordinary forces operating in a confined space: gravity, current, pressure, heat, sharp stone, poor visibility, and human machinery. If you treat it as a living public utility rather than an underground marvel, it is visitable. If you treat it as a bright swimming pool or an ancient temple, it can drown you very quickly.
