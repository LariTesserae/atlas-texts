## Prepare as if visiting an active planetary interior exposed to vacuum

This is not a wilderness excursion and not quite a mountain expedition. It is a world still assembling itself. The main danger is not wildlife, weather, or ordinary volcanic activity; it is that the ground, the air, distance, and perhaps the geometry of the region are all provisional.

An ordinary human should visit only with a specialized expedition, a fixed extraction plan, and equipment capable of surviving an environment somewhere between a high-altitude wasteland, a tectonic construction site, and a weakly hostile dimensional anomaly.

### 1. Do not assume the atmosphere is breathable

The description implies an atmosphere, but not a normal one. It may be extremely thin, chemically unsuitable, intermittently compressed, or simply not compatible with human lungs.

Bring:

- A fully sealed pressure suit, not merely cold-weather clothing.
- Independent breathing gas for at least twice the planned duration.
- Redundant oxygen and carbon-dioxide scrubbers.
- A helmet with a hard visor and internal illumination.
- A suit capable of handling both low ambient pressure and sudden pressure fronts.
- A particulate and chemical filtration system for the “dry mineral taste” carried by turbulence.
- Emergency reserve gas sufficient for a delayed extraction.

Do not remove the helmet merely because the air feels still or because the location appears free of dust. The atmosphere may be changing composition or pressure without visible warning.

### 2. Treat the ground as moving infrastructure

The terrain is not stable bedrock in the ordinary sense. It is more like a crust forming over an immense, active process. A surface that is safe when crossed may buckle, split, tilt, or rise while you are standing on it.

Every member of the expedition should carry:

- A personal seismic and acceleration monitor.
- A laser or radar rangefinder to track nearby surface movement.
- A broad-footed, low-pressure boot system for traction and load distribution.
- A retractable safety line.
- A compact climbing harness.
- A suit-integrated anchor that can attach to a verified stable mass.
- A way to sever the tether instantly if the anchor begins moving with the terrain.

Never anchor yourself to a newly formed ridge, a thin spire, a crystal field, a wall with visible internal glow, or any feature near an active crack. “Solid” is not the same as “stationary.”

Avoid standing:

- Directly over hairline fractures.
- At the foot of fresh walls or cliffs.
- On the edges of shelves.
- In trenches, especially beneath overhangs.
- In areas where fragments are hovering.
- On apparently level surfaces that have no signs of age, weathering, or accumulation.

The lack of scree is not reassuring. It means that normal geological settling has not occurred. A cliff face may fail without warning and without producing a conventional rockfall.

### 3. Keep away from the brightest and warmest stone

The internal glow indicates active heat and perhaps pressure or chemical transformation. The visible light is weak, but the thermal hazard may not be.

Carry:

- Thermal imaging equipment.
- A suit rated for radiant heat and brief contact with hot mineral surfaces.
- Heat-resistant gloves and boot soles.
- A probe for testing rock temperature before touching or crossing it.
- A radiation and electromagnetic monitor, in case the glow is caused by something more dangerous than heat.

Do not enter sheltered clefts simply because they seem protected from the atmospheric turbulence. They may act as heat traps, pressure vents, or openings into actively forming chambers. A crack that looks narrow may widen beneath the surface. The faint red or amber veins should be treated like exposed power lines.

The most dangerous ground may be the ground that looks calm: a dark plate with no glow may be thin over a hot chamber, or may be moments away from being lifted by new material.

### 4. Prepare for atmospheric pressure fronts

There is no ordinary wind, but there are broad moving distortions that can displace objects and alter apparent distance. These should be considered environmental events, not weather.

Before departure, establish procedures for:

- Sudden loss of visual alignment.
- Apparent sideways movement of distant terrain.
- Temporary changes in gravity, inertia, or distance.
- Loose fragments rising or traveling unexpectedly.
- Failure of line-of-sight communication.

During a pressure front:

1. Stop walking.
2. Lower your center of gravity.
3. Secure all loose equipment.
4. Attach to a verified anchor if one is available.
5. Do not chase objects or teammates that appear to have shifted.
6. Trust instrument readings only after they agree across multiple devices.
7. Wait for the distortion to pass before attempting navigation.

Use sealed containers for every object. A dropped tool may not simply fall nearby. It may rise, move sideways, or settle somewhere unreachable.

### 5. Navigation will be unusually difficult

There is no useful horizon, little sky reference, almost no weather pattern, and no reliable scale. The apparent distance to a landmark may change. A ravine may be much larger or smaller than it seems. The landscape itself may be revised between observations.

Do not rely on:

- The stars.
- A magnetic compass.
- Visual estimates of distance.
- A single landmark.
- Preloaded maps for more than broad orientation.
- The continued existence of any route.

Use a redundant navigation system:

- Inertial navigation.
- Gravimetric mapping.
- Active lidar or radar.
- Short-range ranging beacons placed along the route.
- Tethered communications repeaters.
- Multiple independent clocks.
- A continuously recorded route with time-stamped terrain scans.

Mark locations with active beacons rather than paint, flags, stakes, or lights. A mark on the ground may be swallowed, lifted, or buried by new mineral growth. Beacons should transmit their own position history and detect whether they have moved relative to their surroundings.

The expedition should operate from a clearly identified base or extraction point, but the base itself must be monitored. A shelter that is safe at the beginning of the mission may become part of a rising shelf or be enclosed by new stone.

### 6. Keep the mission short

This place has no obvious food, water, or biological support. Even if a human can breathe the air, there is no reason to assume that water exists in accessible form. Pale crystals should not be tasted, dissolved, or used as a water source. They may be corrosive, reactive, toxic, or structurally active.

Carry all necessities:

- Water and nutrition.
- Medical supplies.
- Waste containment.
- Spare suit seals.
- Redundant power.
- Emergency thermal blankets or a heated refuge.
- A means of purifying any collected material, though collection should not be necessary.

Plan for a much shorter surface stay than the available supplies suggest. The main limit may be route reliability rather than oxygen. If the return path changes, an expedition could survive physically but become unable to reach its extraction point.

A good rule would be to turn back at the first sign that the landscape has begun changing faster than the team can map it.

### 7. Treat the ruins as hazardous unknown structures

The structures may be ruins, measuring devices, markers, or parts of the same geological process. Their apparent construction does not prove they were made by people, nor that they are abandoned.

Do not:

- Enter a chamber without scanning it.
- Stand beneath a lintel or between upright slabs.
- Descend the twelve-step stair.
- Touch smooth surfaces or embedded objects.
- Remove fragments.
- Place equipment inside an enclosure.
- Assume that a structure is inactive because it is old-looking.

The stair is especially suspicious. It may be a genuine architectural remnant, but it may also be a pressure channel, a boundary, or an unfinished opening. The fact that it ends at a wall does not mean the wall is solid. It may be a membrane, a sealed passage, or a surface that will become open when the surrounding material moves.

Document the ruins remotely first. If contact is necessary, use disposable instruments on long manipulators and record the structure from several distances. Avoid introducing metal, heat, biological material, or electromagnetic fields unless the expedition is prepared for a response.

### 8. Use strict team discipline

The place will encourage people to become separated. Darkness, scale distortion, and subtle ground movement can make a companion appear close when they are far away, or make a familiar landmark seem displaced.

The team should:

- Never travel alone.
- Remain within tether or confirmed communication range.
- Use suit-to-suit ranging, not voice alone.
- Establish fixed hand and radio signals for “stop,” “terrain shift,” “pressure front,” and “return.”
- Confirm identity after any visual distortion.
- Avoid splitting around obstacles.
- Keep one person dedicated to monitoring instruments rather than sightseeing.

If a teammate disappears behind a ridge or into a distortion, do not immediately follow. Mark the last confirmed position, establish a perimeter, and use ranging equipment. In this environment, pursuit may lead into a landscape that no longer connects to the one the person entered.

### 9. Prepare psychologically

The place may be more unsettling than conventionally dangerous. It has no life, no weather, no familiar scale, and no reassuring signs of age. The warmth in the stone may feel comforting until one remembers that the comfort comes from an active geological interior. The long quiet may make an approaching structural event almost impossible to anticipate.

Travelers should expect:

- Loss of ordinary depth perception.
- Anxiety caused by the black sky and absent horizon.
- A tendency to mistake mineral forms for intentional objects or living things.
- Compulsion to watch the slowly moving landscape.
- Disorientation when a familiar ridge changes shape.
- A false sense of safety during quiet periods.
- Difficulty judging how long an event has lasted.

Use scheduled check-ins and instrument-based decisions. Do not allow the beauty or apparent solemnity of the high country to override procedures.

### 10. Establish a hard abort condition

The expedition should leave immediately if any of the following occur:

- The route beacon network begins reporting inconsistent positions.
- A new fault opens within the planned route.
- A pressure front arrives without being detected by the instruments.
- The suit reports unexplained chemical or radiation exposure.
- The internal glow becomes bright enough to cast clear shadows.
- Ground movement exceeds the team’s ability to remain anchored.
- A ruin changes shape.
- A structure or mineral spire begins responding to human presence.
- The extraction point shifts, disappears, or cannot be ranged.
- Any team member reports hearing speech, music, footsteps, or other non-geological sounds.

The final rule should be simple: do not wait for the world to become visibly violent. This landscape is dangerous precisely because its most important changes are quiet, gradual, and irreversible.

Visit it with a sealed suit, redundant navigation, short timelines, remote observation equipment, and a willingness to leave before the place gives you a dramatic reason. Its defining feature is not destruction. It is unfinished creation—and a human being is poorly suited to stand on anything that is still deciding what it is.
