## Preparation for Visiting

Treat this place as a **high-risk transitional environment**, not as an island, cave, ocean, or weather system. It resembles all of those things, but obeys none of their ordinary rules consistently. Your preparation should assume that the terrain, water, distance, acoustics, gravity, and even the relationship between moments may change while you are standing still.

The most important principle is:

> **Do not arrive alone, do not arrive unanchored, and do not make sound unless you have already determined what sound does here.**

### 1. Do not attempt an unprotected physical arrival

A normal human body would be endangered almost immediately by:

- Violent multidirectional currents
- High-pressure water impacts
- Floating or suspended gravel
- Sharp, newly formed rock
- Sudden changes between air and water
- Thermal fluctuations
- Disorientation caused by unreliable gravity and distance
- Acoustic pressure powerful enough to injure the lungs, inner ear, and nervous system
- Possible temporal or spatial displacement
- Unknown organisms that may not share ordinary biology

Do not arrive wearing ordinary diving equipment, climbing equipment, or weather gear. Those systems assume a stable environment with a recognizable up, down, pressure gradient, and route of retreat. This location offers none of those reliably.

Use a **sealed, pressure-compensated environmental suit** capable of operating in air, water, vapor, and mixed phases. It should provide:

- Independent breathing for much longer than the planned visit
- Redundant oxygen and carbon dioxide systems
- Automatic pressure regulation
- Full facial and auditory protection
- Chemical and biological filtration
- Thermal regulation, even though the water feels warm
- Integrated lighting that does not rely on visible wavelengths alone
- A physical emergency release from the suit
- A way to distinguish the suit’s own readings from environmental readings that may be false

The suit must not assume that a reading is trustworthy merely because it is numerically stable. A pressure gauge, compass, clock, depth meter, or GPS-equivalent may be giving you a measurement from another region, another moment, or another local geometry.

### 2. Protect yourself from the sound

The sound is not merely loud. It is described as having physical force and potentially altering speech, perception, and perhaps the state of the place itself.

Use layered hearing protection:

- Custom pressure-sealing ear protection
- Active noise cancellation
- A mechanical emergency isolation mode
- Bone-conduction communication only if testing proves it safe
- A visual communication system between team members

Do not rely on voice commands. The landscape may break speech into fragments, mimic language, or respond to spoken sound. In particular, avoid calling out, shouting for help, singing, using alarms, or activating a device that emits a tone.

The opening in the trembling lens appears to close at the first sound. Assume this is not poetic coincidence. The first sound may be an environmental trigger. Your arrival equipment should therefore be completely silent. Disable:

- Audible alarms
- Radio chirps
- Suit pumps that produce external tones
- Mechanical clickers
- Voice-activated systems
- Automatic spoken notifications
- Any beacon that announces itself acoustically

Use light pulses, laser-readable symbols, or prearranged hand signals instead. Even light should be introduced cautiously, since the landscape may react to observation or directed illumination.

### 3. Bring a genuine physical tether

A normal rope may not be enough. It might lengthen, cross itself, attach to a different moment, or lead back to a location that no longer exists.

Use at least three independent return systems:

1. **A physical tether** connected to a stabilized arrival point outside the environment.
2. **A temporal or dimensional recall marker** capable of retrieving you without requiring you to walk back.
3. **A personal anchor** carried on your body, preferably something that defines your original identity and continuity rather than merely broadcasting coordinates.

The tether should be marked at intervals with materials that can be seen in air, underwater, vapor, and darkness. Include markers that change in a known way with time. If the markers begin appearing out of order, repeating, aging, or showing signs of having already been handled, stop following the line. It may no longer connect your current position to your point of origin.

Never detach from the tether merely because the destination appears close. Distance is explicitly unreliable. A rock within arm’s reach may belong to another moment, and a visible edge may recede as you approach.

### 4. Establish a silent navigation system

Do not use an ordinary compass. The place has no stable horizon, and magnetic or gravitational orientation may be meaningless.

Before arrival, prepare a navigation protocol based on several independent references:

- The direction of the tether
- The direction of the arrival point’s signal
- A repeating coded light pattern
- Suit inertial measurements
- Local observations of water behavior
- A continuously updated map with time-stamped images
- A record of your own bodily position and sequence of movements

The map should not merely record coordinates. It should record **relationships**:

- Which surface was above which other surface
- Which cracks opened and closed
- Which shadows had which orientation
- Whether water was flowing upward, sideways, or downward
- Whether a route shortened, lengthened, or duplicated
- Whether landmarks remained the same when viewed from different angles

Marking the rock may be dangerous or futile. Edges migrate, ledges swell, and surfaces disappear. If you use physical markers, make them removable and noninvasive. Do not carve into the stone. It may respond to being marked, and the place already appears to absorb intention into its geology.

### 5. Prepare for changes in gravity, phase, and causality

The water can become a vertical wall, hang in sheets, or flow upward. A basin can become a sky. The apparent environment may change without a corresponding transition.

Your suit should include:

- A body-position stabilizer
- Retractable anchoring spikes or magnetic/grappling alternatives, though none should be trusted exclusively
- A compact maneuvering system usable in air and water
- Automatic restraint if the body begins to fall in an unexpected direction
- A system for identifying whether you are currently in air, water, vapor, or a mixed state
- A way to seal the respiratory system instantly if the surrounding medium changes

Do not jump from one surface to another. Even if the landing point appears stable, the act of moving between them may cause you to arrive before you departed, leave an afterimage behind, or enter a neighboring version of the terrain.

Move slowly, maintaining at least one confirmed contact point whenever possible. Before transferring your weight, observe the target surface without touching it. Then touch it with an instrument or tether, not with your hand or foot.

### 6. Protect the eyes and limit direct observation

The place contains unusual light behavior:

- Light pools instead of traveling normally
- Shadows may point in multiple directions
- The ring’s shadows collect into a reflective pool showing a different landscape
- The upper lens reveals contradictory views of the surrounding sea
- The rock may display impossible geological layers

Use a visor with adjustable opacity and multiple spectral filters. Do not stare into:

- The shadow pool inside the ring
- The trembling lens at the highest point
- Reflections in still water
- The interior of the ribbonlike organisms
- Any surface displaying a landscape that is not physically present
- Any region that appears brighter, drier, calmer, or more orderly than its surroundings

Do not assume that a reflection is harmless. It may be an observation point, an exit, a lure, or an alternate position of yourself.

Keep visual records, but do not review them while still in the location. A recording may contain more than the original observer saw, and watching it could create a second route into the place.

### 7. Bring no unnecessary equipment

This is not a suitable environment for sampling, construction, or salvage.

Avoid bringing:

- Open containers
- Loose tools
- Food
- Biological material
- Unsealed electronics
- Drones
- Autonomous probes
- Anything that emits heat, sound, scent, or strong electromagnetic fields
- Personal items with sentimental or symbolic significance unless they are needed as identity anchors

The terrain appears to react to intention and may treat tools as part of its unfinished construction. A device placed on a shelf could become embedded, duplicated, or incorporated into the next geological change.

Carry only what is essential, and tether every item to your body. Assume that any object dropped into the water is lost, even if it remains visible.

### 8. Be cautious around the living things

The fauna is rare, but rarity should not be mistaken for harmlessness.

The ribbonlike organisms are especially dangerous. They appear to alter the movement of water and may contain or display impossible interiors. Do not approach, touch, illuminate directly, collect, or attempt to follow one. If one rises onto the rock near you, remain motionless and allow it to leave. A sudden drop in temperature may indicate that it is nearby even when it cannot be seen.

The transparent shelled organisms may react collectively to changes in the rock. Avoid their cracks and do not disturb them. Their behavior could be an early warning system for imminent terrain movement.

The dark fronds and luminous mosslike growth should also be left alone. The calm circles around the black growth may be temporary zones of altered fluid behavior rather than safe areas. If a plant disappears and reappears elsewhere, consider that a warning about the instability of the entire surface, not merely an interesting biological fact.

### 9. Do not enter the structures on a first visit

The ring of pale uprights and the seven-step staircase should be classified as **prohibited zones** until repeated remote observation establishes their behavior.

The ring is particularly suspicious because:

- Its shadows behave independently
- The inner pool reflects another landscape
- Its stones are evenly spaced but lack tool marks
- Water passes through the uprights without wetting their inner faces

Do not step inside the ring. Do not place an object in its center. Do not look into the shadow pool for an extended period. Do not attempt to cross the ring while holding hands with another traveler; if it is a boundary, it may separate you in ways that are not immediately visible.

The staircase should not be descended. Counted steps are unreliable here. The apparent sequence of seven, eight, and nine steps suggests that the stairway may be indexing different versions of itself. The opening at the bottom may be a cave, doorway, or solid rock depending on the observer’s position or moment.

The delicate clattering from inside should not be investigated. If it begins approaching, retreat without speaking.

### 10. Use strict team discipline

Send at least three people, though the ideal first visit would use remote instrumentation rather than human presence. Everyone should have:

- A distinct visual identifier
- A nonverbal status signal
- A prearranged emergency posture
- A personal tether
- A unique continuity phrase written down but not spoken unless absolutely necessary
- A record of their name, origin, arrival time, and number of team members

The phrase should be used only if someone’s identity becomes uncertain. Do not repeat it casually. If the landscape reproduces it through the rain, rock, or water, cease all verbal communication and begin withdrawal.

Never follow a team member who appears suddenly without confirmation. The place can produce afterimages, fragments of voices, altered routes, or versions of people that may have arrived from another moment. Confirm identity through several details unavailable to an imitation—for example, a prearranged sequence of silent gestures and light pulses.

If someone becomes duplicated, delayed, prematurely present, or leaves an afterimage, do not try to reunite the versions. Withdraw the original team according to the return protocol. Do not allow the anomaly to determine which person is “real.”

### 11. Set a very short mission

The purpose of the first visit should be limited to:

- Confirming that human presence is physically possible
- Measuring environmental transitions
- Testing whether the tether remains continuous
- Determining whether sound triggers changes
- Observing the stability of one exposed rock surface
- Establishing whether the arrival point can be recovered

Do not attempt to reach the highest point, cross the entire plateau, enter the cave, descend the staircase, approach the lens, or investigate the dry world visible in the ring.

Set a hard departure time, but do not trust the local clock. Use an external timer and an automatic extraction condition. The timer should trigger withdrawal even if the team believes it has just arrived or has only been present for a few seconds.

In a place where distance and sequence can change, “we have only been here briefly” is not evidence of safety.

### 12. Watch for signs that the place has noticed you

Leave immediately if any of the following occur:

- Your voice returns from a direction where no one is standing
- The rain begins falling in a regular rhythm
- The water becomes briefly silent
- The same rock appears in two places
- Shadows converge toward you
- A plant appears attached to your equipment
- A crack closes around a tether or tool
- The terrain develops a route that seems designed specifically for your movement
- The distant edge becomes easier to reach
- The staircase displays eight solid steps
- The lens opens when nobody has made a sound
- You see a dry, sunlit landscape outside a reflection or aperture
- The roar resolves into a recognizable sentence
- You encounter a version of yourself
- A familiar person or object appears where it could not have arrived
- The water around you becomes perfectly still

The last condition is especially dangerous. Stillness here is not rest. It may mean that the place has temporarily isolated a volume of space, selected an outcome, or paused the process in which it is forming.

### 13. Departure protocol

Withdraw by the same route only if the route still agrees with your records. If it has changed, use the tether and external extraction system rather than attempting to retrace your steps.

During withdrawal:

- Do not run
- Do not speak
- Do not look into reflective water
- Do not respond to voices
- Do not touch newly formed stone
- Do not retrieve equipment
- Do not separate from the group
- Do not stop because the environment becomes calm
- Do not cross the threshold if the tether is behaving discontinuously

Once outside, quarantine yourself and all equipment. Remain isolated until you have confirmed:

- Your memories are continuous
- The number of travelers is unchanged
- No afterimage or duplicate accompanies you
- Your suit’s clocks agree with external clocks
- Your recordings contain only expected material
- No local organism, mineral, water, or shadow has crossed over
- Your body casts one shadow under ordinary light
- Your name, history, and point of origin remain stable

Do not immediately return for anyone who fails to emerge. The apparent missing person may be ahead of you, behind you, inside a different interval, or no longer the same person. A recovery attempt should be planned from outside after observing the exit for an extended period.

## Mental preparation

The greatest danger may be the temptation to interpret the place.

It looks geological, architectural, aquatic, biological, and cosmological at once. It may invite the mind to decide what it is: a drowned highland, a newborn island, a temple, a threshold, a memory of a planet, or an unfinished world. Resist that impulse. Interpretation may itself function as an act of participation.

Do not assume the place is hostile. Do not assume it is benevolent. Do not assume it is conscious. More importantly, do not assume those categories apply.

It is forming. Its processes may not distinguish between erosion and intention, visitor and material, observation and disturbance. An ordinary human traveler should behave less like an explorer claiming territory and more like a brief, silent object passing through a storm that has not yet decided what objects are.

Arrive quietly. Anchor yourself. Touch as little as possible. Say nothing. Observe only what you can verify. Leave before the landscape finishes deciding where you are.
