## Prepare as if Morrow Array were both a wet industrial plant and a living animal

Do not approach it as a wilderness, a ruin, or an ordinary city. It is a functioning artificial continent whose machinery, water systems, and fauna have been operating in a tightly regulated relationship for an incomprehensibly long time. The most dangerous thing you could do there is assume that something is harmless because it is quiet, motionless, or apparently abandoned.

Your visit should be planned as a controlled expedition, not tourism.

---

## Before departure

### Confirm the atmosphere

Do not rely on the description of warm vapor and ordinary daylight as evidence that the atmosphere is breathable. Obtain an actual atmospheric reading at the intended arrival point.

Bring:

- A sealed respirator or supplied-air mask
- Independent oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitoring
- Filters rated for industrial particulates, aerosols, volatile chemicals, and biological contamination
- A backup air supply sufficient to reach your extraction point
- Eye protection that seals well against vapor and splashes

The smells of hot insulation, mineral salts, and wet iron suggest that the air may be tolerable to human lungs, but the faint sweet odor of the fauna could indicate a pervasive biological aerosol. Even if the atmosphere contains oxygen, it may contain unfamiliar proteins, spores of animal origin, corrosive vapor, or chemicals that humans can tolerate only briefly.

Treat the local air as contaminated until proven otherwise.

### Establish a precise arrival location

Morrow Array has no useful natural landmarks. A raised line may be a highway, a wall, a berm, or the roof of an immense machine. A dark basin may be a shallow depression or a service reservoir extending far beyond visibility.

Your arrival equipment should be able to:

- Mark your location independently of the local infrastructure
- Maintain a stable return portal or extraction beacon
- Navigate without satellite positioning
- Detect vertical levels, enclosed spaces, and large moving masses
- Function in heavy condensation and electromagnetic interference
- Continue operating if submerged or fouled by biological material

Set several redundant beacons. Place them above the waterline and, if possible, on structures unlikely to move. Do not attach them to machinery, valves, animals, cables, or anything that appears to be part of a maintenance route.

Navigation by sight will be unreliable. Vapor will conceal distant features, and the apparently straight horizon may curve around enormous manufactured basins. Use inertial navigation, lidar, radio triangulation, dead reckoning, and a continuously updated local map.

### Arrange permission or a non-interference protocol

If the Array has any governing intelligence, maintenance authority, custodial system, or recognizable signaling network, identify it before arriving. You should know whether you are:

- A permitted visitor
- An unregistered contaminant
- A repairable fault
- A biological specimen
- An obstacle to be rerouted
- A resource to be incorporated

The apparent passivity of the machinery should not reassure you. A place that isolates failures before they become events may respond to your presence with quiet efficiency rather than aggression. Routes may close. Pumps may change direction. Machinery may surround or relocate you without making any dramatic attempt to attack.

Transmit your identity, mass, chemical composition, intended route, duration, and extraction time if a communication channel exists. Ask specifically about:

- Safe walking surfaces
- Restricted districts
- Flood-control procedures
- Faunal nesting and feeding zones
- Electromagnetic hazards
- Decontamination requirements
- What to do if approached by a large stationary organism
- Whether local beings interpret light, sound, vibration, or heat as signals

## Clothing and personal equipment

### Wear a sealed, washable outer layer

Your clothing should be a smooth, non-absorbent environmental suit with as few exposed seams and pockets as possible. Morrow Array’s water and condensation will reach everything. Ordinary cloth will become wet, heavy, and biologically fouled almost immediately.

Prefer:

- A sealed or semi-sealed suit
- Chemical-resistant gloves
- Replaceable outer boot covers
- A hood or helmet integrated with the respirator
- No dangling straps, loose cords, open mesh, or exposed electronics
- A surface difficult for small organisms to grip

The fauna fills seams, grates, walls, and warm surfaces. Anything loose may become a route, shelter, food source, or nest attachment. Small creatures may not be attacking you; they may simply treat your equipment as newly available infrastructure.

### Bring exceptionally good footwear

The ground will be wet, hard, sloped, and unpredictable. Use boots with:

- Deep nonconductive traction
- Chemical-resistant soles
- Ankle support
- Protection from puncture and crushing
- Treads that can be cleaned quickly
- Electrical insulation, unless local authorities specifically require conductive grounding

Do not walk barefoot, in sandals, or in smooth-soled footwear. The shallow water may conceal grates, seams, gaps, cables, suction points, and submerged machinery. Slopes that look flat may have been designed for drainage and may be difficult to perceive through vapor.

Assume any unmarked dark water is deep enough to trap you and any luminous water is electrically or chemically active until tested.

### Protect hearing and balance

The Array’s background hum may be below the threshold of ordinary discomfort but still affect concentration, sleep, balance, and communication. Bring hearing protection that does not eliminate warning signals entirely. Use vibration and visual alarms as well as audible ones.

The long tones emitted by large fauna deserve special caution. They may be communication, territorial marking, system synchronization, or a command transmitted through the surrounding water and structures. If one sounds, stop moving, reduce machinery output, and observe the response before proceeding.

## Medical preparation

### Vaccination is not enough

Local biology may not use familiar pathogens, so conventional vaccination offers limited protection. The greater risk may be allergy, tissue irritation, toxins, or immune reaction to the animals’ secretions.

Carry:

- Broad-spectrum emergency medications
- Antihistamines and corticosteroids
- Antibiotics and antifungals appropriate to your biology
- Antiviral and antiparasitic contingencies if available
- Chemical decontamination supplies
- Sterile dressings
- An eye-wash system
- A way to seal any cut immediately
- A portable medical scanner
- Several days of food and water in sealed containers

Do not drink local water without exhaustive analysis. “Clean” water in Morrow Array may mean free of visible sediment or suitable for local fauna, not safe for human consumption. Water that passes over active conduits may contain dissolved metals, process chemicals, electrical charge, or biological material.

Do not eat the fauna. Do not collect dead fauna as food. A dead organism may already be in the process of being chemically or biologically absorbed by its surroundings.

### Avoid open wounds

Every exposed surface has a tenant. A cut, blister, or abrasion could attract organisms that perceive damaged tissue as a warm mineral leak, a feeding site, or a structural irregularity. Cover wounds before arrival and leave immediately if your suit is breached.

## How to move through the Array

### Stay on designated routes

Do not cross open basins merely because the water appears shallow. Use elevated walkways, service roads, marked platforms, or routes approved by the local system.

The shallow channels are not empty drainage ditches. They may conceal:

- Intake grates
- Pump suction
- Pressure-release vents
- Electrical leakage
- Dense fauna mats
- Buried conveyor systems
- Sudden changes in depth
- Organisms too large to distinguish from the bottom

If you must enter water, test it first for depth, current, temperature, conductivity, chemical composition, and biological activity. Use a pole or remote probe, not your hands or feet.

Never cross a living mat. It may be a feeding congregation, a maintenance layer, a nest, or a load-bearing part of the local ecosystem. Even if the organisms part around you, that does not mean they have accepted your presence. It may simply mean they are routing around an obstruction.

### Keep away from machinery

Do not climb, lean against, open, unplug, or touch:

- Pipes
- Valves
- Grates
- Conveyor trunks
- Gantries
- Sealed panels
- Warm vents
- Fabrication lines
- Dormant-looking instruments
- The “bone-like” pale plates

Morrow Array’s components may be under pressure, electrically active, or responsive to contact. A surface can look inactive while transmitting instructions, carrying heat, or serving as an organism’s sensory organ.

The lack of noise is especially dangerous. Machinery has been deliberately designed not to disturb the animals, so a moving arm or opening gate may give little warning.

Carry a local-frequency detector and a line-of-sight motion sensor. Do not assume that a silent machine is stationary.

### Treat the large animals as infrastructure with instincts

Large forms may be harmless, but they are not scenery. A creature standing beside a sluice for a century may be waiting for a specific pressure change. One lying across a trench may be part of the drainage system, a bridge-like organism, or an immobile animal that reacts only when disturbed.

Give them a wide berth. Do not:

- Climb onto them
- Shine lights into recessed organs
- Touch their limbs
- Walk beneath their suspended bodies
- Remove smaller organisms from them
- Approach their instruments, racks, membranes, or nests
- Attempt to attract their attention

If one turns toward you, stop. Reduce heat, vibration, radio output, and movement. Let it inspect you through whatever senses it uses. Slow, predictable behavior is safer than retreating in panic.

The smaller animals may be more hazardous than the great ones. They are numerous, adaptive, and accustomed to entering machinery. A few could get into your boots, filters, joints, ventilation system, or body seals without any obvious aggression.

## Managing light, sound, and technology

The fauna appears to communicate through vibration, heat, chemistry, and light. Your equipment may be broadcasting continuously.

Before arrival, minimize:

- High-powered sonar
- Active radar
- Flashing lights
- Repetitive radio pulses
- Strong magnetic fields
- Hot exhaust
- Unshielded batteries
- Vibrating motors
- Drones operating at animal height

Use low-intensity, steady illumination rather than strobes or sweeping spotlights. Red light is not automatically safe; the animals may detect wavelengths humans cannot. Ask local authorities what constitutes a neutral signal.

Keep devices in shielded housings. Assume that an animal able to sense electrical charge will notice every transmitter and battery you carry.

Do not test the environment by firing weapons, dropping explosives, generating loud sound, or cutting open machinery. In a place whose stability depends on everything remaining within tolerance, even a small local disturbance may trigger a large corrective response.

## Food, shelter, and rest

### Bring your own shelter

Do not sleep directly on the ground, platforms, or dry-looking metal. Those surfaces are inhabited, heated, sloped, and potentially active. A suitable camp should be:

- Elevated above condensation and shallow flooding
- Sealed against insects and small fauna
- Electrically isolated
- Anchored independently of local structures
- Equipped with its own air filtration
- Away from vents, drains, grates, and warm machinery
- Monitored for pressure, vibration, temperature, and biological intrusion

Do not camp beneath overhead galleries or moving freight routes. Condensation may fall from them continuously, and apparent empty space may be a service corridor.

There is no true night, so bring artificial sleep cues: blackout protection, a stable light cycle, and noise management. The constant pale daylight and industrial hum can disrupt sleep quickly. More importantly, sleeping deeply may cause you to miss a slow change in water level, machinery position, or animal congregation.

### Carry all provisions

Bring sealed food and water for the entire planned stay plus a generous reserve. Do not rely on finding a safe supply station. Production lines may manufacture useful objects, but there is no indication that they manufacture human food, and anything that looks edible may be a local biological product with unfamiliar chemistry.

Pack waste out. Human waste would be an extremely unusual chemical and biological input in a closed system and might attract attention from organisms that are otherwise indifferent to you.

## Environmental hazards to watch continuously

### Water and flooding

The Array is carefully controlled, but “controlled” does not mean human-safe. A drainage sheet could deepen suddenly when pumps change state. A sluice may open without audible warning. A basin that has remained ankle-deep for centuries may be a temporary operating condition.

Maintain a waterline alarm and keep your extraction route above the lowest likely flood level. Never position yourself between a large animal, a sluice, and a wall.

### Heat

The ambient warmth may be tolerable while stationary but dangerous inside a sealed suit. Humidity will prevent effective cooling, and warm surfaces may cause burns without appearing hot.

Monitor core temperature. Schedule frequent rest periods in verified cool zones. Do not stand over vents or place equipment against heat-sinks unless you know what they are doing. The organisms may prefer these areas, and their tolerance does not imply yours.

### Electrical and chemical exposure

Luminous water, wet metal, conduits, and machinery should all be treated as electrically active. Use insulated tools and nonconductive probes. Keep your suit’s outer layer free of conductive mud or mineral film.

Test surfaces for corrosives before kneeling or placing equipment. The Array may use process fluids that are harmless to its ceramic and alloy components but damaging to skin, lungs, seals, or plastics.

### Entrapment and disorientation

The manufactured landscape is broad enough to mimic open country while remaining full of walls, slopes, channels, and restricted passages. Vapor can erase the difference between a nearby structure and a distant horizon.

Maintain a tether or tracked route in complicated areas. Never leave a marked path to investigate a light, sound, animal, or apparent opening without updating the team and navigation system.

## How to behave around the ecosystem

Your goal is to be boring.

Move slowly. Keep to established routes. Make no unnecessary contact. Do not feed anything. Do not remove organisms from your equipment unless they present an immediate danger; instead, leave the area and perform decontamination in a controlled enclosure.

Avoid collecting specimens unless the Array’s custodians permit it. Removing even a tiny organism may disrupt a maintenance relationship invisible to you. A creature in a seam may be cleaning it. A glowing patch may be signaling a pressure condition. A mat of small animals may be regulating temperature or sealing a leak.

Do not repair anything unless specifically instructed. A broken-looking component may be intentionally isolated, awaiting a replacement, or performing a function you cannot recognize. Introducing your own sealants, lubricants, microbes, or tools could be interpreted as contamination or cause physical incompatibility.

If animals gather around you, do not run. Stop and lower your activity. They may be reading your heat, electrical charge, or chemical emissions. Let them finish. If they begin attaching material to you, retreat only if you can do so without sudden movement; otherwise request assistance or wait for a local response.

## Emergency procedures

Have predefined responses for:

1. **Suit breach**  
   Seal the breach, stop moving through water, withdraw to a decontamination point, and do not remove the suit in the open.

2. **Rapid water rise**  
   Move uphill or to a verified elevated route, not toward the nearest visible light. Mark your route and maintain contact with extraction.

3. **Large-fauna vocalization**  
   Freeze, silence nonessential equipment, and observe. Do not answer with sound or light.

4. **Machinery activation nearby**  
   Move perpendicular to the machine’s apparent path only if safe. Never hide beneath a conveyor, beside a gate, or in a drainage channel.

5. **Loss of navigation**  
   Stop. Do not wander. Activate beacons and follow the last verified route. In Morrow Array, continuing in a straight line may take you into a different vertical level or a controlled industrial zone without your realizing it.

6. **Mass animal movement**  
   Get off the water and away from seams, grates, and warm vents. A sudden migration could precede a pressure change, maintenance cycle, or environmental correction.

7. **Failure of extraction equipment**  
   Do not improvise a portal on an active platform. Reach a preselected hardened fallback site and wait for the rescue protocol.

## Psychological preparation

Morrow Array may be more disturbing than overtly hostile worlds.

You will see life everywhere, but none of it will provide the emotional cues humans associate with wilderness. There are no plants, no soil, no decay, no seasonal variation, no empty places, and almost no true silence. Animals may remain still for decades. Machines may continue making things with no visible purpose. A dead organism may disappear into the surface before you can examine it. The entire continent may feel less like a place inhabited by machines than like one enormous organism whose organs happen to be made of factories, channels, and towers.

Expect:

- Difficulty judging distance
- Sleep disruption from the permanent dim daylight
- Unease caused by the absence of natural materials
- Hypervigilance around shallow water
- A feeling of being observed even when nothing visibly reacts
- Confusion about whether a feature is biological, mechanical, or both
- Anxiety from seeing continuous activity without progress or change

Travel with at least one other person if possible. Maintain regular verbal check-ins, even if local acoustics are quiet. Use written route logs and scheduled rest. Do not allow fascination with the ecosystem to override withdrawal times.

The Array’s greatest psychological hazard is the temptation to stay. It is stable, warm, productive, and full of motion without apparent crisis. A traveler may begin to accept its lack of solitude and its refusal to change as comforting. Keep an external clock, a fixed departure schedule, and a person or system outside Morrow Array responsible for noticing if you fail to return.

## The central rule

Do not mistake Morrow Array’s calm for safety.

Its water is managed, its temperature is managed, its machinery is quiet, and its animals are accustomed to living beside mechanisms. That equilibrium is not passive. It is the product of continuous correction. You are an unplanned variable entering a system that has had ages to learn how to absorb variables.

Arrive clean. Remain sealed. Stay on approved routes. Touch nothing. Disturb nothing. Treat every surface as occupied and every quiet machine as active. Leave before the place has had enough time to decide what function you might serve.
