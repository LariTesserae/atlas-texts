## Prepare as if visiting a wet, cold industrial ecosystem—not a mountain landscape

The plateau is not merely covered in vegetation. The vegetation *is* the ground, the weather system, the drainage, the machinery, and much of the animal habitat. An ordinary human should treat the entire surface as potentially alive, load-bearing only by accident, and capable of changing without warning.

Do not arrive expecting a hike. Arrive expecting to enter a slow, continent-sized organism.

### 1. Confirm that the environment is survivable before landing

Do not step out of the vessel until you have sampled:

- Atmospheric oxygen and pressure
- Carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and other biological gases
- Airborne spores, pollen, motile cells, and microscopic organisms
- Temperature, wind speed, and wind-chill
- Radiation and ultraviolet exposure
- Water chemistry
- Local electromagnetic and gravitational irregularities
- Any evidence that the apparent horizon or sky is physically unreliable

The plateau is above most weather, but that does not mean it has calm or clean air. It may have thin atmosphere, intense wind, little thermal buffering, and airborne biological material. The persistent mist should be considered contaminated until proven otherwise. It may be ordinary condensation, or it may be part of the local metabolism.

Use an enclosed landing craft with positive interior pressure and independent air reserves. A respirator alone is not enough if the atmosphere is corrosive, narcotic, spore-filled, or simply incompatible with human lungs.

### 2. Use a sealed environmental suit

Your suit should be designed for prolonged cold wet exposure and biological contamination, not merely vacuum or conventional mountaineering.

Important features include:

- Full face and eye protection
- Sealed joints and gloves
- Independent oxygen supply
- Heating layers and emergency chemical warmers
- Waterproof outer shell
- Replaceable external filters
- Hard-wearing soles and shin protection
- A rigid or semi-rigid lower leg section to prevent stems and jointed plants from wrapping around you
- External lights in multiple wavelengths
- A way to cut or burn through vegetation without releasing large amounts of fluid
- Decontamination capability before reentering the vehicle

Do not rely on ordinary fabric. The vegetation contains ribs, plates, fibers, pores, sap channels, articulated limbs, and hardening tissues. A plant that feels soft may have a stone-like internal structure. Water may emerge from it under pressure. Your suit should not have exposed straps, loose cords, or dangling equipment that can be caught by moving growth.

Carry at least two independent thermal systems. The cold is not necessarily dangerous because of its severity; it is dangerous because everything is wet, the wind is constant, and the rocks and plants seem to retain cold. Hypothermia is likely to be a greater threat than dramatic freezing.

### 3. Never land directly on the vegetation

Choose bare, stable rock if any can be identified. Even then, inspect it continuously.

The green-black mats may look like turf, but they may be:

- Thin skins over water
- Living bridges over cavities
- Decaying layered deposits
- Active root structures
- Pressure-sensitive surfaces
- Temporary structures that close behind animals
- The outer wall of a buried chamber

A landing vehicle should have broad, distributed weight and retractable stabilizers. Avoid placing engines or hot exhaust near vegetation. Heat, vibration, noise, or chemical emissions may provoke a response.

Before leaving the craft, send out small, sacrificial probes. They should test:

- Load-bearing strength
- Subsurface voids
- Fluid pressure
- Electrical activity
- Motion beneath the surface
- Whether the terrain shifts when disturbed
- Whether the apparent surface is continuous with the apparent horizon

Prefer hovering or walking probes over wheeled vehicles. Wheels may sink, become rooted, or be caught in articulated growth. Heavy vehicles may trigger local collapse or be slowly incorporated into the ecosystem.

### 4. Establish navigation that does not depend on the sky

The sky is not a reliable reference. Shadows disagree with their objects. Reflections show impossible conditions. The horizon may bend, invert, or reveal another landscape. Mist can erase distance, and the plateau itself can move.

Do not navigate by:

- The sun, if one appears
- Stars reflected in water
- Shadows
- Distant ridgelines
- The apparent direction of the horizon
- Sound alone
- Landmarks that may be living organisms

Bring a network of independent navigation beacons. Place them on exposed stone, not in the vegetation, and make them communicate by more than one method:

- Radio
- Optical pulses
- Inertial position
- Short-range acoustic pings
- Local gravity or magnetic references, if those remain stable

Each beacon should report its own orientation and movement. Since whole ecosystems can shift sideways and ridges can flatten over hours, a beacon that has not physically moved may nevertheless be standing on terrain that has migrated.

Maintain a physical tether to the landing site whenever possible. Use a bright, conductive, abrasion-resistant line with periodic transponders. A human should not travel farther than the tether and beacon network can support. If the mist thickens, the tether is the route home—not a suggestion.

### 5. Keep away from water and depressions

Water is widespread, mobile, and not behaving normally.

Do not drink from:

- Tarn margins
- Plant pores
- Rock seams
- Condensation
- Pools that appear suddenly
- Water collected from vegetation
- Any source near a clicking plant colony

The water may contain unfamiliar microorganisms, dissolved chemicals, active root systems, or organisms that use humans as nutrient sources. Bring all drinking water with you and use sealed waste handling.

Do not camp in a basin. Water may appear there within hours. The water table may rise in steps rather than gradually. Mist may collect in depressions, and basins may be occupied by unseen animals or connected to root chambers.

Approach the dark tarns only with remote equipment. The rotating sections are especially concerning: they may be surface manifestations of a large organism, a mechanical circulation process, or a spatial anomaly. Do not enter them, touch them, or throw objects into them to test depth.

### 6. Regard every plant as a possible machine and every machine as a possible animal

The distinction between organism and equipment has no useful meaning here.

A stalk may be a structural organ. A root chamber may be a digestive cavity. A black object with pistons may be a juvenile plant. A grove that looks stationary may be grinding stone, pumping fluid, or preparing to move.

Keep well away from:

- Tall, bent stalks
- Trunks that shudder in regular cycles
- Plants with vents or pressure openings
- Articulated limbs crossing the ground
- Areas of rhythmic vibration
- Freshly exposed black or translucent tissue
- Root chambers
- Any plant that produces a mechanical object
- Vegetation that is clicking, pulsing, steaming, or rotating
- Red lichen circles unless their nature is established remotely

Do not cut, collect, feed, ignite, or step on living growth without a clear reason. A wound may release sap, spores, attract animals, trigger a defensive response, or cause the plant to alter its local terrain. Avoid even “harmless” sampling during the initial expedition.

If something appears to be a machine, do not approach it merely because it seems manufactured. It may be metabolically active, capable of movement, or part of a larger structure below the surface. Observe it from a distance and let it complete its transformation if possible.

### 7. Expect the animals to defeat ordinary assumptions

The visible grazers are not necessarily harmless. Their ability to divide into multiple versions makes counting, tracking, and identifying an individual unreliable. A herd that appears to scatter may actually be multiplying, decoying, or changing its relationship to local space.

Do not approach grazing animals, even if they seem docile. Keep a wide perimeter and avoid startling them. If one separates into copies, do not chase, block, or attempt to determine which is original.

For the larger creatures:

- Do not enter plant-chasms
- Do not follow echoes
- Do not investigate calls merely because they sound nearby
- Do not assume a visible head indicates the location of the whole animal
- Do not sleep against a vegetation wall
- Do not stand on any surface that heaves beneath you

Blind, antlered animals covered in flowering tissue may not use sight, and may detect vibration, pressure, heat, or chemical traces. Remaining quiet may not make you invisible. Avoid creating a strong scent signature; use suit filtration and do not carry exposed food.

At night, or during the dim interval that passes for night, return to the craft. If the growth begins to heave, leave the area rather than trying to identify what is moving underneath it.

### 8. Protect hearing and maintain visual discipline

The plateau is continuously loud, and the sound is not background noise. It includes wind, whistles, impacts, pulses, grinding, pressure releases, and probably animal communication.

Wear hearing protection with active environmental monitoring. Simple earplugs may prevent you from hearing warnings, while unfiltered exposure may cause fatigue, disorientation, tinnitus, or panic. Use a system that suppresses harmful frequencies but relays alarms and suit-to-suit communication.

Establish hand signals and visual indicators in case the noise overwhelms radios.

The weak light and incorrect shadows create a separate hazard. Use instrument-based rangefinding and depth sensing. Do not trust naked-eye judgments of slope, distance, or object size in mist. Mark safe routes with active lights rather than paint or flags; biological systems may grow over, mimic, or consume ordinary markers.

Bring polarized and multispectral imaging. The faint green and amber illumination from plant tissue may reveal metabolic activity, but it may also interfere with human vision and sensors. Avoid staring into glowing tissues or lenses.

### 9. Treat the inverted horizon as a major spatial hazard

The temporary appearance of a second, inverted high country is not a scenic event. It may be an optical phenomenon, a folding of space, a neighboring environment becoming briefly accessible, or a failure of local geometry.

If it occurs:

1. Stop all movement.
2. Do not approach the false horizon.
3. Secure yourself to the ground or tether system.
4. Warn all team members immediately.
5. Record the event using independent instruments.
6. Do not look into it for extended periods.
7. Withdraw toward the landing site once the event ends, unless withdrawal itself becomes unsafe.
8. Check personnel and equipment for displacement, duplication, loss of time, contamination, or altered orientation.

The fact that animals disappear toward it means it may permit passage, but that is not evidence of survivability or return. Rain falling upward and the sudden deafening of the biological machinery suggest that the whole plateau may be coupled to the event.

No ordinary human should enter that landscape without an established retrieval method, a redundant beacon on the far side, and a way to verify that the returning person is the same person who departed.

### 10. Keep the expedition short and highly structured

The plateau’s slow changes are fast enough to matter to a human expedition. A ridge can flatten in an afternoon. A basin can fill by noon. An ecosystem can migrate laterally. A familiar route may not exist on the return journey.

Use a strict schedule:

- Initial landing and atmospheric survey
- Short-range probe deployment
- Limited excursion on exposed stone
- Return before the mist thickens or light becomes ambiguous
- Full suit decontamination
- Medical observation
- Instrument and sample quarantine

Do not spend the first visit trying to reach the “far end” of the plateau. The horizon is not stable enough for that goal to be meaningful.

Every person should carry:

- Personal beacon
- Tether connection
- Emergency heat source
- Compact oxygen reserve
- Water
- High-energy food in sealed packaging
- Medical kit
- Cutting tool
- Light source with spare power
- Audible and visual distress signal
- Location recorder
- A physical route card that does not depend solely on electronic mapping

Travel in at least pairs, with a third observer or drone maintaining overhead or rear coverage. Never permit a lone traveler to enter mist, vegetation chambers, or open water margins.

### 11. Prepare for contamination on both sides

The local life may be dangerous to you, but your microbes may be dangerous to it. Do not assume a dead-looking plant is inert or that a tiny sample is insignificant. A human bacterium, fungus, or gut organism could spread through a system that has never encountered it.

Use strict biosecurity:

- No exposed food
- No open wounds
- No discarded waste
- No unsterilized equipment left behind
- No biological samples without containment
- Separate clean and dirty zones in the landing craft
- Full exterior decontamination before reentry
- Quarantine after return
- Medical monitoring for delayed neurological, respiratory, dermatological, or behavioral changes

Pay attention to unexplained condensation, unusual skin sensations, changes in balance, auditory hallucinations, or the feeling that a plant is responding specifically to you. These may be environmental effects—or signs of exposure.

Do not bring living material home unless the expedition’s purpose specifically requires it and you have a multistage containment facility prepared.

### 12. Prepare psychologically for a landscape with no safe category

The most difficult aspect may be the loss of familiar boundaries. There is no dependable line between:

- Stone and organism
- Plant and machine
- Weather and metabolism
- Shelter and digestive chamber
- Landscape and animal
- Reflection and passage
- Motion and growth
- Ruin and construction

An ordinary human mind will try to classify what it sees. That instinct may produce dangerous confidence. A “rock” may pump. A “machine” may root. A “quiet patch” may be an active trap. A “distant sound” may come from an animal beneath the ground. A “mountain” may be slowly being eaten by the plateau’s roots.

Brief all travelers beforehand to expect beauty, monotony, and fear at the same time. The environment may not behave aggressively, but it will not recognize human presence as a special condition. It may absorb, redirect, reproduce, or process a person with the same immense patience it applies to stone.

### Recommended first visit

For a first expedition, send an unmanned atmospheric probe, then a small sealed lander to an exposed rocky ridge. Do not attempt to cross the living turf. Operate for only a few hours. Sample air remotely, map motion, record sound, and observe the nearest vegetation colony without touching it.

If the plateau remains spatially stable, the atmosphere is compatible with containment equipment, and no biological response occurs, a human team may conduct a tethered excursion onto bare stone.

The central rule is simple:

**Do not treat the plateau as wilderness. Treat it as a functioning body whose surface you are temporarily standing on.**
