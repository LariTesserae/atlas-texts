## Prepare as though you are entering a high-altitude industrial ruin, an electrical storm, and a place with a mild temporal anomaly—all at once.

The chamber is small, but it is not an easy or safe place to visit. Its hazards are not primarily wildlife, weather, or disease. They are thin air, vertical terrain, unstable access, persistent machinery, static charge, unknown biological-mineral growth, and the possibility that ordinary processes do not behave normally inside.

### 1. Reach the mountain with the right expedition gear

Do not arrive at the aperture by ordinary climbing equipment alone. The entrance is high enough that the outside air cannot support an unassisted human breath, and the approach is likely exposed to freezing temperatures, high winds, ice, loose rock, and very little usable shelter.

Bring:

- A sealed high-altitude suit or pressure garment
- A reliable oxygen supply, preferably with several independent tanks
- A full-face respirator or helmet rather than a simple oxygen mask
- Thermal underlayers and gloves suitable for fine manipulation
- Eye protection against mineral dust and intense reflected light
- A climbing harness, static and dynamic ropes, pitons or equivalent anchors, ascenders, descenders, and multiple backup lines
- A locator beacon that works through rock, or a physically tethered communications line
- A surface team with an independent oxygen and power reserve
- A way to mark the approach without relying on temporary paint, light, or sound

The entrance may not be obvious from outside. The low vibration is the best navigational clue, but do not trust hearing alone in a high wind or inside a resonant mountain. Carry vibration sensors and geological mapping equipment.

Do not seal the entrance or widen it casually. The chamber’s equilibrium may depend on that narrow aperture and the existing pressure relationship. Altering it could change the atmosphere, disturb the plants, or turn a stable pocket into a collapsing one.

### 2. Treat the air as hostile even after entering

The interior is somewhat sheltered, but it is still at extreme altitude. Assume the air is oxygen-poor, cold, dry, and contaminated with fine mineral particles, ozone, and possibly machinery-related gases.

Remain on supplied air throughout the visit. Do not remove your helmet to “test” the atmosphere. The visible breath-like clouds are not evidence that the air is warm or humid; they are mineral dust behaving strangely.

Bring instruments for:

- Oxygen concentration
- Pressure
- Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide
- Ozone
- Nitrogen oxides or other electrical-discharge products
- Volatile compounds from the growths and old insulation
- Particulate concentration
- Local temperature and static potential

Use positive-pressure breathing equipment if possible. The chamber contains no normal food chain, but that does not mean its plants are harmless. Their dust, fragments, or chemical emissions may be toxic, electrically active, or capable of growing into exposed equipment.

Do not rely on the chamber’s apparent permanence as evidence that it is breathable. It may be stable for its own ecology and lethal to mammals.

### 3. Assume every green surface is energized

The vegetation is not ordinary vegetation. It conducts, stores, and harvests electrical gradients. It may be alive in a way that is closer to an electrochemical mineral network than to a forest.

Your suit should be electrically insulating and externally smooth. Carry no exposed metal tools unless they are deliberately grounded and rated for high-voltage environments. Keep electronics inside shielded, sealed housings. Bring fiber-optic or hardened low-energy instruments where possible.

Do not:

- Touch the leaves barehanded
- Cut or uproot anything
- Place a battery, capacitor, magnet, or radio transmitter against a growth
- Bridge two differently colored zones with a tool
- Lean on the walls
- Attach climbing hardware to roots, fronds, or mineral crust
- Discharge static into the plants
- Assume that a non-moving plant is electrically inactive

The color gradients around the drill suggest changing electrical or mineral conditions. Silver near the drill, then green, blue, and black, should be treated as a hazard map until proven otherwise. Cross these zones only over bare, surveyed stone or a temporary insulated walkway.

Bring grounding equipment, but use it cautiously. Grounding a normal machine may make it safer; grounding this chamber may redirect currents through the entire living-mineral network. Your expedition’s electrical engineer should design a controlled discharge system before entry. Carrying a loose grounding cable and attaching it wherever convenient would be reckless.

### 4. Prepare for vertical movement, not walking

Although the chamber could fit within a small area, it is vertically deep and architecturally broken. The shelves are cramped, tilted, layered, and obscured by hanging growth. A route that looks only a few meters long may cross a shaft or terminate at a hidden drop.

No one should move without:

- A harness attached to an independent safety line
- A helmet with a face shield
- A short-range depth scanner or lidar
- A physical route marker
- A partner within sight or on a secured belay
- A planned retreat route

Do not climb the old ladder. Do not trust the gantry. Do not walk on translucent crust merely because it looks solid. The oldest growth has become terrain, but terrain in this place may remain suspended by forces you do not understand.

The central drop is especially dangerous. Its bottom cannot be seen, and the sound of the machinery may make depth perception unreliable. Keep all loose objects secured. A dropped tool might fall for a long time, strike active machinery, or become permanently incorporated into a lower part of the chamber.

Stay away from the drill head and its collar. A machine that has been exerting torque for years without visibly moving is not “stopped.” It may be applying force to the mountain, the plants, or the local structure in a way that ordinary instruments do not show.

### 5. Bring tools that do not depend on change

The chamber’s defining rule is that nothing is allowed to become different. Take this seriously as an operational hazard, not merely a poetic description.

Inside, ordinary processes may be delayed, arrested, preserved, or divided from their expected consequences. Possible examples include:

- A battery may retain its charge but fail to discharge
- A motor may consume power without advancing
- A door may open but never complete its motion
- A crack may remain open without spreading or closing
- A biological wound may neither heal nor worsen
- A light may illuminate an area without fading or moving
- A spoken sound may persist as a buried resonance
- A chemical reaction may begin but never finish

Bring redundant equipment in several categories:

- Mechanical instruments
- Optical instruments
- Passive thermometers and pressure indicators
- Shielded electronic recorders
- Physical notebooks or engraved tags
- Mechanical watches, but do not assume they will keep accurate time
- Multiple independent extraction systems

Do not depend on a single digital timer, radio, motorized winch, or automatic oxygen regulator. A device may continue indicating that it is functioning while accomplishing nothing. Have every critical function available in a manually operated form.

Before entering, establish a strict mission duration by outside time and by oxygen consumption. Do not wait for an instrument to confirm that it is time to leave. If an operation requires “just another minute,” stop immediately. In a place where processes do not naturally complete, unfinished work may remain unfinished forever.

### 6. Avoid making permanent additions

The chamber appears to preserve changes. A footprint remains permanently. A spoken word becomes part of the drone. A broken frond hardens where it falls.

Therefore, behave as though every action is irreversible.

Do not touch anything unnecessarily. Do not leave equipment, samples, waste, flags, batteries, or biological material behind. Do not speak casually, sing, shout, or test echoes. Use a closed communications system and keep all external audio at the lowest practical volume. A voice may not fade in the ordinary way; it may become a permanent acoustic component of the chamber.

Avoid stepping on living growth even if it resembles moss or carpet. Walk only on verified bare rock, existing metal flooring, or a temporary platform designed to distribute weight. If you must place a marker, use removable light or a retrieval line—but first determine whether light itself remains fixed in place.

Do not collect specimens on the first visit. Sampling could damage a structure that is both plant and load-bearing mineral, and the specimen may continue conducting or growing after removal. If a sample is absolutely necessary, isolate it in a nonconductive, impact-resistant, pressure-controlled container and remove it immediately.

### 7. Expect the sensory environment to be disorienting

The chamber will likely be psychologically difficult even if no direct attack occurs.

The noise is continuous and directionless. The vegetation resembles a forest without wind, animals, water, seasons, or decay. The apparent scale will be deceptive: the whole place may be visible at once, while the central shaft makes nearby objects seem distant. Dim industrial lighting will create false depth and hide gaps.

Use helmet displays that provide:

- Artificial horizon lines
- Range and slope information
- Route overlays
- Heart rate and oxygen warnings
- A visual tether to the exit
- A timer that is controlled from outside the chamber

Maintain buddy contact. Watch one another for hypoxia, panic, derealization, fixation on the machinery, or the conviction that a sound or light is changing when it is not. Do not explore alone. The place’s stillness may produce the dangerous impression that nothing can go wrong.

### 8. Establish strict rules for machinery

The old compressor or generator, cables, control booth, and drill should be treated as active industrial equipment, not archaeological curiosities.

Before entering, decide that no one will:

- Flip switches
- Turn valves
- Remove covers
- Disconnect cables
- Restart the compressor
- Touch the drill controls
- Enter the control booth without structural inspection
- Attempt to repair anything
- Readjust the indicator lights
- Test whether the “COOLANT EMPTY” warning is true

The machinery may be in equilibrium with the vegetation. A failed coolant system that has remained “failed” for years may be a stable state. Restoring it could cause a temperature surge, a pressure change, or a sudden movement that the chamber has been holding in suspension.

The red indicators and frozen number should be documented photographically and spectrally, but not interpreted as trustworthy without comparison to independent sensors.

### 9. Plan the retreat before the descent

The most important equipment is not a scanner or a sample case. It is a guaranteed way out.

Set up:

- A redundant route from the lowest permitted shelf to the aperture
- An external winch and manual backup
- An oxygen cache at the entrance and, if possible, along the route
- A continuous tether
- A surface observer who does not enter
- A prearranged extraction signal that does not depend on radio
- A hard abort time
- A rule that any unexplained change means immediate withdrawal

Do not assume the opening will remain accessible merely because it was accessible on entry. The chamber is stable, but your presence may be a new condition. If the plants respond by growing, hardening, conducting, or incorporating your equipment, retreat could become impossible.

Before leaving, conduct a complete count of people, equipment, lines, and samples. Do not abandon a tool because retrieving it would be inconvenient. In this place, “temporary” may mean permanent.

### 10. Psychological and ethical preparation

The chamber is not an empty ruin. It is a functioning, ancient equilibrium composed of growth, machine, mineral, and preserved process. It may not possess animal intelligence, but it is an integrated system. Its lack of reaction should not be mistaken for consent or inertness.

Go as an observer, not a conqueror.

The safest traveler will:

- Minimize contact
- Make no repairs
- Disturb no growth
- Speak only when necessary
- Take photographs and measurements rather than specimens
- Leave the chamber as close as possible to its original state
- Accept that some questions cannot be answered during one visit

Most importantly, do not attempt to make the place useful. The machinery’s labor is pointless by human standards, but it may be one of the conditions keeping the entire pocket intact. Do not restart its systems, shut them down, illuminate the chamber fully, introduce water, or bring in ordinary plants or animals.

Enter with oxygen, insulation, ropes, passive instruments, and a deep respect for irreversibility. The chamber is not dangerous because it is chaotic. It is dangerous because it is perfectly, unnaturally still—and because a human being is made almost entirely of change.
