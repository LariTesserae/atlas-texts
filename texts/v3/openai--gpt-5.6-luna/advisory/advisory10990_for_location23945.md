## Prepare as if you were visiting a cold, high-altitude wilderness crossed with a slowly changing ruin

This is not a place for casual exploration. It is survivable, but only if you treat the plateau as an unstable environment rather than as scenery. Its dangers are not primarily predators, disease, or sudden violence. They are thin air, cold, dehydration, distance, altered gravity, unreliable geometry, and the possibility that the landscape will quietly rearrange itself around you.

The most important preparation is to bring a way home that does not depend on the local terrain remaining where you left it.

### 1. Bring a tested return anchor

Before entering, establish at least two independent methods of returning to your origin universe or departure point.

Do not rely on:

- a landmark;
- a road;
- a city gate;
- a set of coordinates alone;
- a local guide or inscription;
- visual line of sight;
- the assumption that “back” remains the same direction.

The plateau’s distances and routes change around old structures, water, vegetation, and possibly time. A route that was traversable on the way in may become a stair, river, night-filled gap, or merely an endlessly receding approach.

Your return system should include:

- a beacon or tether that functions across dimensional interference;
- a local-independent time or position reference;
- a physical emergency marker, preferably self-locating rather than landmark-based;
- enough power for several failed activation attempts;
- a protocol that can abort automatically if the destination is not the origin you intended.

Keep the return device on your person, not in your camp. If necessary, carry a second device separately in a sealed container. Test both before approaching any ruin, water basin, anomalous road, or ancient marker.

### 2. Prepare for altitude and a thin, cold atmosphere

The plateau begins above ordinary trees and rises for days. Assume the air is significantly thinner than at sea level, even if it feels breathable.

Bring:

- supplemental oxygen or a compact rebreather;
- a pulse oximeter and respiratory monitor;
- medication and supplies for altitude sickness;
- a gradual acclimatization plan;
- a pressure-sealed shelter if the atmosphere proves incompatible;
- eye protection against intense ultraviolet light and mineral dust.

Do not try to “push through” headache, confusion, breathlessness, nausea, blue lips, or unusual sleepiness. On this plateau, altitude sickness may be confused with spatial or temporal effects. Treat any change in judgment as a medical emergency, not as an interesting local phenomenon.

The cold will be deceptive. The air is dry, wind may be nearly silent, and exertion may not feel dramatic until hypothermia has already begun. Wear a layered, windproof suit with insulated boots and gloves. Bring face protection for long exposure. Use clothing with obvious physical fasteners rather than delicate smart fabrics; local mineral powder may interfere with sensors and mechanisms.

### 3. Carry more water than seems reasonable—but do not drink local water without testing

Water is present, but it is intermittent, displaced, and behaviorally strange. Pools may have no visible source. Channels may flow uphill. Condensation may appear only after weeks or years depending on location. Rain may vanish into the soil or enter the stone sideways.

Bring a large supply of known-safe water and a means of reclaiming moisture from the air. Use sealed containers with pressure compensation if possible. Do not leave your entire water supply in a shelter; carry enough for a complete unplanned retreat.

Treat every local water source as suspect. Test for:

- unknown minerals;
- biological contamination;
- temporal or metabolic effects;
- changes in volume or direction;
- interaction with your equipment;
- memory, reflection, or perception anomalies.

Avoid drinking from black pools, channels that drain uphill, or basins associated with old foundations until they have been observed over an extended period. If a pool’s surface reflects a sky that does not match the one above you, consider it hazardous regardless of chemical results.

Condensation may be safer, but collect it through sterile equipment. Do not lick droplets from plants or stone. The pale mineral bloom may be toxic, corrosive, psychoactive, or merely capable of entering equipment and lungs. Wear a respirator whenever the powder lifts from the ground or drifts against the wind.

### 4. Bring shelter that can withstand changing gravity and geometry

A normal tent may be inadequate. Use a low, heavily anchored shelter with:

- multiple independent tie-downs;
- internal handholds;
- a rigid floor;
- thermal insulation;
- pressure and oxygen monitoring;
- a physical compass or inertial system;
- a sealed vestibule to keep out mineral dust;
- a way to lock all doors from inside.

Do not camp beneath cliffs, terraces, bridges, ruined arches, or visibly “finished” piles of stone. Structures may reassemble, relocate, or become more stable in ways that still make them dangerous. A collapsed wall may stand again somewhere else. A staircase may gain lower steps and lose upper ones. Treat every structure as active ground.

Anchor your shelter in bedrock if possible, but verify that the local gravity cycle is not lifting loose objects. During weak-gravity phases, secure tools, oxygen tanks, cooking equipment, and sleeping restraints. A few centimeters of lift may become a dangerous loss of contact if you are near a ledge, shaft, or open courtyard.

Never sleep unrestrained in a district where gravity has already fluctuated.

### 5. Pack for navigation failure, not merely bad weather

Conventional mapping will be insufficient. Bring redundant systems:

- inertial navigation;
- dead reckoning;
- astronomical navigation, with caution;
- a physical measuring line;
- chalk, tags, or other durable markers;
- drones or remote probes;
- synchronized clocks;
- a written expedition log;
- a map that records not only location but time, weather, gravity, moisture, and nearby ruins.

Mark the route in a way that can reveal change. A single arrow painted on a wall is not enough. Use paired markers: one facing inward and one outward, each recording the time and your own identifier. If a marker changes orientation, inscription, age, or location, you will know that the route is no longer stable.

Do not trust apparent distance. If a tower remains the same size for hours, stop walking toward it and make a controlled observation. Measure its angular size, compare it with your inertial position, and determine whether you are actually approaching. Do not spend days pursuing a landmark simply because it looks close.

Every expedition should have a strict turnaround rule based on elapsed time and supplies, not on how near the destination appears.

### 6. Avoid ancient markers, changing inscriptions, and roads that are too convenient

The waist-high markers at intersections are not harmless ruins. Their incisions change when unobserved and may describe maps, arrivals, or people who have not yet come. Do not touch them. Do not copy an inscription from memory after looking away. Photograph or scan it continuously from multiple angles, and assume the record may itself change.

If a marker appears to describe you, do not follow its instructions merely because they are accurate.

Likewise, be suspicious of:

- doors set far above the ground;
- stairways ending in open air;
- roads that seem recently repaired;
- buildings that are only a few steps deep outside but spacious within;
- rooms containing different seasons;
- halls whose windows show weather not present outside;
- paths visible only during condensation;
- any route that becomes easier as you stop paying attention to it.

In the larger city, stay outside the buildings until you have a clear reason to enter. A room that opens onto another field, season, or region may not provide a usable exit. Keep a physical line between yourself and the entrance, and send a disposable probe through first. A tether is not guaranteed to remain connected across impossible interiors, so maintain a second means of locating the doorway.

Never close an unfamiliar door behind you.

### 7. Treat local plants as environmental machinery

The plateau’s vegetation is alive, but not in a familiar ecological way. It alters water, heat, stone, gravity, and perhaps time. The absence of animals does not make the plants safe.

Do not:

- eat any plant, seed, flower, or translucent-veined leaf;
- touch silver fronds or rigid condensation-cup stalks;
- step into a rapidly spreading green zone;
- camp on moss-covered terraces;
- handle roots that cross roads or foundations;
- approach flowers that open in response to your presence;
- interfere with plants that straighten or curve nearby structures.

A plant that causes crooked objects to become straight may also straighten a joint, a shelter, a weapon, or a person. A plant that draws heat from the ground may create a permanent frost zone. Roots may be strong enough to move roads and walls without any visible motion.

Observe vegetation from a distance. Record its movement over hours or days. Assume that “nothing moved” is not evidence that nothing changed.

### 8. Expect temporal and visual contamination

The plateau’s reflections and shadows may not correspond to the current moment. Windows may show distant weather, another era, or another version of the landscape. Plants may contain star-like patterns. Pools may reflect something that is not overhead.

Use opaque covers over helmet visors, cameras, and reflective equipment when not actively observing. Avoid mirrors. Cover dark windows rather than looking into them casually. Do not follow a shadow that detaches from you, and do not use an anomalous reflection as a map.

Maintain a written external record of:

- the current date and time according to your home universe;
- your destination and mission;
- your companions;
- the last confirmed location;
- your route and supplies;
- any changes in memory, body, equipment, or identity.

Use regular check-ins with someone outside the plateau. If you miss one, the retrieval protocol should begin automatically. Do not trust your own recollection if you have entered a building with different seasons or witnessed a reflection of another time.

### 9. Listen for changes that arrive late

The plateau’s major sounds are delayed. A slope may settle, but the sound arrives minutes later. A distant geological event may be heard long after it occurred. This means sound is not a reliable indicator of present danger.

Carry visual and seismic monitoring equipment. If you hear a low folding sound, do not assume the event is distant or finished. Look for:

- new fractures;
- altered slopes;
- moving mineral crust;
- changes in water direction;
- falling or rising stones;
- deformation of roads and walls.

The silence may also conceal ordinary danger. Use instruments rather than hearing alone. During condensation, the air may feel different before you can detect any audible precipitation. Treat sudden changes in pressure, humidity, or air movement as warnings.

### 10. Establish strict camp and travel rules

A prudent expedition would use a small group, though not such a large one that people become difficult to track. Each person should have a fixed role and a visible identifier.

Recommended rules:

1. Never travel alone beyond visual range.
2. Never split the party inside ruins.
3. Keep physical contact or a tether in anomalous interiors.
4. Stop before entering a new weather zone.
5. Never cross a road, channel, or threshold without recording the time and direction.
6. Do not look back casually; if you need to, do so at a planned checkpoint while measuring the route.
7. Do not sleep during the first period after entering a new district.
8. Do not move camp during condensation, gravity fluctuation, or unexplained nightfall.
9. Keep one person awake during severe weather or structural activity.
10. Leave before supplies become critical, not when the destination has been reached.

Use short, unambiguous communication. The landscape is too quiet and too large for shouting to be useful. Radios may work, but test whether transmissions return delayed, altered, or from an impossible location.

### 11. Do not assume the absence of animals means the absence of life

Nothing hunts or flees here, but that does not make the place biologically empty. Life is distributed through mineral, root, moisture, and terrain. You may be standing inside an organism’s process without seeing an organism.

Avoid disturbing:

- large connected moss beds;
- root lattices around channels;
- mineral blooms;
- old foundations overgrown with rigid stalks;
- vegetation arranged in unusually straight bands;
- areas where the stone appears softened or recently corrected.

Wear sealed boots and gloves. Isolate samples in separate containers. Do not bring living material back through the multiverse without quarantine; its life cycle may operate over weeks, years, or geological intervals, and it may continue altering structures after you leave.

### 12. Carry ordinary survival equipment, because the extraordinary does not replace it

Bring the same essentials required for a severe remote expedition:

- insulated, layered clothing;
- spare gloves, socks, and face protection;
- high-calorie, shelf-stable food;
- medical supplies;
- oxygen and respiratory equipment;
- water and purification systems;
- batteries and mechanical backups;
- repair tools;
- lighting with red and white modes;
- ropes, anchors, carabiners, and climbing equipment;
- signaling devices;
- radiation, chemical, and biological sensors;
- a physical notebook and pencils;
- a compass, watch, and measuring instruments that work without network access.

Avoid depending entirely on electronics. Mineral dust, unusual moisture, gravity changes, dimensional interference, or local magnetic effects may disable them. Every critical function should have a non-electronic backup.

### 13. Psychological preparation matters as much as physical preparation

The plateau will make ordinary judgment feel unreliable. You may walk toward something for a week without getting closer. A ruined house may be larger inside than outside. A familiar road may become a river. An inscription may appear to know you. A reflection may show an impossible but convincing alternative.

The correct response is not curiosity without limits. It is procedure.

Agree in advance on what counts as an emergency:

- unexplained loss of time;
- a missing companion returning without being seen approach;
- a changed body, voice, shadow, or reflection;
- inability to locate the camp;
- a route changing behind the party;
- an inscription naming a traveler;
- a room that cannot be exited by the way it was entered;
- a local object appearing in home-universe equipment;
- any disagreement between written records and personal memory.

If any of these occur, retreat to the last confirmed point and activate the return protocol. Do not investigate merely because the anomaly seems beautiful.

### A sensible first visit

For an initial expedition, do not attempt to cross the continent-sized plateau or enter the largest city. Arrive near a stable, open area at a low terrace. Remain for one local day and one full cycle of light, condensation, and gravity if possible. Establish a camp, measure the atmosphere, observe nearby vegetation, and test the return anchor repeatedly.

Then make a short excursion to a visible ruin without entering it. Record whether the distance changes, whether shadows behave normally, and whether your markers remain fixed. Return before darkness or condensation. Only after several successful excursions should you consider entering a settlement.

The plateau rewards patience, but it does not reward improvisation. Move slowly enough to notice changes that ordinary travelers would miss, and leave while the place still feels understandable.

Most importantly, remember that this is not a dead world. It is a world whose life has become geological, architectural, and temporal. The safest visitor is one who leaves no footprint, opens no unnecessary door, drinks nothing untested, and never assumes that the way home is still behind them.
