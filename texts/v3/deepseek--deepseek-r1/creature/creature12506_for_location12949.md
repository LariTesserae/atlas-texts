The silence within the roar belongs to the **Stone-Sleepers**. They are not fauna in the traditional sense, nor are they flora. They are patient accretions, living stalagmites shaped by cold and condensation and the slow pulse of the foundry’s geothermal blood.

**Form:** Imagine nodules of dense, crystalline chitin, roughly the size and shape of a human torso, though irregular and lumpy. They grow directly from the weeping basalt, often clustered at the lips of the steaming fissures or nestled deep within the dense, spongy cores of the largest moss pillars where residual warmth gathers. Their surface is a mosaic of interlocking plates, coloured like cold slate or tarnished bronze, slick with perpetual condensation. This outer shell isn't rigid armour; it’s layered, like sedimentary rock, and possesses a slight, viscous flexibility. From between these plates, or sometimes through porous sections, extrude thick, root-like tendrils. These are translucent, glassy structures, internally threaded with faint, bioluminescent strands that pulse with a light as dim and cold as the distant fungi – a slow, rhythmic blue-green gleam, perhaps once every few minutes. The tendrils weave into the surrounding moss, burrow into fissures seeking mineral seepage, or anchor the Sleeper firmly to the decaying metal substrate it grows upon.

**Physiology:** They are sessile chemosynthesizers and mineral feeders. The glassy tendrils act as both roots and sensory organs, drawing nutrients directly from the mineral-rich water weeping from the rock and the dissolved metals released by the fungi digesting the old machinery. The faint luminescence within these tendrils is a byproduct of their metabolic processes, interacting with trace geothermal gases and ambient moisture. Their growth is glacial. A new plate forms layer by layer over decades as dissolved minerals precipitate out of the constant dampness, guided by the Sleeper’s slow biological directives. They are cold-blooded in the extreme, their internal processes tuned to the near-freezing ambient temperature; any significant warmth might disrupt their delicate mineral accretion.

**Life Cycle & Reproduction:** Reproduction is equally patient. When a Stone-Sleeper reaches a critical mass (a process taking centuries), a specific internal pressure builds, likely triggered by subtle shifts in geothermal activity or mineral saturation. A fracture line appears along its upper surface. Over months or years, this fissure widens, not through brute force, but through the dissolution and re-precipitation of its own chitinous plates. Eventually, a smaller, immature nodule – a bud – detaches or oozes free, falling onto the damp basalt nearby. This bud, little more than a fist-sized lump of soft, gelatinous precursor material encasing a core of concentrated minerals, will immediately begin secreting its first, thin crystalline plates, anchoring itself to the stone. Many fail, dissolving back into the environment or being absorbed by opportunistic mosses. Those that succeed become new Stone-Sleepers, beginning their multi-century slumber.

**Role in the Ecology:** They are the ultimate expression of the foundry's slow dynamic. They are not predators, not prey in any conventional sense (though the tiny, blind arthropods might graze on the biofilm forming on their damp surfaces). They are inert monuments to persistence. Their presence subtly alters the micro-environment: their mineral uptake concentrates specific elements, perhaps influencing the types of moss or fungi that can grow nearby. The faint light they emit is negligible for vision but might serve as a subtle beacon or territorial marker in the sound-blinded darkness, detectable only by creatures attuned to such slow, cold luminescence. To stumble upon a cluster of them, their glassy roots glistening, their cold light pulsing imperceptibly slowly amidst the roaring dark, is to witness life not as vigour, but as geological patience made manifest. They are the rooted foundry dreaming in stone.
