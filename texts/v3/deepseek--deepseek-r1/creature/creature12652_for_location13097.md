## The People of the Hanging Root-City: The Root-Weavers

They call themselves the **Kethra**, meaning "Those Who Hold the Dew," though outsiders might whisper "Root-Weavers" or "Moss-Scrapers." They are not large in number – perhaps a few hundred at most, clustered in their basalt warrens – but they are the conscious architects and careful tenders of this suspended miracle. To exist here is to be a master of micro-adaptation, and the Kethra embody this utterly.

### Physicality: Sculpted by Stone and Mist
*   **Stature & Build:** Compact and wiry, rarely exceeding five feet tall. Generations navigating narrow crevices and clinging to vertical surfaces have honed their bodies for agility and low center of gravity. Limbs are long relative to their torsos, fingers and toes are slender, strong, and remarkably dexterous, almost prehensile. Bone structure is dense, minimizing fragility against falls or the constant press of rock.
*   **Skin & Covering:** Their skin is a marvel of adaptation. Smooth, hairless, and possessing a faint, waxy sheen reminiscent of the rubbery vines. It ranges in hue from deep, weathered grey to muted olive-green, providing near-perfect camouflage against the mossy basalt. This skin is incredibly efficient at absorbing ambient moisture directly from the humid air and porous rock, minimizing perspiration loss. Fine, almost invisible patterns – like fractal lichen or the veins in a leaf – trace across their skin, potentially aiding in this moisture absorption or thermoregulation.
*   **Senses:** Eyes are large, dark, and slightly protuberant, adapted to the perpetual twilight. They see exceptionally well in low light and discern subtle shifts in the green-gold spectrum. Ears are small and close to the head, often partially covered by folds of skin or moss-like hair (a dark, coarse, moisture-trapping fuzz), protecting them from the constant, deafening insect drone while still allowing them to pick out crucial sounds – the specific chirp of a predator bird, the scrape of stone on stone, the subtle shift in the wind that heralds the life-giving fog. Their sense of smell is acute, vital for detecting ripening air-plants, the subtle decay indicating fungal growth, or the pheromones marking territory on the wind.
*   **Movement:** They move with deliberate, fluid grace. Steps are silent, placing each foot with precision on the smooth, worn stone or thick vines. Their gait is a low glide, minimizing disturbance. When climbing – which is constant – they use friction and balance more than grip, flowing over surfaces like water over stone.

### Culture & Society: The Delicate Balance
*   **Integration:** They are not separate from the ecology; they are a keystone species within it. Their dwellings are carved *with* the rock's natural fissures. Vines are trained over doorways for shade and moisture capture, not cut away. Moss is carefully cultivated on specific walls as insulation and a water reservoir. They understand the life cycles of every insect, bird, and plant, knowing which are food, which are pollinators, which are dangerous, and which are symbiotic partners.
*   **Water Wisdom:** Water is the central tenet of their existence. Every action is weighed against moisture loss. Speech is often soft, minimal, and supplemented by intricate sign language using their long fingers – whispers and gestures preserve precious breath moisture. Tears are a profound waste, reserved only for the deepest grief. Rituals revolve around the fog's arrival: silent meditation on the plateau's edge to feel its first cool kiss, placing porous stone bowls to collect condensation.
*   **Technology & Craft:** Their tech is elegantly primitive and perfectly suited. Tools are primarily chipped basalt (knives, scrapers, adzes) and hardened bone (needles, awls). They weave incredibly strong, water-resistant cord and baskets from processed vine fibers and lichen threads. Their most vital craft is **dew-weaving**: creating intricate nets of fine fibers strung between poles or over basins overnight to catch fog droplets, channeling the precious liquid into sealed gourds or porous rock cisterns carved deep within the basalt. Fire is used sparingly – small, smoldering hearths of compressed dried moss and lichen provide essential warmth in the coolest hours and light for intricate tasks within the dim dwellings.
*   **Social Structure:** Society is communal and deeply pragmatic. Leadership is fluid, based on proven wisdom in water management, ecological knowledge, and carving/dew-weaving skill. Decisions are made in the tiny moss-circled courtyards, often through silent consensus reached after hours of observation and minimal discussion. Individuality is expressed subtly – in the patterns of lichen encouraged on one's dwelling wall, the specific way vines are trained, or the intricate knotwork on a dew-net or basket.
*   **Art & Spirituality:** Their art *is* their survival and their environment. Patterns carved into lintels mimic the veins in leaves or the fractal growth of lichen. Body adornment is minimal: perhaps a polished beetle carapace, a necklace of tiny, iridescent bird feathers, or intricate, temporary patterns drawn in damp moss-paste on their skin for rituals. They revere the Basalt (the enduring giver of shelter and moisture), the Fog (the life-bringer), and the Green Mantle (the collective spirit of the plants that hold the water). Their primary spiritual practice is patient observation and meticulous tending, maintaining the sacred balance.

### Life in the Green Murmur
A Kethra's day begins before dawn, checking dew-nets, observing the fog's density and behavior. Mornings are for harvesting: collecting moisture-swollen moss and lichen (squeezed for water or eaten directly), gathering edible air-plant bulbs, snaring fat grubs from under bark-like lichen, or collecting eggs from tolerant cliff-nesting birds. Afternoons, when the mist burns off and the insect drone peaks, are often spent within the cool dwellings: weaving, tool-making, repairing, tending to infants, or in quiet contemplation. Evenings bring communal tasks in the courtyards – processing food, weaving new nets, sharing knowledge through gesture and sparse words – before the silent vigil for the fog's return. Sleep is deep and still, bodies pressed against the cool, moisture-seeping basalt walls.

They live on the edge of abundance and scarcity, every breath humid, every sound layered with life, every movement a negotiation with gravity and moisture. The Kethra are not conquerors of their harsh, suspended world; they are its most attentive students, its most careful gardeners, and its most perfectly adapted children. Their existence is a testament to life's tenacity, woven root by root and drop by drop into the ancient, hanging stone.
