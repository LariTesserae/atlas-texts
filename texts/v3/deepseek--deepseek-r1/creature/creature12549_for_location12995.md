Here, life clings, thrives, and reclaims, all within the relentless drip and hiss. The fauna is sparse, not for lack of opportunity, but because existence demands a unique kind of resilience – a tolerance for the warm wet, the thin air, the metallic tang leaching from the decaying industry, and the sheer, improbable isolation.

The most obvious denizens are the **iridescent beetles**. Not the tiny, scurrying kind found elsewhere, but larger, slower creatures, their carapaces a shifting oil-slick of blues, greens, and deep purples. They move with a deliberate, almost ponderous grace over the glistening moss carpets and the rubbery leaves of the crimson-bloomed shrubs. Their iridescence isn't just for show; it seems to shimmer differently in the diffuse, steam-filtered light, perhaps a form of communication in this sound-drowned world, or camouflage against the wet, colourful backdrop. They feed on the thickest mosses and the algae films, their mandibles adapted to shear through the gelatinous layers. Occasionally, one might be seen delicately sipping condensation from a hairy vine.

Near the seeping fissures and miniature, perpetually steaming marshes, **ghost-flatworms** glide. Translucent, almost invisible except for a faint opalescent sheen, they undulate through the warm, mineral-rich mud and the algal slime. They lack eyes entirely, sensing their world through chemical gradients in the water and vibrations from the constant drip. They feed on microbes and the simplest algae, thriving in the hottest, most chemical-laden pools where the yellow pipes bleed rust. Their bodies are tough, resistant to the heavy metals slowly poisoning the water table.

Within the deepest crevices fed by steam, where the heat is most intense and the light most dim, dwell colonies of **pale, blind crustaceans**. Shrimp-like but no larger than a thumbnail, they scuttle over the warm rock, scavenging microscopic detritus and bacterial films. Their exoskeletons are chalky white, lacking pigment in their sunless world. Long, feathery antennae sweep the water constantly, tasting for food and danger. They are quick to dart into the smallest cracks at the slightest disturbance – a falling droplet, the groan of shifting metal.

And everywhere, unseen but fundamental, are the **rust-eaters**. Not animals, but specialized microbial colonies thriving in the damp rust blooming on the flaking yellow pipes, the sagging pump-house roof, and the jagged ends of severed conduits. They are chemosynthetic, deriving energy from the oxidation of iron and other metals, slowly dissolving the human structures into the mountain's chemistry. Their presence is marked only by the vivid streaks of orange and red staining the green where metal and moisture meet, and by the faint, acrid smell that sometimes cuts through the mineral sweetness when the steam shifts.

Movement is rare, sound even rarer from the creatures themselves. The dominant symphony remains the *plink-plink* of water, the hiss of steam, and the metallic protests of the decaying structures. The life here is small, slow, and tenacious, perfectly adapted to a world that is both a haven of warmth and moisture and a prison of stone, steam, and slow decay. They are the inheritors of the Crucible, reclaiming the wound patiently, millimeter by mossy millimeter, drip by eternal drip.
