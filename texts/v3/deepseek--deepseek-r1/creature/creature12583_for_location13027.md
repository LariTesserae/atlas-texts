They are called the Stone-Sleepers. Not by themselves, perhaps, for they speak little and name even less, but by the rare lowlander who has glimpsed them vanishing into a shadowed doorway or sensed their silent observation from a high crevice. They are not giants of myth, nor wizened spirits, but *people* shaped as absolutely by the basin's stony dream as the lichen on the rock or the slow drip of water.

**Physique & Presence:**
They are short and dense-limbed, built for conserving heat against the perpetual chill. Their skin, where exposed to the thin, misty air, is the colour and texture of weathered basalt – a deep, grey-brown, thick and leathery, etched with fine lines like the cracks in the cliff face. It holds the cool dampness of the basin. Their movements are deliberate, economical, mirroring the imperceptible shift of stone. A turn of the head is slow, a step is placed with the careful finality of a settling boulder. They seem to expend no unnecessary energy; even breathing is shallow and infrequent, adapted to the thin air. When still, which is often, they become near-invisible, their drab, undyed clothing (rough-spun from mountain goat hair and lichen fibres) and stony skin merging with the landscape. Their eyes, deep-set beneath heavy brows, are the startling colour of the silt pools – a dark, opaque grey that holds the flat, diffuse light without reflection. They watch. Always, they watch.

**Society & Sustenance:**
Their settlement is not a village bustling with life, but a cluster of hibernating stones. Activity is sparse, focused entirely on the minimal needs of persistence. They number perhaps fifty, no more. There is no visible hierarchy, only the shared, unspoken understanding of the basin's rhythms. Their lives revolve around water and the scant offerings of the rock.
*   **Water:** The catchment cistern is their heart. Guarded, maintained with silent reverence. They collect the weeping rivulets in bone or hardened leather vessels, their touch feather-light on the moss to avoid disturbing the flow.
*   **Food:** They scrape the thickest lichens ("stone-moss") from sun-warmed rocks with bone blades, soaking and pounding them into a bitter, gritty paste. They net the translucent insects that hover near moisture, a rare protein source. Occasionally, they snare the dun birds or the slow, cold lizards, using sinew and sharpened bone. Roots from the stunted cliff shrubs are painstakingly gathered. Every morsel is consumed; waste is unknown here.
*   **Shelter:** The stone huts are maintained, not improved. A fallen roof slab is laboriously righted with levers of bone-hard wood gathered far below. Gaps in walls are silently filled with smaller stones. The hides over doorways are replaced only when they crumble to dust, using the tough skin of mountain goats taken on rare, perilous hunts down the gentler slope.

**Culture & Spirit:**
Time holds little meaning in seasons or years. They mark its passage by the deepening of a water-cut groove in the cistern lip, by the slow encroachment of moss over a particular stone slab, by the infrequent fall of a significant rock from the cliffs. Their tools – chipped basalt, shaped bone, braided sinew – are ancient, passed down, repaired endlessly. They possess no decoration beyond the inherent patterns in the stone they handle. Fire is used only in the deepest cold, within the largest hut, fueled by precious, bone-dry lichen and twigs hoarded for generations. Its light is a rare, flickering aberration in the grey stillness.

They communicate primarily through touch – a hand laid briefly on an arm, the press of a shoulder – and through minute shifts in posture or the direction of their gaze. Speech, when it occurs, is a low, gravelly murmur, barely more than a vibration in the chest, used only for essential information. They sing, if it can be called singing: a deep, resonant humming that seems to emanate from the stone itself, matching the frequency of the wind sighing over the distant peaks, or the hollow *plink* of a water drop. It is less a melody and more a merging with the basin's ambient sound.

Their existence *is* their ritual. The careful collection of water, the silent scraping of lichen, the patient mending of a hide – these are acts performed with a focused solemnity that borders on reverence. They seem to understand themselves as temporary tenants within the mountain's immense, enduring slumber. They are not masters of the basin; they are its slow, stony dreamers, persisting within its cool, damp palm, generation after generation, moving at the pace of geology, breathing the air of deep time. To see one is to feel the profound weight of the quiet, the immense patience of the stone, made briefly, softly, animate.

They are the stillness given form, the whisper against the silence. The Stone-Sleepers.
