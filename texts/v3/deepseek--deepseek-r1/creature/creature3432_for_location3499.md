The Moss-Stone Plateau of Tarn Velyn nurtures life not through abundance, but through *insistence*. What thrives here does so by becoming an extension of the stone, the water, and the silence. It is an ecosystem of slow metabolisms, mineral appetites, and profound patience.

1.  **The Stone-Weavers (Lithobionts):** Life begins invisibly, woven into the very fabric of the basalt. Microorganisms – chemolithoautotrophs – thrive in the warm, damp fissures. They feed not on sunlight or organic matter, but on the minerals leaching from the rock itself (iron, sulfur, trace elements activated by the constant moisture and geothermal warmth). Their slow, constant processing subtly alters the rock's surface, creating the faint, glistening black patina seen on weeping cliffs and paving stones. They are the foundation, exuding minute warmth and priming the stone for larger colonizers.

2.  **The Emerald Mantle (Bryo-Symphytes):** Mosses dominate the visible flora, but they are not mere passive carpets. The dominant species, **Lumin-moss (*Viridilapis profundus*)**, possesses a symbiotic relationship with the stone-weavers. Its rhizoids penetrate microscopic channels etched by the microbes, drawing mineral nutrients directly. In return, its dense, deep-pile structure traps and condenses atmospheric moisture, channeling it back to the stone and microbes. Its faint bioluminescence (barely visible in the perpetual gloom, appearing as a deep, internal emerald glow) is a byproduct of its unique mineral processing, possibly attracting specific pollinators or deterring micro-grazers. Hardy ferns (**Tarn Bracken (*Pteris velum*)**) cluster around tarn edges and fissures, their broad, waxy fronds designed to maximize condensation capture in the absence of flowing streams. Their spores are dispersed not by wind (too still), but by water droplets rolling off their surfaces into the tarns.

3.  **The Sentinel Trees (Petrarbors):** The stunted pines (**Weepwood Pine (*Pinus lacrimans*)**) and gnarled birches (**Mistroot Birch (*Betula nebulradix*)**) are marvels of tenacity. Their roots delve deep into cracks, following mineral-rich seeps, often intertwining with thick moss cushions for stability and moisture retention. Their growth is glacial, adding mere millimeters per decade. The Weepwood Pine's drooping branches are adapted to shed the constant drizzle efficiently, while its dark, resinous needles resist fungal rot. The Mistroot Birch's peeling, silver bark reflects the muted light and sheds excess moisture; its shallow, wide-spreading root network is a master of capturing surface condensation and the thin soil formed by moss decay. They produce seeds rarely, only during rare, slightly drier periods when the mist thins just enough for wind dispersal over short distances. Most reproduction is via root suckers, creating the isolated groves.

4.  **Tarn Dwellers (Aqua-Silex):** The dark tarns, though seemingly lifeless, harbor specialized, near-microscopic life. **Stone-fluke Algae (*Petroalga obscura*)** form the base, thriving in the mineral-rich, peat-stained water, photosynthesizing efficiently in the low, diffused light. They are grazed upon by tiny, translucent crustaceans (**Tarn-Skimmers (*Leptostraca taciturna*)**), no larger than a grain of sand, which filter the algae with feathery appendages. They are blind and sluggish, conserving energy in the cold water. The apex predator of the tarn is the **Basalt Leech (*Hirudo saxum*)**, a flat, dark grey creature that clings to submerged rocks, indistinguishable from the stone itself. It ambushes Tarn-Skimmers with a lightning-fast (for this environment), proboscis-like mouth. All tarn life is silent, slow-moving, and produces minimal disturbance on the water's surface – mere faint, slow ripples.

5.  **The People of Velynstead (Homo Saxum):** The human inhabitants are physiologically and culturally adapted. They are typically shorter and broader-chested than lowlanders, adapted to the thin air. Their skin tends to be pale but remarkably resistant to constant dampness and cold, with a slightly waxy feel. Their most notable adaptation is **"Stone-Sense"** – a heightened, subconscious awareness of geothermal warmth gradients and the faintest vibrations through the rock, allowing them to find new seeps, avoid unstable ground, and navigate fog-bound open stretches. Their lives are governed by the plateau's rhythm:
    *   **Builders:** They quarry stone with surprising precision using heat (controlled fires on cold rock, followed by dousing with icy spring water to fracture it) and simple bronze/stone tools. Structures are designed to *channel* water: roofs shed into carved gutters feeding cisterns; courtyards are built around springs; walls are slightly angled to encourage moss growth for insulation.
    *   **Gatherers:** They harvest Lumin-moss (for insulation, bedding, and a faint, safe light source in sealed jars), Tarn Bracken fronds (for thatch, fiber, and food – young fronds are edible), specific lichens, and the coarse plateau grass (for fodder, thatch, and rough textiles).
    *   **"Farmers":** They practice "mineral husbandry." They encourage moss growth on specific rock faces by scraping competing growth and directing seeps. They cultivate edible fungi (**Stone-Cap Mushrooms (*Boletus profundis*)**) in dark, warm, moss-lined root cellars or natural crevices, feeding them crushed rock and peat.
    *   **Mindset:** They are quiet, deliberate, and deeply observant. Their language is soft and guttural, with many words for types of rock, moisture, moss, and stillness. They possess a profound reverence for the plateau's equilibrium and view themselves as custodians within it, not masters over it. Change is viewed with deep suspicion; their technology evolves very slowly, focused solely on better harmonizing with the existing conditions. Their silence is both practical (respecting the environment, conserving energy) and spiritual.

**The Unifying Principle: Symbiosis and Slowness.** Every organism on Tarn Velyn, including its people, exists in a complex, slow-motion symbiosis. Life feeds on the minerals released by water and stone, facilitated by microbes. Mosses create soil and trap water. Trees provide shelter and stability. Humans manage moss growth and water flow, inadvertently creating habitats for other life. The warmth from the stone enables metabolism despite the altitude and damp. The perpetual mist diffuses light evenly, sustaining photosynthesis without scorching. The profound quiet and frozen equilibrium demand minimal energy expenditure. Life here is not a triumphant conquest, but a patient, whispered negotiation with stone and vapor, thriving through integration and infinite patience beneath the vast, soft sky. The absence of conventional fauna is replaced by this intricate, slow-burning tapestry of stone-bound life.
