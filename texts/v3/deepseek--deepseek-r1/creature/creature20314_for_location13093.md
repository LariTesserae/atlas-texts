The inhabitants of the Weeping Stone are creatures of perpetual damp and deafening resonance, evolved not for light or silence, but for the warm, saturated dark and the crushing weight of sound. They are pale, soft, and relentless, thriving in the raw, unfinished geology. Here is what crawls, swims, and clings:

1.  **The Drip-Scuttlers (Crustacea *stillanscursor*):** Translucent, shrimp-like arthropods no larger than a fingernail, swarming in uncountable millions across the algal mats and weeping walls. Their primary sensory organs are not eyes (vestigial pits) but **vibration-sensitive cilia** covering their carapaces. They "hear" the world through the constant drumming of drips, interpreting the unique resonance patterns of safe feeding grounds (thick algae near lights) versus danger (predator movement, collapsing rock flour). They move in skittering, synchronized pulses, riding the ripples of falling water. Their main defense is sheer numbers and near-instantaneous collective shifts in direction.

2.  **Echo-Eels (Anguilliformes *resonarecaecus*):** Slender, blind fish, 6-10 inches long, with skin like wet tissue paper revealing faint outlines of bone and organ. Their heads are dominated by **enormous lateral line canals** – visible grooves pulsing with sensory nerves. They navigate and hunt entirely through the reservoir's chaotic hydro-acoustics, mapping the cavern through the reflected roar. They flicker just below the surface, sensing the minute disturbances caused by struggling insects or scuttlers. Their jaws are small but needle-toothed, snapping with startling speed at vibrations indicating prey.

3.  **Stone-Weep Grubs (Urodela *litholacrima*):** Pale, grub-like salamanders, 4-6 inches long, with vestigial limbs and smooth, perpetually moist skin the color of the weeping limestone. They are **chemical grazers**. Specialized glands in their flattened mouths secrete enzymes that break down the thick biofilms and mineral-laden ooze weeping from the walls. They move with agonizing slowness, clinging to vertical surfaces via mucus-secreting ventral pads, their blunt heads constantly nuzzling the rock. Their only predators are the larger catfish that occasionally venture onto shelves during high-water sloshes.

4.  **Rust-Mites (Acari *ferroputris*):** Tiny, sightless arachnids swarming over the decaying industrial metal. Their bodies are a dull, mottled orange-brown, camouflaged against rust. They possess **iron-reducing bacteria in their digestive tracts**, allowing them to directly metabolize ferric oxides. They scrape microscopic flakes of rust and consume the mineral-rich microbial mats thriving on corroded surfaces. They vibrate intensely near sparking junctions, drawn to the ozone and minute electrical fields – a dangerous attraction that often ends in sizzling demise.

5.  **The Groaner Catfish (Siluriformes *stridorabyssus*):** The apex predators of the shallows. Up to 2 feet long, these sluggish, pale catfish possess **hyper-sensitive barbels** that drag through the silty bottom. Their most striking adaptation is a **resonant swim bladder** connected to modified jaw bones. They don't just sense vibrations; they *produce* them. A low-frequency, grinding hum – barely audible over the ambient roar but felt deep in the bones – pulses from them. This sound stuns smaller prey (dazed scuttlers, echo-eels) and allows them to "scan" debris piles and murky alcoves via echo-location refined for the reservoir's unique acoustics. They lurk near submerged machinery, their pale forms half-buried in silt.

6.  **Resonance Moths (Lepidoptera *tonitruvolans*):** Large (handspan), velvety-winged moths, seemingly silent fliers. Their wings are etched with microscopic ridges that, when flapped at specific frequencies, **cancel out the ambient roar in their immediate vicinity**. This creates tiny, temporary bubbles of relative acoustic quiet. They use these quiet zones to sense the ultrasonic clicks of cave crickets (otherwise lost in the noise) and the delicate wing-beats of potential mates. They are drawn to the industrial lights not for the light itself, but for the concentrated heat and the swarms of midges it attracts. Their bodies are coated in hydrophobic scales that shed the constant airborne moisture.

7.  **Concrete Borers (Coleoptera *caementivorus*):** Thumb-sized, blind beetles with heavily sclerotized, wedge-shaped heads and powerful mandibles. They infest the crumbling concrete structures and submerged wooden debris. Their larvae secrete a **calcium-dissolving acid**, softening the concrete, while the adults grind the weakened material into digestible paste using mandibles reinforced with silica grains scavenged from the limestone. They move slowly, their armored bodies clicking faintly against the concrete, a sound lost to all but the most sensitive vibration hunters (like the Groaners, who occasionally crunch them).

**Life in the Roar:** Communication is tactile, chemical, or vibratory. Mating displays involve intricate patterns of taps or shudders transmitted through water or rock. Predators exploit the noise for ambush; prey rely on camouflage and sheer numbers. The constant moisture dictates everything – desiccation means instant death. The failing machines provide unexpected niches: warmth gradients, novel minerals (rust), electrical fields, and sheltered cavities within decaying structures. The life here is not monstrous, but profoundly specialized. They are the flesh of the weeping stone, the animate expression of damp, dark, and deafening sound, thriving in an adolescence of stone and water that will endure long after the last spark fizzles from the rusting junction box. They *are* the reservoir, breathing, feeding, and reproducing within its saturated, roaring embrace.
