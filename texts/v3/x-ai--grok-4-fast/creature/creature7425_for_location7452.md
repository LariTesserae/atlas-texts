In the heart of this luminous submergence, life persists not in teeming multitudes but in quiet, deliberate anchors—fragile sentinels rooted against the engineered tide, their existence a testament to the builders' foresight and the water's nurturing insistence. They are the chamber's sole inhabitants, these verdant whispers, not a riot of growth but a curated few, each cluster a deliberate act of defiance against the vast aqueous emptiness. No grand kelp towers here, no algal blooms choking the light; instead, slender waterweeds rise from their clayey beds like forgotten quills, their stems threading upward in hesitant spirals, pale green and translucent at the tips where they capture the refracted sun. These are not wild opportunists but cultivated wards—hydrophytes selected for their humility, perhaps descendants of rice shoots or watercress ferried from drier realms above, their seeds coaxed into the troughs by hands that knew the sea's encroachment as both foe and provider.

Consider the waterweed first, Vallisneria-like in form but humbler, its runners creeping along the lower walls in lazy arcs, no more than a handspan in length before they curl back into the substrate. Each plant numbers but a dozen leaves, broad and lanceolate, veined with faint silver lines that pulse subtly under the aquamarine glow, as if the light itself courses through them like blood. They sway not in frenzy but in synchrony with the chamber's pulse—the inflow's whoosh bending them eastward in rhythmic bows, the central eddy twisting their tips into gentle helices that release spores in unseen drifts. Life within them is slow, metabolic fires banked low in the temperate warmth: photosynthesis hums at the edges of efficiency, chloroplasts drinking the amplified rays through water's veil, converting minerals leached from the stone into sugars that sustain not just themselves but the chamber's subtle equilibrium. No flowers bloom here, no seeds scatter profligately; reproduction is clonal, a vegetative creep that mirrors the builders' ethos of restraint, ensuring the greens endure without overwhelming the space they soften.

Interwoven among them are the fern-like aquatics, ceratopteris echoes softened by selection—fronds unfurling in delicate rosettes, each cluster a shallow saucer of feathery pinnules, emerald at the base fading to chartreuse where the currents tease them free. These are fewer still, perhaps six outposts scattered asymmetrically along the perimeter, rooted in pockets of nutrient-rich clay that the masons must have packed with silt from upstream shallows. Their life is one of quiet expansion, rhizomes probing the packed earth like blind fingers, drawing dissolved nitrates and phosphates from the circulating flow—traces of ancient fertilizers, perhaps bone meal or ash mingled in by those long-gone hands. In the light's relentless cascade, they photosynthesize with a faint effervescence, oxygen bubbles detaching from their undersides in silvery chains, rising to merge with the chamber's own froth. These bubbles are their breath made visible, a dialogue with the water: each pop against the ceiling a release, each dissolution a return, fostering the oxygen that keeps the medium limpid and the warmth from souring into stagnation. Predators find no purchase here—no snails to rasp their edges, no herbivorous fry to nibble their margins—for the chamber's sterility is absolute, a vacuum of animal ambition that lets these plants thrive in isolation, their growth measured in millimeters per cycle of the sun.

Yet life in this sanctum is not confined to the visible greens; it whispers in the unseen, the microbial undercurrent that the plants both host and command. On the fronds' surfaces, a thin biofilm clings—a translucent mat of diatoms and cyanobacteria, single-celled nomads that hitch rides on the currents, their silica shells glinting like infinitesimal prisms in the quartz-born light. These are the chamber's unseen laborers, fixing nitrogen from the mineral tang, recycling the plants' excretions into usable forms, their division a silent multiplication that sustains the whole without fanfare. No eyes behold them, no hands tend them; they are the inheritance of the water itself, introduced unwittingly through the vents' inflow, blooming in the warmth to form a symbiotic haze that the naked gaze might mistake for mere sediment. In the eddies' swirl, they dance—pseudopods of bacteria extending to graze on decaying frond tips, protozoa grazing in turn on the bacteria, a food web collapsed to the scale of the unseen, where death feeds life in perpetual, muted turnover.

No larger creatures stir this domain; the absence is profound, a choice as deliberate as the stone's fitting. Fish would foul the clarity with waste, insects disrupt the engineered calm—thus the builders sealed it against such intrusions, their reed-diving kin perhaps visiting only to harvest the greens for above-world pots, surfacing with bundles of weed to feed families on the flooded plains. But within, the inhabitants rule unchallenged: the plants, ancient in lineage yet newborn in this niche, their lives a slow unfolding measured by the light's arc and the water's roar. As currents quicken in the midday blaze, a frond uncurls a fraction more, releasing a fresh bubble that joins the chorus; in the dimmer pulse of afternoon, the waterweeds retract slightly, conserving against the cooling inflow. They are not conquerors but adapters, their roots gripping the clay as if to echo the masons' labor, their leaves a verdant veil over the stone's severity. In this roaring, light-drenched womb, they live as the chamber lives—enduring, circulating, a fragile pulse against the submergence, where every sway and bubble is a quiet assertion of persistence in a world handed over to the waves.
