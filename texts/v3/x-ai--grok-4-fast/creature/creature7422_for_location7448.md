In the veiled hush of this embryonic cradle, life does not conquer the submersion so much as it yields to it, weaving itself into the slow pulse of warm currents and half-formed stone. The inhabitants emerge not as bold colonizers but as quiet inheritors, their forms and rhythms attuned to the plateau's languid becoming—a symphony of adaptation where the vanished builders' echoes linger in bone and habit, transmuted through generations of liquid intimacy. They are the Submeri, a people not born of the surface but gestated in the haze, their lineage traced to those primitive hands that once adzed tufa into domes and lashed coral into bridges. Long vanished? No, merely dissolved into the ecology, their descendants reshaped by the all-encompassing sea: bodies elongated and buoyant, skins translucent like the fish they kin with, gills frilled along necks and wrists like the delicate veins of kelp fronds. They drift more than they walk, their limbs webbed and lithe, propelled by finned tails that unfurl from the base of spines curved like the plateau's spires. Height varies in the dim light's mercy—some no taller than a pumice outcrop at two meters, others stretched to thrice that in the deeper basins, their forms pallid and pearlescent, etched with faint bioluminescent tattoos that pulse in rhythm with the vents' exhales, marking kinships or visions induced by the water's whispering chemistries.

These Submeri live in fluid clans, their society a loose lattice mirroring the overgrown urban imprints: not rigid hierarchies but drifting affinities, bound by the shared breath of symbiotic flora. A clan might claim a cluster of dome-habitats in the valley's heart, where basalt walls, now porous with root intrusions, house multi-chambered warrens. Here, they tend the living buttresses—vines and ferns that pierce stone to form natural arches, their buoyant sacs harvested for air-trapping masks during rare ascents to the hazy veil above. Daily rhythms unfold in patient cycles: dawn's dim shafts (what passes for it in eternal twilight) summon gatherings on stepped terraces, where Submeri entwine in ritual drifts, bodies brushing in high-saturation intimacy, sharing nutrients via communal pools warmed by vent aqueducts. Their tech remains primitive, an extension of the raw geology—adzes of sharpened obsidian for carving pumice lanterns, woven reed nets to corral schools of iridescent fish, and shell flutes that hum low tones through the water, echoing the bubble-song of distant eruptions. Tools are communal, passed like heirlooms through bioluminescent handoffs, their edges dulled not by use but by the silt's gentle embrace.

Yet the Submeri are not solitary in their haunting; they cohabit with the sparse fauna as kin rather than prey, a web of mutual yielding. Translucent fish—sleek dartlings, their scales shifting from sapphire to void-black in the algal glow—school in protective veils around clan edges, nipping at parasitic filaments that might cling to Submeri skin. These dartlings are semi-tamed, lured by fermented kelp pellets and guided in hunts for larger shadows: the eel-like vent guardians, serpentine behemoths with lures like fallen constellations, their bodies coiled around hydrothermal mounds like living roots. A guardian might be courted for its warmth, a Submeri elder wrapping tendrils of vine-harness to siphon heat for a birthing basin, in exchange offering the eel's shed scales as armor against the rare currents that whip up silt-storms. Crustacean scavengers, squat and chitinous like ambulatory barnacles, scuttle the ruins' floors, their methodical clicks a constant undercurrent to the hush. Called pebble-claws by the Submeri, these creatures are both aides and enigmas—clearing debris from habitat thresholds, yet pilfering small offerings left in alcoves, as if bartering with the earth's own memory. In lean cycles, when vents quiet and algae dims, clans might harvest the pebble-claws' molts for reinforced weaves, their armored forms echoing the builders' lost ingenuity.

Beyond the clans, the flora itself lives as a sentient underlayer, not passive but participatory in the plateau's ecology. The kelp-fronds and ballooned ferns are more than sustenance; they are the Submeri's silent councilors, their undulations whispering through water-borne vibrations that induce the hallucinatory visions—half-formed cities rising, or ancestral builders drifting as spectral schools. Some fronds bear symbiotic polyps, coral kin that pulse in response to Submeri emotions, blooming crimson in moments of communal joy or paling to ash during the slow grief of a vent's collapse. Mossy encrustations coat the spires like a living skin, filtering the dim light into edible spores that clans harvest in layered rituals, bodies swaying in the verdant canopies to collect the harvest. These plants, in turn, draw from the fauna's leavings: dartling feces aerating the substrate, pebble-claw burrows channeling nutrient eddies, and Submeri waste fostering the reefs' embryonic growth. The cycle is intimate, a closed breath where death dissolves swiftly—fallen kin interred in tufa crypts, their forms claimed by filamentous weeds that transmute flesh to silt, hastening the geology's knitting.

Weirdness infuses their existence, bending the rules of this softened gravity into daily reverie. Time dilates here; a Submeri lifespan stretches across centuries, marked not by years but by the crystallization of a single quartz vein or the maturation of a coral lattice. Visions from the vents—faint alkaloids that seep into gills like dream-tides—guide migrations between valley basins, clans drifting in buoyant chains to follow bioluminescent omens. Reproduction is a communal merging: pairs entwining in warm pools, releasing gametes into the water to be tended by the lattice of vines, offspring emerging as larval wisps that bond with dartling schools before claiming their clan's tattoos. Conflicts are rare, resolved in slow duets of finned gestures, where aggression yields to the sea's insistent warmth. Yet shadows of the old world persist: in the ruins' depths, forgotten chambers hold etched friezes of surfaced ancestors, their forms air-bound and rigid, evoking a collective unease—a myth of "the Dryfall," when waters rose to drown the builders' hubris.

In this open mid-range expanse, the Submeri and their cohabitants embody the plateau's essence: embryonic, layered, and unhurried. They linger in the dim quietude, bodies half-dissolved into the haze, tending the becoming of stone and sea. No grand empires rise here, only whispers of persistence—clans drifting through emerald mosaics, pebble-claws clicking approval, guardians coiling in patient vigil—as the world knits itself anew, one buoyant breath at a time.
