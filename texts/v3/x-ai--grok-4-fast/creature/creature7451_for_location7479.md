In the heart of this submerged caldera, where the water's chill cradles the pulse of forgotten epochs, the dwellers emerge not as conquerors of the flood but as its quiet stewards—beings woven from the same fibrous essence as the fern-walls and reed-towers they tend. They are the Kelari, a folk born of the drowned highlands, their lineage tracing back to survivors of the world's great inundation, who scaled these jagged peaks only to find the craters brimming with an unforeseen sea. Slender and elongated, like the eels that share their currents, the Kelari possess forms that blur the boundary between flesh and flora: skin the hue of pale sea-sage, mottled with faint green veins that pulse faintly in the diffused light, as if chlorophyll lingers in their blood. Their limbs end in webbed digits, broad and dexterous for pruning the kelp-dwellings with blades fashioned from sharpened abalone shells, while a fringe of gill-fronds arches from their necks, fluttering in the cool 10-degree embrace to extract breath from the mineral-laced water. Eyes wide and luminous, adapted to the refracted blues, they navigate the chamber's intimate vastness with a gaze that catches the subtle shifts of spore-lanterns drifting through hidden alcoves.

Numbering perhaps a few hundred in this clustered domain—no sprawling horde, but a sparse commune bound by the plant-structures' loose geometry—the Kelari live in rhythmic harmony with the chamber's breath. They are not builders in the crude sense of stone and fire, but cultivators, their lives a perpetual tending: dawn finds them gliding on reed-skiffs, poles of braided stem dipping into the silty floor to propel crafts through the open-water plazas, where the fronds of the central tower splay like a communal canopy. These skiffs, hollowed from buoyant bladders and lashed with sinew from eel-harvests, bob gently in the weak currents, carrying the Kelari between bulbous chambers that serve as homes, nurseries, and gathering hollows. Inside these living abodes, the walls undulate softly with the plants' growth, etched runes worn by water into patterns that the elders interpret as ancestral whispers—guides for pruning the overreaching fronds or redirecting the algae-mats that form low walls, dividing private alcoves from shared spaces where stories are sung in low, humming tones that mimic the cephalopods' calls.

Their society unfolds in unhurried cycles, a primitive weave of dependence and renewal, where individual names dissolve into communal roles: the Pruners, with their bone-hooks etched in spiraling motifs, who venture to the chamber's edges to coax the ferns into arching vaults; the Harvesters, who net the darting fist-minnows with woven nets of shed crab-chitin, their iridescent scales ground into pigments for adorning the rune-grooves; and the Echo-Weavers, who listen to the symphony of life—the clacking scavengers aerating the roots, the undulating eels' thunderous hums, the piercing shrieks of plunge-diving birds that skim the surface breaches—and translate it into rituals of song. These beings move with a deliberate grace, their webbed feet kicking lazily to maintain buoyancy, bodies insulated by layers of subdermal blubber that wards off the persistent numbness of the chill. They feed on the ecosystem's bounty: tender fern-tips nibbled fresh, crabs cracked open for their sweet innards, and the occasional cephalopod ensnared in a burst of clicks that the Kelari mimic to lure them close. Yet predation here is gentle, a negotiation—sparing the breeding pods, leaving scraps for the scavengers to cycle back into the silt.

As the Kelari inhabit this place, they deepen its poise, their presence a subtle inversion like the water's effervescent tang. Children, born in the warm hollows of sea-sage bladders, emerge with fronds already unfurling, learning to pole skiffs before they master speech, their laughter bubbling up in gurgles that join the cacophony. Elders, wizened with scales etched by decades of currents, retreat to alcoves where bioluminescent nodes from symbiotic squid-kin illuminate scrolls of pressed algae, recounting the caldera's surrender to the sea—tales of how the peaks once pierced dry skies, and how their forebears wove the first reed-tower to claim this high sanctum from the void. No kings rule here, only the equilibrium of the plants: disputes settled by the water's sculpting, where a divergent frond might be left to grow wild as judgment. In quieter moments, as the light refracts into soft greens and the spores drift like lanterns, the Kelari gather in the echoing voids, their hums blending with the fauna's din—a chorus that claims the chamber not as a cage, but as an eternal, watery kin. They are the life that endures, unhurried and alive, their forms emerging ever more intertwined with the green pulse, guardians of a world where decay blooms into renewal, and the mountain's hollow heart beats on.
