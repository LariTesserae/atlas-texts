The people of the plateau are not conquerors of their land, but echoes of it—etched by the same ancient erosion that smoothed mountains to hush, their forms as weathered and enduring as the stone they hew. They call themselves the Endur, a name whispered in the thin air like a communal exhale, though no one remembers its origin; it might have been carved into the first cistern wall or born from the groan of settling rock. Tall and lean, they move with a deliberate spareness, their bodies honed by the cold's relentless chisel: skin pale and taut, stretched over frames where muscle coils like the roots of alpine vines, efficient and unyielding. No excess adorns them—hair cropped short or bound in fern-fiber braids to ward off the damp's cling, eyes narrowed against the pale sun's indifferent gaze, lashes beaded with perpetual mist. Their lungs, broad and deep, draw the humming atmosphere in slow, measured breaths, adapted to the edge of breathability; each inhale carries the faint metallic tang of high altitude, and exhales fog the air like the plateau's own condensation. Fingers and toes, callused from gravel paths and iron work, bear the subtle marks of frost's prune—scars that flake like weathered bone, yet heal with a verdant resilience, skin knitting back in patterns reminiscent of moss blooms.

These Endur do not swarm the vastness; they inhabit it as scattered breaths, their populations threading the plateau in clans of two to three hundred, each town a fragile constellation orbiting the green islands of vegetation. Births are rare, spaced by the cycles of rain and frost, for the cold seeps into the womb like an uninvited guest, demanding tribute in vigor and number. Children emerge not wailing but quiet, their first cries muffled by the hush, eyes opening to the dome of uneasy sky. They grow amid the communal labors: from dawn's pale light, hands small and sure gather dew from frond edges into woven baskets, learning the land's rhythm before words form. No beasts share their toil—human sinew alone hauls the wheeled carts of compacted soil for tilling, or levers slabs into walls that mimic the subtle ridges. Women and men blur in role, all bodies equal under the austerity; forges glow with charcoal from burned shrubs, where hammers shape tools that must resist the softening touch of prolonged use, iron tempered with chants that invoke the rock's primordial stability.

Their days unfold in a tapestry of quiet urgency, woven from the plateau's sparsity. Mornings begin with the patter of veils of rain, clans gathering at tarn edges to channel water through log aqueducts, voices low as they murmur thanks to the seeps—rituals born not of gods but of necessity, words like "Sustain us in the fray" etched into clay hearths. Midday sees the tilling of lush pockets: iron-tipped hoes break the thin crust to plant seeds salvaged from frost-pruned vines, crops of hardy tubers and waxy greens that pulse with the same tropical vigor as the wild ferns. No markets bustle; goods circulate in shared rounds, a basket of gathered condensation passed from hall to hall, fostering bonds as enduring as the compacted soil. Afternoons yield to craft—baskets woven from creeping vines, whose fibers seem to remember their slow advance across the earth, or paths laid with gravel that shifts imperceptibly when backs are turned. Evenings draw them to low halls, fires crackling in clay pits, smoke curling like memory into the damp fog. Here, stories unfurl not as entertainment but as anchors: tales of the "Flex," the land's dreaming youth when stones walked and peaks roared, now subdued but restless. Laughter is sparse, a creak like expanding vegetation, but songs rise in harmonic hushes, voices blending with the faint groan of settling stone to reaffirm coherence.

Yet the fraying reality of the plateau imprints upon them as surely as roots fracture rock, twisting their essence into something both human and unmoored. The Endur bear subtle anomalies, emergent from the bends in time and physics, as if the land exhales its weirdness into their marrow. In the sparsest voids, where gravity lightens, they leap with an innate grace, bodies arcing like wind gusts across gravel plains, arriving at distant outposts before weariness claims them—leaps that elders teach as "the Breath's Gift," a survival born of the dome's curvature. Within lush clusters, time dilates around them; a minute of savoring the cold's bite stretches to hours in the outer world, allowing meticulous crafts that border on art—frond thatch woven with patterns that predict rain's veil, or cisterns carved to hold not just water but the echoes of inverted reflections from the tarns. Their tools, those hand-forged irons, sometimes yield to touch, softening like clay in moments of doubt, forcing remakes that infuse the metal with whispered intents, as if to remind it of its unyielding form.

Memory frays at the edges too, a continental erosion of the mind. Wanderers on looping paths return with tales of lives unlived—glimpses of parallel selves in the bent light's halos, shadows of beastless hunts or crowded cities reflected in hovering droplets. Some Endur awaken with stones shifted beside their beds, or dreams of mountainous youth where they flex like giants, only to rise with limbs aching as if from cataclysm. The clans guard against this with "Holdings"—communal vigils where eyes remain open through the night, voices reciting the plateau's settled form to pin reality in place. Illness comes not from fever but from the breakdown: a softening of the will, where one drifts into mist-whirlwinds that rewrite personal histories, emerging with eyes that see the spectral rains as kin. Healers, marked by tattoos of crystalline fractals from overnight frosts, brew salves from moss and sap, mixtures that ground the afflicted, pulling them back from abyssal depths glimpsed in tarn mirrors.

In this way, the Endur embody the plateau's paradox: lush in their defiance, cold in their solitude, quiet yet actively tethered to the hush. They are not unchanged by the emptiness that cradles abundance; their society pulses with the same biological rhythm as the vegetation—expansive in verdant moments of rain-fed unity, pruned by frost into sacred isolation. No wars scar their paths, for scarcity enforces peace, and the absence of prey or predators turns conflict inward, resolved in shared labors or leaps across the void. Elders speak of a deeper fray, a time when the dome might shatter and the ancient bones reform, but the people persist, their primitive urgency a living rebuttal. They are the plateau's final sigh made flesh—enduring, emergent, frayed yet whole, holding breath in the immense quiet as if to will the land's coherence into eternity.
