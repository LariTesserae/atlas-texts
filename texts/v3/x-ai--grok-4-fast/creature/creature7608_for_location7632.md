In the drowned highlands, where the ocean's weight etches eternity into stone and secretion, life clings and thrives not as a defiant roar but as a persistent murmur—a symphony of adaptations woven into the fabric of this submerged necropolis. The inhabitants are not conquerors of the ancient structures but their quiet tenants, evolved from the detritus of epochs past, their forms echoing the builders' lost geometries in subtle, organic ways. They are the inheritors of flux, bodies sculpted by the cold's vise and the currents' whims, abundant yet dispersed like stars in a particulate fog, each encounter a fragile negotiation in the dim indigo expanse.

At the heart of it all pulse the chemosynthetic microbes, the unseen architects of sustenance in this lightless domain. They form vast, ethereal carpets over the hydrothermal vents—those scattered, sulfurous beacons that bubble defiance against the near-freezing brine. These microbes are no verdant forests but pallid veils, translucent films of bacterial mats shimmering with faint, iridescent sheens from mineral precipitates. Clustered in symbiotic guilds, they oxidize the vent's hydrogen sulfide and methane, exhaling energy in chemical whispers that sustain the web above them. Some strains form spiky protuberances, like frozen flames of silica, trapping heat and gases in porous lattices that mimic the extinct builders' own calcified hives. Others drift as motile clusters, propelled by flagella that twitch in the syrupy water, colonizing fresh fissures exposed by turbulent scours. They are the foundation, unkillable in their simplicity, their slow metabolism a counterpoint to the chaos, ensuring that even in the sparsest plateaus, a faint chemical glow hints at life's unyielding foothold.

From these microbial nurseries emerge the smallest herbivores and detritivores, the unseen grazers that keep the structures from total entombment. Scavenger amphipods, no larger than a grain of eroded schist, skitter across the fractal-etched surfaces in ceaseless patrols. Their exoskeletons, forged from chitin infused with quartz microcrystals, blend seamlessly with the basalt veins, rendering them ghosts against the stone. They nibble at the microbial films and siphon flocculent sediments from the water column, their compound eyes—tiny prisms of adaptive genius—piercing the haze to detect the subtle pulses of vent warmth. In swarms that coalesce only during current lulls, they burrow into the porous travertine, excavating labyrinthine tunnels that honeycomb the walls like the builders' own forgotten instincts. Predation shapes them too: larger kin, the isopod-like crustaceans, lumber with heavier armor, their legs ending in hooked barbs that pry open agate domes for sheltered lairs. These mid-sized scavengers—bodies the length of a human finger, segmented and barnacle-crusted—emit faint clicks as they navigate the eroded arches, their gills filtering the saline sting while they dismantle fallen polyps or the shed scales of passing fish. They are the cleaners of the necropolis, preventing the slow suffocation of silt, their abundance a sparse carpet that thins into vast, echoing voids between the plateaus.

Higher in the food web, the water teems with the midwater wanderers, forms that exploit the buoyancy of this realm to glide through the open basin. Schools of translucent lanternfish dominate these mid-depths, their bodies elongated into silvery needles that catch and refract the miserly light from above. Each fish is a living prism, scales embedded with photophores that flicker in hypnotic patterns—codes of alarm or courtship that ripple through the group like bioluminescent Morse. They migrate in loose phalanxes, spiraling through the labyrinthine walls to forage on microbial blooms and krill-like copepods ejected from the vents. In the turbulence of a sudden gyre, the school fractures into panicked helices, bodies compressing to slip through fissures mere centimeters wide, only to reform on the leeward side of a spire, their collective glow a brief lantern against the shadow. Nestled among them are the gelatinous drifters: polyps and medusae descended from ancient cnidarians, their bells and tendrils unfurling like veils of frayed silk. These creatures, ethereal and near-invisible, pulse lazily in the currents, nematocysts armed with vent-derived toxins that paralyze prey on contact. A mature medusa might span a meter across, its oral arms trailing in lazy curls to ensnare amphipods or fish stragglers, digesting them in a haze of dissolved enzymes. They cluster in the stagnant brine pockets of cracked domes, reproducing asexually in budding frenzies during calm periods, their polyps anchoring to the quartz lattices like living barnacles, extending into the flow to filter the particulate soup.

The predators, those serpentine sentinels of the fringes, add a layer of coiled menace to the hush. Deep-sea eels, kin to the morays but adapted to this highland flux, coil through the basal fissures like animated shadows. Their bodies, slick with mucus that repels the mineral-laden water, stretch to three meters, jaws unhinging to reveal rows of teeth serrated like the coral spires themselves—evolved, perhaps, from gnawing on the builders' remnants. They ambush from ambush points: crevices widened by erosion, where they undulate in wait, luring prey with bioluminescent lures dangling from their snouts, mimics of vent bubbles or fish scales. A strike is a blur in the dim, the eel's jet propulsion—a muscular contraction that expels water in explosive bursts—propelling it through an archway to seize a cephalopod mid-migration. These eels are solitary philosophers of the deep, their lairs lined with the bones of past meals, exhaling ammonia clouds that sting and ward off rivals. Yet even they bow to the larger rhythms; during the turbulent surges, they retreat into the stone's embrace, emerging only when the currents ebb, their eyes—vast, lidless orbs—scanning the haze for the telltale iridescence of a school.

At the apex roam the cephalopods, the nomadic herds that lend the basin its migratory pulse. These squid-like beings, evolved from ancient nautiloids, form loose pods that traverse the regional expanse, their mantles pulsing with chromatophores that shift from indigo camouflage to flashing warnings of inner light. Bodies the size of a seafarer’s arm, they propel through the water with siphons that intake and expel in rhythmic throbs, navigating the turbulent cauldron with ink clouds deployed as decoys or sensory veils. Tentacles, lined with suckers that grip like living hooks, snatch eels from crevices or herd fish into kill zones. Intelligent in their alien way, they communicate through color waves and jet pulses, forming temporary alliances to exploit vent blooms—diving to snatch microbial-fed crustaceans before ascending to lay eggs in the agate hollows, where the translucent orbs adhere like jewels to the dome's interior. A pod might number in the dozens, weaving through the undulating shelves in a ballet of light and shadow, only to scatter in the face of a whirlpool, reforming leagues away as the currents reshape the plateau beneath them.

Interwoven among these are the oddities, the fringe dwellers that blur the line between fauna and the built world. Symbiotic anemones, rooted in the microbial mats, extend tendrils that mimic the structures' fractal etchings, luring fish into venomous embraces while sharing the energy of chemosynthesis. Tube worms, kin to the deep-sea rift dwellers but here emergent from the extinct reefs, erect pale chimneys of secreted chitin, their feathery crowns filtering the flux for nutrients, plumes waving like forgotten banners in the current's gale. And in the rare, sheltered eddies, bioluminescent jellyfish blooms create ephemeral oases—drifting orbs that attract swarms of smaller life, only for the next downdraft to scatter them into the gloom.

This menagerie endures through symbiosis and cunning, their lives a dance with the environment's mutability. Predation culls the weak, but abundance ensures resilience; a scoured spire might bury a nest, only for the next surge to unearth it, hatches synchronized to the vents' irregular throb. No single form dominates; instead, they form a sparse mosaic, encounters fleeting—a flash of scales through an arch, the distant thrum of a cephalopod herd, the patient scuttle of a crustacean on stone. In the cold silence, broken by the hiss of water and the snap of claws, they embody the builders' echo: life as patient construction, remade in flesh and fin, persisting in the eternal churn of the drowned highlands. As the currents shift once more, carrying whispers of distant rifts, the inhabitants adapt, their forms sharpening in the haze, ever becoming more themselves in the water's unyielding embrace.
