The city of Echolost clings to the knife-edge of the world, a vast plateau suspended at the frayed hem of the sky, where elevation has carved reality into something sharper, thinner, more insistent. It is not a mountain's crown but a table of stone thrust upward by some ancient, unyielding force, its surface eroded not to ruin but to a profound equilibrium—a weathered patina of basalt and obsidian, polished by eons of wind and vapor into forms that seem both deliberate and indifferent. The air here is hot, oppressively so, a humid furnace that presses against the skin like a living thing, thick with the scent of damp earth and verdant decay. Water is everywhere, not in rivers or seas, but in the constant seep and condense: rivulets weep from jagged fissures in the rock, pooling in shallow basins that never quite evaporate, their surfaces trembling with the heat's subtle boil. Mist rises eternally from these, coiling upward to mingle with the low-hanging clouds that scrape the plateau's rim, as if the atmosphere itself is sweating under the relentless sun.

This is a place of lush overgrowth, where vegetation flourishes in defiant abundance despite—or perhaps because of—the altitude's hostility. Ferns unfurl in emerald riot across every ledge, their fronds heavy with dew that drips in rhythmic patters, feeding roots that snake through cracks in the stone like veins of green fire. Vines thicker than a person's arm twist around what were once walls, blooming with flowers that pulse faintly in the normal light of day—petals the color of bruised twilight, exuding a sticky nectar that draws no insects, for there are no animals here, no skitter of life beyond the plants' silent proliferation. The greenery is dense, layered in a saturation that borders on the claustrophobic: canopies overlap in a verdant lattice, roots and shoots intertwining until the boundary between soil and flora blurs, creating pockets of air so thick with leaves that visibility fractures into green-tinted corridors. Yet the scale opens outward, unconfined—a regional expanse where the city sprawls across the plateau like a fossil of human ambition, its districts and avenues stretching to the hazy horizon, multi-featured in their repetition: ziggurats half-swallowed by moss, aqueducts choked with lily pads, plazas paved in tiles now cradles for fungal blooms. It is town-sized in its intimacy, valley-wide in its reach, an urban skeleton reclaimed not by time's decay but by a balanced stasis, where erosion has settled into harmony, every weathered curve and pitted surface locked in eternal form.

But Echolost is no mere ruin; it pulses with the urban ghost of its making—organized in the geometry of forgotten builders, streets laid in grids that defy the plateau's slant, arches and domes rising in layered construction from the stone, their materials fused as if grown rather than hewn. There is no technology here, no gleam of metal or hum of machines; it is pure, biological antiquity, the city's infrastructure woven from the land itself—walls of compacted earth and vine-bound rubble, bridges of woven root and petrified wood, all pre-tool in their primal craft, as if humanity (or whatever came before) shaped it with hands and will alone. The heat amplifies this organic quality, turning the air into a steam bath that clings to everything, making the lushness feel alive, respiring, though no breath stirs the leaves.

And then there is the sound—the deafening roar that defines Echolost, a constant, unchanging thunder that fills every crevice of perception without variation, without crescendo or fade. It is the voice of the plateau itself: a resonant hum born from the water's endless percolation through stone, amplified by the rock's hollow chambers and the dense canopy's echo-traps, vibrating up from the earth's core in waves that make the ground quiver subtly, perpetually. It is not wind or waterfall, but something deeper—a geological hymn, hot and wet, where vapor condenses into audible pulses, reality breaking down at the edges. For here, weirdness frays the seams of the world: gravity bends in the plazas, causing droplets to arc upward before falling, pooling in mid-air for heartbeats before collapsing; shadows cast by the ferns twist into impossible knots, lingering long after the light source has shifted (though the light itself is normal, a steady golden haze from a sun that hangs motionless overhead); and the vegetation whispers geometries, leaves aligning in fractal patterns that suggest hidden architectures, as if the city and the wild are conspiring in a silent, biological code. Time holds still in this equilibrium—nothing changes, no storms brew, no growth advances beyond its lush saturation, no erosion gnaws further. The hot air remains saturated, the mist eternal, the roar unyielding. To stand here is to be enveloped in a frozen symphony of density and openness, where the urban bones of the past entwine with the green pulse of now, and reality, stretched thin at this extreme height, threatens to unravel into the void beyond the plateau's edge.
