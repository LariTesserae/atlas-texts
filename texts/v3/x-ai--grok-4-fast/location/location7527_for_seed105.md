The world here is a drowned highland, a vast plateau of jagged stone that rises like the spine of some ancient, forgotten beast from the abyssal depths below. But rise is relative in this realm of endless immersion; what was once mountaintop air now lies submerged beneath a ceaseless, all-encompassing sea of ink-black water, cold as the void between stars. This is no shallow flood—it's a total deluge, where the liquid presses in from every direction, filling lungs and carving canyons anew, a post-cataclysmic ocean that has claimed the highlands without mercy or retreat. Elevation persists in defiance: these are the lofty remnants of a mountain chain, their peaks and ridges protruding into the murk like half-submerged teeth, reaching toward a surface that's miles overhead, invisible and irrelevant. The water is the atmosphere, dense and unyielding, carrying a chill that seeps into bones and slows the pulse, hovering just above freezing—cold enough to numb exposed stone, yet not so frigid as to birth ice in this perpetual motion.

The terrain unfolds across a regional expanse, open and indifferent to confinement, stretching for leagues in every direction: eroded spires of granite and basalt, weathered smooth by eons of underwater currents that have scoured away the sharp edges of youth. Erosion here is established, a quiet equilibrium where the stone has settled into its form, balanced between decay and endurance—fissures worn deep but not collapsing, slopes gentled into undulating hills that vanish into the hazy blue-black distance. Yet the scale feels intimate in its sparseness; features emerge scattered across the void, with vast expanses of empty water between them, low-saturation expanses where the eye strains against the gloom. A lone pinnacle might thrust upward from the plateau's edge, its base cloaked in swaying fronds, while kilometers away, a sister ridge mirrors it faintly, connected only by the indifferent flow of the sea. Density is a whisper—much space between presences, the landscape breathing emptiness as much as form.

Lushness thrives in this desolation, a verdant defiance against the dark and cold. Vegetation dominates without competition, for no fauna stirs these waters—no fish darting through the currents, no unseen predators gliding in the shadows, only the silent empire of the green. Towering kelp forests rise from the sedimented floors, their blades unfurling like banners in the relentless drift, thick and tangled in places where nutrient upwellings from hidden vents feed their growth. Algae blooms drape the rocks in iridescent veils, pulsing faintly with stored light, while coral-like polyps—strange, vegetable kin—encrust the higher ledges, their forms blooming in asymmetrical spirals that defy earthly logic. These plants are not mere survivors; they are architects of the weird, their tendrils weaving unnatural patterns—loops that echo long-lost runes, fronds that curl into impossible geometries, as if the flora remembers a world before the flood and seeks to remake it in submerged silence. In the cold, their colors mute to deep emeralds and bruised indigos, but their biomass is profuse, a lush carpet over the sparse stone, swaying in hypnotic unison with the water's breath.

Light is a rumor here, dim and treacherous, filtering down from an unseen sun in fractured shafts that barely pierce the upper reaches before dissolving into twilight. Darkness reigns, thick and brooding, broken only sporadically by the pale glow of phosphorescent weeds that dot the vegetation like scattered stars—cold blue lumens that flicker erratically, casting elongated shadows across the eroded stone. The built remnants of some primitive kin add to the strangeness: scattered artifacts of a hand-crafted age, half-buried in silt or overgrown by the relentless green. Stone huts, their walls hewn with crude chisels from the local rock, huddle in shallow depressions, roofs caved in by the weight of water and time—simple domes or lean-tos, marked by the faint etchings of early tools, perhaps axes of bone or flint lashed with vine. Occasional monoliths stand sentinel, weathered obelisks carved with spiraling motifs that mirror the plants' weird curls, as if the builders communed with the flora in their craft. No metals gleam here; tech is raw, manual—remnants of woven nets tangled in the kelp, clay vessels shattered on the slopes, all sparse and partial, like echoes of a civilization that dove into the depths willingly, or was pulled under.

The place hums with activity, dynamic and unceasing, a symphony of motion without life to claim it. Currents surge through the open valleys, loud and insistent—roaring torrents that whip the vegetation into frenzied dances, their thunder echoing off the stone walls like the bellow of submerged gales. Bubbles rise in erratic columns from geothermal fissures hidden in the plateau's underbelly, popping with sharp cracks that reverberate through the water, while the plants themselves contribute to the clamor: hollow stems whistling as water rushes through them, leaves rasping against rock in the flow. It's a loud world, the sounds amplified by the liquid medium, a constant barrage that drowns out thought—waves of pressure that pulse and recede, biological rhythms of growth and sway mingling with the sea's own cycles of tide and thermal drift. No predators hunt, no prey flees, yet the ecosystem pulses in eerie isolation: roots probing the sediment for minerals, spores drifting on the currents to seed distant outcrops, the vegetation expanding and contracting in slow, tidal waves of its own making.

Rules bind this realm strangely, as if the flood rewrote reality's code. Gravity pulls downward through the water, but buoyancy lifts all things equally, making stone feel lighter, plants more ethereal—objects drift lazily unless anchored, and the cold slows time itself, extending the decay of the built to geological spans. Touch the water, and it clings like a second skin, breathable yet oppressive, demanding adaptation or surrender. The weirdness creeps in at the edges: plants that seem to lean toward the faint light shafts as if worshiping ghosts, stones etched with patterns that shift subtly when unobserved, currents that loop in defiance of depth, circling the high ridges like wary spirits. This is a place in balance, yet alive with unrest—cold, dark, loud, and lush in its solitude, a submerged highland where the sea has become the land, and the land remembers its drowning. As you descend deeper into the murk, the plateau's forms blur into the haze, inviting endless exploration across its open sprawl, where emptiness and emergence entwine without end.
