The descent into this place feels eternal, a slow uncoiling through narrow fissures that widen gradually into the profound hush of the depths—though hush is a lie, for the air thrums with an unrelenting clamor that presses against your ears like a living weight. You emerge not into a single chamber but a sprawling subterranean expanse, a regional labyrinth of vaulted halls and sunken basins carved from the bones of the world itself. This is no fresh wound in the earth; erosion has worked here for epochs beyond counting, smoothing jagged primordial rock into undulating waves of stone, polished to a near-flawful sheen by the patient grind of dust and time. What was once perhaps a turbulent underworld has settled into its final, unyielding form: vast plateaus of layered limestone and schist, their edges feathered into subtle ridges, as if the planet exhaled its last fury eons ago and now merely exhales stillness. The scale unfolds regionally, a town-sized valley of interconnected voids—multi-featured with domed ceilings soaring fifty meters high, flanked by terraced ledges and meandering chasms that stretch beyond immediate sight, inviting the eye to wander without promise of an end. Yet for all its openness, the density is sparse, a deliberate balance of presence and void: emptiness dominates, with features scattered like forgotten relics, leaving great expanses of bare, cool stone to breathe in isolation.

The air hangs cool and dry, a temperate chill that seeps from the walls like the breath of ancient sleepers—neither biting nor forgiving, just a steady ten degrees Celsius that clings to your skin without moisture to soften it. No water stirs here; this is a desiccated realm, where the chemistry of life sidesteps the aqueous entirely. The stone is parched, etched not by rivers but by the slow abrasion of windless drafts carrying fine mineral silt, and the ground yields no dampness underfoot—only the faint, talc-like grit of evaporated salts long since crystallized into the matrix. Yet life persists, lush and improbable, in defiance of the aridity. Vegetation erupts in verdant defiance, not as a uniform carpet but in sparse, clustered opulence: thickets of fungal forests, their caps unfurling like leathery parasols in shades of deep emerald and bruised violet, anchored by mycelial webs that sprawl across the stone like veins of forgotten empires. These growths thrive on the non-aqueous bounty of the depths—symbiotic exchanges of spores and minerals, drawing sustenance from the very decay of the rock itself. They are ancient too, their forms echoing the erosion around them: massive, settled brackets and shelves that have ballooned over millennia into primordial shapes, their edges frayed and layered like the strata beneath. Lichens drape in cascading veils from overhangs, and tuberous roots bulge from fissures, pulsing faintly with the slow circulation of sap-like resins. The lushness is in their variety and vigor—dozens of species interweaving in each patch, from feathery sporangia to bulbous puffballs that release their payloads in languid drifts—but they are scattered, these green explosions amid the gray, leaving vast interstitial plains of naked stone to mediate between them.

This is no untouched wilderness, though; the hand—or claw—of construction lingers in the architecture of the place, woven so seamlessly into the natural erosion that it feels like an extension of the stone's own dreaming. Urban in scope and intent, these are the remnants of a biological metropolis, built not with tools or machines but through the tireless labor of long-extinct fauna, their instincts shaping the rock into organized sprawl. Tunnels branch with infrastructural precision, forming layered avenues and boulevards that crisscross the valley—domed haberdasheries of compacted earth and chitin, terraced warrens stacked like apartment blocks, and grand plazas hollowed into the stone where once-processions may have milled. The scale suggests a town or small city, dense in its infrastructural logic yet sparse in occupation now: walls curved with the polish of ancient passage, buttressed by natural arches that have held for ages, all eroded into a harmonious ruin. No metal gleams, no mechanisms whir; this is pure biology's triumph, the builders' legacy reduced to fossilized burrows and silk-lined crevices, now claimed by the fungi that thread through the mortar-like bindings. The built environment layers upon the terrain like sedimentary history—basement levels sunk deeper into the cool substrate, mid-tiers opening to the dim vaults above—creating a vertical city that mirrors the slow settling of the world itself.

Light filters in dimly, a subdued twilight born not of sun but of the faint phosphorescence woven into the ecology. The fungi contribute a soft, ethereal glow, their caps emitting a pale verdant haze that barely pushes back the shadows, while scattered quartz veins in the stone catch and refract it into ghostly motes. Vision strains here, details emerging only after moments of adjustment: the subtle iridescence of a spore cloud, the faint outline of a ledge. It is enough to navigate the openness without vertigo, but the dimness amplifies the sounds, turning the sparse expanse into an acoustic coliseum.

And the sounds—gods, the sounds. They are deafening, a ceaseless symphony that drowns thought, born from the abundant fauna that teems through this underworld. Creatures proliferate in hordes, their numbers lush and insistent despite the sparsity of their habitats: swarms of leathery-winged insects the size of fists, chittering in ultrasonic waves that resolve into a relentless buzz; colonies of burrowing millipedes, their chitinous legs scraping in thunderous rhythms across the stone; packs of nocturnal rodents with velvety fur, their calls echoing as shrill whistles that cascade through the halls like avalanches of noise. Bats—millions of them, perhaps—flit in slow, deliberate spirals from the ceilings, their wingbeats a flapping roar that builds and fades in waves, while unseen arthropods strum their carapaces in resonant hums. The fauna is everywhere yet nowhere dense: scattered flocks roosting in sparse clusters, herds of scuttling invertebrates traversing the open plains in migratory lines, all contributing to the din. It is not chaotic frenzy but a layered cacophony, amplified by the vaulted acoustics—each chirp, scrape, and flap rebounding off the ancient walls, merging into a wall of sound that vibrates your bones. Predators lurk in the echoes, their low growls underscoring the chorus, while the fungi themselves seem to pulse with faint, subsonic thrums from expanding hyphae.

The rules of this world are those of patient endurance: dynamic forces move at a glacial pace, changes unfolding across seasons that span years, gradients so subtle they evade haste. Spores drift in lazy currents, taking hours to cross a chamber; fungal caps unfurl incrementally, revealing new surfaces over months; the fauna's migrations trace arcs that complete only in the span of human lifetimes. Erosion has won, flattening aggressions into harmony—no floods to scour, no quakes to shatter, just the slow seep of time binding built and wild into one. Conflicts resolve in drawn-out balances: a swarm overruns a thicket, only for the mycelium to reclaim it in symbiotic creep. The atmosphere is one of quiet profundity amid the roar—cool drafts whispering through the infrastructure's veins, carrying the dry scent of stone and spore, inviting you to linger in the sparse vastness, to let the deafening normalcy seep into your pulse. Here, in the depths' ancient city, life has settled into its oldest stable state: abundant, layered, and unhurriedly alive.
