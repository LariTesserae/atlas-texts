In the depths of a sprawling lowland, a vast wetland unfolds beneath an eternally starless sky. The air hangs heavy with moisture, warm and thick, clinging to every surface like an invisible shroud. The landscape is a patchwork of shallow pools and soggy earth, stretching as far as the eye can see – which isn't far in the perpetual twilight that blankets the region.

Lush vegetation thrives in this humid environment, creating a dense tapestry of life. Tall reeds and grasses emerge from the water, their stems intertwining to form natural bridges and islands. Broad-leaved plants with waxy surfaces collect beads of moisture, occasionally releasing them with soft, barely audible plops. Moss carpets every available surface, its velvety texture muffling footsteps and adding to the sense of hushed isolation.

The terrain is young, as if recently birthed from some geological upheaval. Sharp-edged rocks jut out from the marshy ground, their surfaces still rough and unweathered. In places, the earth seems to breathe, gently rising and falling as if the land itself is alive and slumbering.

Despite the vastness of the wetland, there's a feeling of intimacy and closeness. The vegetation grows so densely that it creates natural corridors and chambers, each one a world unto itself. The air is thick with the earthy scent of decomposing plant matter and the mineral tang of standing water.

Scattered throughout this primordial landscape are signs of primitive habitation. Crude wooden platforms rise just above the water level, connected by narrow bridges of woven vines. Simple lean-tos of woven reeds provide shelter, blending almost seamlessly into the surrounding vegetation. Here and there, stone tools lie half-submerged in the muck – evidence of early human ingenuity.

The fauna is sparse but not absent. Occasionally, the surface of a pool ripples as some unseen creature moves beneath. In the dim light, the silhouette of a long-necked bird might be glimpsed, standing motionless among the reeds. But these sightings are rare, adding to the landscape's mystique.

Perhaps most striking is the profound silence that envelops everything. The typical sounds of a wetland – croaking frogs, buzzing insects, calling birds – are absent. Instead, there's only the faintest whisper of vegetation brushing against itself in an imperceptible breeze, and the occasional soft patter of water droplets falling from leaf to leaf.

This landscape seems frozen in time, unchanging. The water neither rises nor falls; the vegetation neither grows nor withers. It exists in a state of perfect, eerie equilibrium, as if suspended between heartbeats in the rhythm of the world.
