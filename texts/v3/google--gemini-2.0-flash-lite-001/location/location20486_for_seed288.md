The world is not a world, but a honeycomb. A vast, echoing matrix of hexagonal tunnels stretches away in every direction, lit by a perpetual, dim, bioluminescent twilight. The tunnels are vast, easily spanning several kilometers from wall to wall, though the spaces between the walls are also immense, reaching thousands of kilometers in height. The air is cool, heavy, and utterly dry. Any humidity is instantly absorbed into the walls, a process which creates a subtle, but persistent, high-pitched whine that fills every chamber.

The walls themselves are the product of unimaginable ages of construction. The original builders – it's impossible to say who, or even what, they were – used some sort of living material which has since long since petrified. It feels like rough, aged sandstone to the touch, but the stone is threaded with veins of deep, verdant green, glowing with the internal light. These are not waterways; they are something deeper, a vital system that slowly permeates the entire structure, and which, despite the parched environment, gives rise to the abundant, lush vegetation which carpets the floor of the tunnels.

The plants are alien. They are not like anything found in a surface world. Giant, gnarled trees with phosphorescent leaves tower from the floor, their branches intertwining to form a canopy high above our heads. Beneath the trees, strange, low-growing fungi sprout in a riot of colors and textures, their caps emitting their own soft glow. Vines like living ropes hang from above, reaching towards the ground, each one as thick as a grown woman's waist. The ground, a deep bed of decaying plant matter, is soft underfoot, a primeval compost perpetually renewed.

The life which teems in this vast ecosystem is the most staggering feature. It is a deafening, almost unbearable cacophony. The air buzzes with the chirps, clicks, and howls of countless creatures. Giant, six-legged insects with iridescent carapaces scuttle along the walls and across the floor. Long, sinuous worms with armored segments burrow through the ground. Reptilian forms with too many eyes and feathery appendages flit through the canopy. Massive, winged predators, shimmering with bioluminescence, circle high above, their calls echoing through the tunnels. The density is not high, but the individual noises are amplified by the size of the tunnels and the way they channel sound, and the general effect is overwhelming.

There is no sign of tech. No technology here, nothing manufactured or altered by anything other than the passage of time. The architecture, the living stone and the fungal forests, speak of processes operating over unimaginable spans. The world *is* the structure, a vast, self-contained ecosystem. The only rule is entropy. Everything is slowly, inexorably, settling into greater and greater stability. The vastness of the space is the source of the slowness; every cycle takes centuries.

The weirdness is minimal. Despite the alien nature of the flora and fauna, the rules seem simple and natural. Life and death, growth and decay, the slow ballet of predators and prey. The complete lack of water is a critical piece of the puzzle, and a major key to understanding the environment's contradictions. The 'water' seems to be locked into the material fabric of this world, absorbed into the walls, and the vegetation which thrives there. The creatures are adapted to this impossible dryness: many have exoskeletons or tough, chitinous armor, others metabolize and store liquid within themselves, while still others appear to exist without it.

The built environment is the tunnels. The urban scale is this honeycomb, this unending network. The tunnels are not the product of a single, coherent design. There are blind ends, intersections at seemingly random angles, natural curves that interrupt the rigid geometry. The builders, or whatever they were, didn't make a city, the living honeycomb *is* the city.
