The city-carcass of Aethel hums with a quiet, persistent cold. It’s a city built on a needle of rock that pierces the sky, a spire that leans perpetually into a hard, gray sky. Around the base, the ground thrums with a barely-perceptible resonance as if the stone breathes.

The very air feels old, heavy with the weight of centuries. It is not exactly silent, but the sounds are subtle, layered: the distant, echoing drip of water, the whisper of wind, the slow, rhythmic *thump* that seems to emanate from the city itself. These sounds are not random, but a carefully choreographed symphony of stability.

Aethel is constructed from a dark, smooth stone – something volcanic, polished by wind and age. The city’s architecture is simple but purposeful: long, low structures clinging to the rock face, connected by narrow, winding stairs and walkways. There are no soaring towers, no grand plazas. Everything feels compressed, as if the buildings themselves are huddling against the cold and height.

Water, when it comes, arrives as condensation; thick, clinging fogs that shroud the upper reaches, or sudden, brief showers that leave the stone slick and black. Tiny rivulets of water trickle down the rock, finding their way into carefully placed channels, feeding small, moss-covered basins.

Vegetation is a rarity. Along the edges of the city, there are a few stubborn patches of dark, leathery moss and lichen clinging to the stone, and some hardy, wind-bent shrubs that have found purchase in the meager soil of the higher elevations. Inside the walls, in sheltered alcoves, small gardens are cultivated.

The light is what I would call "normal", though "normality" feels altered here. The sky is always overcast, giving the city a muted, gray cast. The stone absorbs most of the light, creating long shadows and emphasizing the city’s angularity. 

The city's inhabitants, if any, are unseen. No smoke curls from chimneys, no lights flicker in the windows.  It's as though they, too, are frozen in time, participating in an equilibrium that needs no motion. Fauna, too, is almost absent. Perhaps some insects manage to survive, tucked away in the cracks of the stone, or some small, rock-dwelling birds circle the summit. The whole place seems to deny the presence of anything alive.

The only sign of the living is the art - simple depictions of rituals, patterns that cover everything; the pathways, the walls, and the faces of the buildings. These carvings are not crude, but precise, as if designed by a being of stone itself. They are not warnings or threats but celebrations of the same unchanging reality.
