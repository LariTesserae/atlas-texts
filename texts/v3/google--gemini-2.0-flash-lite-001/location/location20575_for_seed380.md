The air hangs thick and cool in the Cloisters of Rust. It tastes of something metallic, a faint tang like old coins or dried blood. The Cloisters are not open to the sky, but lie deep, tunneled into the earth, a warren of stone and metal that echoes with the drip and sigh of water. This is not a place of great beauty, not in the usual sense. Here, beauty is the stubborn persistence of form, the way the relentless moisture has etched intricate filigree into the iron girders that support the cavernous ceilings.

The Cloisters are small in scale, a collection of connected chambers and passageways carved from dark, sedimentary rock. Buildings of a kind, these spaces are connected by archways and ramps of dull, pitted metal, crisscrossed with pipes and conduits that carry an unseen flow. In the heart of the deepest chamber, a vast cistern collects water that condenses perpetually from the dim, humid air. It's a vast pool and seems to be nearly brimmed with its slow gathering.

This is a place of industry, though the industries are long gone, or repurposed. Remnants of long-dead machines, complex and rusted ironworks are everywhere. The air is still, but the scent of grease and oil remains, a phantom of the work that once took place here. These are the husks of factories, refineries, and maybe something more esoteric, too. Whatever they produced is gone, or perhaps it lives on only as a residual influence, a psychic weight cast over the whole place.

The light is dim, filtered by the thick, moisture-laden atmosphere, and further obscured by the structures above. Most of the Cloisters are lit only by the faint bioluminescence of the many fungal growths that cling to the walls. In the dimness, strange shapes emerge.

Fauna is abundant, but mostly hidden. The dampness feeds a rich ecosystem of blind fish and luminescent insects that scuttle through the ruins. Something larger, perhaps the size of rats, moves in the shadows, their presence felt rather than seen. Bird-like forms flit about with a slow, deliberate quality – perhaps the result of genetic modification. These creatures seem comfortable here, adapted to the dark, the damp, and the faint metallic tang of the air.

The rules of this world are subtle and strange. The water, for instance, is not simply water. It seems to have a memory, a subtle sentience. Those who stay here long enough can begin to sense the currents of its unseen flows, to feel the echoes of the actions that took place in these chambers long ago. The erosion is a slow sculptor, its work visible in the intricate patterns carved into the metal and stone.

The dynamic is slow, but not unchanging. In time, the rust spreads, the stone weathers, the water rises. The Cloisters are in a state of delicate balance, a decaying monument to a forgotten purpose. It is a home, in its own way; it lives by the slow pulse of water, a place where the edges of reality blur and the echoes of the past whisper in the quiet, metallic air.
