The air hangs heavy, a palpable dampness clinging to the skin, even now, as the sun, a pale disc behind the perpetual haze, begins its slow descent. This is Aethel, a city carved not from stone or brick, but from something akin to polished obsidian. The material shimmers, catching the light in a way that suggests not reflection, but an internal luminescence, as if the very substance is lit from within.

The city sprawls across a plateau, far higher than it first appears, its foundations lost in a dense tangle of emerald vegetation. This greenery, a vibrant cloak of plants, is surprisingly lush, though its distribution is far from uniform. Some areas are choked with thick, tropical growth, where enormous, fan-shaped leaves cast deep shadows, while others are open spaces of perfectly manicured lawns, the obsidian pathways bisecting them like razor-sharp lines. At the edge of the plateau, the city’s perimeter dissolves into a vast, undulating landscape. The terrain here dips and rises, carved by the slow, relentless hand of water and time. There are deep gorges slicing through the obsidian, the sides of these chasms weeping with a constant, oily sheen.

Water is everywhere in Aethel. It seeps from the rocks, bubbles from the ground in carefully designed fountains, and collects in intricate networks of canals that flow through the city like veins. It has the consistency of fine oil, cool against the touch, and imbued with the same internal light that defines the obsidian. The air itself seems to be condensing, the moisture clinging to every surface, painting the landscape in a sheen of perpetual rain.

The scale of Aethel is immense. It is a city that seems to stretch on forever, a testament to an unseen hand that has shaped and crafted this place with meticulous care. Structures rise in staggered tiers, interwoven with the vegetation, and suggesting an organic, almost symbiotic relationship between the built environment and the natural world.

Despite the obvious signs of construction, the technology here is surprisingly crude. There are no machines, no complex contraptions. The obsidian is shaped and smoothed with tools that look like they're carved from the same material, wielded by figures that emerge rarely, and only at dawn and dusk. They move with deliberation and purpose, but who they are or what they are doing is a mystery. They maintain the city, tend to the waterways, and perform other inscrutable tasks in unbroken silence.

The sense of emptiness is paramount. The city may be vast, but its density is low. There is a profound silence blanketing Aethel. No birds sing, no insects buzz. The only sounds are the gentle trickle of water, the occasional sigh of the wind through the massive leaves, and the rhythmic, rhythmic *thump* of the earth as it absorbs the rains. They are all the city's sounds, though, so they do little to break the feeling of perfect, frozen stability.

There is no sign of animal life, and certainly no hint of animals contributing to any of the city's stability. All that exists is the city.

This isn’t a world in motion – it's a world held in perfect stasis. This landscape doesn't seem to respond to time or change. The perfect balance between the elements suggests a world in a constant state of equilibrium, an unnerving sense that nothing ever evolves, nothing ever decays. It remains, fixed and unchanging, waiting for something, or someone, to break its silence.
