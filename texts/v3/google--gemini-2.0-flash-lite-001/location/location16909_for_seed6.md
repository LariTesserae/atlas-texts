The Heart of Cogswell is a city built within a colossal, perfectly spherical geode, suspended in a warm, perpetually humid atmosphere. The walls of the geode itself are a translucent, subtly shimmering substance, like stretched and impossibly thin glass, letting in a normal, diffused daylight. Outside, the sphere is perpetually wreathed in a swirling, opaque fog, a haze of airborne vapor that looks like the result of continuous industrial emissions. Within, however, the air is clean, although carrying a faint, metallic tang.

The city is formed from colossal crystal structures that have grown together over time, their surfaces and edges precisely cut and polished. These are not organic crystals, but a material grown in vast artificial wombs. The structures are a kaleidoscope of colours — deep emerald, ruby red, and sapphire blue. They support a complex network of platforms, walkways, and towering, seemingly endless industrial machinery. Every surface, every support, every intricate gear feels like part of a single, unified organism. The built environment is all-encompassing, a testament to the total control of a vanished civilization.

The entire sphere is submerged in water. This is not some natural ocean, but a body created by endless streams of refined water, generated by factories integrated into the crystal structures. The water is clear, and the depth seems bottomless, though it remains still and calm. The water moves in slow, patient cycles, ebbing and flowing in the deepest recesses of the sphere.

The scale is cathedral-like, the spaces dominated by the immense structures. The density is extreme, every space occupied by some creation. There is no hint of natural substrate, no bare earth or rock. Everything is artificial, crafted, and meticulously maintained.

The tech is industrial, and clearly powerful. Huge, humming machinery stretches towards the sky. The function of many of these tools is unknown, but their size alone gives them an almost alien power. Gleaming pipes snake through the structures, carrying fluids of unknown composition. Their only purpose could be to feed the endless growth of the crystals, or fuel other processes we cannot understand.

There is absolute quiet. The only sound is the subtle hum of the machines and the gentle lapping of water against the crystalline structures. The absence of fauna is complete — there are no birds, insects, or even the smallest of microscopic organisms. It is a world sealed, self-contained, and devoid of the chaos of natural presence.

The landscape is still in a state of formation. New crystals are constantly being born. Deep within the core of the city, there are areas of raw, unformed material, where the crystals are still in their infancy. The erosion is minimal, but there are signs of slow decay. The structures show no wear, but the edges of some crystal faces show a slow, subtle change, a shift in hue, a fractional softening of edge.

The weirdness is palpable. Cogswell defies easy explanation, and yet, there is a strange beauty to its unnatural harmony. The industrial nature of this place suggests toil and creation, but the lack of dynamic suggests that this cycle has ceased. This feeling is not unpleasant, though. It is a place of profound stillness. The Heart of Cogswell is a paradox: an ecosystem entirely constructed, yet profoundly dead, a monumental achievement that has found its perfect rest.
