The Gristmill, they called it. Not because anyone had ever milled grain here, but because of the way the submerged structures of the place, the only things that *were* the place, slowly, relentlessly, ground each other down.

It was a small space, a kind of rift in the deep sea. There were no natural formations here, no geological surprises. The sea floor was a flat, featureless plain of grey mud. Some unknowable force, perhaps gravity, kept the water here at a constant depth, neither rising nor falling. The water itself was unremarkable, cold but not frigid, with good visibility, allowing the pale light that filtered down from above to reach the structures without much obstruction. The light was sufficient to work by; not gloomy, but subdued, like a perpetual overcast day.

And the structures... they were the source of the place, the reason for the Gristmill's existence. Massive, metallic, and utterly alien, they rose from the mud in a few scattered clusters. They weren't buildings or machines in any recognizable sense. There were no graceful arches, no elegant curves, no signs of design. They were clunky, blocky, and angular, built from some impossibly strong, impossibly dense metal. The metal was pitted and scarred from who-knows-how-long submerged wear. Each massive piece was covered in intricate, almost fractal patterns of tiny, precise holes and etched lines. Some looked like they were welded and bolted together from smaller, equally alien components; others seemed to have been forged whole.

The structures had a function. Even though there were no visible moving parts, and no apparent power source, a kind of low, subsonic hum came from within them, deep enough to resonate in the bones. The hum was barely perceptible, a continuous background silence, but still, almost every piece of the Gristmill's structures vibrated, ever so slightly, with the same frequency. The water itself seemed to be very slowly, very subtly, circulating in response to the structures' presence too. The flow was almost nonexistent, and yet one could see the faintest of currents along the ground.

The structures had a process — a grinding-down, an eating-away of each against each other, slow enough to seem eternal. There were no obvious signs of decay, no rust or corrosion. It was as if the Gristmill hadn't changed at all in millennia. There was no plant life, and no fauna. Everything here was a part of the structures or affected by them.

This place was an engine, a machine so large, so strange, it was almost invisible.
