A low, grey plain. A vast, shallow sea of slate-colored water stretches almost to the horizon, where it meets a sky the same color. But the plain here, at the focus of this place, is not quite water. It is a dense, almost impossibly packed conglomeration of slick, dark stone. Not boulders, not pebbles, but something in between, a solid, interlocking pavement that gives only slightly underfoot. It’s crisscrossed by a thousand tiny, shallow channels, all filled with a perpetual, slow-moving trickle of water.

The air is cool, and thick with a perpetual, fine mist. This fog doesn’t obscure, but rather defines. It gives everything a soft, blurred edge, and fills the numerous, deep fissures that cleave the stone, making the plain even more dense. The water seems to be condensing constantly from the air, and it's difficult to say whether it's raining or not – the surface is always wet.

Vegetation is sparse, almost nonexistent. Here and there, dark, almost black, lichens cling to the stone, or stringy, thin mosses sprout from the edges of the channels. These are the only signs of life, and even they are still and quiet. There are no plants that grow tall here.

The scale of this place is deceptive. It seems small, manageable, but the density of the stone, the complex network of channels, and the endless expanse of water beyond the plain create an odd feeling of being both confined and exposed. The air is still, but it has a strange energy, a hum. Sounds are rare, almost absent, but a close listener could occasionally pick up a low, deep thrumming that may be coming from within the stone itself.

This place is built, though the hand that built it is long gone, or perhaps it still lingers. Here and there, scattered remnants of structures emerge from the stone, as if bubbles frozen in mid-formation. They are angular and metal, constructed from the same dark material as the plain, though more precisely shaped. They are clearly industrial - gears, pipes, and plates are just about still assembled into larger machines, but these mechanisms are twisted and eroded. The gears are frozen or jammed against each other. Some rise to a few meters high, others are mostly level. All show signs of long exposure to the elements; rust and erosion have claimed most of the fine details - it almost feels like the machinery itself has become one with the stone. They are not entirely ruined, though; a faint, pulsing energy seems to emanate from them. 

The light is normal, yet somehow filtered. The sky above is a canvas, reflecting the water, with the sun obscured by the eternal cloud cover. In the dimness strange colors can be observed, but only briefly. Glancing, you might see a flicker of crimson, an emerald flash, the barest hint of violet - but these hues disappear as soon as they appear.

Fauna is rare, and yet there are hints of creatures. Footprints or claw marks along the stone are barely visible, quickly washed in the rushing water. Mostly nothing, but the sense of something nearby, something observing, is persistent.

The weirdness is subtle, but it's there. The stone doesn't "feel" like stone. It feels almost organic, growing, breathing. The water is unusually pristine, yet it has an oily sheen that hints at some unknown substance. The plain itself, while solid, feels somehow temporary, like it could shift, disassemble, or even collapse at any moment.

And the dynamism is the heart of this place. If you stay still, you'll see a constant shifting of shadows across the landscape, an imperceptible quivering of the dense material. The wind, when it comes, seems to rip through the structures and along the streams so violently, but the stone is immutable to it. Sometimes the dark water rises a foot, or the faint thrumming increases to a vibration, or the strange colors flicker and dance over the stone. This is not a place of destruction, exactly, but one of constant, unfettered transformation. A process in perpetual motion.
