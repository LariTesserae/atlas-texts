The world breathes a silent, frigid breath. It’s hard to say what holds the sky aloft, but the gray press of it is absolute. There is no blue, no sun, just the endless, cold expanse of the overcast. The air tastes of wet iron, of the metal that is the skeleton of this place.

Here, in the high, open valley, the dominant material is plant. Not merely plant life, but a total plant-as-substrate: the very ground is a thick mat of interwoven, moss-like fibers, a foot or two deep. It has the consistency of a well-trodden sponge, giving way softly underfoot. Wherever a foothold is pressed, a tiny pool of dark, ferrous water wells up, only to be reclaimed by the surrounding vegetal matrix within seconds. 

The vegetation is a riot of dark greens and deep, bruised purples, a tapestry of dense, interwoven stalks, fronds, and bulbous forms. Everything is cold to the touch, and perpetually damp. Water clings to every surface, not as droplets, but as a subtle sheen, a slick embrace. Even the "sky" sweats a fine condensation.

The world is not silent in the absence of sound. There is a quiet hum that vibrates in the marrow: the rhythmic thrum of unseen engines working. The plants themselves seem to pulse very slowly, an almost imperceptible expansion and contraction. At their heart, if they have hearts, is an industrial purpose. 

Giant, metallic structures, like upended clockworks, rise from the vegetal ground, their intricate, rusted gears and pipes partially buried in the plant-mass. These are the engines, the factories. They don’t seem to "work," per se. They don’t spew smoke or steam or any visible output. They exist; and in their existence, they pulse and hum. Their purpose, if they have one, is inscrutable.

The erosion, or rather the "establishment," is complete; everything is in its own state. The plants and the metal are inextricably linked, each shape being defined and re-defined by the other. The land is an expression of this relationship, a continuous process of becoming and unbecoming.

Fauna are a whisper, if present at all. Some shapes, like crystalline insects built of dark metal, flicker at the edge of the visual field. They move with an unnatural speed, an uncanny, erratic dance. Perhaps they are watchers or maintenance crews. Or perhaps they are simply the chaotic byproduct of the industrial ecosystem.

The world is turbulent. At any moment, the ground might softly heave, sending tendrils of plant-matter upward to envelop a metallic structure, or the metal might break-down and transform into plant matter. There is a deep-seated instability, a perpetual state of flux.

The light is normal – not dim, but just the general overcast. Still, the density of the place warps vision, makes it difficult to focus across distances. From one perspective, the world only extends past the immediate; it's a claustrophobic forest of metal and plant. But from another, the valley extends outwards for miles, with the promise of more metal-plant factories and pulsing vegetation.

This is a place of raw potential, of a nature and a technology in constant negotiation, a place where it seems like anything could be possible, at any second.
