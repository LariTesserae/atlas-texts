The pocket hums. That’s the first impression — not a sound, exactly, but a vibration that registers in bone and breath, a sympathetic resonance. It’s a feeling that suffuses the entire space, which is roughly the size of a cathedral, though it doesn’t present as any recognizable architecture. It appears, at first, to simply *be*, a perfect sphere of warm, amber-tinted water, perhaps twenty meters across.

This sphere is suspended in an impossibly deep chasm, the walls of which are too distant to perceive. The only reference for scale comes from the objects within the sphere, and these are… confounding.

The water itself is the dominant element, and not just in its volume. It *is* the place. It's warm, with a hint of something metallic, like blood, though it’s also undeniably sweet, almost cloying. Light filters in from somewhere above, fracturing into a kaleidoscope of colors that dance on the curved surface. Within the sphere, colossal, intricate structures rise from the unseen seabed, crafted from a material that seems to be a fusion of metal and living plant matter. These structures, in their entirety, make up the entirety of the "built" environment.

They are impossibly tall, reaching towards the light with intricate filigree, adorned with what appear to be massive, pulsing bioluminescent organs. They could be coral, or trees, but the material is a smooth, gleaming metal in most places, and they're undoubtedly mechanical. They shift and flex, not in the way of living things, but in a precise, almost surgical manner. They are industrial in form, but utterly organic in their substance.

The vegetation is indeed lush, but it has no earthly counterpart. Filamentous, iridescent tendrils drift through the water, swaying in currents that seem to be generated by the structures. These tendrils are alive, and in fact are the source of most of the abundant fauna.

The fauna is strange, even for a place so obviously alien. Many creatures resemble aquatic insects, but are made of the same metal-vegetation and possess multiple, whirring wings like tiny, baroque engines. These are the source of the loud sound, a constant cacophony of whirs, clicks, and hisses, overlaid with a deeper thrumming that seems to be the source of the overall hum. There are larger creatures too, serpentine forms with plated exoskeletons that weave in and out of the colossal structures. They are predatory, and their movements are swift and purposeful, a near-constant ballet of hunter and hunted.

The entire sphere feels like an old, ancient thing, a relic from a time when the distinction between nature and artifice had long since dissolved. The structures look as though they’ve been in place for untold eons, worn smooth by the currents, their surfaces covered in a patina of alien life. Erosion is everywhere, but it has only served to sanctify the structures, and to give them depth and texture. Their scale is extreme, with impossibly large "rooms" and "corridors" that are carved into the walls and open to the flowing water.

The dynamic nature of the environment is relentless. The structures expand and contract, seemingly altering the tides and currents within the sphere. The mechanical fauna constantly moves, and the predatory fish are forever hunting. The ecosystem is in full swing, and survival is a constant game.

The weirdness is inescapable. It seems reasonable to assume no part of this place was built for humans, or by humans.

But there is something more. The sphere itself is a kind of prison. The chasm that contains it is a vast, empty thing. Outside the bounds of the sphere, all is dark, silent, and unknown. This realm is made of something else entirely—or, perhaps, nothing at all. This might be a place of endless beginning, or of eternal decay and entropy. But within the humming sphere of water, life goes on undeterred.
