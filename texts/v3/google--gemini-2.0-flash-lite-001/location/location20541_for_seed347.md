The air hangs heavy, a cloying, moist presence that settles on the skin like a second layer. It smells of wet stone, fertile earth, and something metallic, a faint tang that hints at hidden depths. Below, the ground is a labyrinth of submerged structures and towering plant-life. It's a city, or what's left of one, reclaimed, choked, and woven through by the insistent force of the world itself.

The elevation is deceptive here. It might feel like you stand in a valley, but the truth is the world has folded around you. Once-lofty buildings are now partially submerged, their upper reaches still piercing the surface while their foundations descend into a murk unknown. The water, a constant presence, is neither a clear lake nor a raging river, but something in between. It fills the spaces, creeps between the stones, and finds every hollow and crevice. It’s an omnipresent veil, turning the world into a series of shimmering, distorted reflections.

Evidence of the cold is everywhere, but it isn’t the biting, wind-swept chill of a mountain peak. Here, it’s a damp, pervasive cold, a bone-deep marrow seep. Everything *feels* cold, damp, yet the sky remains dim-lit, with the sun sometimes not quite breaking through the dense plant canopy overhead.

The landscape is breathtakingly lush. Giant ferns, their fronds broad and glistening, unfurl from every surface. Mosses, thick and vibrant, cling to the weathered stone, the exposed roots of the fallen, and the remains of crumbling walls. Strange, gnarled trees, their bark a mosaic of greens and browns, twist skyward, their branches intertwined, forming a living canopy that further obscures the already muted light. Dense, alien vines, thick as a man's torso, snake around the structures, pulling them into the earth, yet simultaneously holding them together. There are even plants which have adapted to the aquatic world and appear to be grown in pools or artificial canals within the ruins.

This is a world of young, violent erosion. The features are sharp, defined by the relentless work of water. The stone, once smooth, is now etched, carved, and worn away by the ceaseless flow. Buildings have been split and sheared by the force of the land. The very foundations have been undercut; sections of the city have slumped and collapsed, as time and nature grind down the artifacts of its builders.

The scale is grand, a multi-layered world stretching out in every direction. The immediate surroundings are densely packed, but every step reveals new vistas. Down submerged corridors, between the giant trees, along collapsing ramps emerge vistas, each more striking than the last. Twisted archways frame distant, water-filled courtyards, and obscured avenues beckon towards unknown destinations. The built environment is of a scale that once held many tens of thousands of souls.

The silence is the most unsettling element. There are sounds, of course – the drip of water, the rustling of leaves, the creak of stone – but they are muted, swallowed by the dense atmosphere. There is no roar of activity you might expect in such a vibrant ecosystem. This almost complete absence further enforces a sense of latent power, an impression of vast forces in a slow but unyielding process.

The fauna here is abundant, but mostly hidden. The city is teeming with life, but the creatures are adapted to the environment. The waters are disturbed by unseen forms, a constant, subtle churning. Insects, iridescent and unfamiliar, flit through the air, flitting between the foliage. Strange, long-limbed amphibians, their skin a mottled blend of greens and browns, watch from the submerged shadows. You catch glimpses of larger creatures, too – shadows in the depths, fleeting movements in the trees. The activity cycles of the fauna appear to include day and night, even if the light does not visibly change.

There is nothing in the landscape that feels *weird*. This might be because the strangeness has become so commonplace; the new normal, of a place defined by its transformation. The way the flora and fauna have adapted, the processes of erosion and reclamation, are all within the bounds of a natural system. The only truly unusual aspect is that the system has swallowed a city and continues to work upon its remains, and that it has done so with a remarkable degree of balance.

The rules of this world are set by the active forces of nature and the absence of technology. The dynamic is relentless: erosion, vegetation, water, and life combine to transform everything. The city is not a ruin; it is a current state, a snapshot in a long process of renewal. The cold is a force that slows, but doesn’t stop, the action. The fauna survive by their wits and by moving into the many niches and hiding places the environment allows.
