The wind, a thin, biting thing, traces the spine of the world. It’s a world of stone teeth, jagged peaks that claw at a sky heavy with icy, bruised clouds. The mountains are young, a furious choreography of uplift and collapse still playing out. Stone crumbles beneath the wind’s kiss, but the dominant impression is of sharpness, of harsh angles. Ridges meet the sky as though carved by some colossal, impatient hand.

Here and there, the stone weeps. Seeps of water, born from the infrequent rains that manage to claw their way over these heights. They glisten, momentary jewels, before the wind whips them away. The moisture, however, is enough. Enough to sustain.

The lower slopes are wrapped in a choking embrace of what could generously be called vegetation. Not forests, not even scrub. Imagine instead a felt carpet, clinging to the rock, the color of dried blood and dust, threaded with veins of pale, hardy moss. The density of the carpet is deceptive; it's layered. Things grow atop things, seeking every scrap of sun and moisture. These are not plants as we know them, but something like them. Broad, leathery leaves with a metallic sheen, growing in dense whorls, or clusters of spiky, chitinous stalks that shiver faintly in the wind. These are built things, in a way; layered and intertwined - a product of slow growth.

The built environment. It is what dominates the valleys. Here, nestled amongst the stone, are towns and cities. Not of any material familiar to us. The buildings are grown, or built by means we cannot quite understand. Stone is incorporated, forming the foundations and the skeletal structure. But between those bones are woven materials, like hardened, glistening webs, creating structures that seem to breathe, to shift subtly in the light. They have a biological quality, a sense of having been gestated in the landscape. 

The technology used is crude and direct, but also clever. Tools. Simple tools. Yet they are used with an intimate understanding of the materials. Every building is hand-crafted, meticulously shaped to fit the contours of the land, and to harness the scant resources available. The people work slowly, their movements deliberate. They are surrounded by abundance, but the resources must be carefully preserved.

The light is normal—the sun a pale disc occasionally piercing the clouds. The wind never ceases, but silence is its companion. The echoes of the wind pass through the stone, absorbed by the intricate structures of the cities. The only other sound is the soft rustle of the strange vegetation, a whispering that hints at secrets.

Life teems here, too. Fauna is abundant, but unseen. You might catch a glimpse of a flash of iridescent wing, or a scurrying shadow, but most creatures stay hidden. There is no hunting. No need. They are an intrinsic part of the city. The structures are built to protect them, to provide refuge. 

There are slow cycles at play here, not of life and death, but of growth and shift. The cities grow, expand organically, reconfiguring themselves. The stone mountains themselves are slowly worn away, and the settlements change with them, but this change is measured in ages. The relationship between the world and its inhabitants is deep. There is no sharp distinction between the land and the built environment; they are bound together in a vast, slow dance of mutual creation.
