The air hangs thick and heavy, a palpable presence tasting of iron and something else, something green and fecund, a buried memory of decay. Almost no light penetrates the perpetual twilight that blankets the valley. Overhead, a ceiling of oiled canvas, impossibly vast, stretches from one hazy horizon to the other. Rain, a fine, almost imperceptible mist, drifts down, clinging to everything. 

The ground is a carpet of deep, dark moss, spongy underfoot. It drinks up the moisture, but never seems to saturate, always ready to receive more. Towering, impossibly thick stalks of a plant like giant horsetail, the size of factory smokestacks, rise from the moss. They are dark, almost black, and their surfaces gleam with the constant condensation. These are not individual plants, but a single, massive organism, a rhizomatic network that underlies the entire valley. Their tops are lost in the dimness above, but occasional gusts of wind — a product of the valley's slow, erratic currents — bring down a fine rain of what looks like spore dust, black and oily.

The only sounds are the rustle of the giant plants, the drip of water, and the occasional, almost imperceptible *thrum* that seems to pulse through the ground itself. It is a slow, rhythmic vibration, the heartbeat of the valley, and it seems to emanate from the network below.

The valley floor is not truly flat. The giant plants stand in shallow depressions. Between many of these is a low, swampy ground, thick with reeds that are also impossibly large, and shallow pools of dark, still water. The water is clear, yet seems to absorb the little available light, reflecting back no more than a vague, dull shine.

Wherever the water pools, there is *life*. A proliferation of monstrous insects — chitinous carapaces gleaming in near-darkness — crawl, fly, and swim throughout the lower reaches. They are unlike any creatures on Earth, with too many eyes, too many legs, bizarre feeding structures. They emerge from the ground, from the plants, from the water, in a silent, seething mass. Though present in seemingly infinite number, they make little to no noise.

Evidence of the industry is subtler. Wires made from some unknown, black, flexible material snake between the giant plants as though they were living vines, running down into the shallow holes, and then down, into the dark ground. They don't provide light. Instead, they seem to absorb what little light there is, and perhaps, energy. If you scrape at the ground, or cut one of the giant plants, you will find intricate webs of metal, as though the valley itself is suffused with the bones of some vast machine. But these structures are not constructed, not built by any intelligence that can be seen. They are grown, like the plants, a part of the valley's natural processes.

The lack of light, the density of the plant life, the water, have given rise to an ecosystem unlike anything elsewhere. The insects feed on the plants, the plants feed on the water, the valley feeds on… something else. It takes nutrients from the land, the air, and even from the insects themselves. Any fallen insects are quickly consumed by the moss, and the spores released constantly from above. The whole system is self-contained, a closed circuit of consumption and re-generation.

But there are other signs. The smell of the air, the rhythmic thrum in the ground, and the presence of the black, metallic webs, reveal the truth: this is no natural paradise. This is an industrial wasteland, powered by an unseen force, where everything, every living thing, is either an input or output in a process of silent, slow-motion, efficient production. Its goal is a mystery, as inscrutable as the darkness itself, and the process is as unstoppable as the endless rain.
