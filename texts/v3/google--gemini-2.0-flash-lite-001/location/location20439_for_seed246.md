The wind howls, a constant, keening dirge across the plateau. It whips, nearly invisible, but you feel it first as a chill that cuts through every layer of clothing and flesh, a premonition of the cold. The air itself feels thin, almost brittle, as if liable to shatter under a harsh word or sudden movement. 

The plateau is vast, but not in the way of a desert or ocean. It’s a small, intimate vastness. The scale is set by the structures that dot the landscape, the remnants of a city, or multiple cities, now fused into a single geological formation. Their rough, squared geometries are softened by the relentless wind and cold, ground down over eons. The plateau, itself, is a flattened ruin, the slow grinding of erosion having reduced any original peaks to gentle swells.

Everywhere, stone. The primary material is a black obsidian, polished smooth by time and the wind. It’s cold to the touch, and when you can get close enough, reflections of the sky shimmer on the walls. The very air around the structures seems to vibrate with ancient resonances. The buildings are close together, a maze of pathways and forgotten plazas. What were once streets are now canyons, worn and deep.

Water is a persistent presence. The air is damp, carrying a metallic tang, as if it were exhaled from the dark stones. Condensation clings to every surface. Patches of moss, a vivid, almost unnatural green, cling to sheltered cracks and niches, the only splash of vibrant color in this monochrome world. Occasionally, a sudden rain will come, not a gentle shower, but a brief, brutal downpour of icy needles. They clatter against the obsidian, each drop a tiny hammer blow, deepening the carvings of the wind.

Life is rare, and it hides in the lee of the wind. A few hardy lichen species grow, clinging to the stone, and on occasion, a winged creature, something like a falcon but with unnaturally large wings, will ride the updrafts, its call a piercing shriek. The fauna is sparse, but the ecosystem, despite its apparent emptiness, is in constant, dynamic motion. The wind, the rain, the slow creep of the moss, the infrequent, dramatic shift of light, all work in concert, shaping and reshaping the landscape. 

The density is astonishingly low. Even within the city walls, you can walk for hours without seeing anything but the stark obsidian, the wet stone, the flashes of green, and the play of shadows. The emptiness is not a void; it is a space, pregnant with forgotten memories and silent, patient watchers. The light is normal, but filtered. The cloud cover, dense and low, diffuses the sunlight, casting an even, gray pall over everything. The obsidian reflects it, making the world seem to emanate an internal, cold glow.

The wind is not just a force of nature. It carries sound, a chorus of whispers and echoes. The shriek of the falcon, the grinding of grit against the stone, the soft patter of rain, the distant groan of shifting rock—all of these meld into a single, overwhelming soundscape. The sounds are not chaotic; they possess a strange, underlying order, like a melody played on an instrument of air and stone. The sounds carry other things too—something of the past and the future, secrets only the stones know completely.

There are no tools, nothing that can be called Technology. But this is not a world untouched by intelligence. The very cities here, the way they are arranged and molded, suggest that they were built by something other than wind and water. Perhaps some species, long vanished, with a deep understanding of the stone, the wind, and the cold.

The weirdness is the deepest cut in this landscape. The sheer age of the place, the uncanny silence of the "empty" spaces, the echoes of a lost song, the geometry of the structures. But there’s something else, a feeling of unease that sits just beneath the surface, a sense that the world isn’t quite as it seems. The way the shadows shift, the occasional flicker of movement at the edge of your vision, the feeling of being watched by something ancient and patient. This is a place where reality bends, where the veil between worlds is thin. Time itself might be different here.

The rules of this world are not immediately apparent. There’s a certain logic to the wind, the cold, the erosion - the physical laws hold sway. But there are rumors of other forces, of stone that remembers, of shadows that can steal away, and of dreams that can bleed into reality. To exist in this place is to accept its strangeness. To survive, you must listen to the wind, watch the shadows, and trust your instincts.
