The air hangs cool and heavy in the Under-City, a place of secrets and stone, of sudden downpours and the echoing drip of unseen water. Light, filtered through a thousand cracks and fissures in the ceiling far above, paints the vast cavern in a muted palette of grey and ochre. The smell is of wet stone, of something ancient and slow, and the faintest metallic tang that hints at the presence of things not quite of this world.

The Under-City isn't a city in the familiar sense. There are no buildings in the way we'd understand them, no towers or homes with doors and windows. Instead, the "city" is a confluence of geological features, a sprawling honeycomb of tunnels, chambers, and colossal open spaces carved into the living rock. The stone itself is a kind of conglomerate, a dense mosaic of petrified life, speckled with embedded crystals that glint with inner light. Erosion is barely a factor here, for the Under-City is still being formed. It is a place of emergent geology, where the very stone is in a state of becoming, shifting and growing, as it shapes itself. Great walls and pillars that reach up and out were not constructed. They came to be.

The sporadic moisture creates a strange, almost sculpted texture across the stone surfaces. The dampness condenses, clinging to the walls, collecting in shallow depressions that might fill with a shimmering water during a shower. Water, even in this low concentration, is a sculptor here. It etches intricate patterns into the walls, as though the rock itself is being gently unwoven.

Vegetation is a rare sight. Small patches of lichen, the color of tarnished silver, cling to the more porous surfaces, and sometimes delicate, phosphorescent fungi bloom in sheltered, damp corners. These fungi give off a dim, internal light, like tiny, submerged stars. Their scarcity speaks to the harshness of the environment, a place where life struggles to find purchase.

Sounds are, for the most part, swallowed by the porous stone. Silence is the city's prevailing ambiance, a silence punctuated by the drip, drip, drip of water, or the distant echo of a falling stone. A low hum pervades everything, a deep thrum that resonates through the rock.

The inhabitants, if one can call them that, are hard to see. Fauna is rare to nonexistent, save for the very occasional skittering of something in the shadows. But the Under-City hints at its own intelligence, its own agenda. It has been built, but not by hands. There is an order to the layout, a pattern to the tunnels, a logic to the chambers, but none of it feels human. The "technology" here is unlike any known. It's not about tools or machines, but about using the inherent properties of the rock itself. Channels and conduits are etched into the stone, and they lead to places and possibilities that no surface dwellers can see or imagine.

These "channels" and other carved elements appear to allow for flows unknown to the surface dwellers. When rain arrives, it can fall in unexpected places, and even move through the stone like it were air.

The dynamic nature of the place is inescapable. The light shifts constantly as the sun's position changes. Small tremors, felt more than heard, rumble through the ground. The very atmosphere is in motion, pulling and pushing in mysterious directions, as if the Under-City breathes around the hidden, nascent life that seems to guide it.

The Under-City's scale is vast. The open spaces are gargantuan. The ceilings are lost in the dimness above, and the tunnels stretch further than the eye can see. Paths seem to lead to endless possibilities, and there is a sense of something important always just out of reach, in a far distant chamber. The density presses in from all sides. The Under-City feels fully occupied—not in the sense of a population teeming with life, but in the sense that every inch of space is used, every stone given purpose, every pathway charged with potential.
