The world is a vast, shallow bowl of grey. It stretches to the horizon in every direction, a seemingly endless expanse of smooth, worn stone. The stone isn't entirely flat; there’s a gentle undulation, as if the ground itself breathes. The air hangs heavy and cold, a damp blanket clinging to everything. Tiny droplets of moisture condense from it, tracing patterns on the pervasive grey.

Vegetation is utterly absent. Nothing grows here. Instead, the landscape is scarred with the residue of what might have been. The largest features are shallow depressions, as if enormous, smooth stones had been scooped out and removed, leaving a perfect negative space. These depressions are partially filled with the same grey material as the rest of the world, but here, the stone appears to be subtly different, with a dull, almost oily sheen.

The light is dim, diffused by the perpetually weeping atmosphere. A weak, grey sun, or something like it, is barely visible, veiled by the constant condensation. The lack of harsh shadows gives the world a ghostly, ethereal quality.

The sound is *deafening*. It’s a constant, low, grinding roar. It’s hard to tell where it comes from. It seems to rise from the stone itself, a vibration that permeates everything, a deep hum that resonates in your bones and makes your teeth ache.

Scattered across this immense bowl are the remnants of structures. They are small, crude, seemingly hand-worked from the same grey stone as the rest of the world. They are little more than rough boxes and irregular cubes, the hand-marks of their makers still evident. They are widely spaced, like the remnants of a forgotten civilization. Despite their primitive construction, they show some refinement: carefully angled surfaces, smooth planes, and a tendency to be aligned with the wind. The arrangement suggests deep consideration, something ancient humans would have considered ‘sacred geometry’.

Despite the cold and the lack of vegetation, fauna abounds. It's difficult to see, at first. In this dim, churning landscape the eyes need to adjust. The life here isn't the kind you expect. The dominant creatures, the ones that seem to make up the great majority of life, are *stone-worms* — huge, segmented creatures that seem to be made of the same gray rock as the surroundings. They move slowly, grinding their way through the stone in a constant search for something, though what, it's impossible to say. They leave trails of a slightly darker, more reflective material behind them, the substance with the oily sheen that fills the depressions.

The stone worms are not the only life, however. There are also small, flying creatures, like iridescent beetles with six wings. They flit erratically through the air, their wings producing a high-pitched whine that adds to the deafening roar. In the depths of the depressions, where the oily sheen gathers, larger creatures stir. These are more difficult to observe, but they seem to be composed of living stone, too, though less like worms, and more like giant, slumbering mollusks.

The world is active. The wind, which seems always to be blowing, slowly erodes the landscape. The stone worms grind their way across the surface, constantly reshaping it. The iridescent beetles feed on the oily substance, and possibly something else, that the worms leave behind. The air is always in motion, creating a constant, low-level mist. The entire environment works in a strange, slow-motion ballet, a dance of stone and moisture, driven by the ceaseless, deafening roar.

This is a place of profound age, of a kind of primordial, end-state. The terrain has slowly, over untold ages, worn down to its essential form. The structures are decaying. The light is dim, life is adapted, even the very air is consumed and remade. This isn't just a physical place, but a process, a slow, patient, grinding of existence into the base elements. And at least here, whatever the base elements are, they're abundant.
