The scavengers are wisps. Fleeting shadows against the ochre canvas, they are defined by their fragility, almost an afterthought in the grand, roaring symphony. They are the inheritors of the silence, of the spaces between the notes. They are born small, always have been, and grow smaller still, pressed by the immensity of the world. They navigate the labyrinthine, plant-made cathedrals like mice in a forgotten temple, their movements governed by the relentless rhythm of harvest and flight.

They are clad in ochre-stained clothes woven from the same fibrous plant-matter that dominates the landscape, but so finely spun that it barely obscures the skin. They are nearly hairless, their features refined by generations of adaptation. Their eyes are large, almost luminous, reflecting the muted light, and their ears extend slightly, perhaps an echo of the bygone metal horns of their ancestors’ age; this helps them to sift meaning from the world’s chorus.

Their tools, crude and simple as they are, are extensions of a desperate practicality. The hooks are curved to pluck at the metallic remnants, to dislodge the last of a forgotten knowledge. The hand-axes are for breaking open fruiting bodies, for carving tiny pathways from the dense, pulsating interiors of the plant-structures — to reach the core.

They live in the interstitial spaces, the gaps and alcoves where the plant-structures weave but do not completely enclose. They carve dwellings into the very weave of the plants, creating living nests that pulse with the rhythm of the world. Their lives are defined by cyclical patterns. They are bound by the pulse, the seasons of growth and decay. The roaring chorus guides them.

Their lives are an open book, written in the language of survival. Food is rare, a bounty from the plant-fruits and the occasional, unlucky insect-thing that strays close to their dwellings. The scavengers hunt, not with might, but with cunning, with traps and nets woven from the same fibres they call home. They are careful, methodical, understanding the rules of the symphony.

But they are also watchers.

From their hidden perches, they bear witness to the colossial ballet of the plant-fauna. They observe the intricate patterns, the geometric shapes that manifest in the plants’ growth. They register the melodies within the roars, the precise choreography of the insect-like giants as they shape and reshape the world. They understand, as best they can, that they are not alone. And the knowledge has bred a strange mixture of awe and fear.

They have developed a rudimentary form of communication, a series of clicks and whistles that echo the plant-fauna’s language, a language of necessity and mimicry. They also seem to communicate through thoughts, a primitive telepathy, perhaps born from the constant, thrumming presence of the colossal organism that surrounds them. This mental language is a delicate thing, shared in moments of danger, amplified by the humid air. It is a whispered prayer of survival and resilience.

What drives them? Does hope flick in the darkness, the thought of a life beyond survival? Maybe they dream of recreating the metal tools, of breaking free from their shackles, from the cycle of dependency. Maybe they dream of a day when they are masters of their own destiny, when they can sing a song of their own, that can perhaps reach and answer the symphony of the world. At the moment, they are almost ghosts, mere echoes of their ancient, metal-working ancestors, destined to persist in the vast embrace of the ochre bowl, living between the notes of a song they cannot yet understand, yet have begun to learn.
