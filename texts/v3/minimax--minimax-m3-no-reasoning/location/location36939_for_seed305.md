# The Lumen Shelf

You arrive at the edge and the edge goes on for a long time.

It is a high place — call it a plateau, though plateau is too polite a word for it. The Shelf rises from whatever lies below in long, sharp-shouldered ramps of exposed rock, broken only by the slow leak of meltwater down their faces. The mountains that border it are young in the way a wound is young: their edges still cut, their fractures still fresh, the stone not yet having learned how to lie down. A million years from now this will be a softened country, but right now it is still a recent thing, the way a thought is recent in the moment it has just been thought.

It is cold. The cold is not a weather — it is the substance of the place. It has weight and presence. It comes off the ground in the morning in slow, deliberate sheets, and it sits in the rocks at midday like something poured into them. The air is thin enough that you can feel each breath as a small labor.

But the light.

The light is enormous here. The sun, unfiltered by any atmosphere worth mentioning, comes in almost sideways and very white, and the Shelf is so high that the sky is the color of polished tin for most of the day, and the rocks throw back every photon they are given. The stone itself, in places, has a kind of vitreous sheen to it — a glaze, almost — that turns the light from a thing you stand in to a thing you stand on. You cannot find a shadow that doesn't have a second shadow inside it. The light is, in places, brighter than you would like.

And everywhere, packed in, the living.

The Shelf is not empty the way high cold places are supposed to be empty. The Shelf is *full*. Full in a way that resists the word. The vegetation is not a vegetation that grows up out of the ground; it is a vegetation that has *agreed* with the ground to become a single thing, a thick living crust two feet deep in places, a felted, layered, breathing mat of mosses and lichens and the small blunt alpine things that don't bother with flowers because the season for flowers is a rumor here. It is a wet crust. The water comes from everywhere and nowhere — out of the rock, off the underside of the fog, off the breath of the creatures themselves. The Shelf sweats. The whole place is damp the way a held breath is damp: not raining, not dry, just *moist* with its own slow exhalation. There are pockets of standing water in the swales between the stone ridges, and they are not still, they are *moving*, gently, as if the crust under them were breathing them in and out.

The fauna is the part that begins to make you uneasy.

The fauna is the part that is, frankly, too much.

There is no animal here you can call by a name you trust. The closest things to familiar are the grazers — long-bodied, low, many-legged, the size of dogs but shaped like nothing you have ever seen a dog shaped like. They move in slow processions across the crust, heads down, mouths working at the living felt the way a loom works at cloth. There are millions of them. They make a sound like a sound you would associate with a much larger thing: a low, continuous, milling roar, the sound of ten thousand small bodies all deciding at the same time to take the next step. They are not in a hurry. They are never in a hurry. But they are *constant*, and the sound of them is the sound of the place.

Above them, the air is not empty either.

The air is full of things that are not quite insects and not quite birds. They are, depending on the angle, somewhere between. They are small, mostly, but they are present in such numbers that the air has a texture. They hum. The hum is not a sound the ear wants to receive cleanly — it is a sound the ear receives *as* a hum, a continuous electrical-sounding drone that you feel in the back of your teeth if you stand in it for any length of time. The light catches them. Their bodies are made of something that is not quite chitin and not quite metal — something rigid, segmented, faintly *glittering*, in a way that reads as mechanical before it reads as alive. When the sun hits a swarm of them at the right angle, the whole sky above the Shelf goes faintly gold and a little bit singing.

This is the part of the place that is industrial.

There are no buildings here. There are no walls, no chimneys, no roads, no antennas, no smokestacks. There is nothing that anyone has put here, and nothing that anyone has made. But the place *runs*. The place runs the way a factory runs. The grazers are not eating — they are *processing*. They move across the crust in disciplined, overlapping circuits, and what they leave behind in their wake is not a pasture but a *substrate*: a changed surface, a worked surface, a surface that has been through something. The winged things in the air are not swarming — they are *ferrying*. They take what the grazers leave and they carry it, in measured parcel loads, to the dark fissures between the stone ridges, where it is taken down into the rock by yet other things, slow pale things that live in the cracks and that you only see when a slab shifts and the light pours in and there they are, working, with their long jointed appendages, deep under the world.

There is electricity here. Not the kind that comes out of a socket — but the kind that comes out of a *system*. The winged things carry a charge. You can hear them before you feel them, and you feel them before you see them, and the air around the densest columns of them tastes of a thing you cannot name but that your tongue recognizes. On clear nights — and the nights here are clear, because the air is too thin to hold a thought for long — the columns of ferrying things in the air light up, faintly, the way a wire lights up when you put a current through it. The whole Shelf, on a clear night, is faintly mapped in slow pale lines of moving light, and the lines are not random: they are *routes*, the way the lanes of a highway are routes. The place has a *circulatory system*. The place has *infrastructure*. The place has logistics.

The pace of it is, in human terms, geological. A grazer walks. It will walk for the rest of its life, which is a long time. A fissure-worker works. It will work for a long time. The whole vast enterprise of the Shelf is conducted at a tempo so slow that you could sit on a rock and watch a single circuit of a single grazer for an afternoon and not see it complete. The dynamism of the place is, in the small, almost zero. The dynamism of the place, in the large, is the dynamism of a continental plate: a slow grinding, a slow turning-over, a slow certainty that the work will be done because the work has always been done and the workers are not in any kind of rush.

But the sound.

The sound is not slow. The sound is the only thing that is not slow. The sound is the sound of *all of it at once* — the milling of the grazers, the hum of the ferrying things, the long subterranean rumble of the fissure-workers, the occasional percussive crack of a stone shifting under the freeze, the faint, almost subliminal whine of the air itself as it passes over the electrified columns. The sound is loud and constant. The sound is the sound of an industry. The sound is the sound of a thing that has been running, without pause, since before you arrived and will be running, without pause, after you leave. The sound does not have a rhythm. It has a *density*.

The weirdness of the place is not in any one thing. The weirdness is in the *consistency* of the strangeness. Every part of the place is doing what every other part is doing. Every part of the place has the same patient, mechanical, slightly luminous intensity. There is no soft edge. There is no place where the Shelf becomes ordinary. The crust under your feet is a felted economy. The water in the swale is being drunk. The water in the air is being breathed. The water in the rock is being moved. The light on the stones is being worked with. The cold is being endured, by everything, with the same deliberate long-suffering, and the *patience* of it — the immense, uncomplaining, almost *dignified* patience of a place that is doing a job it has not been asked to do and will not be thanked for — is the strangest thing here, and the strangest thing here is strange indeed.

The Shelf does not care about you.

The Shelf, you begin to suspect after a while, is not aware of you. The Shelf is a process. The Shelf is a vast, slow, packed, bright, loud, cold, damp, almost infinitely patient *process*, and you are standing in it the way a leaf stands in a river. The river does not know the leaf. The river does not need the leaf. The river is going to do what the river is going to do, and the river is going to do it for a very long time, and the river, when it has done it, is going to be very slightly different from how it was before, and that is the only kind of change the river knows.

You sit on a stone. The stone is sharp. The stone is young. The stone, like everything else, is part of the process.

A grazer passes you at a distance of perhaps three feet. It does not look at you. It is working. Its many small feet make a soft continuous *tss tss tss* on the crust, and behind it, the crust is, very slightly, *different*.

You stay for a long time.

When you leave, the Shelf is louder than when you arrived, because the wind has come up, and the wind carries the hum of the ferrying things down to where the grazers are milling, and the grazers, in some way you cannot see, are *answering* — a long, low, slow *murmur* rising from the crust itself, as if the whole place were talking to itself in a register just below the one you can hear.

The Shelf goes on.

The Shelf has been going on.

The Shelf will be going on.
