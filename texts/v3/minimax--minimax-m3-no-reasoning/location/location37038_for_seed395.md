The floor is not yet a floor. It is the underside of a continent deciding what shape to wear, and it has not yet decided.

The air, by contrast, has decided. It is a warm, thick, motionless-feeling broth of vapor — never rain, because rain requires falling and nothing here is permitted to fall. The humidity rides along the surface, presses into every pore, hangs in layers one above the other until the light, when it comes, enters like a guest arriving through wet cloth. It is the only water there is. There has never been another kind. The rivers that will one day cross this basin have not yet been invented. The ocean that will one day hold the continent has not yet been convened. There is only this: vapor, and warmth, and the slow turning of an atmosphere against itself.

The basin is vast. Vast the way a sentence is vast when it does not stop. You could walk for a season in any direction and the air would thicken and thin and thicken again and the carpet would never lift. The elevation is the elevation of a held breath: barely above the mean of things, a low, low place, shallow as a saucer, the kind of place where the crust has only just remembered it is permitted to dip.

The carpet: a continent-spanning pressing of low growth — pads, threads, fans, things that look like crumpled velvet and things that look like wet paper and things that look like nothing at all until you kneel. It is one organism, or it is many organisms behaving as one, which is the same thing here. It drinks the air directly through itself. It has no roots, or its roots are the mineral itself, pressed against, tasted, slowly coaxed. It is the color of rust and old bone and the underside of a leaf left in a cupboard. It does not flower. It does not need to. It grows at the only pace this place knows, which is the pace of geological breath — slow enough that you could sit and watch a single pad swell over an afternoon, fast enough that over a year the carpet advances by the width of a hand.

From the carpet, rising with the slow inevitability of a sentence coming to its verb, the city.

It is not a city of hands. It is not a city of tools, of axes, of pulleys, of the lever. It is a city of mouths and time. A city built the way a pearl is built — by being laid down, again and again, by small soft bodies that carry paste in their jaws the way bees carry pollen, that pack it into position with the side of their heads, that wait while the warm wet air sets it, and then begin again.

The towers are not tall. They are wide. They spread before they rise. Each one is a series of platforms inset from the one below, so that what you see from any distance is not a skyline but a thickening of the horizon, a low stair-step geometry that reads at first as geology — as the patient layering of a young river delta — and only when you come close resolves into passages, ledges, ramps, the slow spiral of a thousand inward galleries where the paste is always being moved. The builders do not live in the city the way you have lived in cities. The city is their digestion. It is what they have made of the air and the mineral and each other. They pass through its channels the way blood passes through a body that does not know it is alive.

They are the size of the smallest finger of a child. They are pale, the color of wet dough. They move in continuous, unhurried flows, never quite a crowd, never quite solitary, and the sound they make is the sound of the place: a small clicking against the paste they carry, a soft industry heard through walls of paste, like rain heard through a wall, like the inside of a beehive if the beehive were made of stone and took a thousand years to build. The city is the quietest thing on the continent. The city is the quietest thing that has ever existed on this continent.

What else lives here, beyond the builders, lives small and quiet and wet.

There are flat things that fold themselves into the ledges of the towers and are not seen so much as inferred, by the slight rhythmic displacement of air against the paste. There are long thin things that uncurl across the warm damp walls when the humidity thickens, and pulse, and are gone. There are slow round things that browse the carpet in shallow arcs, leaving behind a darker trail where the surface has been pressed, the way a slug leaves silver elsewhere, except that here the trail darkens, because what is being eaten is not the carpet itself but the air-held dust that has settled into the carpet's upper felt. The fauna is abundant. It is so abundant that every surface has something on it, every ledge has something beneath it, every pause between breaths is occupied by a small life going about its small life. None of it speaks. None of it has any apparatus for speaking. It moves and is quiet and is again.

Above, the weather does what weather can do without water. There are storms that are only air — vast slow pressurings of the vapor, rolling across the basin in seasons that may or may not be seasons, because the planet is young enough that its tilt is still being negotiated. There are calms that are not calms but only hours of slightly less vapor, which the carpet accepts with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be, and which the builders ride out by retreating deeper into the galleries, where the humidity is always held, the way a candle is held inside a lantern. The light is normal. The light is the light of an afternoon that has not yet decided to end. Through the wet air it arrives softened, ambered, the color of a thing looked at through honey.

The geology is the youngest thing here, and knows it. The ridges that will one day be ranges are presently suggestions — long low swells, the crust's first tentative rehearsal of folding. Beneath the carpet, where the towers have pressed longest, the mineral has begun to remember that it is allowed to compact, to change color, to take on the faint vertical stripe that one day will be called a stratum. The erosion is coming. It has not yet arrived. You can feel it approaching in the way the lower galleries occasionally weep a thin gray runnel when the humidity presses too hard — the first rivers, not yet rivers, only the rumor of rivers. The city rests, for now, on intention. Intention, plus the paste, plus the patience of the builders, plus the cooperation of the air.

If you put your ear to the lowest platform of any tower, you hear the small dark industry: paste being laid, paste being carried, the small bodies of small creatures working in the small wet dark. If you do not put your ear to it, you hear the air, and only the air, and the air says nothing, because the air has nothing to say that is not the slow turning of itself.

There is no name for this place. The carpet has no name for itself. The builders, who have never made a sound louder than the click of paste against paste, have not invented the category of name. It is simply what is here. A vast, low, warm, packed, quiet, active place, with no water and all the lushness that vapor can support, with no hands and all the architecture that time and small jaws can build. A continent deciding to be a city. A city deciding to be a continent. The floor still deciding what it is. The air, long ago decided, holding everything above it like a hand held just beneath a chin.
