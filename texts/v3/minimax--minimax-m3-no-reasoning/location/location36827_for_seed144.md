# The Brass Ledges

The wind is the first thing you notice.

Not because it howls—it doesn't. It comes in long, slow pushes, the air thickening and thinning like a chest breathing. It pushes because it is *always* the elevation pushing back, the ridgeline bleeding heat off itself in shimmering curtains you only catch at the corner of your vision. The sun here is not a thing in the sky so much as a fact of the stone.

You are standing on a shelf of basalt and copper-stained schist at roughly three kilometers up, on a sequence of terraces that cut across the flank of a long-dead volcanic ridge. The terraces are not natural. They are not quite artificial, either. They are what happens when something with patience works a mountainside for a hundred and fifty years and then stops working and lets the mountain have its say.

---

## The Stone and What Grows There

The rock is dark. Not the elegant dark of obsidian but the work blunt dark of basalt riddled with iron, with feldspar, with seams of chalcopyrite that have oxidized into weeping greens and blues that crawl across the grey like mold across bread. In the right light—and the light here is almost always *almost* the right light, the sun sitting heavy at a low angle, casting long blues from the shadows and a brittle gold onto every flat surface—the whole mountainside appears to be sweating verdigris.

Vegetation is a negotiation. There are plants. They are not abundant. They have the look of things that have argued their way into existence one cell at a time: low mats of grey-green cushion plants, the silver-felted rosettes of a dry-adapted composite, occasional *Polylepis* with their peeling red bark standing crooked against the wind like elderly men with bad knees. These trees are not tall. They are not meant to be. They have grown along fault lines where the rock has cracked deep enough to hold moisture, where a rare overnight frost will melt down into the substrate and linger for weeks before the heat drinks it. There are also, in the gullies and the lee of a few select boulders, the startling dark red trumpets of a *Mutisia*-type climber, and the pale ghost-flowers of a *Descurainia* that only opens in cloud.

The moisture, when it comes, comes at three in the afternoon. The clouds boil up over the western peaks—you can see them if you turn, a paler range further off, crowned with snow that doesn't know it's almost the equator—and they come in like a slow invasion. First the light goes flat. Then the wind dies. Then the pressure drops, and your ears pop, and the air tastes of iron and ozone. Then the rain. Not a storm. Not a downpour. A *settling*. A grey veil of water that hangs in the air and coats everything and then, an hour later, lifts. The stones steam. The cushions drink. The *Polylepis* drip from every peeling strip of bark for hours.

In the dry hours—which are most hours—the dew does what the rain doesn't. It forms on the underside of the stones, on the north faces, on the corrugated iron roofs of the few buildings, and it is drawn up by the capillary action of a hundred small roots and gathered, before the sun claws it back into the air.

---

## The Heat

The temperature is a fact you cannot escape and cannot ignore. It is not the wet heat of the jungle below. It is a *dry* heat, something with grit in it, something that dries the spit in your mouth and tightens the skin on the backs of your hands. By noon the rocks are too hot to touch with bare skin. The air shimmers above the dark stone in slow rivers. The sky above is the kind of blue that does not exist in places that are not this high—a blue so deep and so saturated that it hurts to look at it for long, and the lighter a man faces into the sun, the more his eyes water and the more the world around him bleeds into bloom.

At night, the heat goes. Quickly. Completely. The stone exhales. By two in the morning you can see your breath, and the puddles in the shallow basins have a skin of ice on them, and the *Polylepis* are outlined in white against the black of the slope. The temperature swings twenty-five degrees between dusk and dawn. The matter wakes every decade or so to find what look it has now.

---

## The Ledges and What Has Been Built There

And then there are the ledges.

The terraces are not the only mark of intention on the mountain. There are buildings. They are not *many*, but they are *there*. There are perhaps a hundred of them in the whole of the upper valley—stone-walled, tin-roofed, small. Some are homes. Some are workshops. Some are what were once homes and once workshops and are now something else, where the brief mechanical pulse of the place has slowed to a slower beat that still finds its rhythm.

The buildings cluster along the middle terrace, where a thin spring emerges from a crack in the cliff and runs, for about a hundred meters, before vanishing back into the gravel. Around the spring the settlement has gathered itself—a huddle of low roofs, a small plaza of fitted stone, a thin-threaded stream of clear water that everyone drinks from here.

The tech is not absent. It is just *old*, and quiet, and integrated. There is a mill at the spring—a stone-crushing mill for the ore that still comes out of the upper slopes, a small operation that runs on a diesel that coughs to life twice a day and then shuts itself off. There is a fence of corrugated tin around the *paddock* — a paddock of perhaps half a hectare, where a handful of *llamas* and one or two alpacas graze and lean, and where, on certain days, a small donkey cart will be hitched and a man will drive it down the switchback to the town that lies, by another valley below, in the warmth of the lower slopes. There are power lines. They come up the mountain on poles that lean. They feed a few bulbs. They do not feed many bulbs.

The buildings are not uniform. Some are of fitted stone, the schist of the mountain cut and laid and mortared with a lime that was burned here, in a kiln that is now a kiln-shaped heap. Some are of adobe, the clay of the lower slopes mixed with the chaff of a small grain that is grown here, in terraced plots that catch the morning sun. Some are of *corrugated* — tin, iron, scraps of roofing that have been worked, then been reworked, then been reworked again, until the original sheet is unrecognizable.

There are also *remains* — there are the faint walls and the low foundations of buildings that are no longer buildings, that have been *weathered* back into the slope, that have become, in their slow erosion, *part* of the mountain. A man who did not know the place would not see them as buildings.

## The Valley of Care

In the open valley — between the terraces, where the slope eases and the *Russian River* — no, the *river*, it is just a river, a small, fast, cold river that comes down from the snow of the further peaks — *flows* — there is the *habitat*. There are *vicuñas* here, small and quick and almost unbearably elegant, in herds of eight to twelve, grazing the cushion plants down to a fuzz. There are *culpeos* — fox-colored foxes, the color of the rust on the tin roofs — that hunt the viscachas that live in the loose stone walls of the abandoned terraces, the viscachas themselves small and rounded and absurd, and you cannot understand how they survive the cold until you give up. There are *tinamous* — small, plain, plump birds — that move through the *Polylepis* in small flocks. There are *condors*. There are *always* condors. They ride the thermals off the dark stone all day, slow circles, and you do not see them so much as *glimpse them* — a shape on the darker curve of the sky, then gone, then a shape again.

There are also, less *presumed*, *alpacas* that are not white and not black but a *grey-blue*, a herding, a color that does not occur in alpacas. There are *alpacas* that are not alpacas. There are *alpacas* that are *blue*. There are people in the settlement who will not talk about the blue alpacas. There are people in the settlement who will only talk about the blue alpacas. There are people in the settlement who have seen the blue alpacas at three in the morning, on the upper terrace, when the ice is on the puddles and the light is low. They are not all the same people.

---

## The Sound

The sound is sparse. The wind is the constant. The wind is the dominant — a low, constant, continuous hum in the rigging of the tin roofs, a slow rattle that does not stop, that only *lessens* — that lessens when the cloud comes, that lessens in the *lull* of the *3 p.m.* lull. There is the *tick* of the *mill*. There is the *clatter* of the *hooves* of the *vicuñas* on the *stone*. There is the *thin, high, thin* whine of the *power lines* in the *wind*. There is, at *dusk*, the *drip* of the *dew* from the *eaves* of the *tin*. There is, at *dawn*, the *bell* — a small, a single, a thin brass bell — that is rung at the *chapel*, that is not a clapel but a *shape* — a small stone shape with a cross on it — at the *plaza*, at the *spring*, at the *beginning* of the *day*.

There is, at *night*, the *quiet* — a *quiet* that is not *silent* but is *full* — of the *stars* on the *stone*, of the *cold* on the *skin*, of the *thought* of the *man* who is *thinking* in the *dark*, of the *man* who is *sleeping* and the *man* who is *not sleeping*.

There is, at *all hours*, the *low, low, low* hum of the *earth* itself — the *earth* is not *dead*, the *earth* is *slow*, the *earth* is *thinking* — a *hum* that you do not *hear* so much as *feel*, in the *teeth*, in the *bones*, in the *deep* of the *deep*.

---

## The Underlying

And the place has *underlying*. The place is, in its *weirdness*, *unusual*. The place has *rules* that are not *rules* of *physics*, that are *rules* of *feeling*, of *thinking*, of *being*. The place is *alive* in the *slow* way of the *mountain*. The place is *thinking* in the *slow* way of the *mountain*. The place is *patient*, and *long*, and *old*, and *waiting*.

The blue alpacas are part of this. The *sound* of the *bell* is part of this. The *dew* of the *3 a.m.* is part of the *feeling*. The *feeling* of the *place* is that it is *being watched*, by something *slow*, by something *patient*, by something that is *not* the *condors* and *is* not the *vicuñas* and *is* not the *people*, by something that is *part* of the *place* and *part* of the *stone* and *part* of the *dew*.

You do not *see* this something. You *feel* it. You *feel* it in the *back* of the *neck*, in the *slow* of the *eye*, in the *deep* of the *deep*. The *place* is *alive*. The *place* is *thinking*. You are, in this *place*, a *guest*.
