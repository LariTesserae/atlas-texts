There is a place where the mountain has only just begun to know itself.

One approaches from a saddle of broken rock where the path — if it can be called a path, more a memory of footfalls pressed into clay — narrows between two fins of dark stone. The stone is sharp. Knives of it protrude from the ground at angles no weathering has yet softened. You can scrape a knuckle on a passing boulder and come away with skin, sometimes with blood, and the scratch is the color of iron because the stone has iron in it. The fins and spines and split ribs of this place have only recently risen into the air. Geologically, it is barely a child. Children draw sharp pictures.

Into this young wound the green has already poured.

It is not a green you would recognize from any lowland. The colors are bruised. They are the purple-brown of wet bark, the gray-green of moss that has been rained on for nine straight days, a kind of celadon you would call the color of old glass if you could find glass here. There is no glass here. The light has to make do with itself. And the light is dim — not because the sun has failed, but because the elevation is high enough that the clouds are not above you but *with* you, walking beside you, brushing your shoulders. They sit low. They sit thick. They thin in patches sometimes and a column of yellow-white sun will lance down between two shoulders of cliff, but mostly the day is a kind of dusk that never resolves. Shadows are soft. Distances are kind. A stand of trees ten meters away can look like a stand of trees a hundred meters away and you will not know until you have walked into it and the trees have walked around you.

The village — there is a village, although the word feels too grand for what it is —

The village is small. It could fit inside a cathedral if the cathedral were made of grey weather and held no statues. A scatter of stone-and-thatch buildings huddles along a single terraced shelf that the inhabitants have carved out of the hillside. They have done this with patience and with hand tools. Every wall you see is the work of hands holding hand tools. There are no metal reinforcements, no milled lumber; the houses are stacked rather than built, stones fitted to stones the way water fits to water, with mud and moss and the damp that is in everything here as fillings in the joints. The roofs are thatch so thick it has become its own small ecosystem — moss, lichen, the resin of plants, beetles, the silvery threads of something that might be spider or might be neither.

There are perhaps thirty buildings. They are spaced. None of them crowd the others. Between every two roofs there is a gap wide enough to walk through carrying a basket, and in the gap there will always be a tree, or a fern thicket the height of a man, or a slow trickle of water running down the path in a thin dark tongue. The damp is everywhere. It beads on every surface that will hold a bead. It darkens the stones at their bases. It glistens on the leaves of the ferns. Your clothes are damp within twenty minutes of arriving and you cannot remember exactly when they became damp; the moisture arrived without entry.

The buildings are small. They are dimmer inside than out. Smoke from the fires inside finds the thatch and goes nowhere, and so the people keep their fires small and their doors open, and the smoke wanders the village like a slow animal, low to the ground, curling around ankles.

The village is inhabited but feels, in the way that small places sometimes feel, as though it is being *visited* by its inhabitants rather than *lived* in. People are here, and they seem to be working — you catch them in glimpses, in the gaps between roofs, in doorways — but their working has the air of a thing that has been rehearsed. They move like they have always moved. They glance at you and the glance is half-finished.

And then you become aware of the fauna.

It is not a thing that announces itself. This is the strangeness.

You cannot, at first, account for the quality of the silence here. The mountain is damp. The mountain should be loud with the sound of water — and water does sound, the small rivulets run with their small perpetual whispers — but there is a *lack*, somewhere in the lower register, that you do not name until much later. The lack is of birdsong. There are birds. There are extraordinary numbers of birds. But the birds here do not sing. They move. They swarm. They have, somehow, replaced song with motion.

Look up.

Look up and the canopy is moving. It is moving the way a single sheet of silk moves when a hand is drawn beneath it. Something is travelling through the branches, not falling, not flying, but *transiting*. It is travelled by hundreds of the same small body. Their bodies are dark, smaller than sparrows, with a gloss like wet ink. They have no visible necks. They press themselves flat against the upper surfaces of branches and stream along them in long dark currents, two or three abreast, and where the current splits around a fork the split rejoins on the other side and the rhythm is not interrupted. They make no sound. They make the leaves move, and the sound of their passage is the leaves themselves, a faint continuous rustle that the ear eventually stops hearing because the ear, given enough repetitions, will tune a sound out.

This is only the first thing. The first fauna.

There are others.

There are beetles — vast quantities of beetles, in colors that belong to metals or to beetle wings in other places: a green of certain old copper pipes, a violet of certain old bruises. They work the damp. They are everywhere — in the thatch, in the moss, in the seams of stone walls, beneath the bark of every tree. They work at a scale that means you do not see them, you see the *evidence* of them: dust, frass, the fine saw-tooth pattern they leave on leaves. There is a constant, very faint, dry whisper from this labor. It sounds like a paper being crumpled very slowly. It does not stop. You could fall asleep to it and it would still be there when you woke.

There are things in the water. The thin dark tongues of water that run down every path hold small pale crustaceans of a kind you cannot name, and they move in pulses — every few seconds the whole tongue of water twitches as something travels along its length — and the pulse throws up a faint click. Clicks, separated by silence, separated by clicks. This is the only fauna-made sound you can hear without pressing your ear to the earth.

There are invertebrates in the soil that you will never see but whose presence is felt as an almost imperceptible hum through the soles of your feet if you stand still long enough on a path.

There are frogs. There are frogs in such abundance that the underside of every leaf above a certain height has one or two sitting on it, and their droppings fall as a constant very fine mist, the smallest rain, into the lower leaves, and you walk through it and the falling is so fine you think at first that the damp has begun to condense on you from below. The frogs are mostly silent. Occasionally — rarely — one will make a sound. A single clear note, like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass, and it will be answered by another, somewhere else, and they will pass a single phrase back and forth, and then stop, and the silence returns.

There are things with more legs than you can bring yourself to count.

There are spiders, but the spiders are large here, and they build webs that span the gaps between buildings, and the webs are not decorative but load-bearing — the damp makes them heavy — and so the spiders maintain them constantly, and as you walk the village you are walking under and through a low complex architecture of damp silk that catches your hair and your sleeves and your shoulders. The spiders themselves are pale. They sit in the centers of their webs and they are not frightening, which is what is frightening about them. They have expressions you can almost read.

There are birds that mimic the sounds of other animals. They mimic them quietly. You will think you have heard a beetle, and look up, and find a bird. You will think you have heard the click of a water-crustacean, and a bird will turn its head on the roofbeam.

The fauna is overwhelming. The fauna is everywhere. The fauna is the place. There is no rock here, when you look closely, that is not in motion. There is no leaf that is not worked. There is no inch of moss that is not a city.

And yet the place is silent, almost silent, the small sounds so numerous and so soft that they cancel each other out and you are left with the sound of your own breathing and the sound of your own footsteps. It is, in the end, the quietest place you have ever been. It is, in the end, the most full of life.

The people here live inside this. They do not seem to find it strange. They step around the tendrils of moss that are slowly consuming the door frames. They duck under the webs without breaking them. They leave the lights inside their houses low because they have discovered, presumably over many generations, that the fauna prefers dimness, and that where the fauna is undisturbed there is less of the almost imperceptible scraping against the skin at night. They speak softly. When they have to say something, they cup their hands and they say it, and the fauna presses in around the shape of their words without breaking the quiet.

If you sit with one of them — if you can find one willing to sit with you — and you ask them what this place is, they will offer a name, but the name will not be a name you have heard before. It will be a word that means something like *the wet shoulder* or *the place the green is loud* or *the listening*. They will not translate it further. The fauna will brush past your ankles as they explain. You will not know if they have finished speaking or if they have decided to let the place finish the sentence for them.

The mountain is young. The mountain will, in time, become less sharp. Its fins will round. Its stones will fall from their present angles and lie down flat on the ground and become a kind of gravel that future generations will mistake for old, will mistake for always-here. The green will thicken, or thin. The water will cut its channels deeper, or find new ones.

But for now the mountain is young, and the green is loud, and the animals are here, and the people are here, and the silence is here, and you, having stepped off the path and into the place, are here too.

You will be damp for the rest of the day. The damp will not come out of your clothes for a week. You will be unable, for a long time after you leave, to settle into a quiet room, because the silence will feel like the kind of silence that is full.
