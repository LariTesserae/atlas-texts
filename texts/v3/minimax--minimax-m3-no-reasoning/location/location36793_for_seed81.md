# The City That Forgot to Be Alive

The train grinds upward for forty minutes before the windows finally clear the last of the cloud bank, and then there it is: a settlement sprawled across a shelf of dark basalt, eight hundred meters above a valley that is no longer green.

## The Plateau

The plateau is old. You can feel it in the way the stone has settled into itself — basalt columns leaning against one another like tired men at a rail, their joints packed with a pale mineral dust that the heat has half-vitrified into glass. Every surface that could have been sharp has been rounded, but not softened; the erosion here is patient rather than gentle. A century of monsoon-season winds, each one carrying the same gritty water across the same stones, has left the rock with a skin like the inside of a mouth. It looks almost biological, which is the first wrong thing, because nothing here is biological.

The city spreads across roughly nine square kilometers of this shelf and the lower slopes below it. From the train window, the first thing you notice is that the buildings are not crowded. They stand apart from one another with the formality of old money — low industrial halls of poured black concrete, their roofs flat and dark with condensate; ventilation stacks that rise like organ pipes, each one capped with a copper cowl gone green in the wet heat; and threaded between them, the pale ribbons of covered walkways, raised half a meter off the ground on short stanchions, so that the seasonal floods can pass beneath without notice.

The walkways are how you get around. You do not walk on the ground here. The ground is not safe.

## The Air

It is always wet, and it is always hot, and the two together produce a sensation that the locals call *the bath*. By midmorning the humidity sits at something like ninety-four percent, and the temperature, even in what passes for shade, holds between thirty-six and thirty-nine degrees Celsius. You do not sweat here so much as become a membrane through which moisture moves. Clothing darkens within an hour. Paper, left out, does not wrinkle so much as slowly become a thick, damp cloth of itself.

The condensation is on every surface. Droplets form on the underside of the walkways and fall in slow irregular rhythms, so that walking beneath them is like walking through a very fine, very patient rain. The basalt weeps. The concrete sweats. The copper cowls, where they have been allowed to oxidize, weep copper-green tears down their flanks. When the wind comes up in the late afternoon, it carries a smell that is half mineral, half ozone, and entirely without organic sweetness — no grass, no rot, no flowers, no woodsmoke, no cooking food, nothing that would tell a visitor's nose that people live here. Only machines, and the water they are slowly digesting.

## The Rigs

You came here to see the rigs, so the foreman tells you, and then he laughs in a way that is not quite a laugh, and takes you to the edge of the plateau.

Below you, descending in a series of broad terraces cut into the valley wall, are the geothermal installations. There are hundreds of them. They look, from above, like a forest of metal stalks — each one a borehole capped with a heavy valve assembly, painted in industrial yellows and reds that have faded into the wet air to a kind of bruised orange. Steam rises from them in plumes that the wind immediately tears into ragged sheets. The sound is everywhere and nowhere: a low, sustained exhalation, like the planet breathing through a sheet of pierced metal. Occasionally one of the valves will shift, and there will be a sharper hiss, and a pulse of heat will reach your face even at this distance.

There is no flame. There is no smoke. The rigs do not burn anything. They open, and the heat comes up. The heat turns a wheel somewhere, and the wheel turns a generator, and the generator makes the lights in the settlement blink in a sequence that the engineer in charge says is "almost steady." The rigs have been operating, in some form or another, for one hundred and twelve years. The original boreholes are walled up now, capped and abandoned, and new ones have been drilled beside them, and beside those, and so on, until the slope is more metal than stone.

## The Settlement

The town — *asabīya*, the locals call it, though they will not tell you what the word used to mean — grew up around the rigs in the usual way: a few engineering sheds at first, then a foundry to cast the valve parts, then a storehouse, then housing for the workers, then a hospital for the workers, then a larger hospital, then a second foundry, then a school that taught the children of the workers how to read the gauges, and so on, until what had been a few sheds on a basalt shelf was, by some official measure, a city.

The buildings are honest about what they are. There is no ornament. A door is a rectangle in a wall. A window is a hole glazed with a thick, ribbed glass that is always running with condensation on the inside and never on the out. The walls are poured in lifts, and the joins between the lifts are visible, and no one has ever painted over them, because painting would only be undone by the wet in a season. The concrete is the color of a bruise. The roofs are flat because no one expects rain to fall at an angle here; the monsoon comes straight down, when it comes, and the roofs are sloped just enough to let the water run off into the gutters, and the gutters run into the channels, and the channels run down to the collection ponds at the base of the slope, and the ponds are hot.

The people — and there are people, nine thousand of them according to the last count, though no one is quite sure — live in rows of identical low houses, each one with a small courtyard of poured stone and a covered cistern of dark water. They are not unfriendly. They are simply not forthcoming. A man at a tea stall will sell you tea. A woman in a market will sell you bread, of a sort — a dense, mineral-grey loaf that tastes faintly of the heat and the yeast and not much else. They will not, as a rule, ask where you are from, and they will not tell you, as a rule, how long their families have been here. The answers to both questions are longer than the conversation would bear.

## The Sound

In the early morning, before the heat has fully asserted itself, the settlement is almost silent. There is the drip of condensation from the walkways, and the far-off hiss of the rigs, and occasionally the clatter of a maintenance cart on the rails that run between the boreholes, and that is mostly all.

A bird, very rarely, will pass over on its way somewhere else. You will hear it before you see it — a single high note, attenuated by the humidity, that seems to come from every direction at once. The locals will stop what they are doing and look up, and for a moment the whole place will be holding its breath, and then the bird will be gone, and the rigs will hiss, and the condensation will drip, and no one will mention it.

By late afternoon the wind picks up. It comes up the valley from the south, smelling of nothing, and it moves through the settlement in long slow currents that you can see by the way the steam from the rigs leans and then rights itself. The cowls on the ventilation stacks begin to turn, slowly, with a sound like a heavy clock running down. Somewhere a piece of loose sheet metal taps against a stanchion at a regular interval, *tok, tok, tok*, and no one fixes it, because it is useful as a kind of metronome for the engineers on the night shift.

At night the rigs glow. A faint, dim, near-infrared glow, visible only if you have been here long enough to develop the trick of seeing it — the heated stone under each borehole radiating the day's accumulated warmth back into a sky that is, tonight, overcast. The town does not put up much of its own light. A few yellow bulbs at intersections. A single red beacon on the highest ventilation stack. The walkways are unlit; the people who use them know the way.

## The Rule

The ground, as I said, is not safe. The ground is hot. The ground has been hot for one hundred and twelve years, in some places, and the heat has begun to do something to the stone that the engineers do not like to talk about. The basalt, in certain of the older terraces, has begun to *flow*. Not molten — not yet — but plastic, in the way that glass is plastic, or pitch, or the inner thigh of a thigh. You can stand on a terrace that was poured seventy years ago and feel it give, very slightly, beneath your weight. The old walls have begun to lean. The old boreholes, walled up and abandoned, have begun, in three documented cases, to *rise*, as if the earth beneath them is exhaling, very slowly, and pushing them up by a centimeter or two a year.

No one is sure what this means. The engineers have a name for it — *the softening* — and a theory, which is that the heat of the rigs is migrating downward into the bedrock and changing its crystalline structure, and a counter-theory, which is that the softening was always there, in certain seams of the basalt, and the rigs have simply made it visible. They are monitoring it. They have been monitoring it for forty years. The monitoring equipment is more reliable than the engineers who tend it, which is a sentence that means more here than it would elsewhere.

The rule, the only rule anyone will tell you, is this: do not go down to the ground. Use the walkways. Use the carts. If a cart breaks down, wait for another. If a walkway floods, wait for it to drain. If you must, in some extremity, set foot on the ground, do not stand still. Move. The ground, here, is not a thing to be stood upon. It is a thing to be crossed, quickly, in the way that one crosses a sleeping animal whose disposition is unknown.

## The Feeling

There is a feeling you get, after a few days here, that is hard to describe. It is not unhappiness, exactly. The people are not unhappy. The tea is hot. The bread is adequate. The work is regular, and pays enough, and the engineers are competent, and the rigs do not, as a rule, fail catastrophically.

It is more that the place is *patient*. It is waiting for something, in the way that a long equation waits for the value that will balance it. The basalt is patient. The water is patient. The heat, patient above all, is doing its slow work on the stone, and the stone is patient in return, and the people who live on top of all this patience have, without quite intending to, become patient themselves. They do not rush. They do not waste. They do not, as a rule, speak of the future. They speak of the next shift, and the next delivery, and the next inspection of the softening, and the next cup of tea, and so on, in a long sequence that has no obvious terminus.

You will leave, on the train, after a few days, and the train will grind back down through the cloud bank, and the windows will close over the plateau, and you will find, somewhere in the descent, that you have begun to breathe more easily, and that the air has a smell to it again — wet earth, leaf rot, the distant green suggestion of living things — and you will understand, for a moment, what it is that the city on the plateau is missing, and what it is, also, that it is becoming, in its patient, half-vitrified, water-soaked way, instead.

The city is not dead. It is not alive. It is something in between, a third thing, a settlement made of stone and steam and human habit, and it is doing something to itself that no one has yet named, and it will go on doing it, in the same dim light, in the same wet heat, under the same unlit walkways, for as long as the heat lasts, and possibly longer, and possibly forever, and possibly not, and the locals will not speculate, and the rigs will hiss, and the condensation will drip, and the ground, very far below, will continue, very slowly, to soften.
