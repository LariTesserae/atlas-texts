# The Salt-Plains of the Long Count

The wind does not move. This is the first thing the eye learns to accept, and the last thing the mind believes.

---

What stretches before you was once a mountain range. It is no longer that. What was once a mountain range became a high plateau, then a dissected tableland, then a pediment, then a pediplain, and now, after epochs so numerous that the sky itself has had time to change color twice, it has arrived at this: a plain of pale mineral that runs in every direction to the curvature of the world. The rocks here are not igneous, not sedimentary, not metamorphic in any sense a hand-specimen could confirm. They are residue. They are what remains when everything that could be weathered, oxidized, dissolved, or carried away has been weathered, oxidized, dissolved, or carried away, and what is left is the memory of stone told in the grammar of salt. Their color is the color of old bone left in a dry place. They ring faintly when struck, and they are never struck, because nothing strikes them.

The plain is not flat. It is *flatness itself*, so perfectly denuded that the eye, seeking the horizon, finds instead a faint upward bowing — the planet's curvature becoming the only topography. A person standing at the center of a plain would have to walk for hours before the curvature hid them from view, and in that time the walker would not have ascended or descended by more than the width of their own hand. This is what *ancient* looks like when it has stopped being patient. There are no cliffs. There are no valleys. There are no dunes. Only the faint suggestion, miles away, of a slight darker band where a slightly harder stratum has resisted one more fraction of a millimeter of erosion than its neighbors, and so projects by a hand's-breadth from the surface, casting a shadow so thin it has to be looked for.

The elevation is extreme. The air is thin and dry beyond any desert the walker has known. It does not taste of dust; it tastes of nothing. Breathing it feels like breathing a vacuum that has been politely reminded to contain oxygen. Above, the sky is a deep, depthless, indeterminate blue — darker at the zenith, paler at the horizon, and showing no clouds, because clouds require water, and there is no water, and there has been no water for longer than the rocks themselves have been rocks.

---

And yet.

And yet.

---

The first one you notice, if you are new here, you mistake for a shadow, or a flaw in your vision, or a trick of the light. It is standing very still, perhaps a hundred yards out, and it has the silhouette of nothing you have a name for. Tall — taller than a man by a factor that makes the eye flinch. Narrow in a way that suggests not slender but *attenuated*, as if it had been drawn out lengthwise by an artist who liked the vertical. It is the color of the plain, and so it is almost invisible. It has legs, in the sense that it has several vertical arrangements of itself that touch the ground and support the rest. It has a body, in the sense that there is a central vertical mass. It does not have, in any sense your language has words for, a head.

There are many of them. Once you see the first, the eye learns the trick, and they are *everywhere*. Not crowded, not yet, but distributed across the visible plain in a density that grows on you — ten, then twenty, then a hundred as you turn. They stand at no particular spacing. They are not arranged. They are simply present, the way trees are present in a forest, except that they are not trees, and this is not a forest, and there are no trees, because there is no water.

They are not moving. They will never move. This is not a guess; it is the rule of the place. Whatever process brought them into being, whatever purpose they were made to serve, that process is finished, and they are what remains. They are the fauna of a world that has eaten itself past the point of life, and what is left standing is not life exactly, but the sculpture of life, or the rumor of life, or the memory of life pressed so hard into the substrate that the substrate has taken the impression as its own. They breathe, in the sense that the air beside them shimmers slightly, the way air shimmers above a hot stone — but the stones here are not hot, and the air is not warm, and the shimmering is something else. They have surfaces that are not quite opaque. Looking at one for a long time, you begin to see, *through* it, the faint pale band of the plain behind, as if the creature were made of glass, or salt, or salt that had learned a posture.

They do not react to you. If you walk up to one and put out a hand, you will find that it is harder than the rock of the plain, and colder than the air, and that the cold does not pass into your hand so much as *occupy* it. If you press your ear against it, you will hear, from very far inside, a sound like the last note of a bell that was struck before you were born and has been ringing down through the ages at the threshold of perception. You will not be able to tell whether it is one sound or many. You will not be able to tell whether it is fading or has simply become so faint that it has reached an equilibrium with the silence of the world.

---

At the foot of one of the nearer figures — the one you walked to, perhaps, the one whose bell-sound you listened to — there is a small pile of stones. Not a cairn, not quite. A cairn implies intention, a cairn implies a hand that placed each stone. These are simply the stones that happened to fall here over the long centuries, or the stones that were pushed here by the wind that does not blow, or the stones that the figure itself has, by some process you cannot perceive, *drawn* to its base the way a lodestone draws iron. They are arranged in no order. They are the color of the plain.

Beside the pile, half-buried in the pale mineral, there is something else. A tool. It might be a hand-axe, if hand-axes were knapped from this substance, and if the knapper had six fingers, and if the tool were meant for a hand twice the size of yours. It is small. It is unmistakably a tool. The working edge is still sharp, after however many thousand years it has lain here. Someone stood here. Someone knapped this. Someone held it and looked out at the plain and saw what you see, and then they left, or they became one of the figures, or they were always one of the figures, and the tool is what they set down when they stopped needing it.

There are other artifacts, if you walk. A grinding stone, its surface worn not quite flat but worn in a long gentle curve, the way a surface is worn by something that has been moving across it for a very long time in a particular direction. A small mound of cut mineral, no longer recognizable as having been cut. A line of darker stones set into the pale ground, running straight for a distance that the eye cannot account for, because straight lines do not occur on plains of residue — they have to be made, and making them takes will, and will is not something this place encourages. But the line is there. Whoever laid it walked with a purpose that outlasted them.

---

The light is normal. This is, in its own way, the cruelest thing. The sun — or whatever serves as the sun here, a small bright disc, white, unfeatured, hung at a fixed altitude in the unchanging sky — travels a small arc and sets, and rises, and sets again, and the shadows of the figures lengthen and shorten and lengthen with a regularity that would be comforting if it were not so obviously mechanical. Day and night. Warmth and the absence of warmth. The cool of the air is constant; the sun warms the pale surface of the plain by a degree or two at midday, and that warmth is given back to the sky in the long evening with the indifference of a thing that does not care whether it is warm or not. The shadows are the color of indigo. The plain at noon is the color of milk. At dusk it is the color of ash. At midnight, under stars that are very bright and very still and very far away, it is the color of nothing at all, and the figures are darker absences against the dark.

There are no seasons. There is no weather. There is no wind, ever; there are no clouds, ever; there is no rain, has never been rain, will never be rain; there are no rivers, no lakes, no springs, no dew. The plain is dry beyond dryness. It is a dry so perfect that the word *dry* has to be reinvented for it, the way the word *flat* has to be reinvented, the way the word *old* has to be reinvented, the way every word the walker brought with them has to be set down and a new one picked up from the salt.

---

The figures are not all the same. At first you thought they were. They have the same general posture, the same vertical attenuation, the same near-transparency, the same silence. But as you walk among them you begin to see variations. One is slightly thicker at the base, as if it had once been rooted. One has, near its top, a branching — a Y-shape, as if a limb had been lifted and never lowered. One is smaller, only man-height, and it is leaning, very slightly, as if it had been caught mid-step and frozen. One is enormous, far larger than the others, and from a distance you cannot tell whether it is one figure or several standing very close together, and when you come up to it you find that it is several, that they have grown into each other, or through each other, or that the salt has grown around them and joined them into a single pale mass, and at the joint there is a faint sound, a chiming, as if the bells inside had synchronized.

They are not arranged in any pattern. But they are not *not* arranged in any pattern. There is something statistical about their distribution — a clump here, a sparse area there, a long avenue of them running in a direction that, if you trace it on the ground, leads to the line of darker stones, and the line of darker stones leads to a low rise that is not a rise, just a place where the plain is, by a hand's-breadth, slightly higher than elsewhere. And on the low rise there is a circle of figures, and in the circle there is a tool, and beside the tool there is a smaller circle of worked stone, and inside the smaller circle there is nothing at all, and the nothing is very carefully arranged.

You do not go into the circle. You do not need to. You understand that whatever the nothing is, it is the center, and the figures are arranged around it, and the line of darker stones is the road to it, and the tools are the offerings, and the offerings have been here for a very long time, and the silence has been here longer.

---

The sounds of the place are almost nothing. The wind does not move. The sun does not hum. The plain does not creek or crack or settle, because it has already settled, into its final form, and will not settle again. The only sounds are the ones you make: your footsteps, which are loud in the way that a single drop of water is loud in a cathedral; your breathing, which is the loudest thing in the world; and, if you put your ear to one of the figures, the bell-sound, the long-decaying tone of something that was struck in the deep past and has been ringing down through the centuries at the threshold of perception, and which you cannot tell is one sound or many, or fading or eternal.

Sometimes, very rarely, you will hear two figures chime in sympathy — a soft clear note, like a finger on the rim of a glass, that seems to come from nowhere in particular and to die in the air without an echo. You will not be able to tell whether the chiming was real or whether the bell-sound inside one of them had finally, after however many centuries, synchronized with the bell-sound inside another, and the two had briefly agreed.

That is the loudest the world ever gets.

---

The world does not change.

The figures stand. The plain lies. The sun arcs its small fixed arc. The stars wheel overhead in their ancient configurations, and the configurations are the same as they were last night, and the night before, and the night before that, back to a beginning that no longer matters because the beginning and the present are the same shape. The shadows move at the rate shadows move, and at dawn they are long, and at noon they are short, and at dusk they are long again, and the figures cast the same shadows tomorrow that they cast today. A tool at the foot of a figure will be in the same place tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. The line of darker stones will not shift. The circle on the low rise will not be disturbed. Nothing here is going anywhere. Nothing here has anywhere to go.

This is the end of erosion. This is the place where erosion ran out of things to do and sat down and was still. This is the landscape after the landscape, the ecology after the ecology, the weather after the weather. The figures are what fauna looks like when fauna has stopped being fauna and has become geology. They are not alive. They are not dead. They are the third thing, the thing that the word *fossil* only gestures at, the thing that salt becomes when salt has been still for long enough that stillness has become its nature and motion has become unthinkable.

And yet.

And yet.

When you stand very still, in the long cool afternoon, and the sun is behind you, and your shadow is laid out before you across the pale plain, and the figures stand in their patient rows, and the silence is so total that you can hear the blood in your own ears — when you stand very still, and look at one of the figures, and the figure looks back at you with whatever it is that it does not have eyes to look with, you feel — you cannot call it a thought, you cannot call it a knowledge — but you feel that the figure is *waiting*. Not for you. Not for anything. Just waiting, in the way that a bell waits after it has been struck, in the way that a stone waits after it has come to rest, in the way that an empty circle of worked stone waits, in the center of a plain, in the middle of a world that has finished with itself, for whatever comes after the end of waiting, which is also whatever came before the beginning of it.

And you realize that you have been here a long time.

And you realize that you have always been here.

And you realize that you will always be here.

And the realization does not frighten you, because there is nothing here to be frightened of, and nothing here to be frightened for, and the silence receives it, and the plain receives it, and the figures receive it, and the long cool afternoon receives it, and it is the same as the plain, and the figures, and the silence, and you cannot tell the difference between any of them anymore, and the sun arcs its small fixed arc, and the shadows lengthen, and the bell-sound rings on, and the world is still, and the world has always been still, and the world will always be still.

And that is the place.
