The lowlands stretch in every direction, but "stretch" is the wrong word — they *crawl*. Nothing here has finished forming. The ground beneath would be basalt if it had cooled long enough to decide, but instead it cools in spirals, slow tides of glass-ceramic that have not yet learned to be still. You walk across something between a field and an unfinished thought.

The heat is not the heat of sun. There is no sun. There is a sourceless warmth that seeps up through the substrate, a kind of geological embarrassment, as if the planet cannot quite contain what it is becoming and so sweats. The air is dense with vapor, and the vapor carries the smell of iron and of something older — a mineral breath, the exhalation of a world still being welded together. You taste metal at the back of your throat. Your skin glistens without sweat, just condensation, as if the atmosphere is always on the verge of raining and never quite commits.

Everything here is constructed. This is the strange part. There is no soil, no sediment that was not laid down with intent. The plains are tessellated — you can see the seams if you kneel and squint — a vast tiling of heat-set slabs in muted ochres and bone-greys, fitted with a craft that has nothing to do with precision and everything to do with patience. Some of the slabs are newer than others. The new ones still glow a dull orange at their centers, like cooling loaves. The old ones have begun to migrate, lifting at one corner, shifting a hand's-breadth over a century, as if the whole tessellation is rearranging itself according to a grammar no one alive could pronounce.

Above the tiling, the sparse things. Towers, mostly. Not tall — the lowlands forbid tall — but clustered at intervals so wide that from one you cannot see the next, only the heat-shimmer where the next ought to be. The towers are made of the same material as the ground, but built rather than laid, hand-stacked courses of a dull pewter-colored brick. Primitive tools made them. You can see the chisel marks. You can see where a hand slipped, where a course was set down wrong and then pried up and re-set. Some of the towers are obviously abandoned mid-course, just truncated stumps with their working-scaffold still clinging. Some are inhabited, in the sense that something moves in them.

That something is not animal. There is no animal life here, and never has been. What moves in the towers is closer to weather — a slow peristalsis of the constructed environment itself, heat moving through the bricks, condensation creeping down interior walls, the towers breathing. From a distance, in the dim light, you might mistake one for a kiln still in use. Up close you can hear the faint interior ticking of expansion joints, the deep slow groans of a structure settling against the ground that will not settle.

The light is dim and sourceless. It is the light of overcast on Earth, but there is no overcast. The vapor hangs everywhere, uniformly, and the sky is simply the color of something forgotten — a milky tarnish, neither bright nor dark. Shadows are vague. You cannot tell the time of day because there is no day. There is only the slow rotation of the heat, a kind of dim pulse, the world dimming and slightly less dimming without ever becoming either dark or light.

Water. Yes. The wetness is the world. It weeps from every joint in the tiling, gathers in shallow basins that have no names, slides down the sides of towers in unforced sheets, condenses on every surface and drips. The slabs sweat. The bricks sweat. There is no standing sea, but you cannot go a step without hearing water move — a thin industrious hiss, everywhere, like a world that is constantly being wrung out. The vapor does not rise so much as thicken and then surrender, falling back. Nothing evaporates cleanly. The water just keeps being water, in different places.

The vastness. You feel it the way you feel weather. The tessellation extends past any horizon — a horizon here is a polite convention; you can walk toward it and the ground just keeps being ground. There are storms, but they are storms of geology: a section of tiling will suddenly bloom with new heat, a vent will open between two slabs, a tower will tilt and right itself over hours. These are not events, they are weather. The world is its own weather. You stand in the middle of an unfinished continent and watch a slab the size of a city rearrange itself into a spiral pattern that will be walked over and forgotten within a week.

The quiet is full. It is the quiet of a kitchen before anyone is awake, a held breath. But listen: the ticking of the bricks, the hiss of water in seams, the deep subterranean chuckle of magma moving under the tiling, the occasional groan of a tower deciding to lean a degree further west. None of it is loud. All of it is patient. You begin to hear, after a while, a kind of rhythm — not a heartbeat, more like breathing, the constructed world inhaling heat and exhaling vapor, endlessly.

And the wrongness. You feel it in your teeth. The tiling does not always connect properly. Sometimes, walking across, you come to a place where a slab has been laid that could not have been laid by any hand in any geometry — it tucks under itself, it is larger inside than outside, the bricks at its corners meet at angles that work but should not. You cannot look at the towers directly. When you try, they seem reasonable; it is only in your peripheral vision that you see them curve in directions that take a moment to translate into thought. The heat does not always behave like heat. There is a place, perhaps half a day's walk from where you are, where the vapor forms shapes that almost nod to you. You have not gone to look. You suspect you will not.

This is a world still deciding what it is. It was built by hands that have gone, or by something that was not hands, using tools that were not metal, in the dim wet heat of a place that will not finish being itself for a long time. Walk carefully. The seams are slick. The water always knows where you are.
