# The Sheltered Hollow

You find the place by following the sound.

It begins as a low, almost geological hum—a thrumming that travels through the stone of the mountain itself, felt in the molars and the backs of the hands before the ears can parse it. The path in is narrow: a single-track stairway worn into the flank of a volcanic plug, the rock here pumiceous and faintly warm even in the dim half-light of the overcast. The stairway does not so much climb as it threads, doubling back on itself through cracks and ledges until the sky above is just a memory and the only direction is *deeper.* 

## The Bowl

The stairway opens without ceremony into the bowl.

It is perhaps two hundred paces across, perhaps less—the eye is deceived by the things that fill it. A city fills it, of a sort, though the word *city* implies more planning than what is here. The bowl is roughly circular, with high stone walls that curve inward at the top, almost meeting, and the entire space is filled with the particular dim gold of a sky that cannot be seen but is certainly present somewhere above. The light has no source. It simply is, the way humidity is. Everything is lit as if by the last hour before a permanent dusk, and the shadows are soft and do not quite resolve into the corners of things.

The floor of the bowl is terraced. Someone—something—has, over what feels like a great span, built the slopes into concentric rings of low stone walls, each perhaps knee-high, each carved with the patient attention of people who did not expect to leave. Within each terrace, packed tight as teeth, the dwellings: wattle-and-thatch longhouses with frames of cured wood, some black with age, some newer-pale, and roofs that are not thatch at all but living mats of woven vegetation, thick and damp-looking, breathing slightly in the still air.

There are no chimneys. There are no windows. The longhouses have doors only, low and oval, and from each door there issues, faintly, the same warmth one feels in the stone of the stairway.

## The Green

Vegetation is everywhere, and yet there is no water.

The plants are *carnivorous*—or something close. Ferns unroll from cracks in the terrace walls, their fronds a deep blue-green and glistening with a sticky resin that catches what little light there is. Trailing mosses, pale as bone, hang between the longhouses. At the base of every wall, a thick mat of pitcher-shaped growths, each one the size of a clenched fist, the lids slightly parted, a dark sweet smell rising from them in a slow breath every few heartbeats. The plants are not decorative. They are the city's stomach.

Lichens crust every roof-ridge, and where the stone is bare, the bare patches are *intentional*—worn smooth by hand or by passing bodies, the paths through the green marked by use. Some of the pitchers are tended: their lids propped open with tiny splints of wood, the rims cleaned, the dark fluid inside ladled out into clay bowls. You realize, looking closer, that what you took for thatch on the roofs is not woven at all but a continuous tissue of living plants, roots threading into the rafters, the whole longhouse breathing as one organism.

## The Sound

The sound is constant.

It is the hum heard through stone, multiplied by ten thousand throats. Not a song—a *content.* A continuous low sound, the city's metabolism made audible: the creak and drip of the plants, the rustle of movement inside the longhouses, and beneath it all, the deeper note of the *animals.*

The fauna is the bowl's true population.

You see them first as movement in the high corners: pale soft-bodied things, each as long as an arm, clinging to the ceiling rock with many small limbs, their bellies translucent and faintly pulsing. They are *flying* things, though they do not fly—they launch, glide on skin membranes, land with a soft thump on a roof, and begin to feed. The pitchers open for them. The roof-mats part under their weight. There are hundreds of them in view at any moment, and the sound of their soft landings, their small squeaks, the *tap-tap-tap* of their limbs on stone, is the medium through which all other sounds travel.

On the terraces, larger shapes move. Six-legged grazers, low and patient, the size of large dogs, with blunt heads and rolling eyes and mouths that work sideways across the moss and the lichen, never lifting their heads. They leave a wet trail behind them and the plants they crop regenerate within minutes. Smaller things run between their legs, quick low things with too many feet, and once in a while one of the soft flyers drops from the ceiling and takes one and there is a brief scuffle and a brief silence and then the sound resumes.

At the bowl's center, in a wide open ring where the terraces stop, a different kind of animal stands. There are perhaps a dozen of them. They are tall—up to your chest—and bipedal, with long flexible necks, small heads, and hands at the ends of their forelimbs that are almost, *almost* hands. Their skin is a grayish-mauve, loose and folded, and their eyes are the size of plums and very dark. They are watching the bowl. They are always watching the bowl. When you emerge from the stairway, every one of those eyes turns toward you at once, and there is a moment of perfect stillness, and then they return to their watching.

## The Long Time

The place is *old.*

This is what the eye tells you after the first overwhelm. The terraces are not built—they are *carved,* the original stone of the bowl still present under the walls, the walls themselves only the parts of the stone that the builders chose not to remove. Erosion has done the rest: the sharp edges of everything have been rounded; the once-bright stone of the stairway is now a soft yellow-gray; the longhouses have sunk slightly into the terraces, their floors worn below the wall-tops by generations of feet. Moss has filled the cracks that the builders did not fill themselves.

Nothing here was built quickly. Nothing here was built to last. It has simply, by the slow accumulation of use, *outlasted.* The plants are not planted. They have come in, generation by generation, and been *accepted.* The animals are not domesticated. They have arrived, found a niche, become part of the architecture. The long-armed watchers at the center are not, you realize, the city's rulers. They are its *gardeners.* They move through the terraces twice in a day, and they cut, and they carry, and they feed what they cut to the pitchers and the roof-mats, and the city breathes out and breathes in.

## The Air

The air is warm and wet and thick. It is the warmth of a held breath, the wetness of a mouth. It smells of the pitchers—a dark, sweet, almost animal smell—and of the resin of the ferns and of the grain of the longhouses' cured wood and of the particular musk of the many soft-bodied flyers, who mark their roosting spots with a small bright scent that you cannot name but recognize as *home.* There is no wind. The air does not move unless something moves through it, and when it does move, it moves *slowly,* as if reluctant to leave.

You do not see the sky from here, but you can feel that there is one, somewhere far above, a small bright opening that lets in a thin column of cooler air that falls through the bowl's chimney and spreads out at the floor, displacing the warm. The creatures do not mind it. The plants lean toward it. The watchers at the center, when the cool air comes, raise their long necks and let it pass over them and their eyes briefly close.

## The Long Now

You stand at the edge of the bowl. The stairway behind you has, you realize, closed—not with a door, but with the slow growth of moss across its mouth, which has happened in the time you have been looking. The way out is not closed, exactly. It is *prioritized.* The city would prefer you stay.

The watchers at the center are watching you again.

The hum of the bowl is the sound of a place that has been here, in this form, for a very long time, and that will be here, in this form, for a very long time still. The ferns will unroll. The pitchers will open and close. The soft flyers will launch and land. The long-armed gardeners will move through the terraces and cut and carry and feed. And somewhere, sometime, the moss over the stairway will part again, and the city will release what it has held, and the long now will continue, unchanged, the way an old stone continues to be a stone, the way a low hum continues to be a hum, the way a warm wet place at the heart of a mountain continues to be *home* to the ten thousand things that have made it so.
