# The Conservatory

You arrive at it the way you'd arrive at a town found on a folded map, by following the curve of a path that doesn't so much lead anywhere as deepen into itself. The elevation isn't dramatic in the mountaineering sense—no cliffs, no roaring switchbacks—but the air has that thinned, attentive quality you get a thousand meters up, where each breath tastes of stone-water and pine resin. The cold sits on the surface of things like a thin film of glass.

Then the trees begin, and they do not stop.

## The Mass

They are not trees in the ordinary sense, though they have trunks, though they have bark. They are closer to a substance the forest has agreed to call *trunk*, a thick upright column of living wood stacked so densely that the eye cannot find the gaps. Imagine a forest that forgot it was a forest and remembered, instead, that it was a city. The boles press against one another like the shoulders of people waiting through a long rain, and the bark between them has knit itself into a kind of masonry—living masonry, sweating masonry, dark and ribbed and slightly breathing.

Up close, the surface of this consolidated trunk-wall is not flat. It is a dense field of ridges, knots, burls, and grafted seams where branches that tried to go in different directions have compromised over decades. Mosses and liverworts live in the seams; small ferns uncurl from every horizontal lip. Lichens spread across the bark in pale green-grey constellations: here a smear of *Lobaria*, there a constellation of pixie-cup. The bark weeps. Not dramatically—it doesn't run, it doesn't drip—but a slow cold exudation darkens the wood in long vertical streaks, and at every crease a drop the size of a pea will, if you watch for half a minute, gather and fall.

The cold is what makes the weeping possible. The air is near saturation; the temperature is just above freezing; the bark is just below it. Water condenses on every surface that faces the sky, and runs inward toward the trunk-heart of the forest, and the forest drinks.

## The Town

A town has been built into this. That is the strange thing. Or perhaps the town has been grown into it, since no human tool has been used for a very long time, if ever.

There are buildings. The bones of them are the trees themselves—great columns where the trunks have grown together and been, over generations, hollowed by rot and kept open by some patient intelligence. Around each trunk-cluster, a structure has been elaborated in lattice and thatch: woven willow laid down in diagonal fans, moss packed into the wall cavities for insulation, roofs that are themselves gardens—turf roofs dense with sedum, saxifrage, low alpine thyme in flower. The roofs of one building lean over and become the floor of the next; a stair lands not on a street but on a rooftop meadow, and from that meadow a door opens into a hall whose ceiling is the inside of a living trunk, the grain of the wood curved overhead like the ribbed vault of a small chapel.

The scale is hamlet-sized. There may be forty of these dwellings, woven through the forest mass in no geometric plan—some single-trunk pods, some long arcaded halls built along a particularly straight seam between boles, some half-buried under the turf so that the chimney is a stub of stone and the rest is a green mound breathing cold air from its vents.

There are paths, but the paths are not what you'd call streets. They are the spaces between roots where the forest has agreed not to grow, kept clear by constant foot-traffic—kept clear the way a forest path is kept clear, by the bodies of the inhabitants wearing the same line into the earth each day. They wind at floor level, where the roots make a slow sea of pale wood. To walk them is to be inside a wooden grotto, head just below the first branches, looking up between the boles at slivers of cold white-grey sky.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet is itself a sound—a kind of held hush, the way a cathedral is quiet not because nothing is happening but because everything that is happening has been absorbed by mass. The trunks take the wind and break it into murmurs; the mosses take the rain and release it in slow dripping. What sound there is comes from water, mostly.

A persistent pattering: condensation falling from leaf to leaf, a hundred small droplets per second descending through the canopy in soft percussion. A deeper, occasional tone: the groan of a trunk settling as the temperature shifts, a low creak that travels through the wood and up through the soles of your feet. The hiss of fog through the upper branches. Far away, the muffled percussion of a small stream running somewhere—always somewhere close, never quite seen—in the root-layer underfoot.

Occasionally a bird. The fauna is thin here, what fauna there is. A few winter wrens in the lower canopy, a hermit thrush or two, the high distant piping of something that might be a pika on a stony ledge above the treeline. Most of the animals are smaller than your hand: beetles moving through the moss in tides, springtails in the wet litter, a salamander tucked into a seam in the bark, eye catching your torch and going gold and then away.

## The Light

It is dim. Even at midday, the light that reaches the path is the light that has been chewed by fifty meters of wet needles and lichen and woven thatch before it arrives at your feet. It comes down in pale grey shafts that move slowly as the wind moves the canopy, and where it lands it greens—every surface it touches blooms with a more vivid green than the shadows have, so the forest seems to be lit from within, green on green on grey.

In the mornings, the dim is bluer and colder; in the afternoons, warmer, almost amber; in the evenings, the fog thickens into a low ceiling and the world becomes a grey wool, and the lamps of the inhabitants—small clay vessels of pine-knot fat, tended by hands you almost glimpse going by—begin to glow along the paths like a string of pale orange seeds.

## The People

If there are people. You see them rarely. A figure crosses a rooftop garden and is gone into a doorway before you can make out more than a long cloak of woven bark-fibre and a gait unhurried as the rain. A voice speaks from inside a hollow trunk, low and brief, and is answered by another voice from a different hollow. They seem to keep to the inner volumes of the buildings, as if the cold outside were merely an exterior to be insulated against rather than a climate to be inhabited.

You can tell they are there from the modifications to the wood. Doors have been cut, then re-grown over with bark in a controlled scar that the inhabitants open and close; a window has been made where a branch was bent sideways in youth and grafted back into the trunk, leaving a permanent oval of thinner growth; a stair has been notched into a root with small precise cuts that have healed over into dark callus ridges. The town has been lived in for a very long time, and the wood has had time to forgive the living.

## The Process

The town is not finished. It is still being added to. You can see the new construction: a roof being thatched with fresh-cut reeds, a wall being packed with moss, a doorframe being coaxed into the curve of a young trunk that has been bent, over years, into the right shape. The work is slow, and the workers move slowly, and the forest approves.

At the same time, decay is at work. An older hall has its roof sagging where a central trunk has died and begun to soften, and from the softening trunk a riot of fungi has erupted—bracket fungi in shelves, a sulphur-yellow bloom climbing the inner wall, a smell of damp sweetness when you pass close. Other buildings have already been given back to the forest: doorways grown over, thatch collapsed into the floor, the interior now a hollow in the green mass where only birds nest.

The forest is patient with both directions. It grows and it takes back, and the town is the membrane between the two—a thin and ongoing negotiation.

## The Cold

It is cold the way a stone cellar is cold, the way a spring is cold—not bitter, but present in everything. Your breath hangs in it as a faint fog. The moss is crisp underfoot and releases its moisture when stepped on, then slowly recovers. The trunks radiate their own faint chill, a thermal mass the forest has accumulated from the cold air above and is slowly releasing. To sit against one is to feel the cold come up through the bark and into your back in a long slow wave.

And yet the interiors of the dwellings, you sense, are not as cold as the outside. The thatch and the moss and the packed turf insulate; a small fire in a clay hearth keeps the air a few degrees warmer; bodies keep bodies warm. You are being kept out as much by the climate as by the architecture.

## Departure

You walk the path until the path ends, which it does by simply thinning into the root-mass until you cannot tell whether you are still on a path or have become part of the forest floor. The town recedes behind you in the green wool of the canopy, and you carry its sound—the drip, the groan, the small far voices—back out into the cold air of the open slope, where the wind finally finds you.

You do not see anyone follow you out. You are unsure whether you were welcome, or whether your presence was simply noted, the way the forest notes rain: as an event that passes through.
