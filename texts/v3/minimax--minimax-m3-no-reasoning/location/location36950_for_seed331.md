The basin is small enough to know by heart. From the rim — what remains of the rim — you could walk its full circumference in a quarter of an hour if the ferns didn't pull at your shins, if the moss weren't a foot deep and giving. Once this was a bowl carved into something harder. Now it is a bowl carved into something softer. The rim has been worried down to a low, broken ring of mounds. Rain has done most of the work, and rain has been doing it for a very long time. The stone is rounded. The stone is forgiving. If you press your hand against the western mound, you can feel it weeping — a slow, cool exudation that pools in your palm and smells of nothing. The air is so wet that everything is weeping. Everything is either shedding water or accepting it.

The light comes through in pieces. Late morning here, and the sun is almost directly overhead, but the canopy — if you can call it a canopy; it is more a scattered arrangement of broad-leaved things — breaks the light into coins and slow-moving geometries across the floor of the basin. The floor is the strange part. Pale. Almost white. Almost too flat. Where the ferns and the moss have not yet claimed it, the surface has a quality of having been *decided*, not worn down. It catches the wet light and holds it a moment longer than stone should. If you kneel and put your ear to it, you can hear — or imagine you hear — a faint, low cycling, like the place is breathing in geologic time. You cannot be sure. You keep kneeling there longer than you meant to.

There are structures, or what pass for structures. Scattered, the way teeth are scattered in an old mouth. A pillar, perhaps — no, the remains of a pillar, three meters tall and listing slightly, made of a material that looks like basalt and behaves like basalt until you notice it has no grain, no crystals, no mineral logic. It is warm to the touch in the cool morning, and it does not weather. The ferns climb it without rooting into it; they simply lean. A ring of low walls — knee-height, broken in three places — encircles something at the basin's center that I cannot quite describe. It is not a building. It is more like a *settlement of light*: a vertical column, very narrow, very faint, that begins at the white floor and ascends until it disappears into the brightness above. It does not flicker. It does not move. Birds avoid it. Insects do not. I have watched a beetle walk through it and come out the other side unchanged and unbothered, and I have watched the column persist through rain, through a small frog leaping through it, through the night. I have not touched it. There is a sense that touching it would be fine, and a stronger sense that touching it would not be.

The fauna is everywhere and the place is quiet, and these two facts coexist by some arrangement I have not solved. The basin is loud with life if you know how to listen, and almost silent if you do not. There are the small sounds: the wet ticking of beetles working through leaf litter, the slow uncurling of ferns audible only because the air is so still, the faint high keening of something in the canopy that may be a bird and may be an insect and may be neither. There are the larger presences: shapes that move between the trees with the deliberateness of things that are not being hunted and not hunting, only attending. I have counted eleven distinct morphologies in an hour of sitting still, and I am sure I have missed more. A pale quadruped with too many joints in its legs drinks from a pool at the eastern edge, and its tongue is the color of the column of light, and it does not startle when I shift my weight. A bright flat thing — wing? frill? — unfolds from a low branch and folds again, three meters away, and the air moves with it and nothing makes a sound. There is a constant low presence of something on the ground, an aggregate intelligence or just a density of small bodies, that I can feel through the soles of my feet when I stand still long enough.

The pools are not all water. Some are water. The pool near the western rim collects the weeping from the mound and holds it in a perfect oval, and dragonfly-things — they are not dragonflies; their wings beat wrong, their abdomens are too long — cross it in slow lanes. Other pools are not water. There is one, near the listing pillar, that looks like water and behaves like water except that it is convex. It stands in a low meniscus above the lip of its basin. Things drink from it without disturbing it. I do not know what it is made of. I have not tasted it.

The temperature is the kind of warm that does not require comment. It is the air you forget you are breathing. After an hour here, the wet and the heat agree on you, and your clothes darken at the small of your back and at the creases of your elbows, and you stop noticing. The basin maintains it. The basin is warmer than the rim. The basin is its own weather: a thin mist that rises after the sun is fully up, that thins by noon, that returns at the approach of the brief afternoon rain. The rain comes most days. The rain comes gently. The rain does not so much fall as accumulate — the wet air thickens until drops begin to form on every surface, and then the drops let go in unison, and the basin receives it like a courtesy.

Erosion has done its work here. Whatever violent act made this place — whether it was carved, collapsed, landed, or unfolded — is so far in the past that the violence is no longer legible. The violence has been smoothed into grammar. The mounds are not rubble. The pillars are not ruins. The white floor is not a scar. Everything that was once dramatic has been patiently edited by rain and root and time until it reads as landscape. Only the column of light refuses the edit. The column of light is too quiet to be dramatic, too persistent to be ignored, and too patient to be ancient in the way the rest of the basin is ancient. The basin is ancient because it has aged. The column is ancient because it is still doing whatever it was always doing, and what it is doing has no scale I can recognize.

I have been here three days. The creatures have begun to ignore me in a way that suggests I have always been here. A small thing with too many legs has walked across my notebook while I was writing in it, and the track it left in the wet page is still legible an hour later. A pale root — it may be a root; it may be something else — has begun to grow around my boot. I have not moved my boot. I will move it when I leave. The light is bright and the air is wet and the fauna is overwhelming and the place is mostly quiet and I am sitting in the middle of an old, gentle, impossible bowl, and the bowl is taking me in the way it has taken everything else: slowly, and without comment, and without end.
