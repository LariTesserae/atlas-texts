# The Upper Fabrication

The altitude here is honest. Ten thousand feet, maybe eleven — the air has a metallic taste, the kind that sits on the back of the tongue and reminds you that oxygen is something you can run out of. The sky is a flat, scoured pewter, not cloud-cover exactly but the permanent haze of a place where weather comes from the ground rather than falling from above. There is a horizon, somewhere, but it is interrupted by the things that stand between here and wherever else.

The terrain is raw. Not old. *Emerging.* It is a moonscape recently produced — a long, sloping shelf of shattered pale stone, like the inside of a quarry that nobody bothered to backfill. The rock is fractured into flat planes and sharp ridges, a geology that hasn't yet had the decency to weather into something softer. Every surface looks scraped, recent. Some of the stones are stacked in geometries that almost suggest intent but don't quite get there. There are no cairns, no ruins. Just arrangements.

At the center, or near it, the equipment is running.

The towers rise out of the broken rock like coniferous trees in a clear-cut: a grid of them, a forest of them, dark skeletal steel that hums at a frequency you feel in your molars before you hear it. Each tower is perhaps a hundred meters tall, latticed, with platforms at intervals and thick cables drooping between them in catenary curves. They are not decorated. They are not even finished — some of them have raw girders jutting at the top, untrimmed, threaded with cable runs that trail loose ends. The fabrication is incomplete. More towers stand skeletal in the middle distance, some no more than stanchions with crossbeams, like a child stopped halfway through building a model bridge.

The light is poor. Even at midday the haze diffuses everything, and the steel of the towers and the stone of the ground share the same gray, so the eye keeps losing the place where one ends and the other begins. The cables catch what little there is and turn it into thin silver lines. There is no glare. The whole place reads as a black-and-white photograph somebody left out in the damp.

The damp is everywhere. A wetness that doesn't fall so much as *lodge*. The stones sweat. Where the rock has been ground to a powder by the tower foundations, that powder has turned to a thin paste, and you can hear your own boots in it, a soft sucking. There are no rivers, no standing water you can find, but every surface is filmed, and in the deeper cuts between the outcrops there is a slow, oily gleam that might be water and might be machine-fluid. Steam issues from vents at the base of certain towers — not continuously, but in slow irregular breaths, as if the ground itself is respiring. The smell is ozone, stone-dust, and something organic underneath, like wet wool.

The fauna is, against all expectation, busy. Things move in the empty spaces between the towers. At first you take them for shadows thrown wrong, or for the slow motion of cables. Then you see them. They are low, dark, ungulate creatures of some sort — too far to make out clearly, but you can count maybe thirty or forty of them moving in a loose herd through the broken ground at the foot of the nearest tower. They are not alarmed. They are not tame. They simply work the ground the way any animal works a feeding ground, with the unhurried priority of something that has lived here longer than the towers have. Smaller things flicker at the edges: raptor silhouettes wheeling high, taking the long updrafts off the heated rock. Their cries come down thin and remote. The place is, in its strange way, alive.

The sound is mostly absence. The hum of the towers is felt more than heard, a pressure. The wind is the dominant thing — it comes in long, slow gusts, not howling but pressing, moving over the broken rock with a low moan that the geometry of the stone amplifies into something almost vocal. The animals do not call. The machines do not clatter. There is a distant, irregular *thock-thock* from somewhere far up the slope, perhaps a pile-driver, perhaps a rivet, perhaps nothing — it is too far to read. The sound carries a long way here because there is nothing to absorb it.

The cold is real. You keep your hands in your pockets. The moisture on the stones has begun to skim with frost at the shaded edges, a thin white that will be gone by noon. Your breath makes small pale ghosts. The animals, when you see them more clearly, are thick-coated, heavy-shouldered, something between a mountain goat and a thing you'd not want to meet in a doorway. They breathe out plumes of vapor as they work.

The towers, you realize after a while, are not all the same. The closest ones are most complete — running, humming, their cables taut, their platforms laden with equipment you cannot name but that glints with the suggestion of purpose. The ones further out are progressively more skeletal, until at the edge of vision they are only uprights, then only foundations — concrete pads poured into the rock, square, deliberate, each with bolts protruding, waiting. The grid is being built outward from somewhere you haven't been. There is a sense of a process that has been underway for a long time, and is not yet done, and will not be done for a long time.

The ground is doing something too. As you watch, a slow avalanche of stone-dust slips down the face of one of the larger outcrops, gathering at its base in a gray tongue. The frost advances and retreats. The steam pulses. The animals move. The towers hum. None of it is fast. None of it is aimed at you.

You stand in the wet, in the dim, in the cold, in the sound of your own breathing and the slow exhalations of a world that is being assembled around you, and the place does not care, and the place is not finished, and the place is not, despite everything that has been built here, *settled* — it is still doing the work of becoming itself, layer by indifferent layer, while the dark animals graze in its machinery and the wind moves through its incompleteness.

This is what an emerging industrial high place feels like: not the clean line of a finished factory, but the slow, partial, weather-taken erection of a system that will outlast anything that grazes at its feet, built in a place where the rock was only yesterday an inconvenience to itself.
