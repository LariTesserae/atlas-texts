# The Cold Plateau of Lith-Khor

The plateau is wider than weather should be. You stand at its center and the horizon takes a half-day to reach by foot, walking on foot. To the east it ends in a cliff three days long; to the west it slopes down across four days of broken stone into a country you cannot see from here, only suspect, the way you suspect another continent from a single gull. Above, the sky is the close pale blue of high places — thin enough that the sun, even at noon, looks like a coin pressed against paper. The air is cold the way spring water is cold: not freezing, but always a little below what your body wants. You breathe and the inside of your nose tingles. You breathe again and your breath makes a small ghost that doesn't rise, that just sits against the inside of your lungs.

There is a city here. There has been a city here for a very long time.

---

The first thing you notice, before you see it, is the wet.

Not rain — there has been no rain for two days. But the wet is everywhere. It beads on every upward-facing stone. It runs down the inside of walls. It gathers in the mortarless joints of the masonry and emerges as a thin black trickle lower down, staining the courses of stone beneath with a dark vertical streak that, over centuries, becomes a feature, a stripe, an element of the building's own pattern. When you put your palm flat against any wall, your hand comes away faintly damp. The dust that crunches underfoot is a damp dust. The air itself feels like it has just been wrung out. The plateau, for some reason of its own geography, condenses. Clouds come in off the lower country to the east at dusk, climb the cliff, lose their warmth, and weep themselves out across the stones; by morning the city is wearing a film of water like a skin of sweat, and the sun, when it comes up, takes an hour to dry the upper surfaces and never quite dries the lower ones, and the inside ones never dry at all.

So the city is wet. The city has always been wet. You can read the wet into the architecture. The builders — whoever they were, whoever the current residents are descendants of — knew the wet and built for it. Streets are not flat but slightly dished, like the bed of a stream, so that runoff gathers and moves downhill in thin sheets rather than puddling. The streets are paved with flat stones laid with deliberate gaps between them, and the gaps are the gutters. Doorways are recessed under lintels that project a hand's breadth, so that water running down the wall falls clear of the threshold. Roofs — where there are still roofs — are of overlapping stone slabs, set at angles that shed water outward, and the eaves drip onto worn grooves in the paving that carry the water on. Everything is angled, edged, sloped. The whole city is a slow machine for moving water from where it falls to where it is wanted.

Where it is wanted: the cisterns. The cisterns are everywhere, and they are the city's most constant architecture. They are sunken squares and rounds, lined with fitted stone, fed by every roof and street within their catchment. Some are open to the sky and brim with a dark mirror that is never still, because of the wind, because of the small leaves that fall into them, because of the rain. Others are roofed over with stone and entered through a low opening, and their water is black and very still and very cold. The water in the covered cisterns is what the people drink. The water in the open ones is what they wash in, what the animals drink, what the few plants grow from.

There are not many plants. The plateau does not love plants. It is too high, too cold, too thin of soil. What grows here is what has learned to be small. There is a tough grey grass that comes up between paving stones and in the abandoned courts; it survives because it has learned to grow when there is water and stop growing when there isn't. There are lichens on the north faces of walls — pale green, almost white, like a rash. There are mosses in the permanently damp gutters, in the bottoms of the disused cisterns, dark and tight and slow. And there is one shrub, low and gnarled, that grows only in the cracks of certain walls in certain orientations, and it has small hard leaves and no one is sure if it flowers because no one watches it at the right time. The people do not farm. The people do not garden. There is nothing here to farm or garden. What food there is comes from elsewhere — from the lower country, traded for; from the herders who bring their small flocks up in summer and down again before the cold closes in.

So the vegetation is sparse and the soil is mostly dust and the dust is mostly crushed stone and the stone is mostly the city's own past, broken down by the wet and the cold and the slow tread of feet and hooves over centuries into a fine powder that gets into everything, that coats the leaves of the shrub, that lines the cisterns, that blows across the plaza in a thin haze on windy days.

---

The city is dense in a particular way. It is not tall — the buildings are mostly two or three storeys, and the highest of them is four, and even the four-storey ones are squat, with thick walls and small windows set deep. But it is dense in the sense that it fills its ground completely. The plateau has edges, and inside those edges it is city, almost without gap. Street, building, street, building, plaza, building, street. The streets are narrow, often only wide enough for two people walking abreast, and they wind, they do not grid. They turn around cisterns. They go up short flights of steps to bridge a change in level. They duck under low lintels into covered passages that come out on the other side of a building into a different street. The buildings share walls. The buildings are built, in many cases, on top of older buildings whose roofs are now their floors, and those older buildings are built on top of still older ones, and so on, so that the city is stratified, layered, a palimpsest of itself. In some places you can see it: where a wall has fallen and the rubble been cleared, you find a floor, and beneath that floor another floor, and beneath that another, and at the bottom of three or four of them you find the dressed stones of the original street level, with the cobbles still in their pattern, polished by feet that stopped walking a thousand years ago.

Most of the city is empty. Most of the buildings are roofless. The people who live here — and there are not many, perhaps a thousand, perhaps fewer, distributed across the city in clusters — occupy a fraction of the available rooms, and they tend to cluster near the working cisterns, near the gate to the eastern cliff path, near the lower edge of the city where the herders come up. The rest is stone. Empty stone, going slowly back to the ground. Stone that is wet and growing its lichens and its mosses and dropping its flakes into the gutters. Stone that is silent.

The silence is the second thing you notice.

The city is quiet the way a forest is quiet when the wind has died. There is sound, but it is small sound. The drip of water from eaves. The soft grit of footfall on stone. The far-off bleat of a goat from somewhere out of sight. A dog barking once and then stopping. Voices, when there are voices, are low — not because anyone has decreed they should be low, but because the air is thin and lungs have to work harder and people have learned not to waste breath on volume. Conversations happen close. Friends stand shoulder to shoulder and murmur. Mothers sing to children with their mouths near the child's ear. The acoustic of the narrow streets is such that a shout will bounce three or four times before it dies, which discourages shouting, and a whisper carries farther than you would expect, which encourages whispering, and the result is that the city, even when full of life, has the texture of a library.

There is almost no fauna. You will see, if you are patient, the small brown birds that nest in the eaves and feed on insects in the cisterns. You will see a lizard on a sunny wall, a quick dark shape that vanishes into a crack. There are mice in the lower buildings. There are flies. There is, somewhere in the city, a dog or two — the herders bring them up. There are the goats themselves, and the occasional alpaca-like beast with a sad face and long neck, kept for wool and meat and the small amounts of manure that the people use to coax the lichens and the mosses into being slightly more abundant. There are no large predators. There are almost no large animals of any kind. The plateau is too high and too thin to support them, and the city is too built-up to admit them, and the people, who are not hunters — have never been hunters, so far as anyone can remember — have no interest in them anyway. The ecosystem here is mostly water and stone and lichen and the people, with the goats and the birds and the insects as a small punctuation.

---

The city is active, but its activity is slow activity. It is the activity of weather and water.

In the morning, the eastern cliff is in cloud. The cloud comes over the edge and rolls inward like a slow white tide, and as it comes it loses itself against the stones, dripping, condensing, and by midmorning the city is wearing fog. The fog sits in the streets. The fog fills the courtyards. The fog muffles sound further, until even the drip of water seems to come from somewhere else. Then, sometime around the sun's highest point, the fog begins to lift, and you can see it happen: a thinning at the edges first, the way frost thins; the upper storeys emerging first, then the middle, then the street level; and finally the cloud is gone, withdrawn back over the cliff, and the city is bright and wet and the stones steam faintly in the sun.

In the afternoon, the wind comes up. It is a west wind, mostly, and it is dry, and it has crossed the whole width of the plateau to reach the city, and it has picked up dust and dropped most of it and now it is just dry wind, and it dries the upper stones and lifts the dust from the streets and blows it into the eyes of anyone walking westward. The wind sings in the eaves. It moans in the empty windows. In the higher, roofless buildings, it makes a low hooting sound that people have learned not to listen to.

In the evening, the wind dies. The air cools quickly — the sun drops below the western rim of the plateau and the cold comes down from above like a lid. Condensation begins again. The stones start to bead. The cisterns, open to the sky, become still and begin to fill with the night's weeping. The people withdraw into their rooms, where small fires of dung and scrub burn in stone-lined hearths, and the smoke, finding no easy way out through the few small windows, sits in the upper part of the room and slowly filters through the stone-slab roofs and out into the night air. The city at night is dark and cold and the sound of it is mostly the sound of water, dripping, running, gathering, the city breathing in and out through its stones.

This is the cycle. It does not change. It has not changed, so far as anyone knows, since the city was built. The people are tuned to it the way a tide-pool is tuned to its tide. They wake when the fog begins to lift. They work in the middle hours when the air is warmest and the stones are drying. They stop when the cold comes back. They sleep when the cold is deepest, and they dream, perhaps, of warmer places, but they do not go to them. The plateau is theirs. The plateau has been theirs for as long as there has been a them.

---

Who built the city, no one now knows. The people who live in it have lived in it for as long as their memory goes, which is not very far — six, seven generations of grandmothers, maybe a little more. Before that, the stones. Before that, the wet. There are stories, of course. There are stories of people who came up from the lower country and found the city empty and lived in it. There are stories of people who built the city themselves, hand by hand, stone by stone, in some long-ago age when there were more people and more will. There are stories of people who are not people, who built the city and then went away, and whose bones, if they had bones, are in the lowest layer, beneath the lowest floor, in the dressed stones that no one has seen in many lifetimes. The stories do not agree. The stories are not asked to agree. The stories are told at the fires in the evening, and they are listened to, and they are not tested, and they are not disbelieved. They are held.

The architecture suggests a great labor and a long one. The fitted stones of the cisterns, the carefully dished streets, the precisely set lintels, the layered construction of the upper buildings on the lower ones — none of this is the work of a year or a generation. The architecture also suggests that the builders understood water very well, understood cold, understood the wet that is the city's constant weather. They built a city that worked with its place instead of against it. They built a city that could be left and still survive. The city has been left, in stretches, many times. The city has survived. The city will survive whoever is in it now, and whoever is in it after them.

---

The tech is almost nothing. There is no metal here — or very little; what metal exists is traded up from below, and it is precious, and it is used for small things: a needle, a knife, an ornament. There is no pottery of any quality; the pots are rough and handmade and they break and are remade. There is no wheel. There is no writing, or there is writing but it is done in the wall-markings that no one now reads, the scratched signs on certain stones in certain buildings, which may be names or counts or prayers and which no one alive can interpret. There is fire, and there is the manipulation of fire: dung-fueled, stone-contained, used for warmth and for cooking and for the small heat-rituals that are part of how people here keep themselves human. There is the manipulation of stone: stacking, dressing, fitting, but done with stone tools and bone tools and the slow patience of hands. There is the manipulation of fiber: wool, spun and woven into cloth, used for clothing and for the saddle-bags of the herders and for the ropes that hold the roof slabs in place against the wind. There is the manipulation of the goats: shearing, milking, leading, killing. There is the manipulation of water: catching, storing, carrying, sharing. That is the technology. That is the extent of it. There is nothing else, because nothing else is needed. The city works. The plateau provides what it provides. The wet and the cold and the stone and the people go on.

The people themselves are small and dark and not numerous, and they move through the city with the familiarity of long tenancy. They know which streets flood. They know which cisterns are drinkable. They know which buildings are still standing well enough to sleep in and which have started to lean. They know which of the old paths down the cliff are still passable and which have fallen away. They know the lichen that is good for what ails you and the lichen that is not. They are not rich and they are not poor. They have what they have, and they do not seem to want much more, and the absence of wanting is not a virtue but a fact, the way the absence of trees is a fact.

---

If you walk to the eastern edge of the city, which is the cliff, you find the gate. It is not a gate in the military sense; it is just an opening in the line of buildings where the city stops and the path begins, a gap in the masonry where the stone gives way to the rough track that zigzags down the cliff face. From the gate you can look down and see, far below, a country that is green and warm and full of things you have forgotten about: forests, rivers, low hills, distant smoke from distant fires. The people who come up from that country are the herders, in summer, and the traders, less often. The people who go down are few: young people, sometimes, who feel the pull of the warmer air and go to find what is down there, and who mostly do not come back. The city lets them go. The city does not mind. The city is patient. The city has time.

From the gate, also, you can watch the weather being made. The clouds come in off the lower country and rise up the cliff face in a slow white pour, and you can stand at the very edge and feel the first wet of them on your face, and you can see them come over the lip of the cliff and spread out across the plateau, and you can see the city ahead of you beginning to soften into the fog, its upper storeys first, then its middles, then its streets, until you are standing in the middle of a white silence and the city is gone and only the wet on your face and the cold in your lungs and the slow drip of water from your own hair tells you that there is anything here at all.

Then, as the morning goes on, the fog will lift. The city will come back, storey by storey. The stones will emerge from the white, dripping. The cisterns will reappear, brim-full of the night's take. The people will emerge from their rooms and begin their slow day. The drip will go on dripping. The wind will go on waiting. The cold will go on being cold. The plateau will go on being the plateau, vast and wet and old and patient and lit, in its particular way, by a sun that is always a little further away than you would like.

You will go on, too. You will walk back into the city, along the dished streets, past the leaning buildings, over the polished cobbles of a road that was a road before your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother was born. The wet will be on your hands and in your boots and in the wool of your coat. The silence will be around you like another kind of weather. The city will not notice you. The city does not need to notice you. You are a part of its going on. That is enough. That is what the city is for.
