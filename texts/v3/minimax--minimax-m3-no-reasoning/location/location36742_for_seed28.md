# The Singing Hollow

A bowl in the earth, perhaps a quarter-league across. The first thing is the sound.

It begins before the eyes adjust. A roar. Not the roar of water or wind, but something more intentional, more built — a sound with architecture in it. Coming up through the ground. Coming down from the sky. It lives in the teeth and the fingernails and the soft hollows behind the eyes. It is the sound of a place that has been ringing for a very long time and has forgotten how to stop.

## The Walls

The hollow is ringed by towers. Not tall — three, four stories at most — but they press together shoulder to shoulder along the inner rim like a congregation leaning inward. Their walls are pale stone, or something like stone; the color of salt, of old bone, of fungus left too long in lamplight. They are deeply, extravagantly carved. Every surface breathes with relief: not images, exactly, but patterns that move when the eye lingers on them, as though the figures are crawling slowly into the stone or out of it. The carvings have no gods in them, no kings, no battles. They show objects — a bowl, a key, a folded cloth, an open mouth, a closed mouth, a hand turned palm-up — repeated and repeated and repeated, smaller each time, spiraling inward, never quite reaching a center.

The architecture is old, but the oldness is strange. Some towers have sunk to their knees, the carved walls bulging outward as though the stone has softened. Others stand with the crispness of things just completed. The builders — whoever they were — do not appear to have been people. The doorways are wrong. They are too narrow at the top, too wide at the bottom, and they open not onto darkness but onto *noise*, a louder version of the sound that fills the hollow, as though every door is a mouth singing.

There is no metal in evidence. No iron strapping, no bronze hinges. The doors — and they are doors, not doorways only, because many are closed — are made of the same pale stone as the walls, hung on wooden pins that should have rotted centuries ago and have not. Whoever the carvers were, they did not need hinges. The doors pivot on whatever they please.

## The Floor

The floor of the hollow is a garden. This is the part that does not belong.

In a place so dry that the air tastes of powder and old paper, where no rain has fallen in living memory (no one's living memory, anyway), there is a jungle in a bowl. Vines as thick as thighs climb the lower stories of the towers and disappear into the carved patterns, threading through the mouths and the open hands. The leaves are dark and waxy and shaped like nothing in particular; they are the color of dried blood when the light catches them, of black water when it does not. They do not rustle. The sound of the hollow is too loud for rustling. But they move — a slow, tidal motion, all together, leaning east, leaning west, like seaweed in a current that has nothing to do with wind.

There are flowers. They are too red. They grow from cracks in the pale stone and they are the size of a man's torso and they are open. They are always open. Something inside them is bright, the same brightness as the light above, and the brightness moves.

There is fruit. Knobs of pale yellow, heavy on low shrubs. They have a smell that is almost water — the idea of water, a memory of water, a rumor. No one eats them.

There are no paths. The vegetation is not arranged. It simply is, in the way of weeds, in the way of moss, with the patience of things that expect no opposition. And yet, in a few places, the growth has been cleared back — by hand, with wooden tools, the cuts still fresh-pale against the dark bark — and there are footfalls worn into the floor. Not many footfalls. Enough to suggest a people who live here and walk among the vines as though the vines are the weather.

## The Light

The light is wrong. It does not come from above. There is no sun in this story, no visible source. The brightness pours in horizontally, in sheets, from somewhere beyond the western wall — a light without a lamp, white and sour, the light of a place being interrogated. It falls through gaps in the towers, through windows that have no glass, through the carved mouths, and it strikes the flowers and the leaves and the pale stone and makes them all the same color, the same bleached-bone color, for an instant, before the angle changes and the colors come back.

The light is always at the same angle. It does not move.

## The Sound

Now. The sound.

It is not one sound. It is many. It is a sound like a city heard from a great height: voices, hammering, wheels, the breathing of a thousand sleeping things. It is also a sound like an organ, very low, very old, played by hands the size of rooms. It is a sound like the sea in a seashell, if the sea were made of voices. It comes from everywhere and from a single direction, which is the same direction, which is the center of the hollow.

In the center of the hollow there is a pit.

The pit is round. The pit is about the width of a house. The pit is full of a white noise that is not noise. It is full of a humming. It is full of a kind of attention. Things that are placed at the edge of the pit do not fall in; they are refused, gently, by a force that has no name in any of the local languages. Pebbles, dropped, drift to the lip and then lift, slowly, and set themselves down on the rim, and the humming gets a little louder, as if in thanks.

No one has gone in. No one can say what would happen if anyone did. The carvers, whoever they were, knew about the pit. Some of the deepest carvings, the ones that spiral smallest, are of the pit itself: a circle, a circle, a circle, a circle.

## The People

There are people. Few. They live in the towers. They are small, brown-skinned, dressed in layers of pale cloth that they weave from the fibers of the pale yellow fruit, though no one eats the fruit, and no one will say where the weaving was first learned. They carry wooden staves. They do not speak. This is not because they have no language. It is because the sound is too loud, or because speaking has been given up, or because speaking is no longer necessary. They communicate with a kind of touching — palms together, fingers interlaced, small precise movements that the sound cannot drown out. Sometimes they touch the carvings. They touch the vines. They touch each other's faces, with a tenderness that has something liturgical in it.

They do not look at the pit.

They do not look at the pit.

They never look at the pit. This is the rule. There is only one rule, and they keep it absolutely. Children are taught it before they are taught to walk. Visitors — if there are visitors — are taught it within minutes of arriving, with a quickness and seriousness that admits no exception. Whatever the pit is, whatever the sound is, it does not require looking. It only requires not being looked at. It is content. The hollow is content. The towers are content. The vines grow. The flowers open. The fruit ripens and falls and is gathered and woven into cloth. The carvers, long gone, are content. Everyone is content, in a way that is very close to grief.

## The Air

The air is warm and very dry. The warmth is a thing with weight; it sits on the shoulders. The dryness is the dryness of a place that has never known water — no humidity, no dew, no fog. The skin cracks within hours of arrival. The eyes ache. The lips split. But the air is also sweet, in a way that waterless air should not be; sweet with the smell of the fruit, sweet with the smell of the stone, sweet with something underneath that is not a smell at all but a taste, a taste like the inside of a bell. The air is loud. The air is full of a low pressure, a kind of leaning-in, as though the hollow is a held breath.

## The Fauna

There are animals, but they are not animals. There are small pale shapes that move through the vines — the size of a cat, the shape of nothing in particular, the movement of something that has learned to move without sound and is doing it on purpose, to be polite. They have no visible eyes. They have no visible mouths. When the people touch them, they purr, a purr that is also in the key of the pit-humming, and the people smile, and the purring gets a little louder, and for a moment, briefly, the great sound of the hollow seems almost to quiet, as if a door has been left open somewhere in a far room.

There are birds. Three of them, perhaps, or four. They are bright red. They are silent. They do not fly. They sit in the carved mouths of the western towers and watch the pit with one eye each. They are the only things in the hollow that look at the pit. The people pretend not to notice them. The people pretend very hard.

## The Feel of the Place

To stand in the hollow is to feel watched, but not by anyone present. To feel expected, but not by anyone arriving. To feel that the place is old, much older than the towers, much older than the carvers, that the pit and the humming were here first, and everything else — the towers, the vines, the flowers, the people, the birds — grew up around the sound the way coral grows around a sunken bell.

The light is bright. The sound is deafening. The garden is lush. The water is absent. The buildings are dense. The population is sparse. The feeling is of a place that has been *going on* for a very long time and intends to go on for a very long time more, and has, in its long patient going-on, forgotten to stop, and does not particularly mind.

The people weave. The birds watch. The pit hums. The flowers open. The fruit ripens. The carvings deepen by a hair's breadth each century, the figures crawling further into the stone, the patterns tightening, the mouths opening wider, the hands reaching further.

Whatever the hollow is for, it is not finished.
