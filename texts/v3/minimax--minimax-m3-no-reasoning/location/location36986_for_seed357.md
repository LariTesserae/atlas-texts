# The Hanging Caldrons of Vasthuun

---

The altitude doesn't feel like altitude. That's the first wrongness, and it's the one your body accepts before your mind catches up. You're standing at roughly nine thousand feet — you can tell from the way the light bends at the horizon, from the bruised indigo of the upper sky, from the thin electric sting in each breath — but the air down here, in the basin, is warm. Wet-warm. Breath-warm. The kind of warm that is also a sound: a perpetual low hiss, as though the sky is exhaling through a million small mouths.

## The Roof

Look up.

The roof is roots. There is no sky overhead, only the underside of a vast horizontal lattice of woody fibers, pale tan and slick, weaving in and out of itself so densely that the sun filters through as a thousand scattered specs, not one sky. The lattice sags in places, balloons in others. It is perhaps forty feet above you at its lowest, two hundred at its peaks, and it stretches as far as you can see in every direction, an inverted country. Water condenses on every surface up there — every root-hair, every node — and falls in a constant fine rain that doesn't feel like rain. It feels like standing inside a lung.

From this lattice, on stems as thick as cathedral pillars, the **caldrons** hang.

## The Caldrons

They are not built. The word is wrong. They are *grown*. Each one is a sphere of woven green — vines and stems and broad fleshy leaves layered over a hard inner shell of compressed fiber — and each is between thirty and two hundred feet across. They hang at varying heights. Some dip their bottoms into the standing water below; some hover twenty feet above it; some rest on the knobby tops of older, dead caldrons that have petrified into woody hills. The locals — there are no locals, but if there were — would name them by their states.

The **green ones** pulse. You can see it. A slow tidal heave, as though something breathes inside them. The skin of living leaves ripples outward from some central point, then subsides. Liquid sloshes audibly within. They smell, up close, of cinnamon and rot and something green and metallic that has no name.

The **split ones** are giving birth. Great vertical fissures run down their sides, and through these fissures pour seedlings — pale, fist-sized bulbs trailing long pale roots that whip in the air as they descend. Some land in the water and float, slowly rotating, trailing root-hair like beards. Some catch on the rim of a neighboring caldron and begin, immediately, to burrow in. The sound they make entering living wood is a wet whisper, like a fruit being bitten into.

The **brown ones** are dead and burning. From a dozen of them, columns of smoke rise straight up into the root-lattice and are absorbed. Sometimes a flame leans out of a fissure and licks the air. Sometimes a great sheet of dead vine drops — a hundred yards of withered brown length crashing into the water, into another caldron, into you, if you are not careful. The brown ones smell of woodsmoke and wet ash and a sweetness like burned honey.

The **white ones** are the oldest and the strangest. Their outer leaves have gone bone-pale and translucent, and through them you can see the inner architecture: a perfect sphere of darker fiber, and within that, suspended in clear liquid, a smaller green sphere, and within that, suspended in darker liquid, a still smaller sphere — nested, like a Russian doll, perhaps ten layers deep, each one turning at a slightly different speed. The white ones make no sound at all. They are the silent ones. They are the most watched.

## The Water

The basin is half-filled. The water — it is not quite water; it is too warm, too thick, and it carries a faint suspended green that catches the light and turns it slightly gold — reaches to your shins in the shallows and to your neck in the deeps. It is not unpleasant. It smells of nothing. It is *moving*, very slowly, in a great slow gyre. Once a minute a swell passes through it from some distant caldron-collapse, lifting you half an inch and setting you down.

Things grow out of the water. Everywhere. **Spires** of reed-like plants, twenty, thirty, fifty feet tall, pale green, perfectly vertical, slightly luminous at their tips — they crowd the water so densely in places you cannot pass. Between them, broad rafts of **floating leaves** the size of tables, each one bearing at its center a single upturned cup-flower that drips a slow amber syrup into the basin. **Tendrils** move beneath the surface — you can see them, slow and purposeful, dark against the gold — and occasionally one curls up over a floating leaf and deposits, in the cup-flower, a small green egg. The flower closes around it. You hear the muffled wet crunch of acceptance.

## The Sound

It is loud. Not loudly. *Loudness as mass*. A constant sound-pressure that comes from everywhere.

The hissing of the falling water from the root-lattice above.

The slow wet *thock-thock-thock* of caldrons, somewhere, always, pulsing — a tidal heartbeat, out of sync with itself, a dozen cadences layered.

The creak and groan of the root-lattice itself as it sways and resways in a wind you cannot feel down here.

The whisper of the splitting caldrons, of the burrowing seedlings, of the floating eggs being turned.

The distant roar of the burning brown ones, a sound like a far-off waterfall, audible at all times, occasionally punctuated by a long woody *crack* as a major limb gives.

A very high, sub-audible hum from the white nested ones, felt more in the jaw than the ear.

And beneath it all — this is the part that makes the place feel inhabited even though it is not — a vast slow rhythmic *shhhhhhhhhhhh*, as though the whole basin were being rocked. It comes from the gyre. It comes from the caldrons. It comes from the roots. It comes from nowhere and from the place itself. It is the sound of a place thinking.

## The Light

The light is gold and green and moves. Because the root-lattice sways and the water reflects and the leaves on the caldrons turn, the light is never still. It dapples. It pulses. It travels across your skin in slow wandering shapes, and wherever it touches you it feels fractionally warmer. There are no shadows, only deeper lights. You cannot tell where any single beam comes from. The light feels, somehow, *grown* — as though it were being exhaled by the place rather than received from a sun you have forgotten.

## The Air

The air is full. Full of water vapor, warm with the heat of a thousand small fermentations, sweet with the syrup of the cup-flowers, acrid with smoke from the burning ones, and — under all of it — a deep vegetable musk, the smell of green wood being slowly converted into more wood. It coats the back of your throat. After an hour you stop noticing; after two hours you realize you have stopped noticing because the smell is now *you*.

There are no animals. There are no creatures. There is the slow turning of nested spheres in the white caldrons, and you do not look at them too closely, and you do not ask what they are. There is the tendril-deposited egg in the cup-flower, and you do not ask what hatches. The place is utterly full of doing and utterly empty of watchers, and you understand, after a time, that the watching is the part you have brought with you, and that it does not belong here.

## The Feeling

You are standing in a greenhouse the size of a valley, at altitude, in heat, in wet, in a light that is being made for you by the place itself. You are inside a *thing*. The thing is growing. The thing is tearing itself down and building itself up at the same moment. The thing has no name and does not need one, and has been doing this for a very long time, and will do it after you.

The water is warm around your shins. A cup-flower sways past on a raft of leaves and drips amber syrup onto your wrist. It is sweet. It is faintly alive. A seedling — pale, bearded, the size of your fist — drifts slowly past you, rotating, descending, looking for a wall to burrow into.

Above you, a brown caldron gives a long woody crack, and a sheet of withered vine begins its long slow fall.

The light moves.

The water moves.

The sound does not stop.

You stand in the basin, and you are very small, and the place is very alive, and it does not care about you, and you love it for not caring, and you would like to leave, and you would like to stay, and you cannot do either, and the golden light moves across your face, and a caldron somewhere splits, and a new green thing begins.
