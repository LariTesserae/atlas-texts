The air inside the bowl is the color of old brass. It tastes of sulfur and iron filings, of something between a struck match and the belly of a battery. You do not arrive here so much as descend into it — through a fissure in a ridge of black obsidian, down a chute of fractured glass where every handhold cuts, and then the walls fall away and you are standing at the lip of something that is not a cave and not a crater but the seam between the two.

It is small. You could walk its perimeter in twenty minutes if walking were possible — but the floor is a single tilted slab of andesite, fourteen meters across, glowing dull cherry along its fractures, and the heat comes up through the soles of your boots in slow waves that blur your vision.

The vents are everywhere. They perforate the slab in such density that there is almost no stone left between them — each one a thumb-wide hole rimmed with yellow sulfur crystals and crowned with a feather of pale vapor. The vapor doesn't rise so much as shiver. There is no draft. The air is already saturated with itself.

And around each vent, packed so close they touch, are the things.

They are like nothing so much as the moving parts of a clock seen from very close. Each one is the size of a thumb joint — a hard segmented oval, dark amber to bronze, with legs folded tight against the body when still and extended in a slow rotary motion when feeding. Their carapaces are faceted, faintly iridescent in the dim red light, and where two of them meet they make a sound like a fingernail drawn across the teeth of a fine comb.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They coat every square inch of stone between the vents, layered two or three deep, and their movement is a single slow tide: a constant shifting, parting, and re-closing, as the creatures at the outer edge of a cluster crawl over the backs of the creatures in the middle and the creatures at the center hold their position at the vent mouth, their undersides pressed flat against the stone, feeding.

What they feed on is the gas itself. If you kneel and watch a single vent long enough you can see it: a creature settles, its belly-segments splay, a soft brush of golden cilia emerges and fans into the vapor. The cilia are wet. Not with water — with something thicker, something that beads and trembles on the bristles like mercury. It catches the sulfur compounds, the hydrogen sulfide, the rising heat. The creature's body processes them. You can hear it process them: a faint wet grinding, the chitin of its mandibles working against itself in slow rotations, like a millstone turning at the pace of grass growing.

There are different ones. You begin to notice, after you have stood still long enough to be climbed.

The smallest, near the eastern lip of the slab, are nearly black and almost motionless — their grinding so slow it is more vibration than sound. They are the oldest. They remember, in their body's chemistry, conditions that no longer obtain. As the pocket has grown hotter they have retreated to the margins, where the air is coolest, and they feed on the trickle of outside atmosphere that enters through the fissures above — a thin, cold, oxygen-poor breath that is to them a kind of manna. They are nearly dead. They are not quite dead.

The mid-sized ones, bronze and amber, are the general mass. They are the colonizers, the ones that swarm to any new vent within minutes of its opening. They are the loud ones. Their grinding is the principal sound of the pocket — a steady mid-frequency drone that you feel in your back teeth when you stand in the middle of the slab. They are the teeth of the place.

The largest, near the western tilt, are dark red, almost the color of the heated stone itself. They are slow — one rotation of the mandibles per several seconds — and their sound is a low rumble that you feel in the soles of your feet. They process something different. The vents in that part of the slab vent heavier gases, more mineral-laden, and these creatures have evolved to extract from them a denser food. Their bodies are heavier. Their carapaces thicker. When you disturb one it does not flee. It simply closes its ventral slit and sits, and after a moment you understand that you are the one who has moved on.

None of them have any feature you would call a face. They have no eyes, no antennae, no mouth in any direction you can recognize. Their entire sensorium is chemical and thermal and tactile. They know where they are by the gradient of the air. They know what they are doing by the concentration of the gas. They know you are there only as a warm obstruction that displaces their preferred atmosphere. After a moment they treat you as part of the stone.

There is no death here that you can see. A creature that stops grinding — whether from cold, from displacement, from some imbalance in its internal chemistry — does not decay. It dries. Its cilia retract. Its carapace darkens to a deep bronze, then to black, and over the course of days it is ground down by the creatures around it into the same pale gray paste that the live ones excrete, and that paste is fed upon, and the slab is slowly painted with the dust of its own inhabitants. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves the system. Even the gases that escape the vents are reabsorbed by the chemistry of the walls and returned to the slab as fresh mineral deposits, and the slab releases them again, and the creatures process them again. It is a perfect loop, and it has been running since the pocket was a crack in cooling magma, and it will run until the heat beneath the slab is finally exhausted and the vents seal for the last time and the creatures slow, and slow, and stop.

To stand at the lip is to be inside an ear. The sound comes from everywhere at once and has no source you can point to. It is the vents — high, thin, whistling where the gas escapes through narrow throats, lower and rougher where it pushes through wide cracks. It is the creatures — the constant mid-frequency grind, ten thousand tiny mills running slightly out of phase with each other, so that the overall sound has a slow shifty texture like a fabric rubbed between the fingers. It is the rock — the small reports of thermal fracture, the deeper groans as the slab adjusts, the occasional sharp tink of falling glass. It is the heat itself, or so it seems: a low hiss at the edge of hearing that may be the air against your skin or may be the stone radiating against the air.

It is loud. After ten minutes your ears stop trying to parse it and it becomes a kind of weather. After twenty you cannot hear your own voice without shouting. After an hour you have the sense that the sound is the place and you are a temporary interruption of it.

The creatures do not see you. When you step among them they scatter from your boot in a brief flare of motion and then close behind you, and you can feel their legs against your ankles like the teeth of a fine comb drawn upward. They do not bite. They have nothing to bite with. Their only weapons are patience and density.

If you stand still long enough, they will climb you. They find the seams of your clothing, the folds behind your knees, the warm dark place under your collar, and they settle there and begin their slow grinding against the fabric. It is not uncomfortable. It is almost warm. After a time you stop noticing it except as a faint vibration against your skin, a purr you can feel but not hear above the roar.

The vents themselves are not stable. They open and close. A new one will push through the andesite overnight — you will hear it as a sharp retort, like a bottle uncorking, and the creatures nearest the spot will scatter in a small radial wave and then begin, immediately, to colonize. An old one will seal itself, its rim crusting over with iron sulfide, and the creatures that were clustered there will disperse into the general mass with a sound like a handful of coins poured onto a stone floor.

This is how the surface stays alive: the vents are always moving, and the creatures are always following. Nothing stays put. The pattern on the stone — the orange and yellow stains, the black crusts, the dull red heat-fractures — shifts over weeks. Over a season, the whole field will have rewritten itself.

The slab is not level. It tilts slightly toward the western wall, and there the vents are larger and more numerous, and the creatures there are larger too, and the sound there is deeper. The smallest creatures live near the eastern lip, where the fissures let in a breath of outside air and the temperature drops just enough to slow their metabolism to a near-halt. They are nearly black. They move only when the slab shudders from some deeper tremor.

And there is always a tremor. The whole pocket sits on top of something that is not yet finished making itself. Down below the slab — you can tell because the heat is not uniform, because certain patches of stone are too hot to kneel on, because every few minutes a subsonic throb passes through the rock and the creatures pause for a half-second in their grinding — there is magma, or the remnant of magma, or the next magma, working its way up.

The slab is new. You can see it in the sharpness of its edges, in the way the obsidian walls of the fissure above still hold the conchoidal fracture of a recent break. The pocket is being born. It is not yet the size it will become — the walls still grind against each other when the heat expands them, shedding fresh glass onto the floor in pale tinkling falls, and the air is still finding its chemistry, still sorting out what it will be.

In a hundred years it may be cooler. The vents may consolidate. The creatures may evolve or die or change their chemistry. The slab may crack and settle. For now it is a single held moment of formation, dense and loud and close, and everything in it is running hot.

There is no water. Anywhere. There has perhaps never been water. The chemistry here does not need it. The creatures' bodies are built around sulfides and chlorides and the dense silicated breath of the deep stone, and what they excrete is not waste but a kind of paste — pale gray, fine-grained, slightly warm — that they spread behind them as they crawl, and that dries within seconds into a thin crust that the next tide of creatures will grind to powder and feed on again. The slab is slowly being painted with the digested residue of itself. The pocket is, in a small and literal sense, eating itself.

There is no plant. There is no green, no chlorophyll, no photosynthesis. The energy comes from below, not from above — from the redox of the vent gases, from the chemical disequilibrium between the hot interior of the slab and the cooler chemistry of the air. The creatures are not animals in the sense you mean. They are closer to machines, or to organs of a single body. A lung. A grinding stomach spread across the rock.

The light is incidental. The slab glows a deep dull red along its fractures, and the vents give off a pale sulfurous yellow that hangs in the still air like dust. The creatures' carapaces catch both and return a faint bronze shimmer. There is no shadow. The light is so diffuse and omnidirectional that nothing casts one. Things simply appear darker or lighter against the general dim glow, and your eye, after a while, stops trying to organize the scene by light and starts organizing it by heat — you see the hot patches as bright, the cool patches as dim, the vents as white-yellow stars in a field of dull red.

You cannot tell the time. The pocket has no day, no season, no weather. The temperature varies — a degree here, a degree there — but always within the band where the creatures can live. If the slab shudders and a new fissure opens above, a breath of cooler air may descend, and the creatures nearest the lip will slow their grinding to a third of its speed, and the sound will drop in pitch for a moment, and then resume. That is the closest thing to a morning.

Outside the pocket, somewhere above, the world is cold and dry and quiet. You came down through a fissure in a ridge of black glass, and if you climbed back up through it you would emerge onto a flank of loose scoria and pale pumice, under a thin high sky the color of tarnished silver, and the wind would cut you immediately, and you would look back down and see only the fissure — a dark line in the dark rock, breathing a thin yellow vapor — and you would not be able to tell, from up there, that anything was inside it.

The pocket is hidden the way a vein is hidden, or a thought. It is not secret. It is simply not in scale with what surrounds it. The mountain is older and larger and colder by many orders, and the pocket is a single bright organ inside its long cooling body, and the mountain does not know.

Inside, you cannot think about the mountain. There is no room in the mind for it. The pocket takes all of you — the heat against your skin, the noise against your ears, the smell of iron and sulfur against the back of your throat, the faint bronze shimmer of the creatures against the dull red glow of the stone. The pocket is at the threshold of bearable for a soft and watery body. You sweat and the sweat evaporates before it can run. Your eyes water and the water is gone in a breath. You are being dried out by the air as you stand there. You are being slowly turned, by chemistry, into something the pocket could use.

This is not a hostile place. It is simply a place that was not built for you. It was built — if it was built — for itself, for its own slow churning, for the ten thousand grinding mills on its tilted slab and the dull red heat that feeds them. You are a visitor to an organ. You can stand at its edge and listen to it work, and after a while you will have to leave, because your lungs will not tolerate the air for more than an hour, or your skin will not tolerate the heat for more than two, and when you climb back up through the fissure and out onto the cold flank of the mountain, the wind will feel like a kindness and the thin silver sky will feel like a mercy and you will be unable to explain, to anyone you try to tell, what it was like down there.
