# The Pit

The place is a hole in the ground. That is the first thing, and you might as well begin there, because for a long time — a long time measured in decades and truck-counts and the slow thickening of root mats — there is nothing else to compare it to. A hole dug into the side of a cold hill country, into granite and sulfide-stained schist, then kept open by the same engines that made it. The rim stands two hundred meters above the lowest bench. Walking from one side to the other, if you were fool enough to walk it, would take most of a day. The benches step down in regular terraces, each one cut sharp as a fresh bruise into the country rock — no rounding, no softening, just the geometry of a bucket teeth and the patience of enough explosives. Rain has not yet had time to do its work. The edges are still brittle, the dust still pale.

It is cold here even in July. The walls are too steep and too high to hold the day's warmth; by mid-afternoon the air at the lowest bench is colder than the air on the rim was at dawn. In winter the cold is its own geography. It pools. It sits on the benches in flat blue layers. Frost feathers spread across the diesel-staining of the haul roads and stay there, because the sun never reaches the floor of the pit at this latitude, only lays a brief, indifferent stripe across the eastern wall before retreating.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not rain often — three, four weeks can pass with nothing but a grey scrim of cloud and the steady industrial exhalation of vapor off the mill ponds — but when it rains, the pit remembers. The benches hold it. Each terrace lip has a small ditch running along its toe, sometimes choked with sedge, sometimes a clear rivulet a hand's width across, and the water moves down them in threads and sheets until it finds the sumps. The sumps are vast shallow rectangles of green-brown water, and from each one a pump pulls a column of water up over the rim and out into the tailings. Between rains the moisture stays. It condenses on the back of every steel plate. It beads on the cables of the suspension bridge that spans the upper pit. It rises as a fine mist from the warm runoff channels where the crushers dump their slurry, and this mist drifts and settles, and everything beneath it — every I-beam, every bolt head, every inch of conveyor belting — develops a thin wet skin that never quite dries.

The plants have come.

This is the strange thing, the thing that does not announce itself but accumulates. The vegetation is total in the sense that there is no exposed mineral surface that has not, given time, been claimed. The benches are not bare benches. They are terraces of moss so thick your boot sinks into it and comes up black to the ankle, and out of the moss rise waist-high thickets of willow and alder, and out of the thickets rise the slender pale verticals of birch and rowan, leaning this way and that in the constant downdraft from the pit walls, and out of the verticals climb the vines — honeysuckle, wild grape, in places a feral clematis that has run a hundred meters up a conveyor support and burst into white flower at the top. The pit is a bowl and the bowl is full of plants. The plants have made the geometry their own. They have softened the bench edges into curves. They have buried the older haul roads under a felt of grass and herb. Where a truck once ran, a moss track runs now, and where the moss track was first laid down ten years ago, a willow stand is now closing over it, and the willows are themselves being climbed by the clematis, and the clematis is being eaten, slowly, by something that might be a fungus or might be just the pit's own patient rot.

But the structures remain. They are scattered. A primary crusher stands on a mid-level bench, a squat concrete thing with a steel mantle around it, and a feeder conveyor climbs out of its top and runs up the wall on trestles that are themselves wrapped in creeper. A switch house, single-story, red-painted, sits on a spur road that has not seen a train in eleven years; its roof has a birch growing out of it. A pump station perches on the lowest sump, a low metal building, its intake grates trailing green weed in the moving water. There is a bridge — there is always a bridge — cable-stayed, the towers a hundred and twenty meters tall, the deck a thin ribbon across the upper pit, and the cables are strung with vines that have been carried up there by birds, presumably, and now hang in long green tassels in the still air of the cable plane. There is a coarse ore bin at the rim, and a fine ore bin beside it, and a stack of tailings pipeline that runs along the south wall, and none of these things are hidden, exactly. They stand among the plants. The plants do not hide them. They embrace them.

The sound is the first thing anyone who works here learns, and the last thing they remember. It does not stop. The pit has a voice and the voice is a composite roar built up out of dozens of contributions, none of them loud alone but all of them together a weight on the chest. There is the crusher — a low rhythmic pound, like a giant heart, every two seconds, never varying, and the sound comes back off the far wall and comes back off the near wall and meets itself in the middle and thickens. There is the conveyor belt, a long hiss, like wind in dry grass but unbroken and at a fixed pitch, and a deeper rumble beneath it where the idlers turn. There is the mill, which is mostly out of sight over on the rim but whose bass note travels down into the pit and lies in it like weather. There is the haul truck on a far ramp, climbing under load, the engine note rising in pitch as it passes — and then a long time later, descending empty, the engine note falling. There is the pump at the sump, which on most days you cannot hear at all but on certain days, when the air is cold and still and the humidity is right, you can hear it as a kind of pulse beneath everything else, a pulse and a sigh. There is the wind, sometimes, when it gets into the pit, and the wind in a pit is its own phenomenon — it does not blow across, it falls down, in sudden cold sheets, off the rim and into the bowl. When the wind falls, the sound of the machines fills the space it leaves, and the pit is louder again.

You get used to it. That is the wrong way to put it. You do not get used to it. You learn to hear it differently, the way you learn to hear your own breathing when you stop trying to listen for it. After a while the roar is the silence. When the pit does stop — for a shift change, for a maintenance window, once a year for a week in late August when the whole operation idles — the absence is the loudest thing you have ever heard. People who have worked here for twenty years say they cannot sleep during the shutdown. The quiet keeps them up.

The light is dim. This is not the soft dim of a forest, where the dimness is a green mosaic under a canopy. This is the heavy, mineral dim of a deep excavation under cloud. The sky above is a small pale rectangle, sometimes grey, sometimes a thin winter blue, and the sun, when it is there, reaches only the upper benches. The lower benches work in their own light, a kind of reflected twilight, the colour of tin, in which the mosses glow faintly green and the steel of the conveyors is dark and the water in the sumps is black and still. At the very bottom, where the sump is and where the haul trucks come down to be loaded, you can work all day and never see the sun. The miners wear headlamps even at midday, and the beams sweep across the rock and the willow thickets in long arcs that catch the moisture in the air and turn it briefly gold.

There is not much fauna. A raven or two, nesting on the bridge towers. A fox that has learned to time its visits to coincide with the blasting, when the small things it eats are flushed out of the wall. A moose, once, which came down the haul ramp in October and was photographed standing in a sump with water to its belly, looking up at the crusher as if waiting for an explanation. Trout in the sumps, stocked illegally years ago and now reproducing somehow in the cold water, rising in the evenings to insects that have themselves come from nowhere. The moths at the mill lights. That is about it. The pit is too loud, too damp, too disturbed. Animals pass through. They do not stay.

What stays is the slow. This is the dynamic of the place — its own slow, which has nothing to do with the speed of the machinery, which is in fact very fast, which is in fact the only fast thing here. The slow is the patient overlay. The slow is the moss creeping another centimeter up the concrete apron of the crusher foundation, year on year, until one day a foreman notices that the brand name stamped into the pour is no longer legible. The slow is the willow seedling that took root in the crack between a conveyor idler and its mount and is now, eight years on, holding the idler slightly off true with its root, so that the belt tracks a fraction of a degree to starboard. The slow is the tailings pipeline that has been dripping iron-rich water onto a bench for so long that a red-orange creek has established itself, complete with its own microflora, its own iron-oxidizing bacteria, its own peculiar tiny ecology, all of it running downhill toward the sump at a rate the eye cannot see but the chemist can measure. The slow is the cold doing its work, the freeze-thaw opening new cracks in the fresh-cut rock faces, the cracks filling with meltwater, the meltwater freezing again, the faces beginning — only beginning, the work has just begun — to fracture along the lines the explosives left.

The place is not beautiful. It is not trying to be beautiful. It is a working hole in the ground in cold country, full of machines that have not stopped running, and it is being quietly buried by the same climate it was cut into. In another century, if the machines stopped, the hole would still be there — sharper at the edges than it is now, because the rain and the freeze and the patient root would round the bench corners and bring down the conveyor trestles and pile the willows up against the bridge piers. The bridge would fall last. The bridge would take a long time to fall. The cables would rust, the vines would thicken, and one winter an unusual weight of ice would pull a cable down, and the cable would take another with it, and the deck would tilt, and then one more winter would finish it, and the ravens would have to find somewhere else.

But the machines have not stopped. The machines have not stopped, and so the pit remains what it is: a hole, cold and damp and deafening, with the green pressing in from every side and the grey sky far above and the ore going up and out on belts and trucks and rails, and the ore being replaced by a hole a little deeper, a little wider, a little more carefully maintained, year by year. The moss climbs. The willow grows. The willow is pruned back by the crews in summer, with machetes, so it does not foul the conveyors. The crews do not think about what they are doing. They are maintaining the operation. The willow comes back. The moss comes back. The cold comes back. The sound does not stop.
