# The Floor of the Long Drowning

There is no horizon here. There has not been a horizon for a very long time, and the things that remain have stopped asking for one.

You are standing — if you can call it standing, if the word means anything on a surface that is mostly remembering what ground used to feel like — on what was once the roof of the world. The Spine of the Second Continent. The place where mountains were not a metaphor. Now the mountains are sediment. The sediment is underwater. The water is warm.

---

The water is the first thing, and it is everywhere, and it is not water.

It is the color of weak tea left in a cup too long, a milky amber that lets light through only as suggestion. You can see maybe forty meters before shapes dissolve into a uniform haze. Beyond that, the world is a rumor. It moves, but slowly, with the kind of patience you associate with things that have already won. There is no current you can feel against your skin, but the algae-fields shift anyway, in long slow undulations, like the breathing of something vast asleep on its side.

The surface is eighteen meters above. You know this because sometimes — only sometimes — something dark and large passes between you and the light, and for a moment the world above is occluded, and then it isn't, and the muffled percussion of whatever-it-was travels down through the column of liquid to arrive at your feet as a vibration rather than a sound.

You do not look up.

---

The terrain is gentle past the point of gentleness. It has been eroded past the question of geology into the answer of geology. What were once peaks are now rises — long, slow, whale-backed swells that climb ten, twenty, thirty meters from the valley-floors before subsiding again. The slopes are gradients you could walk in your sleep. There is no angle anywhere that bites. The whole landscape has the topography of something that has been licked.

The substrate is a strange thing. Not quite rock. Not quite soil. It has the give of compacted clay but the coloration of old bone — that yellowed ivory of things that have lain in dark places. When you press your palm into it, your hand comes away smelling of calcium and something sweeter, something like the ghost of a fruit you cannot name. The substrate is alive, technically, in the way coral is alive: it accretes, it grows, it lays down another millimeter per century. But it is so slow that you have to take its word for it.

Strewn across the floor, half-buried, are the shapes that give the place its occasional name in the old fishermen's stories: the *clenched fists of the first ones*, *the spilled teeth of the sky-god*, *the stones that remember*. They are stones, but they are not. They are too regular. Too deliberate. Hexagonal pillars lie toppled like dropped cigars; domes the size of rooms sit cracked open with their interiors crystallized into forests of translucent needles; there are walls — actual walls, low and crumbling — that describe angles no erosion would have chosen. You do not know who laid them. The fact that the question occurs to you is, itself, a kind of evidence.

The walls have gone soft at the corners. The pillars have been kneaded by time into suggestions of their original geometry. Whatever they were, they were here before the water came, and they are still here, and they will be here when the water is gone, and the water will not be gone.

---

Vegetation covers everything that is not stone. It is the lushness of a place that has had eternity to learn photosynthesis and has learned it thoroughly.

The dominant form is a bladder-kelp that rises from the substrate in clusters of translucent pods, each pod the size of a closed fist, each cluster the size of a grove of trees. They are pale green, almost the color of dilute jade, and they pulse — not with any rhythm you can predict, not with the regularity of a heartbeat, but with something that feels more like attention. When you move near them, the nearest pods contract very slightly. When you stand still long enough, they expand again. They are watching you, or they are breathing around you, and you cannot tell the difference.

Between the bladder-kelp groves, a fine grass-like filament spreads in dense mats — it is not grass, it is something that wishes to be grass and has spent geological time getting the silhouette right. Underneath it, smaller things: rust-colored mosses, white lichens that glow faintly when disturbed, and everywhere the creeping threads of a fungus-network that runs through the substrate like a nervous system. When you step on it, somewhere far away a pod contracts.

Above the bladder-kelp, suspended in the water column at varying heights, drift the long streamers of *veil-weed* — ribbons of bioluminescent tissue that can be forty, sixty, a hundred meters long, hanging vertically in the still water like the partitions of a cathedral made of light. In the dim light they are the closest thing the place has to sunbeams. They glow a cold blue at their bases, fading to green at their tips, and they sway without wind, in currents you cannot detect, in patterns that take seven minutes to complete.

If you swim up into them — and you will, eventually, because the temptation is the kind you cannot architect your way out of — they part around you like hair, and for a moment you are inside a curtain of pale light, and the world is the size of your body, and you can hear your own blood.

---

The temperature is warm. It is the warmth of bathwater that has been left too long and has not yet gone cold, that has somehow stabilized into a temperature that is neither heating nor cooling but simply *is*. It presses against your skin the way a held hand presses — not urgent, not insistent, just present. You cannot tell whether it is the water that is warm or whether you are.

There are thermal layers. You learn them by the differences in how your chest tightens or opens. Near the substrate, warmer. Higher, slightly cooler, but never cold, never anything you would call bracing. The transitions are soft. Nothing here shocks you.

---

Fauna is rare.

This is the thing that makes the place feel haunted. The abundance of everything else — the water, the growth, the slow geological certainty — makes the absence of animals louder. You see evidence of life. The grazed patches on the bladder-kelp. The white skeletal sheds of something molting, scattered between the pillars. The occasional smooth track in the substrate like a snake's passage but wider, hip-width, and deeper than any snake would press.

But you do not see the thing that made the track. You never see the thing that made the track.

Once, in your second hour, you see a shape — a long, pale, undulating shape, three meters maybe, four, drifting in the dim water about sixty meters off, its outline softened by distance and the amber murk into something almost abstract. It moves with the unhurried confidence of a thing that owns the column of water it occupies. It does not look at you. It does not need to look at you. It passes behind a rise and is gone, and the veil-weeds it displaced take seven minutes to settle back into their original pattern.

After that, you wait to see it again. You do not see it again.

You find, later, in a hollow between two toppled hexagonal pillars, a clutch of small round things, pale, leathery, the size of plums, warm to the touch. You do not know what laid them. You do not touch them more than once. You remember, afterward, that you did not touch them more than once, and you cannot remember deciding not to.

---

The light is dim. The light is dim the way a library is dim — not absent, just *held*. It filters down from the surface eighteen meters above, refracted and dispersed by eighteen meters of amber water and whatever lives in that amber water, and arrives at the floor as a soft, sourceless glow that has no shadows. Things are visible. Nothing casts a shadow. This makes depth hard to judge, makes the floor feel both nearer and farther than it is.

The veil-weeds supplement it. Where the bladder-kelp groves are densest, the overlapping glow of their bases creates small bright pockets — not enough to read by, but enough to see faces by, if there were faces here to see. Between the groves, the light fails more thoroughly, and you find yourself navigating by the geometry of what is *less* dark, the way you might navigate a forest at dusk by the gaps between trees.

You do not bring a light. You decide, somewhere in the first half-hour, that you do not want to bring a light. You are not sure you can articulate why. There is a sense that illumination here would be a kind of trespass. That the place has its own light, and your light would be louder than the place.

---

The silence is not silence. The place has a sound, but the sound is so low, so distributed, so woven into the medium, that at first you think there is no sound at all.

There is the sound of water moving against itself, but only at the threshold of perception. There is the sound of the bladder-kelp pulsing — a wet, soft, almost organic *tok* — but only if you press your ear to a pod, and even then you are not sure you are not hearing your own pulse translated through the medium. There is the sound of the substrate: a slow, almost subliminal grinding, the sound of calcium being laid down, the sound of geological time being deposited one angstrom at a time.

The loudest sound is the one you make. Your breathing is enormous here. Your footsteps are enormous here. When you shift your weight, the substrate answers with a faint, wet exhalation, and you become aware of yourself as a kind of small disturbance, an event in an otherwise unbroken stillness.

---

There are the rules.

You do not know them all. You know some of them.

You know that the substrate, when you stand still on it long enough, begins to grow up around your feet. It takes hours. It would not trap you. But you do not stand still for hours.

You know that the bladder-kelp does not like being touched. You know this because you touched a pod, once, in the first minutes, and it contracted so sharply that the whole grove contracted with it, and the contraction sent a pulse through the fungus-network and out across the substrate, and you felt the floor of the world briefly tighten under you like a stomach, and you did not touch a pod again.

You know that the veil-weeds, if you swim up into them, will let you go. They will not hold you. But they will leave a residue on your skin that smells, faintly and persistently, of something that is not water, and the residue will not wash off for days, and during those days other things in the water will not come near you, and you will not be sure whether this is protection or exile.

You know that the pillars and the domes and the low walls — the *stones that remember* — are not safe to sleep against. You know this without being told. You know it the way you know not to put your hand in a fire. There is a quality to the stone. A faint wrongness. A quality of *having been oriented toward something that is no longer here*. You do not lean against them. You do not sit in their lee. You give them a wide berth, and the wide berth feels correct, and you do not examine why it feels correct.

---

The dynamic is the slow dynamic. The dynamic of an ecosystem that has stopped competing and started collaborating. The dynamic of things that have been here long enough that eating and being eaten have become less interesting than *continuing*.

The bladder-kelp grows. The fungus-network spreads. The substrate accretes. The veil-weeds sway in currents you cannot feel. The pale thing you saw moves somewhere beyond the next rise, in patterns you will never trace.

Once, an hour in, you see a slow vertical fall of pale matter drifting down through the amber water — a kind of snow, but organic, made of small translucent discs that turn and glint as they descend. They land on the bladder-kelp pods, and the pods brighten faintly where they land, and the brightness persists for a few minutes before fading. It is fertilization. It is spring. It is the slowest spring imaginable, occurring at the rate the place decides, which is not your rate.

You stand and watch it for eleven minutes. You are not bored. You are, for eleven minutes, the most awake you have been in years.

---

The weirdness is the substrate of the place. It is not a feature. It is not something that happens *in* the landscape. It is what the landscape *is*.

The amber water is not water. Not exactly. It has the viscosity of water but the memory of something thicker. Light behaves strangely in it — not refraction, not quite, but something adjacent to refraction, a kind of bending that is more *suggestion* than law. When you look at a distant pillar, it is not where it appears to be. It is approximately where it appears to be. Approximately, here, is not a comforting word.

The bladder-kelp watches you, but it does not watch you with anything you would call eyes. It watches you with the patience of a thing that does not need eyes because it has been watching since before eyes were invented.

The pillars remember. You do not know this empirically. You know it the way you know a song you have not heard in decades — it is in your body, not your mind. When you pass close to one, you feel a faint pressure behind your sternum, like the beginning of a word you are about to say. The word never arrives. The feeling does.

The substrate is warm. Warmer than the water. Warmer than it should be, geologically, at this depth, in this climate. There is something underneath it. You do not dig. You understand, without being told, that you do not dig.

The veil-weeds, sometimes, when the light is very dim and you have been very still for a long time, begin to glow brighter. Not all of them. A few. As if in sequence. As if a signal is being passed from one to the next, from the far ones to the near ones, until the nearest one — the one nearest you — pulses a single slow blue, and you feel, absurdly, that you have been addressed.

You do not know what was said.

You do not need to know what was said.

---

You are alone on the floor of a drowned world. The world is warm, and the world is dim, and the world is older than forgetting, and the world is alive in ways that do not require you.

You sit on a rise. The substrate is soft under you. A bladder-kelp grove fifteen meters away contracts, very slightly, and then expands. The veil-weeds sway. Far away, the pale shape you saw is somewhere, doing what it does. The hexagonal pillars lie toppled in their slow accumulation of lichen. The water moves against your skin. The light holds you.

You stay a long time.

You do not leave because you are lost. You do not leave because you cannot. You stay because the place has decided, very gently, that you may.

---

When you finally rise — and it is hard to rise, and you do it slowly, with the care of someone leaving a room where someone is sleeping — the bladder-kelp nearest you expands once, fully, as if in farewell, and then settles, and then resumes its patient attention to the amber water.

You swim upward. The veil-weeds part for you. They leave their residue on your skin. You do not mind.

The surface, when you reach it, is quiet. The air above is different from the water below. The air above is thinner, less patient, more urgent. It surprises you, at first, that anything lives up there. It surprises you less, later, that so little does.

You climb out. You stand on the surface of the world, where there is still a horizon, still a sun, still weather.

You smell faintly of the deep place for the rest of your life. Other things in the water, when you go back to them, will not come close.

You are not sure, even now, whether this is protection or exile.

You suspect it is neither.

You suspect it is the place continuing to remember you, for as long as it takes.
