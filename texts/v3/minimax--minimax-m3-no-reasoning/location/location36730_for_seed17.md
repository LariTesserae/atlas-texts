# The Glass Sea at Altitude

Begin with the sound. There is no other way into this place.

It is a roar without edges, without beginning, without end. The wind is the first thing — it comes down off the snowfields to the north and falls across the water like a thing poured, a thick cold current of air that has been moving for so long that it has forgotten, if it ever knew, how to stop. The water is the second thing — the wind picks it up and turns it into small perpetual waves, chop the color of slate, chop that never swells and never dies, chop that has been striking the volcanic glass of the shoreline for longer than anything alive has been here to hear it. The two noises — wind against water, water against glass — braid together into a single continuous pressure that sits in the chest and behind the eyes. After a few hours you stop trying to parse it. After a few days you stop being certain you could hear anything else if it played directly into your ear.

This is equilibrium. Not stillness. Stasis built out of unending motion. Nothing is changing because the forces that drive the change have balanced perfectly with the things they act upon, and the agreement between them has held for so long that the holding has become the place.

## The water

The water is everywhere. That is the first visual fact, the second sensory fact, the third fact of every calculation you make about being here. The plateau on which this sea rests is high — high enough that the air stings the lungs, high enough that the light goes thin and pale and the sky has the washed-out grey of a winter that will not end — and yet the water covers it. Continent-scale. The far shore, if there is a far shore, is a smudge of mountain teeth on a horizon so distant that a fast boat would not reach it before the second day.

The water is cold. Not the friendly cold of an autumn lake, but the working cold of something that has never been warm — three degrees, four degrees, a temperature at which the body begins to miscalculate its own boundaries. Where the depth is shallow — and it is shallow for a long way out from the shore, because the plateau is flat and only gently tilted — the water is the color of a knife blade, a pale translucent silver. Farther out it deepens into a black that does not reflect. You cannot see the bottom anywhere. You cannot see the bottom in the shallows either, because the bottom is volcanic glass, and volcanic glass at depth is the same black as the water over it.

## The shore

There is no shore.

This is the second strange fact, and you do not quite believe it for the first hour. A shore, in any meaningful sense, requires that the land has had time to meet the water, to argue with it, to soften under its persistence. Erosion. The wearing down. Sediment. Sand. The accumulation of small things that make a beach, that make a margin where one element hands itself over to another.

This plateau has not had time. It is young. The geological forces that raised it have only recently — in the long calendar of stones — let go, and the surface is still the surface it was when it emerged: black volcanic glass, sharp as broken bottles, sharp as obsidian flake, sharp as the moment of cooling. The water meets it and finds no purchase. The waves strike the glass and the glass strikes back, and the sound of the meeting is the most prominent single noise in the whole deafening world. The waves do not round the edges. They chip them. They take their own glass and tumble it, briefly, before the lack of soiliness lets it sink.

So there is no beach. There is a margin — a hard line, a contradiction — where the dark water comes right up to the dark rock and the two abut each other like the edges of a broken plate fitted imperfectly back together. You can stand on the glass within a foot of the water and your boots will not sink. You can walk out across the plateau along the natural shelves and ridges the lava left, and you will never be standing on anything but glass.

It is beautiful and it is hostile and you cannot stop looking at how the light catches its fracture planes.

## The vegetation

There is none.

This is the thing that does not, on first inspection, compute. There is water. There is light. There is room. And yet nothing green or rooted or fungal or lichenous has ever taken hold here, because taking hold requires a substrate, and the substrate is sterile volcanic glass that has not yet begun to weather into anything that could feed a root or anchor a holdfast. The sea is not a graveyard of plants. The sea has never known plants. It is pre-botanical. The water itself is full of the chemical precursors of soil, suspended, drifting, unassembled.

The emptiness of the place, the sense that it is mostly nothing per cubic meter, is partially the emptiness of this absence. Even in deep ocean, even in winter tundra, there is usually something — a microbial crust, a diatom film, the smear of a lichen — that makes a claim on the rock. Here, there is nothing. The wind has scoured the surface above the waterline. The cold has prevented any colonization below it. The glass is clean, dark, reflective where the sun finds it, and untouched.

## The fauna

And yet — and this is the strangeness, the small whisper of weirdness that keeps the place from being merely desolate — the water is full of life.

It is everywhere once you learn to look for it. In the first few hours you will see nothing; the surface is dark and the dark does not yield. Then a shape will pass beneath the boat — long, slow, the size of a forearm, vaguely fish-shaped, vaguely not — and you will understand that the water has a population. Then another. Then a school. Then, in the colder water farther out, the pinkish bloom of a swarm so dense it stains the sea for half a kilometer, a cloud of something smaller than a fingernail, drifting and feeding and being fed upon.

The fauna is pelagic. None of it roots. None of it attaches. None of it grazes on the bottom, because the bottom is sterile glass and offers nothing. The food web is built from the water itself — from the bacterial mats that grow around the few volcanic vents that still seep heat from the young geology below, from the rain of organic matter that drifts in on the wind from elsewhere, from each other. The creatures here are mostly transparent or silver, mostly small, mostly numerous. They drift in the cold currents in patterns that look, from a distance, like weather. There are larger things too — you can tell by the way the small things part, by the slow dark shadow that moves beneath the surface and does not surface. You do not see them clearly. You are not meant to see them clearly. They are the same color as the deep water.

They have never encountered a plant. The word *plant* would mean nothing to them. Their world is water and each other and the slow pulse of the vents, and has been for as long as their lineage runs.

## The structures

Human — or human-shaped — things appear, but only at long intervals.

A pipeline emerges from the water near a headland and runs along the glass for a stretch before disappearing into the sea again. A platform on stilts, far out, lit at night by a single red light that you can see for an hour before you reach it. A refinery or a pumping station or a thing that might be both, low and angular, its silhouette leaning slightly into the prevailing wind as if it has been patiently pressed for decades. Concrete. Steel. The smokestacks trail their exhaust sideways into the same wind that drives the waves, and the exhaust dissolves into it without ceremony.

The structures are scattered. There might be a dozen in a hundred kilometers. They are not a city. They are a punctuation — the only marks on the glass that suggest someone has stood here and made an argument with the place. The argument appears to have been a quiet one. The structures hum. Some of them throb with the slow rhythm of pumps. A few are derelict, their paint gone, their metal leaning into the glass in attitudes of slow and total surrender. The rest still run. What they are doing, you cannot, from outside, say. Processing the water. Mining something below the glass. Pumping from the vents. Harvesting the fauna. Nothing about them explains why they are here rather than somewhere easier. They are small. The sea is vast. The sound of their engines — and there is a sound, a working mechanical drone — is consumed the moment it leaves them, swallowed by the larger roar the way a match is swallowed by daylight.

They are lit. At night the lights of the structures are red, amber, the cold white of halogens. They look, from a distance, like the lights of a ship seen through fog. They do not warm anything. They only make small claims against the dark.

## The light

The light is what you would call normal if you had to, but it does not behave normally.

The sun is up there, somewhere, behind the high cloud that the wind has not let break in a season. It is a pale disc, slightly brighter than the rest of the overcast. It does not warm. It only illuminates. The light it casts is flat and grey and shows everything with equal clarity — the fracture planes of the glass, the dark water, the small white caps on the chop, the structures in the distance. Shadows are short or absent. There is no golden hour. There is no dramatic noon. The light is steady, evenly distributed, slightly cold, and it has been like this for the entire time anyone has stood here and made a note of it.

It will be like this tomorrow.

## The sound, again

You come back to the sound because the sound is the place. The wind does not change its pitch. The waves do not change their rhythm. The volcanic glass does not change its voice. Together they produce a constant broadband roar that has no silence in it — no gap, no pause, no place for a quieter sound to insert itself. When you speak to another person here, you speak close to their ear, and you still cannot be certain they have heard. When a machine starts or stops on one of the platforms, you do not hear the change so much as feel a small redistribution of pressure in your inner ear, a new low note added to or removed from the chord.

The sound is not a sound. It is a property of the place, like the temperature or the cold or the slope of the glass. It has been here as long as the wind has been here. It will be here as long as the wind is here. The wind is here because the plateau is here, and the differential between the cold air above the water and the colder air above the snowfields is here, and the geography that channels the wind is here, and none of these things are in the process of changing. This is what equilibrium means when it is allowed to run for a long time: a place that has settled into its own voice and will not be talked out of it.

## The whole

It is vast and it is cold and it is empty in the way that a cathedral is empty — full of structure, full of meaning, full of the slow patient work of forces, and almost devoid of the things we usually count as presence. The water is everywhere and it is mostly water. The glass is everywhere and it is mostly glass. The wind is everywhere and it is mostly wind. The structures and the fauna are scattered thinly across an expanse so large that a human being, even a busy one, even a loud one, even one firing up all the engines of their attention, occupies a vanishingly small fraction of it.

The place is not hostile to you. It has not decided anything about you. It is simply other, in the way that weather is other, in the way that an ocean is other. It is doing what it does. It has been doing what it does. The sound it makes is the sound of doing it.

If you stand on the glass and face the water and the wind presses against you and the roar presses against you and the cold presses against you, you can feel, for a moment, what it is to be in a place that has finished becoming itself. Not finished in the sense of decay. Finished in the sense of arrival. It has come to rest in its own shape, and it is staying there, and you are a small and temporary witness to a long and unhurried stillness that was not made for you and does not need you.

The waves strike the glass.

The wind moves over the water.

The structures hum.

The fauna drifts.

The sun, behind the high cloud, illuminates without warming.

The place does not change.
