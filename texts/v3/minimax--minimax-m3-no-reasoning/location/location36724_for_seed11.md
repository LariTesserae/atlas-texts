# The Hollow Brow

## I. The Crest

The ridge runs north-south for nine days' walk and no one has ever seen both ends of it. It is not so much a ridge anymore as a *spine*—the last calcified remnant of mountains that once wore their proper height. Eons have shaved them down. What remains is a long, gentle, spine-shaped welt of earth, perhaps two thousand feet at its highest breath, dropping away on either side into long approaches of tilted stone and fractured shale. The wind here is a permanent resident. It does not pass through; it *lives* on the Brow, and it has lived here so long that the very rocks have learned its voice.

A traveler climbing the eastern approach hears it first as a low drone, then as a roar, then as something that ceases to be wind and becomes a substance pressed against the eardrums. The Brow is a wind funnel. Air from the eastern lowlands and air from the western lowlands meet at this ridge and argue with each other in tones so constant and overlapping that the human ear eventually gives up parsing them and accepts the whole thing as a kind of weather.

People who live here do not speak louder. They speak differently—clipped, consonanted, the vowels eaten by the air.

## II. The Stone

The stone of the Brow is wrong.

That is the word the cartographers use, the geologists, the few stubborn academic expeditions that have made the climb. *Wrong.* It is not granite. It is not basalt, not schist, not any of the proper-named stones. It is older than proper names. When broken it does not split along clean planes but along curves, and the broken faces have a faint banding—almost a grain—that suggests the rock was laid down in slow motion, the way a thick syrup might be poured and then cooled for a million years.

The color is a pale iron-grey with, in certain lights, a greenish undertone, as if somewhere inside the stone something is still photosynthesizing. In other lights the green vanishes and the stone looks merely dead. No one has ever found a fossil in it. No one has ever found a crystal in it either, though there are reports of small, fist-sized cavities where, if you cup your hands around the opening and shade them from the wind, you can feel a faint cool exhalation on your palms.

The stone does not lichen. The stone does not moss. The stone simply persists, and this is one of the reasons the geologists call it wrong.

## III. The Vegetation

But the soil that has accumulated in the cracks and shelves of the stone—this soil is *alive*, and riotously so. The wind, for all its violence, carries seed. It carries, more importantly, *mist*, which the long-warm climate condenses out of every passing cloud along the ridge's flanks. The result is a peculiar ecology: thin, persistent damp threaded through a place that should, by rights, be desert.

Ferns grow here. Not the modest ferns of shaded understory but tree-ferns with trunks the thickness of a man's thigh and fronds that unroll in the slow air pockets behind boulders. Mosses—not the green carpets of cooler hills but a pale, almost luminous moss that thrives on mist alone, that does not so much grow as *condense* out of the air and into being. There are orchids rooted in the cracks of the supposed-wrong-stone, blooming in colors no herbarium will accept: a blue so deep it looks black, a yellow so saturated it seems to hum.

And climbing, always climbing—the vines. A creeper the locals call *speaker-vine*, with grey-green leaves shaped like elongated hands, and small pale flowers that never open fully, that always look like they are about to say something. The vines climb the bones of the wind-shelters. The vines climb the abandoned stonework. The vines climb the standing stones, if you let them.

## IV. The Shelters

The shelters are old.

No one knows how old. The wind has eaten any inscription that might have once marked them. They are low, half-buried structures of the wrong-stone, roofed with slabs that must weigh what a small family weighs, that must have taken a small family to lift. The doorways are narrow and low—designed, the feeling is, for a shorter people, or a humbler posture. Inside, the floor is sunken a foot below the outside grade, and the walls curve inward to meet the roof in a continuous arch.

There are perhaps forty of these shelters along the visible length of the Brow, scattered, never clustering more than three or four together. Most are roofless now, the great slabs tumbled into the fern growth below. Some are intact. In a few, someone has laid a fire and left a scatter of blackened bones and a single carved wooden bowl, and the bowl is always the same shape, always the same dark wood, and no one can say where the wood came from because there are no trees on the Brow that size.

Whoever built these shelters did not build to live in. They built to *wait out the wind*. They built for the deafening. The structures are tuned to it—shaped to create, just inside the doorway, a pocket of comparative stillness, a silence shaped like a human body. In such a pocket, you can sleep. You can think. You can hear another person speak, if they put their mouth against your ear.

Outside the pocket: the roar. Always the roar.

## V. The Fauna

There is not much.

A lizard the length of a forearm, slate-grey, that basks on the southern faces of the boulders and vanishes into cracks at your approach. Its belly is, unexpectedly, a vivid orange. When it runs, it runs in short zigzags, and you can see the orange flash like a dropped coin.

A bird, perhaps. A thing of brown and indeterminate shape that rides the wind rather than fighting it—splayed flat against the airstream, motionless, then lifting in long slow arcs without once seeming to flap. It nests in the lee of the larger stones. Its call is a thin wire of sound that almost, almost, makes it through the roar, and when you hear it you cannot tell whether it is a real sound or a memory of sound or your own pulse.

Insects, of course. Small persistent flies that swarm the eye and the mouth and have learned, somehow, to navigate the wind. A pale spider that builds no web but wanders at night along the lee sides of boulders, hunting the flies, and in the morning leaves a faint silk thread on the stone that catches the mist and glitters.

And one other thing. Something larger, occasionally glimpsed at the far ends of the ridge where the Brow fades into haze. A shape, a moving shape, that may be a single animal or may be a small herd. No one has ever gotten close enough to describe it. It moves against the wind. The locals call it the *Long Walker*, and they speak of it the way one speaks of weather.

## VI. The Sound

It must be spoken of.

The sound is not wind. It has wind in it, the way an orchestra has a single fiddle in it, but the sound is more than wind. It is the voice of the place itself, the accumulated exhalation of stone that has been here since before proper stone had a name. It is a low continuous tone, around F below middle C, that never stops and never varies and never modulates, and superimposed on it are the wind-shrieks, the gust-crashes, the long sliding moans of air moving across curved stone.

In the lee of the larger shelters, the sound drops to perhaps half-volume, which is still louder than a shout. In the open, conversation is impossible. Even shouting is a small and foolish thing. People who live here have developed sign, or they have learned to read the lips of someone an inch from their face, or they have simply stopped talking and learned to wait.

The sound goes on at night. The sound goes on when there is no wind at all, which is never, but which in the imagination of someone who has listened too long feels possible. The sound is, in the end, the most reliable feature of the place. The stone erodes. The ferns die and regrow. The shelters fall. But the sound is permanent, and the sound is the place.

## VII. The Weather

The weather is the sound's accomplice.

The Brow sits where two climates meet. To the east, the lowlands are warm and intermittently wet—the long rains come every few weeks in the warm season and last a day or two, then withdraw. To the west, a different regime: cooler, drier, more fickle. The Brow takes from both and gives nothing back.

In a single day you can stand in sun, then in mist, then in a horizontal rain that arrives sideways and stings the skin, then in a clarity so sharp you can see the haze on the far ends of the ridge resolving into the suggestion of further ridges, fading into a distance that may not be a distance at all but a trick of the light on the wrong-stone.

The clouds love the Brow. They pile against it, crawl over it, hang in tatters off its flanks. There are days when the ridge stands clear above a sea of cloud, and the wind roars up out of the white beneath you, and you feel that you are standing on the spine of the world.

There are days when you cannot see your hand in front of your face for the mist, and the wind is so thick with moisture it feels like being underwater, and the roar is so complete it feels like being nowhere at all.

## VIII. The People

There are people.

Not many. A few hundred across the whole length of the ridge. They live in the shelters, or in low stone-and-turf structures they have built against the lee sides of the larger boulders, half-buried, half-grown-over with moss and the speaker-vine. They herd nothing. They cultivate nothing, or almost nothing—a few root vegetables in pockets of deep soil, a creeping bean that climbs the stone. They gather. They are gatherers of the mist-forest, of the fern-shoots, of the pale mushrooms that fruit in the still air behind the boulders after the rains.

They are a quiet people. They have to be. They sign. They wait. They sit for hours in the wind-pockets of the old shelters, listening to the roar, and their faces, when you can see them in a rare moment of stillness, are patient beyond the patience of most humans—calm, contemplative, slightly *elsewhere*.

They make things. Small things. Wooden bowls, always the same shape. Knives of bone and the wrong-stone, ground to a working edge. Nets of tough vine-fibre, woven so finely that, when held against the light, they look like skin. They trade these down into the lowlands, where they fetch good prices from people who want an object that has been made in the sound, in the roar, that has been finished by hands that have learned a particular patience.

The people do not speak of the Long Walker except at certain seasons. They do not speak of the small hand-shaped flowers of the speaker-vine except in signs that mean, roughly, *almost-time*.

## IX. The Turbulence

And yet the place is not still.

It is the opposite of still. The wind tears. The mist shifts. The clouds roll. The ferns unroll. The vines creep, visibly in a single day, climbing an inch over the surface of the stone. The moss condenses, condenses, becomes. The insects swarm and vanish and swarm again. The lizard darts. The bird rides. The Long Walker moves, somewhere, along the ridge. The people sign and gather and wait.

It is a place of constant small change layered over an enormous slowness. The stone erodes at a rate no one can measure in a lifetime. The shelters fall, one slab at a time, across centuries. The soil thickens, fractionally, in the cracks. The ridge itself, this long spine of the world's oldest stone, persists, will persist, will continue to be shaved and weathered and have its slow substance carried away on the wind, until at last it is flat, until at last it is a long low swell in a landscape that has forgotten it was ever a mountain.

But that day is not today. Today the Brow stands. Today the wind roars. Today the mist gathers and the ferns unroll and the pale moss condenses out of the air and the speaker-vine climbs its slow inch up the side of a fallen slab. Today, in a small wind-pocket behind an ancient shelter, someone sits with a wooden bowl in their lap and listens, and waits, and prepares to say something that the vine, almost, almost, has been about to say.
