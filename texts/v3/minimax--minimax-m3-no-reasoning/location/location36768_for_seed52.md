# The Reservoir at the Bottom of the Sky

The river doesn't so much flow as *accumulate*. From where I stand on the levee of polished basalt, I can watch the water gather in a long silver braid along the valley floor, pooling in basins that no hydrologist would recognize — some of them uphill of others, some clearly defying gravity, their surfaces tilted at angles the eye wants to correct and cannot. The light here is the color of a photograph left too long in a drawer, a dimness that has the curious quality of being *even* rather than dark. No shadows fall. The sun, when I have seen it, is less an object than a place where the dimness briefly permits itself to thin.

The city occupies the low ground with the patient sprawl of a settlement that knew exactly what it wanted to be and has since gone past wanting. It is built from a material that I have to call stone because nothing else fits, but it is not any stone I know — a deep blue-grey with internal striations that move, slowly, like the grain of wood grown over centuries, except that here a century is something else. The buildings go up in tiers, terraced into the natural slope of the valley's southern flank, and the tiers are connected not by roads but by what I can only describe as *graded slopes of attention*: surfaces that are stairways for those who believe in them and impassable to anyone who doesn't. The locals walk them with the ease of long acquaintance. I have not yet learned which parts to believe in.

There are perhaps three thousand people. Maybe more. Counting is difficult, because the population is not entirely here in the usual sense. Some of them are *reflected* in the lower terraces — silhouettes visible only at the corners of vision, going about their business in mirrored buildings that seem to exist half a step behind the real ones. I am told they are not ghosts. I am told they are *antecedents*, and that the distinction matters in the building of municipal policy. I have chosen not to ask further.

The vegetation is the loudest thing here, in its quiet way. It is not so much planted as *negotiated with*. The gardens of the lower city are dense with a species of broad-leafed plant — almost a banana, almost a fern, almost a succulent — that decides for itself whether to be any of these on a given day. Its leaves change tactility with the weather, hardening into ceramic when the air grows dry and softening back to flesh when the river breathes. Between the buildings, the plants climb to the second and third stories without visible means, and the householders have accepted this as a form of tenancy. Rent is not discussed. The plants pay in humidity.

The humidity is in fact the great civic achievement. The valley has been *cultivated* for damp. The young basalt still weeps mineral moisture from its seams, and the river is partly an engineered thing — a slow canal network that conducts runoff from the highlands, augmented, I am told, by water drawn from somewhere the engineers decline to specify. There are cisterns under every plaza, shaped like inverted ziggurats, and they fill from sources that do not always agree on the direction of *down*. On some mornings a faint mist rises from the flagstones, and on those mornings the reflected city becomes briefly the more real of the two.

The fauna is sparse in the sense that visitors expect, but not in the sense the place is. There are fish in the river — silver, eyeless, and not entirely fish — that surface at dusk and feed on the mist. There is a species of small dark bird that builds nests only in the chimes that hang from the eaves of certain houses. The birds and the chimes have come to depend on each other, and the resulting music — because that is what it becomes — is the closest thing the city has to a clock. The chimes ring when the birds adjust them, and the birds adjust them according to a schedule known only to themselves.

Above the city, the basalt continues to *do* something. I have watched it for an hour. The young stone is not stable. It extrudes, slowly, in places where erosion has not yet had time to round the edges, and where it extrudes it carries with it the dim blue-grey material of the buildings, as though the city is being *grown* from a single continuous substance and the architecture is only the more articulate part of a single thought. There are seams where the stone is still warm. The engineers — the ones with the peculiar attire and the longer-than-human attention spans — visit these seams regularly and listen to them with instruments that have no obvious function.

The sound of the place, when I am quiet, is the sound of water in a basin: a faint lapping, a faint overflow, a faint trickle down unseen slopes. There is also, beneath this, a sound that I think of as the city thinking: a low harmonic that is not quite mechanical and not quite biological, perhaps the resonance of all that layered stone being gently added to itself. Once, near midnight, I heard something like a chord.

I have not yet decided what the city is *for*. The residents are unhelpful on this point. They are courteous, they offer me food that tastes like a memory of food, and they assure me that I will understand in time. In the meantime, they suggest, I should walk the graded slopes of attention, listen to the chimes, watch the reflected population going about its antecedent business, and pay no attention to the basalt seam at the top of the valley, which is *not*, they say, doing what it appears to be doing.

The river accumulates. The city thinks. The plants pay their rent. I am here for now, learning which parts to believe in.
