# The High Fumarole Towns

The sound comes first. Always the sound comes first.

It has been raining for six days, and the sound of water on the high city's corrugated roofs is the sound of ten thousand snare drums played at once, a hammered silver sheet of noise that fills the basin and folds back on itself. Slate sheds, iron sheets, old tile, newer polymer, a chapel of beaten copper — every roof a different drum, every drum tuned to a different pitch, and above the rain another sound, the low moan of the steam vents that give the place its name. The fumaroles have been breathing for a century and a half. They do not pause for weather. They exhale day and night, a sustained vowel that you feel in the teeth and the soles of the feet, the way a cathedral organ might feel if the cathedral were built over a furnace. Tourists write that it sounds like a whale. Locals do not write about it. Locals have stopped hearing it, the way a person by a waterfall stops hearing the water. Children, sometimes, still hear it, and stand very still on the iron bridges with their faces turned up.

The basin sits at the chin of a much larger mountain — a flank of black andesite that climbs east for another three thousand meters and is never climbed, because above the town the rock is sacred, or poisoned, or both, depending on which grandmother you ask. The town occupies a kind of shelf. To the west, the shelf falls away in a long slope of loose scree and red soil toward a valley that is usually lost in cloud. To the east, the shelf ends at the foot of the sacred rock in a near-vertical wall of columnar basalt, the kind of formation that looks like it was poured by someone who knew what they were doing. The columns are wet. They are always wet. They weep from a hundred points even on dry days, and today, on the sixth day of rain, they are streaming. Each column has its own trickle, and the trickles meet in a stone gutter at the base that runs, ankle-deep, along the back of the old town.

The town is built into the weeping wall. That is the first thing to understand. There is no debate about this. The builders of the first settlement — a mining crew, in the beginning, here for the silver-and-tin veins that lace the andesite — found the hot springs and the natural overhangs and simply built into them. Two centuries later, the city is a stratified thing, a hundred and forty years of wall-leaning construction, every generation adding a story, a balcony, a buttress, a new pipe. Houses rise straight out of the rock on the eastern side, their back walls literally the basalt columns, and the rooms are warm even in winter because the springs run behind the plaster. On the western side, where the shelf opens out, the buildings are freestanding — three and four stories, narrow, timber and brick, roofed in whatever has held up best: tile on the oldest, iron on the next, and a new alloy that does not rust on the newest, which glints in the rain like dulled tin. Avenues run east-west along the contours. They are cobbled. They are steep. They are crossed by a hundred iron stairways that go up and down between the levels, and the stairways are where the children play because the rain runs down them in sheets and the sheets are, briefly, to a child, a kind of river.

The springs are the town. Not metaphorically. The hot water comes up through the rock at a steady eighty-two degrees, in seventeen distinct vents, and has come up at that temperature for as long as anyone has measured. It is piped — gravity-fed, no pumps, because the slope does the work — into the houses for washing and bathing, into a series of public baths at the center of town, into a long trough that runs down the main avenue and was once used for ore-sorting and is now used, mostly, for ducks. There is a steam laundry on the third tier that does linen for half the region. There is a small distillery on the fifth tier that makes a clear liquor from local maize and sells it in blue bottles. There is, in the lowest tier, a hot spring that has been the same hot spring for one hundred and forty years, and the stone around it is the yellow of old bone from the constant mineral load. The springs never run cold. In winter, when the snow comes down to this elevation, the steam rises off the town in columns that the wind pulls apart, and from the valley below — when the valley is visible, which is rare — the town looks, the old postcards say, like a steamer lit at every porthole. The postcards are not wrong.

Above the springs, above the houses, the sacred rock rises. It is about six hundred meters of black stone, almost bare, with a few scrubby bushes in the cracks and a pair of nesting raptors that nobody talks about. The rock is what gives the town its weather. Moist air comes up the valley from the south, hits the cold face of the rock, and falls as rain. It rains here more than almost anywhere in the region. The locals will tell you, with the half-pride, half-resentment of all mountain people, that they get two hundred and forty rainy days a year. The priests — and there is a small temple near the upper springs, where the water first emerges, attended by two priests and a cat — will tell you that the rock is weeping for reasons of its own. The geologists, who come sometimes, with their hammers and their canvas bags, will tell you that the rock is simply cold and the air is wet and that is all. Everyone is partly right. The rock weeps, and the town has learned to live inside the weeping.

The vegetation, such as it is, clings to the slopes below the town and to the few pockets of soil on the shelf. There is a pale grass that grows between the cobbles of the lower avenues and that the children pick and braid. There are some fruit trees — guava, a few apple, a struggling cherry — planted in walled gardens where the soil has been carried up in baskets over generations. There is a small market garden on the southern edge that grows potatoes and a kind of bitter local cabbage. The trees are not tall. The wind off the valley comes up the slope and bends everything. There is moss, of course, a great deal of moss, on the north faces of walls and stones. There are ferns in the drain channels. There is a tree, a single ceiba, in the courtyard of the old mine office, that is taller than any other plant in town and that the mine office was built around. Nobody will say why the ceiba was left. The ceiba is the only living thing in town that is not actively bent.

The animals are sparse and small. There are the ducks. There are the cats, which are many. There are dogs of an indeterminate but uniform type — yellow, medium-sized, wet most of the time, and possessed of an expression that locals read as philosophical. There are the raptor pair above. There is, in the upper pools, a small bright frog that does not exist anywhere else; it was named for the town in 1891 and is now on a coin. There used to be more. There is talk, in certain rooms, of a creature that lives in the sacred rock and that comes out only on the longest nights, but this is the kind of talk that the priests discourage and the children encourage and the dogs, sleeping in doorways, seem to find unremarkable. The fauna, on the whole, has made its peace with the wet.

The town is industrial, in its way. The mines are mostly played out — the deep shafts are closed, the smelter chimney has not smoked in forty years — but the water, the steady hot water, has been put to work. The steam laundry is the largest employer. The distillery is second. There is a workshop on the fourth tier that fabricates parts for the regional rail line. There is a small power station, built into the rock in the 1930s, that takes the hottest of the springs and runs them through a heat exchanger; it powers the lights and the few motors and, on the worst nights, a single electric heater in the chapel. The technology is old. Most of it has been here for decades. It is the kind of technology that is not replaced because replacing it would require importing engineers, and importing engineers would require explaining the place to engineers, and nobody wants to do that. The machines clank. The machines have always clanked. A visitor in 1902 described the sound of the steam plant as "the breathing of some buried god," and a visitor in 1962 described it as "a kettle that has forgotten how to whistle," and a visitor in 2011 described it as "kind of creepy, actually." All three are accurate.

The light is the light of high places in weather. It is gray. It is soft. It arrives diffuse through the cloud deck and falls, evenly, on the wet stone and the wet iron and the wet tile, and there are no hard shadows anywhere, and the colors of the town are the colors of wet things: the rust-orange of old iron, the black-green of moss, the dull yellow of mineral-stained stone, the dark red of the soil, the pale green of the cabbage, the blue of the bottles in the distillery window. When the cloud thins — and it thins sometimes, for an hour, in the late afternoon — a diffuse light comes through that is almost gold, and the steam off the vents catches it and the town looks, briefly, like a painting of itself, and then the cloud closes back and the gold is gone.

The sound, again. The sound is always. Under the rain on the roofs, and the moan of the fumaroles, and the clank of the steam plant, and the distant, brief whistle of the train in the valley below — under all of it, the sound of water. The springs in their stone channels. The springs behind the plaster of the houses. The springs running in the gutters along the wall. The springs in the public baths, where the old people sit in the morning and the mothers bring the children in the afternoon and the teenagers go, secretly, at night. The springs in the trough along the main avenue, where the ducks paddle in circles. The water has been running for a hundred and forty years and will run, the geologists say, for a thousand more, and the town is built over the sound of it, and inside the sound of it, and the sound of it is, in some way that nobody can quite put into words, what the town *is*.

A woman crosses the main avenue in the rain, her shawl pulled up, her bare feet in wooden sandals. She passes the duck trough. She passes the blue bottles in the distillery window. She passes the closed smelter, where a single chimney stands against the cloud. She goes up the iron stairs. She goes into her house, where the back wall is the basalt, and the basalt is warm, and the water is running somewhere behind the plaster, and she closes the door, and the rain goes on, and the fumaroles breathe, and the town endures, in its wet, weathered, high, strange, and patient way.
