# The Cirque of the Long Light

The first thing is the silence, which is not silence at all but the held breath of a place where the air is thin and there are no trees to swallow sound. You come up over the last saddle and the world flattens suddenly, and the quiet meets you like the inside of a shell. The wind is moving but it is the small wind of high places, the wind that lives between stones and cannot organize itself into a howl. There is a faint hiss somewhere below and ahead of you, and a soft, repeated call like a hammer tapping on felt, far off, then farther, then not at all.

The basin fits inside a long breath. You can stand in its center and throw a stone against any wall. The walls go up several hundred feet, almost sheer, in dark ledges and fresh-cut faces where the rock still remembers being broken. The cirque was gouged out by a glacier that has, only recently, pulled itself back into the higher shadows. Geologically, recently. The stone is still young. There are no rounded shoulders here, no weathered domes. The granite is the gray-blue of fresh-split slate and it catches the sun in long, sharp plates, each one casting its own crisp shadow, so that the cliff face looks stacked rather than carved, layered like the pages of a book set on edge.

The sky above is a coin. That is how it feels — a small, impossibly bright disc squeezed between the shoulders of the ridge, the color of polished tin, almost white at the center, paling out toward a thin, ragged blue at the very rim. The air at this height is sharp and dry and it tastes of nothing, and the light comes through it without being filtered into kindness. Every edge is defined. Every shadow is its own black thing. A loose stone on the ground beside you will throw a shadow so precise you could trace it with a fingernail, and beside that shadow, where the sun catches the mica in the rock, a small galaxy of glitter goes off, spangling and dying, spangling again as the wind moves the air.

The cold is not the biting cold of windchill. It is the still, almost reverent cold of places where the air has come to rest. It settles into the folds of your clothing and stays there, patient. Your breath makes small ghosts that hang a moment before the sun burns them out of existence. Where the sun is not, the rock holds a chill that has been stored there since the previous night, and probably the night before that, and probably for many nights, and that cold comes up through your boots if you stand still long enough.

---

The dampness of the place is not a wetness you can easily name. It is the dampness of condensation, of melt, of things that have happened a moment ago and are happening again. On the north-facing wall of the cirque, where the sun never directly lands, the rock is furred with ice — a thick, soft rime that has grown in long feathers out of the cracks, each feather the size of a forearm, the color of skim milk where the light finds it and of tarnished silver where it does not. In the early part of the day, when the sun first clears the eastern rim, this rime begins to weep. It does not fall. It loses its grip on the stone and begins, very slowly, to slide. You can hear it if you are quiet — a faint, continuous whisper, like a sleeve drawn across a tabletop. By mid-morning the feathers are gone and the rock beneath them is dark, almost black, with melt, and tiny streams have begun to run out from every horizontal joint in the granite, dropping their thin curtains of water into the basin below.

There is a tarn at the foot of the cirque. It is small — you could walk its margin in two minutes if you walked quickly, and in five if you walked the way the place asks you to walk, which is slowly, watching where you set your feet. The water is the color of weak tea where it is shallow and the color of ink where it is deep, and it is utterly still, mirror-still, and in it the ring of cliffs is reflected so exactly that for a moment you cannot tell which way is up. The bottom is gravel and pale silt, and the silt holds, in some places, a soft orange stain from the iron that has leached out of the rocks above. The tarn does not freeze except at the very edges, because somewhere beneath it, where the roots of the cirque go down into the mountain's warm places, there is heat. There are vents. There are three of them, and they are the reason for almost everything you can see.

The vents breathe. They are cracks in the rock, the longest of them perhaps a body's length across, and out of them comes a thin, constant steam that smells of sulfur and something older, something mineral and green, the smell of the earth's inside. The steam rises and is taken by the air and becomes, in the cold parts of the cirque, a kind of weather. Where the sun falls on it, you see the faint, shaken pillar of it, a column of nothing made briefly visible by the difference in the air. Where the sun does not fall, it becomes fog, low and pale, that lies in long bands along the floor of the basin and drifts between the rocks like a slow animal. In winter, the steam freezes as it rises, and the vents become chimneys of ice, and the hiss of them is louder, and the whole cirque fills with a glittering, ice-crystal fog that catches the sun and breaks it into rings and pillars of light that have no business being there.

---

The plants of the place are concentrated where the heat and the water meet. They are not the plants of the valley below. They are their own thing. Where the vents warm the ground, and where the meltwater runs, the floor of the cirque has grown a kind of garden — a low, thick, breathing carpet of mosses so deep that your foot sinks into them to the ankle, and they exhale, when you step, a soft green smell of wet peat. Through the mosses, in summer, come the flowers. They are small. They are ferociously colored. The cold and the thin air and the unfiltered light have done something to them, and their blues are a blue you would not believe if you saw them on paper, and their yellows are almost orange, and there is a kind of saxifrage that grows in tight, hard cushions and is the exact shade of dried blood. The leaves of some of them are red, although they are not supposed to be red, and no one who comes here can quite explain why. The theory is the light. The theory is that up here the sun speaks a different language, and the plants have learned it.

The mosses give way, as you climb the lower shelves of the cirque, to a kind of grass — stiff, wiry, blue-green, growing in tussocks that are taller than they look and hide the ground beneath them. The grass is loud if you walk through it, but you do not walk through it. There are paths. There are many small paths worn into the cirque floor by hooves and by feet, and they braid between the rocks and the vents and the tussocks like the traces of water. The grass holds a kind of seed-head in late summer that is pale and feathery and, when the wind is right, comes off the stems in long silver threads that drift across the basin and catch in the lichens on the rocks and do not come loose until the snow melts them down. There are dwarf willows too, no taller than your knee, growing in the warmest pockets, with round leaves the size of a thumbnail and a pale, papery bark, and there are places where the willows have grown so thick they form low, half-domed thickets, and the ptarmigan shelter in them, and the snow piles in among them and stays long after the rest of the cirque has thawed.

---

The animals are everywhere. There is no hour of the day when something is not moving in the basin. On the scree at the foot of the cliffs, the marmots whistle their long, rising whistle — two notes, the second higher — and you hear it echoing back from the walls so that it seems there are ten marmots and there are only two. The ibex come down in the early morning and again in the evening, in lines of three or four, picking their way along the ledges with the casual certainty of creatures that know the place down to the grain. They pause on outcrops and look across the cirque with the yellow indifference of their eyes and they chew, slowly, and they move on. There is a fox, or there are foxes — no one is sure of the number — and their color is so close to the color of the rocks that you only see them when they move, and they move often. There is a stoat, white now, that crosses the snow at the margin of the tarn in a long, sinuous, unhurried line, and disappears into a crack in the rock, and the crack closes behind it, and there is nothing.

Above, in the air, the birds do their work. There are ravens — there are always ravens — and they ride the thermals that come up off the sun-warmed walls of the cirque with the slow, considered wingbeats of things that are not in a hurry, and their calls come down to you as if from a great distance, hoarse and rolling. There is a smaller hawk, the color of slate, that hunts the marmots. There is a great bird — lammergeier, the bone-breaker — and you do not often see it, but you see its work, because the ledges at the foot of the cliffs are littered with the broken bones of small animals, and the stones around them are stained, and the bones themselves are pale and polished and look almost like flowers. The lammergeier eats the bones. It drops them onto the rocks from a height and then comes down to collect the splinters. You can hear the drop if you are quiet — a faint, distant, percussive tap — and then nothing for a long time.

There are insects. There are flies of a kind you have not seen before, with bodies that look as if they have been dipped in copper, and they hover over the vents and the warm ground in a column that does not disperse. There are small, black beetles that run the paths between the tussocks, and a kind of moth that is the color of the lichen and rests on the lichen with its wings spread, and disappears when you look at it.

---

The things that have been built here are few. There is a shelter against the eastern wall — a low, half-circular wall of unmortared stone, open to the south, with a floor of flat stones and a hearth at its center. The hearth is a ring of stones that have been split, not cut, and the splits are clean and sharp, and the smoke-black on their inner faces is old but not ancient. Beside the shelter, half-buried in moss, there is a piece of worked antler — a tine, sharpened at one end, the other end shaped into a kind of wedge. There are a few more of these, scattered. There is a pile of stones that has been arranged, carefully, into a cairn the height of a man's knee, and beside it a line of stones, low and long, that runs for a few paces and then stops and then begins again, as if the line had lost its purpose and tried to remember it. There is a hollow in the rock that has been polished smooth by use, the polish of many hands over many years, and the hollow is the exact shape of a cupped palm, and if you put your hand into it, it fits.

The metalwork is small. There is a copper pin, bent but still whole, that someone lost in the moss a long time ago and that has turned the moss around it green, and then black, and then green again, and now the moss is growing over it, and in another fifty years it will not be there at all. There is a knife — a short, leaf-shaped blade of dark iron, the tang wrapped in something that was once hide and is now nothing. Whoever used these things did not stay. They came up here in the warm months and they went down again before the snow came, and they left behind them only the things that the wind and the water and the plants could not carry away.

---

The place is active, and yet, somehow, it is quiet. The quietness is not the absence of motion. It is the presence of a kind of muted, ongoing negotiation between all the things that live here, and the negotiations are conducted, as much as anything, in movements that do not make noise. The steam goes up. The water runs down. The ice melts at the margins and freezes at the center. The grass grows in summer and dies back to its roots in winter and grows again. The ibex climb and descend. The marmots whistle. The fox hunts, somewhere, in the rocks. The lammergeier waits, somewhere, in the air. The wind, which is the only large sound the place allows itself, comes over the rim of the cirque and falls down into it and is broken there into many small winds, and none of them are loud.

The weirdness of the place, if there is a weirdness, is not in any one thing. It is in the way the things are put together. It is the way the cold and the heat sit side by side without negotiating, so that you can stand with your face in the sun and your feet on ice and your hand on a rock that is warm from the vents, and all three of those things are true at the same time. It is the way the light behaves at this height, falling on every surface with a clarity that is not quite the clarity of the world below, as if it has not been fully translated. It is the way the tarn holds, in its still water, an upside-down cirque that is more vivid, in some way, than the right-way-up one, as if the reflection is the original and the actual cliffs are a copy. It is the way the silence itself is not silence but a kind of pressure, the pressure of all the small, ongoing negotiations that are happening just under the threshold of hearing, and the way that pressure, if you stand in it long enough, begins to seem like attention, as if the place is watching you, not unkindly, to see what you will do next.
