# The Hollow of Slow Rain

It sits in a basin. That is the first thing — the way the ground folds around it, the slopes rising at the edges like cupped hands held just below the rim. The basin is small, no more than you could cross on foot in a few minutes, and the slopes that frame it are gentle, weathered low hills of yellowish clay and pale stone, their flanks mostly bare but spotted here and there with thin tufts of dry grass that have gone the color of old rope. From a little distance the hollow is invisible; you only find it when you come around the last shoulder of ground and look down into it. Then it opens, this sheltered cup, and you see what is there.

The light inside the basin is dim. Not dark — the sky is pale and overcast above, a flat cloud-cover the color of bone — but dim, as if the air itself were a thin gauze filtering the brightness. Nothing casts hard shadows. The walls of the hollow, where the slopes meet the floor, are softened, blurred at their edges. You can see everything clearly, but without emphasis. It is a place where sight is patient.

The floor of the basin is not flat. It is a low, swelling mound at the center, like the back of something breathing, and around this central rise the ground sinks slightly inward, forming a shallow moat of bare earth and cracked stone. You sense the damp here before you feel it. The air is warm — warm the way a held breath is warm, warm and faintly close — and it carries a moisture you cannot see but can taste just behind the teeth, the kind of damp that gathers on skin and on stone and on the few scattered leaves and never quite leaves. It must rain here sometimes. The ground says so. There is the look of water about the lowest places — a slight darkening of the soil, a faint tide-mark on the upturned edge of one fallen slab, the dull, polished sheen of clay that has been wet for so long it has forgotten how to be dry. But there is no water now. There has been none today, perhaps not for days. The damp is a memory the earth keeps for itself.

Around the basin's edge, half-buried in the slope or lying where they long ago fell, are stones. A scatter of worked stones, mostly. You cannot tell at a glance that anyone shaped them — the weathering has been generous and patient — but here and there a flat face emerges from the moss, a corner that remains angular where the rest has softened, a groove that runs too straight to be the work of rain. They are few. They are spread out with great spaces between them, the way teeth are spread in an old mouth. There is the stub of a wall in the eastern curve of the hollow, no higher than your knee, built of small flat stones stacked dry and leaning now slightly inward as if tired. There is what might once have been a lintel lying flat across two uprights near the northern edge, forming a low table-shaped form. There is a ring of small stones set in the ground at the southwestern rim, perhaps a hearth, perhaps something else — the uses have drained away along with whatever mouths once made them.

The vegetation is sparse. It has the look of having tried, over long years, and mostly lost the argument. Mosses have done best — they are everywhere, in colors ranging from a dull green to a kind of silvery olive, painting the stones and the bare patches of earth with their quiet shag. Where the central mound rises there are a few thin shrubs, knee-high and woody, their branches reduced to gray armature and put out now only at the tips with a spattering of small leathery leaves that seem to be holding their breath. A single fern grows from a crack in a fallen block, its fronds standing unnaturally still, dusted at the edges with the pale ghostly growth of mold. There is lichen on every stone that has lain long enough to receive it, and a thin crust of it creeping up the low wall's remaining surfaces. All of this growth is slow — visibly, unmistakably slow. If you sat and watched for an hour you would see no leaf turn, no tendril advance. The plants here are not in the business of changing. They have arrived at some stasis that suits them.

You notice, after a time, the absence of birds. There are no birds. There are no insects that you can see, though once you have stood very still for a long moment you might hear — or imagine you hear — a faint rustling in the dry grass on the rim, but when you turn toward it there is nothing, and you understand it was the grass leaning against a neighbor under some infinitesimal shift of ground. There are no animals here at all, not even the small ones. No spiders in the corners of the broken stones, no ants drawing their lines in the dust, no wasps at the old hearth-ring. The hollow is not dead — the mosses are green, the shrubs have leaves — but it has been emptied of the quick, the moving, the warm-blooded. Or perhaps it was emptied a long time ago and has simply forgotten to refill. The places where they would have nested — the nooks in the standing stones, the sheltered concavities of the broken wall — are all quite uninhabited, a little dusty at the bottoms, but no more disturbed than that.

Sound, here, is a thin thing. There is no wind. The air does not move. The moss does not stir, the fern does not bend, the leaves on the shrubs hang in the still warm damp as if painted. What you hear is the small muffled ring of your own footfall when you step, and then, after the ring has faded, something beneath sound — not silence exactly, but the felt sense of a place where no sound has been needed for a very long time. The most that happens, acoustically, is occasionally a drop of condensed moisture leaving the underside of a stone and falling onto the earth with a small sound, a sound so quiet you might spend a long time unsure whether you heard it.

And yet the place does not feel abandoned. That is the strange thing. It feels *kept*. The stones, though scattered, do not seem wrecked; they seem placed and then left. The shrubs, though sparse, are not choked by the brambles of unchecked growth; something about their distribution suggests they were chosen once and then tolerated. The basin itself, the cup of land with its low rise at the center and its shallow moat at the rim, has the geometry of intention — the curve is too even, the placement of the largest worked stones at the four cardinal points is too deliberate, to be entirely the work of water and gravity. Something arranged this place in the loose way a hand arranges objects on a table, with gaps, with room, with care but without fuss. And then whatever hand it was withdrew, and the rest was the work of weather.

Time, here, has come to rest. Nothing is moving toward anything. The lichens grow at the speed of lichen — invisibly, in any span of attention you can offer. The water in the soil neither rises nor falls. The stones do not shift. The light does not change; the sky is the same flat bone-colored gray at morning as it will be at evening; nothing in the basin throws its own moving shadow. If you left now and returned in a year, you could not say with certainty that you had been away at all. The place does not wait for you. It does not, properly speaking, do anything. It simply is.

Stand in it long enough and you stop thinking of it as a ruin. There was a name for it once, and a use, and people who came here in the warm dim to do whatever the stones were for. The stones themselves have forgotten, the way the basin has, the way the mosses have, the way the shrubs in their thin greyness have. What remains is the form of the arrangement — the curve, the four stones, the rise, the moat — held in its own stillness like a held note allowed to die out, the air still faintly remembering the tone. You, the watcher, are a small disturbance walking through this held shape, neither welcome nor unwelcome, simply passing through, leaving no mark on the moss as you go.
