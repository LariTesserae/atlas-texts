# The Canopy-Town of Suwarrow's Reach

---

The first thing is the sound. You arrive hearing it before seeing anything: a roar that isn't one sound but a hundred thousand small ones braided together, the layered cacophony of a living city that has forgotten what silence is for. Insects. Birds. The wet slap of hides against woven frames. Somewhere far below, water. Always water.

The town is built into a fissure in a basalt cliff face, halfway up a mountain that takes an entire morning to climb. From a distance, in the rare break between rain squalls, you can see nothing but the cliff itself — dark stone weeping pale mineral runnels down its skin — and what looks like a smear of green-black moss halfway up. That is the town. That moss is the town.

Suwarrow's Reach sits where four hot springs emerge from a crack in the mountain's spine and spill, always, down through a notch in the rock to plunge a thousand feet as a permanent ribbon of vapor. The water doesn't pool at the bottom; it vanishes into a marsh that has been there since before anyone could remember. The town wraps the hot springs and the fissure, and the fissure is its body.

---

The architecture is plant and stone in a marriage so old no one alive remembers which came first.

The frame of every dwelling is a single living tree — not cut, not lumbered, but trained. The ancestors of this place found a mountain cedar with a particular temperament, a slow-grower whose heartwood flexes instead of cracking, and they bent them while young, lashing them with vine-cord to shaped basalt stones, and the trees grew into the shapes they were given. After a hundred years a bent cedar forgets it wanted to be straight and becomes the shape of the house it was made into. Some of these frames are six hundred years old and still putting out leaves. The houses are not houses so much as ongoing collaborations between a tree and the people who keep coaxing it along.

Between the frames: woven mats of split-bamboo and reed, thatched with the silver-gray lichen that grows only above this elevation, and roofed with the great cupped leaves of the *halberd-fern*, layered ten deep and waxed with rendered tallow. Moss fills every gap, deliberately encouraged, so the walls breathe and hold warmth. When it rains — which is to say, always — the rain does not enter. It runs down the curved leaf-roofs in thousand small falls, collects in bamboo gutters, and pours into the central channels that are the town's veins.

The dwellings are not on the ground. They are *between* the roots.

The cliff fissure is a hundred feet wide where the hot springs emerge, narrowing to nothing thirty feet above and a hundred below. The people of Suwarrow's Reach have built no floors. They have built their platforms into the living cedar roots that grip the basalt on either side of the fissure, so the town hangs in the cleft like a shelf-fungus, with the hot springs running through it and the rain falling through it from above and the marsh-light rising through it from below. Walkways are living wood, dark and springy underfoot, smelling of cedar and wet moss and faintly of sulfur.

The hot water runs everywhere.

It is the first thing you learn when you arrive, and the thing you never stop noticing. Channels of stone and split-log carry the spring-water in precise paths through every level of the town, by gravity alone, calibrated over centuries. The water is too hot to touch where it first emerges — you keep your palms above it and the heat pricks — but it cools as it descends, and by the time it reaches the lower levels it is bath-warm, and at the lowest roots it spills out into the air and falls.

Every level of the town has its water-function. Topmost, the scalding channels heat the lichen-roofs and keep the air warm and the mist thick. The middle channels run beside the cooking-platforms — great flat stones the women have been blackening for generations with lard and ash, and on them now steam-trays of river-reed packed with tuber and root and the meat of the small highland pig. Lower down, the warm water is for washing, and the bathing-stones are polished smooth by a thousand backs. At the very bottom, the water leaves the town. There is a final stone lip, carved by people whose names are forgotten, where the warm runoff pours over and becomes rain in the empty air below, joining the ribbon-fall that has been falling since the mountain made the spring.

The mist rises up from below and falls down from above, and the town sits in the middle of it, and you can never see more than forty feet in any direction unless the wind tears the mist open for a moment and you see the dark root-systems of the cedars fading into the gray above and the gray below, and below that, a long, long time later, the pale glimmer of the marsh.

---

The animals are everywhere and they are the point.

The cedar-trees are a forest above the town and a roof to it, and in their branches live the *hook-beaks* — black, long-winged, squawking birds the size of small children, that nest by the thousand in the upper levels and feed on the flying insects that breed in the hot water. They smell, sharply, of wet feathers and fish. They are not owned. They are tolerated, the way a people tolerates weather. When they scream in the morning, the town wakes. When they go quiet in the evening, the town sleeps. Their droppings are collected — it is a child's task, the daily round — and packed into clay jars and traded down the mountain, because the *hook-beak* guano is what makes the highland terracing possible at all.

In the middle levels, between the walkways, in the warm wet corners of every dwelling, the *sloth-moss* grazes. It is a creature, not a plant. It is the size of a large dog, soft-bodied, gray-green, with a wide rasping mouth that it puts against the lichen of the wall and slowly eats, an inch an hour. There are hundreds of them. They are kept. They are eaten, on the long-occasion, though their flesh is pale and tastes of cedar. More importantly: they keep the walls from overgrowing. Without the *sloth-moss*, the lichen would close every passage in a season. With them, the lichen stays in the breath-line — a thick green band along every wall, always exactly as tall as a *sloth-moss* can reach, and no higher. The walls of every house end at the same height: the moss-line.

Below, in the warm-water channels, the *glass-eels*. Silver, translucent, the length of your forearm, in such numbers that the channels look like moving metal. They eat the algae that would otherwise clog the water-flow. They are caught, dried, eaten, traded. A bad eel-year is a hunger-year. A good eel-year is the only reason anyone marries in late summer.

The pigs are kept in pens of living willow on the lowest platforms — eight families to a pen, communally — because their rooting keeps the cedar-roots from growing through the walkways. The pigs are small, dark-furred, foul-tempered. They know their names. They come when called, usually.

The marsh below is its own ecology and its own warning. Twice a year, in the high summer and the deep winter, something rises out of it — a smell, a sound, a long low bellowing that goes on for hours — and the whole town goes quiet, and the hook-beaks wheel in the sky above in formations that are not quite flocks. No one goes down to the marsh in those seasons. The lowest platforms are emptied. The pigs are brought up. The elders stand at the rim of the cleft and sing a song that has no words and last an hour, and after the bellowing ends the town resumes. This has been the rule for a thousand years. What the bellowing is, no one has ever agreed.

---

The people.

They are dark-skinned, short, broad-shouldered, with calloused hands and the particular stoop of people who have climbed all their lives. They speak a language dense with verbs for *temperature of water* and *depth of root*. They do not weave cloth — there is no need; the air is always warm, always wet — but they weave everything else: mats, baskets, rope, the great hanging fish-traps that drop from the walkways on weighted lines. Their tools are stone, bone, fired-clay, and the hardest of them is the *hook-beak* beak itself, split and ground, which they use as a chisel for shaping cedar.

The town has a chief, but the chief decides mostly when to open and close the water-channels for cleaning, and arbitrates disputes over root-rights. A house is not a building, it is a *relationship* with a tree, and the tree belongs to the family that first bent it, but only as long as the family keeps up its half of the conversation — pruning here, guiding there, plastering the moss-line, fetching tallow for the roof. A family that neglects its tree finds, over years, that the tree has begun to straighten back. After three generations of neglect, the dwelling is open to claim by anyone willing to start over. This has happened seven times in recorded history and is the subject of all seven of the longest songs.

The town has no metal, no wheels, no writing. It has the *year-rope*, a single braided cord that runs the length of the central walkway, and each year a new knot is added in a particular fiber, and a particular colored thread is woven through the knots of the years in which certain things happened — the great flood, the year of the long bellow, the year the third spring opened. The rope is now four hundred feet long and weighs eighty pounds and is kept in a cedar box at the town's center, and it is the closest thing they have to a history.

---

The weather is the town's true master.

Rain falls, in this place, eight days out of ten. It falls straight down, sideways, and back up (when the wind shifts). It falls as mist and as column and as the slow patient drip of a cliff weeping. The hook-beaks love it. The sloth-mosses slow down in it, and the elders complain that the *sloth-mosses* have grown fat this wet season and the walls are getting away from us. The eels are most active in it. The pigs refuse to leave their pens in the heaviest rains and stand packed together, grunting, their small black eyes miserable.

Lightning is common and is treated with indifference, because the cedars are the tallest things for miles and have been taking the strikes for centuries. The sound of a cedar being hit — a great wet green *crack*, and the long slow rumble of the upper trunk falling — is just part of the weather. The fallen cedar is dragged, branch by branch, to the lower platforms and is the occasion for a feast.

The wind is rarer and is feared. When the wind comes up from the marsh, it brings the smell of the bellow-season and the town's eyes go to the lowest platforms. The wind also brings, sometimes, traders from the lower mountain, who climb for three days to reach the cleft and then sit, soaked, on the upper platforms and bargain for eels and guano and the rare pale lichen that grows only on the highest cliff-faces. They bring, in exchange, dried marsh-reed and the small carved clay figurines of the *moss-line*, which the people of Suwarrow's Reach will not make for themselves.

---

The atmosphere.

It is warm, even at the cool edge of the cool-season. It is wet, but never cold. The mist is in your lungs from the first breath and you never stop being aware of it — a fine damp pressure against your face, a softening of every edge, a world seen through gauze. Sound travels strangely in it. Voices carry a long way and then stop. The hook-beak screams layer and braid with the constant rush of the springs and the deeper, farther sound of the marsh and the highest, thinnest sound of wind across the upper cliff-faces, and the whole is a kind of music the town does not notice any longer, the way you do not notice your own pulse.

The light is gray-green, filtered through leaves and mist together. Shadows are soft. Color is muted. The strongest color in the place is the orange of the lichen at the moss-line, vivid against the dark wet cedar, and the pale silver of the sloth-moss, and the occasional shocking red of a *hook-beak* in flight, catching a momentary slant of sun through a break in the cloud.

At night — when there is a night, when the cloud lifts enough to admit a moon — the hot springs glow faintly from some mineral in the water, and the town is a constellation of dim orange threads running up and down and across, and the sloth-mosses sleep in their wall-niches with their soft bodies heaving slowly, and the hook-beaks are silent, and you can hear the water.

You can always hear the water.

---

This is a place that has found its balance and is holding it, trembling, in the cupped hand of a basalt cliff above a marsh that bellows twice a year. It is a place where every wall is alive, where the rain is the second ruler, where the lower platforms are emptied in summer and winter for reasons no one will fully name. It is a town made by training trees to be architecture, fed by hot springs, kept warm by mist, kept alive by a careful negotiation between the people and every other living thing in the cleft.

It will be here when you leave. It was here before you arrived. It will go on being here in the small fierce way that a place goes on being a place: a sound, a smell, a particular quality of damp light, a particular arrangement of green and gray and orange, the constant patient negotiation of the moss-line, the slow turning of the cedar-roots around their shaped stones.

The water falls. The cedars grow. The hook-beaks scream the morning in.
