# The Half-Carved Sanctuary at Tengmire

The approach is a long, slow bend through stone that has not finished deciding what it is. Slabs of raw granite shoulder up through thin soil, their faces still bearing the chisel-marks and pry-grooves of work interrupted — or abandoned, or paused for reasons no one living remembers. The work is not old, exactly, but it has the quality of old. Time has moved differently here, patient with itself.

The basin is small enough to feel held. One could stand at its rim and throw a stone across, and the stone would clatter twice — once off the far wall, once into the water. The walls rise perhaps four times a man's height, mostly sheer, mostly vertical, the kind of cut that suggests both intent and the threat of more cut to come. Bedrock is exposed everywhere. The settlement has been *let into* the stone rather than built *upon* it.

## The Water

A spring rises at the basin's north end, not from any visible crack but from a low, mossy throat that seems to exhale rather than pour. The water is cold and very clear, carrying a faint mineral taste — iron, and something else, something that prickles at the back of the throat. It pools in a shallow basin the diggers have deliberately widened and deepened, its surface so still it serves as the settlement's only mirror when the light cooperates.

From the pool, a narrow channel runs the length of the floor, chiseled into the living rock with a regularity that suggests years of careful work. It spills over the basin's southern lip and begins — begins only — its journey down the mountainside. A thin bright thread of water, audible as a continuous hiss from anywhere in the settlement, white-noise against which all other sounds must place themselves. In wet weather the channel becomes a creek. In dry weather it becomes a question.

The walls weep. Everywhere the bare stone is wet with condensation or seepage; the granite here is shot with fine fractures that carry groundwater from somewhere higher up. This weeping gives the rock a darker patina in vertical streaks, and supports a riot of moss and liverwort in every horizontal crack. Where the diggers have carved alcoves — and they have carved many — the stone within sweats and glistens.

## The Vegetation

Despite the elevation, despite the cool air, despite the rock that is the dominant substance here — the green is overwhelming. It is not forest. It is the green of things that have learned to live in a wound.

Mosses have claimed every unmortared surface. They are thick as velvet in some places, shaggy in others, a dozen species layering themselves from pale celadon at the driest margins to deep almost-black green in the constant damp of the lower walls. Where the seepage concentrates, small ferns have taken root in the cracks, their fronds the size of a man's hand, trembling in any breath of air. Lichens crust the chisel-faces in pale gray-green rosettes.

Three small trees stand at the basin's eastern edge, where a natural fault provides a deeper soil pocket. They are crooked things, contorted by wind, their bark furred with moss. Their branches meet overhead in summer to make a ragged canopy that drips constantly. They are older than the settlement. They may be the reason the spring is here.

In late summer, a smell rises from this green — green and wet and cold, the smell of things growing very slowly in places that do not want them.

## The Carving

This is what defines the place: it is being made.

The walls are inscribed. Not with writing — with forms. A frieze runs around the basin at chest height, a continuous band of figures, animals, abstract glyphs, all cut into the rock with patient hand-tools: chisel and mallet, iron-point and wooden wedge. Some sections are deep and finished, their surfaces polished to a soft sheen by years of touch. Others are sketched only, faint outlines waiting to be deepened. Others again are roughed out but abandoned, the figures only half-emerging from the stone, their other halves still trapped in the rock.

No one knows what the frieze will become. No one knows who began it. The diggers — for they call themselves this, though they are stonemasons, sculptors, something else — speak of it only obliquely. They say it was here when they arrived. They say it was less here then. They say the stone remembers.

They work, in any case. Always two or three are somewhere on the walls, their chip-chip-chip audible across the basin, sometimes a sharper crack as a wedge drives a flake free, sometimes the groan of a larger block being levered out. The sound is never absent. It is the settlement's pulse.

## The Settlement

Roughly thirty people live here. They have built no above-ground structures — or rather, they have built few. Instead they occupy the alcoves the diggers have carved into the basin walls, each alcove deepened and shaped into a small room, fitted with a wooden lintel and a hide curtain. Smoke rises from a few; most are unheated. The rooms are dark, damp, and cool, and the people who sleep in them seem pale and quiet.

The center of the basin holds a larger chamber, cut deeper than the rest, roofed with a single massive slab that has not yet been detached from the surrounding rock. Here the spring emerges. Here a fire burns always — or nearly always — its smoke finding its way out through a chimney carved up through forty feet of granite, a vertical shaft that the wind makes to moan in different keys. The fire is tended; someone is always sitting near it. They will offer you tea.

There is a path down — the path you came in by — and several paths that peter out into unfinished galleries, half-carved tunnels that descend a little way and stop, their faces raw and unstamped, waiting.

## The Atmosphere

It is always cool here. Even at midday in summer, the air in the basin carries a chill off the wet stone and the snowmelt that feeds the spring. In winter it is colder than the elevation would suggest, because the basin traps cold air and the rock holds it.

The light is good but particular. The basin faces roughly east, so morning light reaches the floor directly, falling across the frieze and making the chisel-marks throw long shadows. By afternoon the basin is in shade except for a bright blade of sun that slides down the western wall, illuminating one figure after another in the frieze as the hours pass — a slow diurnal cinema, a procession of forms the diggers have come to read like hours on a clock.

The air is very wet. Fog gathers often, sometimes in the morning, sometimes in the evening, sitting in the basin like a lake until the sun or a breeze breaks it up. Sound carries strangely here, muffled in fog, sharp and close in clear air. The diggers' chipping, when it comes, can sound as though it is happening inside your own skull.

## The Sound

The settlement is loud, in its way. Three voices, mostly:

The **water**, always — the hiss and small splash of the channel, the distant deeper sound of the spring's throat, the patter of drips from every weeping face of stone.

The **chipping**, the small percussive industry of the diggers, sometimes single, sometimes layered three or four deep, the rhythm of which becomes almost musical after an hour's listening.

The **wind** in the chimney, which sings. It is not a pleasant song — it is a low, uneasy note, sliding unpredictably through harmonies as the gusts change, occasionally breaking into a whistle, occasionally into something that sounds almost like speech. The diggers seem not to hear it anymore.

There are other sounds: the knock of wooden tools being set down, the soft footfall on wet stone, the occasional low conversation. But these are guests under the three principal voices.

## The Fauna

Almost nothing lives here by choice. The elevation and the cold and the wet select against it. A few birds nest in the crooked trees — small, drab, querulous things whose calls are swallowed by the larger sounds. Occasionally something larger passes through: a fox on some errand of its own, a hawk circling above the basin. It does not stay. Nothing stays that is not a part of the work.

The people do not seem to mind. The absence of animals has the effect of making the human presence — the few of them there are — more concentrated, more visible against the stone and the green.

## The Rule of the Place

There is no law here that anyone will tell you. There is, instead, a custom, which is this: you may stay if you are working. If you are not working, you will be asked, gently, to begin. If you have no skill that can be applied to the frieze or to the channels or to the deepening of the rooms, you will be given a task. There are always tasks. The stone is not finished.

The diggers do not speak of what the frieze depicts or will depict. If pressed, they will say only that the stone knows, or that they will know when it is done, or that the work is the meaning.

Visitors come, sometimes. They leave.

## The Unfinished Quality

The place has the feeling of a sentence interrupted mid-word. The frieze circles the walls but does not close; it breaks off at two points where fresh carving begins, as though the older carving was a draft and this is the true version, or as though the older carving was abandoned when the diggers understood, or misunderstood, something. The galleries end in raw stone. The channels run where they have been cut and no further. The spring emerges from a place no one has yet had the courage to deepen.

There is a sense — not ominous, exactly, but present — that the place is not waiting for the diggers to finish it. It is waiting, with the patience of stone, for the diggers to finish *themselves*.

The chipping goes on.

The water runs.

The green grows, very slowly, in the cracks of things that are not yet finished becoming what they will be.
