# The Vault of Slow Rain

You stand at the mouth of a sinkhole.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere — a pale, aqueous glow that doesn't fall so much as *seep* down through the open throat of the crater above, filtered through three hundred feet of hanging roots and the perpetual mist that clings to them. It is midday somewhere far overhead. Down here it is the color of old bone, of weak tea, of an eye seen through gauze.

The air is warm. Heavily warm. The kind of warmth that doesn't relieve itself when you sweat because there is nowhere for the moisture to evaporate to. It just sits on your skin, beading, and is joined by other beads of moisture — drifting from the leaves above, condensing on every surface, gathering in pools at the base of every column. You are inside a lung that breathes water.

## The Floor

The floor of the sinkhole is not flat. It has settled, over what must be many centuries, into a series of soft terraces — natural steps in the limestone where layers have given way at different rates. Between the steps run shallow channels of slow brown water, the color of tea brewed too long. The water is warm. Where it pauses, in the deeper basins, a thin skin of something iridescent floats on its surface — neither oil nor algae but something between, a film that shivers when a drop falls into it from above.

The limestone itself is the color of butter when it is dry, and the color of tallow when it is not. It has been carved, gently, by the patient water into low rounded forms — no sharp edges anywhere, no dramatic drops. Even where a step descends, it descends softly, like a lip.

## The Columns and the Lungs of the Place

What strikes you first, when you look up, are the roots.

They descend from the rim of the sinkhole and from the trunks of the trees that grow along it — banyan-like things, ficus-like things, things with no proper name that you have ever heard. The roots come down in thick ropes, dozens of them, and where they meet the air of the chamber they sprout. They sprout *downward*. Pale white root-tips reach for the floor like fingers searching for a glass of water in the dark. They thicken. They become pillars. Where they finally touch the limestone they have become trunks in their own right, and from those new trunks more roots descend.

This is a forest growing upside down. It is also a forest growing right-side up. The geometry is unclear. It is not a mirror — it is more like a slow negotiation between the two directions, and the forest has forgotten which is which.

The original floor of the sinkhole must be somewhere beneath all this, but you cannot see it. The roots and trunks have become architecture. There are chambers within the root-mass — small grottos walled by curving pale wood, floored by the same warm tallow-stone. The air inside them is closer, damper, and the light is greener.

## The Marks of Hands

In one of the side chambers — and there are several, opening off the main bowl like cels around a hub — someone has been.

Not recently. The marks are old. There is a low wall built of stacked stones, no taller than a knee, ringing what might once have been a hearth. There are scraps of something that might be pottery or might be the broken shells of very large nuts, scattered and half-buried in the silt. There is a dark stain on one wall that is not quite a handprint but is not quite not one either.

There are no other artifacts. No metal. No worked wood. Whatever people lived here — and you suspect they did not live here so much as *inhabit* it, the way a bird inhabits a particular branch — they left almost nothing behind. Or almost everything has been taken back.

The chamber has the feeling of a mouth after the last syllable.

## The Water Itself

The water is alive, in the older sense of the word.

It moves, but not with current. It *percolates*. You can watch it for an hour and see almost no motion, then notice that the pool that was on your left has migrated, by millimeters, to your right. It carries warmth up from somewhere deeper. It carries down with it small things — pale seed-cases, fragments of root-skin, the husks of small dead things — and it deposits them in layers on the submerged stone, building terraces of their own inside its own terraces.

There are fish in the water. You did not notice them at first because they do not move like fish. They hang. They drift. They are the color of the water itself, with two small dark eyes that are the only sure sign they are alive. Sometimes one surfaces — slowly, with the unhurried dignity of a bubble — and takes a piece of the iridescent skin on the surface, and goes back down. They have no names. They are not large.

If you sit very still at the edge of one of the larger pools, more of them come. They arrange themselves in your vicinity with what feels like consideration. They are not afraid of you. They are not anything about you. They are simply present with you, in the way the water is present with the stone.

## The Heat and the Sound

The heat is from below. You cannot see the source, but you can feel it through the soles of your feet when you stand still long enough — a low pulse, almost a frequency, that suggests something far down is exhaling into the rock. The stone radiates. The water carries. The air receives.

Sound is minimal. The drop of water from a root-tip into a pool — *tok* — and then the long silence that follows. A creak of wood, somewhere up in the root-canopy, as a trunk sways under its own increasing weight. The wet rasp of something moving across limestone — and when you turn quickly, you find only a small pale thing, no longer than your thumb, withdrawing into a crack. Its legs are many. It moves deliberately. It is in no hurry.

The loudest sound, by far, is your own breath.

## The Dim Light and What It Reveals

Because the light is so weak and so flat, the colors of the place have drained into a narrow range — bone, tallow, tea-brown, the faint green of new root-flesh, the silver of the iridescent films. There is no blue. There is no red. The world has been rendered in the palette of something very old, or very tired, or very patient.

And yet — when a cloud passes overhead, and the mist up at the rim of the sinkhole thins for a moment — a shaft of true light comes down. It falls through three hundred feet of humid air and arrives on the floor still strong enough to throw shadows. For perhaps thirty seconds the chamber is transformed. The roots overhead glow. The water gleams. The pale walls of the grottos burn briefly gold. You can see every detail of the place — the fish suspended in their impossible stillness, the lines of the old low wall, the shape of the dark stain on the grotto wall — and then the mist thickens again and the place returns to its dim dreaming.

You find yourself waiting, in the long hours, for those shafts.

## The Animals

There are more animals than you first noticed.

There is the small pale many-legged thing in the cracks. There are the still fish in the pools. There are things that move along the underside of the horizontal roots, upside down to your orientation, hanging by hooks or pads or slow adhesive — small dark shapes that pause and regard you with grouped eyes before continuing on their way. There are worms, pale and pink, that move through the damp silt. There is something larger that you glimpse only once, sliding along a far terrace and then gone — low, sinuous, the color of the stone.

None of them are loud. None of them are fast. They all move with the tempo of the place — which is the tempo of the water, which is the tempo of the heat rising through the stone, which is the tempo of roots growing in the dark.

The fauna here is not aggressive. It is not even particularly alert. It is simply *continuous* — a slow circulation of small lives through a small architecture, the way the water circulates, the way the heat circulates, the way the dim light, in its rare shafts, circulates.

## The Long Sense

You understand, after you have been here for some time, that this place is not dying and not being born. It is *being*. It has been being for a very long while. The roots have grown and the water has risen and the silt has layered and the fish have drifted and the small things have scratched their pale paths through the cracks, and the place has settled into a pattern that holds all of them. It will hold you too, for as long as you stay.

When you leave — and you will leave, because the dim light is not enough, in the end, for a creature evolved for the open sky — you will carry the warmth of it on your skin for hours. You will feel the absence of that wet dim patience like a small withdrawal. And somewhere above, where the sinkhole opens to ordinary weather, the trees will go on sending their roots down into the chamber, and the chamber will go on receiving them, and the water will go on percolating, and the fish will go on hanging in the warm brown light.

The place will not notice your leaving.

It will go on being, very quietly, the vault it has always been.
