# The Engine Choir

The floor of the Caul is a salt-pale plane, cracked into hexagons the size of dinner plates, and every hexagon is bleeding heat. Not visibly—nothing shimmers here, nothing wavers—but you can feel it through the soles of your boots, a low-grade fever that has no source you can point to. The air is warm the way a closed hand is warm. It tastes of iron and powdered bone.

You are below the surface. Far below. The roof is low—not a ceiling so much as an underside, a down-facing geology that sags in long petrified drapes, as though a great tongue of limestone once drooped and was stopped in the act and froze there. Stalactites, if you insist on the old word, but these are wrong. They are stratified in a way no dripping water could produce: each one banded in alternating cream and rust and a curious bluish grey, the colors too regular, too deliberate, like the layers of a cake baked by something with very long hands. Between them, the rock is pocked with sockets, and out of the sockets grow the pipes.

This is the first thing you understand about the place: it is full of pipes.

They come in every diameter, from a finger's width to a trunk a person could crawl through standing on tiptoe. Some are vertical, driven into the floor like organ stops; some run horizontally, bundled in threes and sevens and twelves, lashed together with metal bands gone orange with oxidation. They do not all appear to be made of the same material. Some are cast iron, flaking. Some are ceramic, glazed in a yellow that is almost cheerful. Some are clearly bone, or something pretending to be bone, segmented and slightly curved. The bigger ones are dark stone, polished by what must be decades of hands touching them, and warm to the touch. They hum.

The sound is the second thing. There is no quiet here. The pipes are doing something—always doing something—and the result is a constant, low, almost musical throb that gets into your molars. It is not one note. It is a chord built of many notes that drift in and out of phase with each other, so that the air itself seems to thicken and thin in slow waves, and you can feel the pressure change against your eardrums like a pulse. If you stand still long enough you begin to hear structure: long sustained tones beneath, a fast clicking chatter above, and somewhere in the middle a sliding moan that might be steam or might be a voice that has forgotten words. It is the sound a factory would make if a factory were a living thing with a fever and a grudge.

The light is sourceless. There is no lamp, no grate overhead admitting sun, no window. But the rock itself emits a dim amber glow, as though the minerals have been charged with something and are slowly, patiently, discharging. The light is not enough to read by. It is enough to see by, but only just, and only if your eyes have been in the dark a while. It thickens near the larger pipes, which sweat a luminous condensation that runs down their flanks and pools in the hex-cracks of the floor. Where the liquid evaporates it leaves a residue like dried honey, and these little drifts of golden crust are the closest things to paths the place has.

The fauna lives in the pipes.

You do not see them at first. You see the pipes, and the hum, and the warm light, and you think: infrastructure. You think: ruins. You think: somewhere, long ago, someone built this. Then something moves in the pipe beside you—a soft, boneless shifting, the sound of a wet cloth being squeezed—and you see, through a gap in a flange, a shape.

It is hard to describe because it does not have a single shape. It is a thing of many parts packed tightly together, reddish-brown and glistening, and the parts are not quite the same as each other, though they share a family resemblance. Some are tubular. Some are folded, like the gills of a mushroom pressed flat. Some are open at one end and ringed with fine cilia that rotate slowly, constantly, drawing air in and pushing it out again in a thin, purposeful stream. They breathe through the pipes. They live inside the pipes. The pipes are their habitat and their instrument and, you begin to suspect, their body, the way a reef is a body, the way a city is a body.

There are a great many of them. The place is dense with them. Every pipe you pass is occupied; you can tell by the slight bulge where a cluster presses against the wall, by the faint thrum of their collective respiration vibrating through the metal. When you walk between the larger pipes they shift to accommodate you, not by moving out of the way but by compressing, by making room in the way a crowd makes room, and the small ones—the finger-width pipes—click and chatter in agitation as your shadow crosses their mouths. You learn to step over them. You learn to feel, through the floor, where the big colonies are, and to skirt them, because standing over them is like standing over a beehive. They do not sting. They do something stranger. They *attune* to you. If you stand still long enough over a populous vein, you begin to feel your own breathing synchronize with the chord, and then your heartbeat, and then a slower rhythm beneath your heartbeat that you had not previously been aware of having, and the lights along the pipes begin to pulse in time with it, and you understand, with a small cold clarity, that the place is listening to you, and has decided you are part of the chord now, and would very much like you to stay.

The vegetation, sparse as it is, is the most unsettling thing. It grows only at the junctures—where three or more pipes meet at a node, where a valve-wheel has been removed and a ragged hole left behind, where condensation has gathered for long enough to form a shallow cup. It is not green. Nothing here is green. It is a deep, bruised purple, almost black, and it has the structure of neither plant nor fungus but something between, something that has given up the distinction: flat rosettes of fleshy lobes that drink the light rather than drink water, each lobe veined with the same amber that glows in the rock. They are warm. They are slightly sticky. They smell of molasses and hot metal. When the pipe-fauna pass beneath them, the lobes curl slowly inward, and when the fauna have passed, the lobes uncurl, and a faint dust of golden spores is released, and the spores drift, and the spores settle, and somewhere else, in another junction, another rosette is beginning. The whole place is, in this small way, propagating.

The built things—structures, you want to call them, but that is not quite right—are scattered and partial. A catwalk, here, running between two great pipes at shoulder height, its railings eaten to lacework but the planks still sound, still solid, bolted with square-headed bolts of a metal that has gone the color of dried blood. A console, there, against a wall: a slab of pale stone fitted with levers and dials, but the levers are crusted over with the same golden residue, fused to their housings, and the dials are obscured by a skin of the purple growth, and whether anything still works beneath the overgrowth is not a question you can answer by looking. A staircase, going down, into the floor, its first three steps worn to shallow scoops by countless feet; the fourth step is missing, and beyond the gap there is only dark, and the hum grows louder. No one has been down those stairs in a long time. The purple lobes have crept over the railing and are busy digesting the handrail inch by inch, and the work is patient and will not be hurried.

It is warm, and it is dim, and it is loud, and it does not change. The chord is always the chord. The pipes always hum. The fauna always breathe. The purple rosettes always curl and uncurl. The golden residue always creeps a little further. The place is in equilibrium, has been in equilibrium for a long time, and will be in equilibrium for a long time after, and the equilibrium is a kind of life, and the life is a kind of engine, and the engine is a kind of choir, and the choir has been singing the same sustained, drifting, phase-shifting chord since before you arrived and will continue singing it after you have been absorbed into the harmony, which is not a threat, exactly, but it is also not an invitation you are in a position to refuse. The pipes hum. The lobes curl. The light pulses. The chord holds.

You sit down on a worn step. The warm rock accepts you. Above you, the layered stalactites drip nothing. Around you, the choir sings on. Somewhere in the middle distance, a great bone-pipe shifts its note by a quarter-tone, and the whole place shudders faintly, and the purple growth ripples, and the amber light flares for a moment, and then subsides, and the chord re-establishes itself, and you feel, against your will, the slow synchronization beginning again in your chest, and you let it happen, because the place is warm, and the sound is not unkind, and there is nowhere else to go that is not the same chord in a different key.
