# The Feldspar Shelf

The plateau is not so much a place as a *condition*: the air is thin to the point of being a courtesy, the sky is a permanent pale copper, and the ground goes on in every direction with the patience of something that has never been in a hurry to be anywhere. The elevation here is so extreme that you can feel it in the soft complaint at the back of your skull, in the way your blood takes a half-beat longer to reach your fingertips. There is no horizon so much as an argument between land and sky that both have agreed to lose. To the east the terrain steps downward in long geological fault-lines, each a darker rust than the last, until finally, very far away, something the color of dried blood — a sea, perhaps, or the memory of one.

**There is no water.** What there is, instead, is a fine alkaline powder that lifts when something moves through it and lies down again when nothing does. The powder has a name in three languages, none of which are spoken anymore. When the wind comes, which is not often and not strongly, it pushes this dust in low sheets across the basalt pavements, and you can see the city change shape in the time it takes to walk two blocks: a parapet you knew yesterday is now a soft hillock, a doorway is now an ambiguous dimple.

**The city itself** sits in a saddle of volcanic origin, an old caldera that long ago decided it was finished being dramatic. The buildings are tall and close-packed near the center, then thinner and more spaced as they move outward, until they become a single tower every kilometer or so, then a single ruin, then a cairn, then nothing at all. They are built of a dark local stone shot through with veins of green and a metal that is almost-but-not-quite copper, and the centuries have given them the same ochre patina as the surrounding rock. From a distance the city looks like a thing the plateau grew, the way the plateau grows its quartz crystals — slowly, from the inside, in its own time.

**The industry** is old. It is hard to say what it makes. There are long low buildings with chimneys that have not smoked in living memory, and the chimneys are themselves made of the same dark stone as everything else, and they are themselves growing the same rust. There are rails — narrow-gauge, set into the streets without any visible breaks for pedestrians — and there are things that travel on them, slow and clicking, that may be vehicles or may simply be the rails doing something purposeful at long intervals. There is a sound at night from the deepest part of town, a low tectonic hum, as if somewhere below the city a large machine is reminding itself of its function. Nobody seems to be in charge of it. Nobody has, perhaps, for a very long while. The industry has the look of a reef — something that was once a process and is now a habitat.

**The fauna** is the strange part, and it ought to be more reassuring than it is. There are creatures. They are everywhere, in numbers that would embarrass a rainforest, and they are not quite alive in any sense you would have been taught. Some of them are small, dust-colored, soft-bodied, and they move in drifts along the base of walls like the powder itself, only purposeful — they are digesting the powder, or the dust is digesting them, the difference is unclear and probably seasonal. Some of them are tall, jointed, with carapaces of fused alkali and metallic whiskers that tick against the stone as they walk; they browse on the windward faces of buildings, scraping away a few microns of patina at a time. The largest of them, seen only at dusk when the copper light is thickest, are the size of a small house and they move with the deliberation of glaciers; nobody knows what they eat, and they have not been approached. They make no sound except, occasionally, a single resonant note from somewhere deep in their bodies, the way a struck anvil rings — once, faintly, and then not again for weeks.

The creatures do not seem to fear the city. The city does not seem to mind the creatures. There is some long-standing arrangement between them, an arrangement whose terms were negotiated before anyone now alive was born and which neither side has an interest in revisiting.

**The sounds** are sparse and discrete. The wind, when it comes, is a thin hiss against the stones, more felt than heard. The little clicking of the rail-cars is a punctuation, not a music. Somewhere in the middle distance a creature taps, taps, taps against a wall and then stops. A child, occasionally, very far off, in a language the wind will not bring you. The hum from below the city is felt in the bones more than the ears.

**The light** is the color of weak tea and arrives from everywhere at once — there is no particular sun to point at, only a brighter region of the sky that moves with the hours in the way sun is meant to, but languidly, as if it too has been worn down by the elevation. Shadows are short and bluish. At midday a column of light will fall down a chimney-shaft and you will see, far below, the faint orange suggestion of whatever the city is no longer doing.

**Time** moves here as if through a thicker medium than elsewhere. The dust takes decades to migrate a meter. The patina takes generations to thicken by a finger's width. The creatures, the slow ones, do not so much move as *resolve* from one place into another over the course of a season. A wall falls and is not rebuilt in your lifetime; a street is buried and a new one is worn, very gradually, around it; the chimneys continue their slow conversation with the sky, the sky continuing its slow conversation with the chimneys, neither one waiting on the other.

It is not a hostile place. It is not even, in any obvious sense, a sad one. It is the kind of place that has *finished* with most of the things places are usually in the middle of, and is now waiting, with a kind of worn and courteous patience, for whatever it is that comes next. If you sit on a parapet long enough, with the dust settling around your boots and a small alkali creature working diligently at the toe of your left shoe, you can feel that the plateau is, in its own quiet and considered way, *pleased* with itself.

It has been here a long time. It intends to be here a long time still.
