# The Verdant Flats of Lirath-Kesh

The horizon here does not behave as horizons should. It curves, sometimes gently, sometimes with a suggestion of an edge that the eye refuses to finish. Looking out across the Flats, the traveler first sees a single shade of green — a deep, almost metallic emerald, the color of lichen on wet iron, or of certain beetles. Then, as the eye adjusts, the green begins to stratify.

There is no water. There has never been water. The chemistry of Lirath-Kesh is built on something else: a dense, volatile fluid that pools invisibly in the capillaries of the upper crust, vapor at midday, syrup at dusk. It does not quench. It does not flow in any way a hydrologist would recognize. The plants — if they are plants — drink it from below, through structures that resemble roots only when seen at a certain angle, and otherwise look more like the way a magnet looks when drawn on iron filings.

The flora is lush beyond any reasonable definition of *lush*. This is the first thing that strikes a visitor and the last thing they manage to put into words. The ground is carpeted in vegetation that grows in the absence of almost every known input. There are no seasons here. Nothing dies and falls. Nothing rots. The plants are immortal in the way that crystals are immortal — not because they live forever, but because the concept of *death* has been edited out of the local physics. Leaves unfurl, mature, and then, instead of senescing, simply *stop*, becoming part of a static sculpture of greens, browns, occasional furious violets, occasional pale greys that should be fungal but are not.

The effect is of a garden that has been perfectly preserved at the peak of its becoming. A spore-pod from a million years ago is still half-emerged from its parent stem. A frond three meters across is caught at the moment of opening, the way a flower is caught in ice, except there is no ice. There is only *then*, preserved in a layer of local time that does not advance.

---

The light here is the kind of light that the word *golden* was invented to describe, though it tends more toward amber, more toward the color of heated glass. It arrives from no clearly identifiable source. The sky is not blue, not grey, not pink. The sky is a uniform off-white, like the inside of a shell, or the page of an old book left too long in a sunny room. Occasionally a shadow passes across the Flats, slowly, and one looks up expecting a cloud, but there is nothing. The shadows pass anyway, as if something very large and very far away is moving over a feature that does not exist.

This is the first sign of the *strangeness*. Most visitors do not recognize it as such. They make a note, perhaps, that they felt briefly cold. Then they walk on.

---

The ground is young, geologically speaking. The Flats emerged — and here the word *emerged* is doing considerable work, because there is no agreement on what they emerged *from* — only a few hundred thousand years ago, or possibly a few days ago, depending on which instrument is consulted. The instruments themselves do not agree. A theodolite set down at the edge of the Flats will, on three successive readings, give three different elevations, and one of them will be a small, offended letter, in a script no one admits to having seen before.

The features are sharp, almost deliberately so. Ridges cut the green at the horizon like the blade of a knife that has not been used. Cliffs are not weathered; they are *placed*. The young terrain has not had time to soften, but in places where softening would have occurred, instead there is a kind of visual insistence — a refusal of the world to be other than it was the instant it became. Erosion has been, in some local sense, *postponed*. Water has not run here. Wind has not scoured. The air is still. The air has always been still. The air, the visitor eventually understands, is the same air, the very same molecules, looping in slow, immortal gyres between the ridges.

---

There is no fauna. This is the second thing that strikes a visitor, after the *lush*. It is a wrongness. The vegetation is so rich, so varied, so suggestive of niches and diets and the entire chain of *eating and being eaten*, that the absence of any moving animal life produces a low, constant cognitive dissonance, like a held chord that does not resolve. There are no insects. There are no birds. There are no worms, no mites, no bacteria, no fungi. The ecosystem is a single kingdom, standing alone, elaborately, obsessively self-sufficient, a library of just one letter of the alphabet repeated into infinity.

And yet — the visitor is watched. There is no doubt of this. The Flats have a quality of attention. One feels the gaze the way one feels sunlight on closed eyelids. It does not come from any visible point. It comes from the *green* itself, or from the light, or from the off-white sky, or from the way the air moves (does not move). There is something here, and it is aware, and it is not an animal.

---

The sounds are few. The Flats are mostly quiet, in the way that a held breath is mostly quiet. A footstep on the crusty upper layer of the vegetation produces a sound like a small, polite clearing of a throat. A breeze — when it comes, which is rarely — sounds like a long syllable being pronounced very slowly, with the lips not quite closed. There is a constant, very low hum, below the threshold of most human hearing, that is not mechanical and is not biological and is, if one manages to detect it, slightly upsetting in the way that a single repeated musical note is upsetting, after a while, after a long while, after forever.

---

And then there is the *tech*. The tech is the part that no one talks about afterward.

It is not made of metal. It is not made of plastic. It is not made of anything the survey instruments can parse. It is present in the Flats as *tendency* — a curvature of space, a slight preferential directionality to the way light bends around certain stalks, a faint luminescence, deep within the heartwood of the larger growths, that is not bioluminescence and is not fire. There are structures, in the loosest sense — alignments of certain trunks, certain ridges, certain clusters of the violet blooms that, when the visitor stands at the right spot, form a pattern that is not a pattern but a *coordinate*, a kind of address.

The Flats are an installation. The Flats are an experiment. The Flats are a sentence, half-written, in a language that has not yet been spoken. The tech is so far beyond the modern that the modern is not a useful frame of reference; the tech is the substrate. It is the *ground* of the place, in more than the literal sense. The vegetation is the surface manifestation of something the visitor's culture has no name for, something that has, in the time it took to read this sentence, become slightly more of whatever it is, and slightly less, simultaneously, in a way that is locally consistent.

---

The traveler, at the end of the day, finds that they cannot remember the walk in. The Flats behind them appear to recede as they turn, not because the distance is increasing but because the *Flats themselves* are rearranging, gently, in the visitor's wake, like a hostess tidying a room after the guests have gone. The visitor does not feel lost. The visitor does not feel found. The visitor feels *catalogued*. The visitor feels *set down*. The visitor feels, in a way that is not unpleasant and not pleasant and not anything in particular, that they have been somewhere that is not a place, and that the place has noticed them, and that the place is not yet finished with what it is doing, and that this is fine, this is correct, this is the only state in which a place like this can be.

The light stays the same. The green stays the same. The shadows continue to pass across the Flats, from no source, toward no destination.

Nothing changes.

Nothing has ever changed here.

Nothing will.
