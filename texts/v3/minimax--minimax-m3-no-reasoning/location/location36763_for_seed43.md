The chamber sits well below the surface — descended by a worn staircase through three short landings, each landing a landing only in name now, its treads smoothed into shallow scoops by what must be generations of wet feet and softer bodies. The brickwork begins the moment the daylight does, and what reaches the bottom is not daylight anymore but a dusk-colored wash sifted down through gaps in a grating far above, through still air and a kind of thin, suspended mist that hangs the lower stairwell in haze. By the time you have come down the last of it, your eyes have given up on the stairwell entirely and have begun to take the room instead.

The room is not large. It has the feel of a single room — a great one, perhaps, if you measure by old buildings, but no more than a generous hall, smaller than a tithe-barn, larger than a kitchen. The ceiling is vaulted, low at the edges and rising to a low dome at the center, and the whole of it is brick. The brick was laid by hand, every one of them slightly different — some fat, some pinched, some bearing the thumbprint of the maker at the corner, some warped slightly in the kiln so that the courses do not run straight but wander, gently, like slow handwriting. There is mortar between them, but it has mostly given up its grip. Where it has fallen away, the cavity behind is dark, and sometimes a thin rootlet from somewhere far above has threaded itself down through a dozen feet of fill and is dangling there, a pale hair in the seam. The bricks themselves are a deep, warm red in the better light — the kind of red that holds fire in it — but the damp has darkened most of them to the color of dried blood, and in the wettest seams they are nearly black, and there are places where a thin crust of something mineral, pale green and rust-orange, has bloomed out along a fracture and traced the failure like a botanical illustration.

The floor is brick too. Here the wear is older. The bricks at the threshold are crisp, but ten paces in they have been walked into something else — flattened, polished to a dim gloss by water and time, the joints between them opened by the long soaking and then silted up again with a fine dark mud that the water itself has laid down. You can feel the channels. They are not made channels; the brick was laid flat. But the water has chosen, over the years, to follow certain courses, and the courses it has chosen are now subtly lower than the courses it has not, so that the floor is a topography of memory — three or four slow-moving rivulets that wander between raised islands of dryer brick. The water is clear in some of these, opaque and rust-colored in others where it has picked up a load of mineral from the walls. It moves. It moves always. The room is not still.

The temperature is the temperature of a body, of held breath, of summer — warmer than the stairwell above by a margin you feel on your face as soon as you round the last landing. The source is not visible. There is no hearth, no lamp, no obvious vent of hot air, but somewhere below this floor, somewhere the brick sits on, the earth is warm, and the brick has remembered the warmth and gives it back without complaint. The air holds it. The water holds it. You stand in it. Condensation forms on everything cooler than you are — on the underside of the dome, on the inward curve of the walls, on the metal rings set into the brick at intervals, rings whose purpose is no longer readable — and the condensation hangs there as beads, as drops, as a fine mist that you can almost see as a skin on the air, and then it falls. It falls continually. From the dome in a slow, irregular patter that is more felt than heard, distinct and distinct and distinct, never quite the same drop twice. From the walls in long trickles that have worn the brick beneath them into shallow, scalloped grooves. From the metal rings in heavier drops, because metal is colder than brick and pays its debt faster. The whole ceiling is weeping, and the weeping is what fills the room with sound.

For the room is loud, loud in the way that a thing which is itself almost quiet becomes loud when it is going on always. There is the water. There is also the life.

The life is the thing the room has become.

The walls are furred. Not with moss — there is no moss here, or very little; moss needs a different arrangement of light and patience. But with growths of other kinds: a black, soot-like fungus that climbs the lower three feet of brick in a tide-mark; patches of pale shelf-fungus bracketting out from the damper seams; long, pale threads of something like a stretched cobweb, but not spun by anything you would call a spider, hanging from the dome in drifting curtains that the condensation keeps heavy and that the air, barely moving, makes to sway. And on the floor, in the channels, where the water is slowest and the warmth is most constant, a green-black mat of something between a slime and a moss, through which the water moves without disturbing it, the way a river moves through a meadow.

The creatures are everywhere. They are small — there is nothing here larger than a fist, and most are much smaller. They are dense. They are layered. The eye finds them first at the edges of the light, where the brick meets the deeper dark, and then everywhere else, and then the eye gives up and accepts that the room is a kind of animal in itself.

On the walls: a continual slow traffic of pale slugs, each the length of a finger, working their way up and down the brick in tracks of dried silver. They eat the fungus. They are eaten in turn by something else. Under the lowest course of brick, where the floor meets the wall and the water sits longest, a zone of fine black amphibians — newts, perhaps, or something like them, sleek and quick, who flick from crevice to crevice and disappear with a sound like a wet thumb drawn off a slate. Centipedes, many-legged and rust-colored, hunting in the seams. A scatter of small grey spiders whose webs are not webs but single long threads, strung across the channels like fishing lines, weighted with dew.

In the water: a stirring. You can see it before you can see its cause. The channels move with something — small, lithe, dark-backed things that swim in the film of water between the silt and the brick and that rise, sometimes, with a small silver yawn to gulp at the surface, and disappear again. Fry of some kind. Thousands of them, perhaps. They are not native to this room; nothing here is native. They came down the stairwell as eggs, on feet, in the bellies of birds. They stay because the room will let them.

In the air: the insects. The insects are the room's voice. There are gnats in clouds so thick near the dome that the eye registers them as a faint, moving fog. There are larger things — moths the color of the brick itself, settling on the brick with wings folded, indistinguishable from the brick until they move. There are beetles, heavy and lacquered, who fall from the ceiling with a small dry report and lie on their backs and right themselves. There are things you cannot see but can hear: a high, dry ticking from one corner, a low, continuous hum from another, a sharp, infrequent chirp from a third. The room is a music of small instruments, each played by a different small musician, none of them in tempo with any other.

And the sounds of the room are not only the sounds of its life. There are other sounds, made by the room's own body. The brick, cooling and warming through the slow days above, makes small sounds — a tick here, a low crackle there, the report of a century's worth of small stresses settling at last. The water in its channels moves over irregularities in the brick and makes small sounds — a hush, a gurgle, a sudden clear note as a thread of it falls from a step into a deeper pool. The metal rings in the walls, contracting in the warm damp, make small sounds — a slow, near-inaudible creak, the sound of a ship in still water. And beneath all of this, felt more than heard, a low, continuous vibration from somewhere below the floor, as if the room were sitting over the throat of a great breathing thing. The warm earth. The room's furnace. The thing that keeps it all going.

Above the floor, set into the brick, are the remains of what this room was. There are basins — shallow, rectangular basins, brick-lined, three of them along the far wall, each one silted up to within a hand's breadth of the rim and holding its own small pool of warm water, its own small ecosystem of the things I have just described, miniaturized. There are channels cut into the brick between them, deliberate channels this time, that join to a central channel that runs the length of the room and disappears beneath a low arch into darkness. There are low brick platforms, wide enough to lie on, set at the room's edges — beds, perhaps, or benches. There are shelves in the walls, some of them still holding shards of clay vessels, some of them holding only the dust of clay vessels. There are iron rings in the ceiling, green with age, that once held something that hung. There is, set into the floor at the room's center, beneath the dome, a flat circle of bronze, green-black, that was once a fire-pot and is now a well-cap, and the warmth rises through it most strongly of all.

The light here is normal — meaning, it is light enough to see by, not more. The room has two sources. One is the grating far above, which lets down a thin, pale column that strikes the floor near the stairwell and dies out before it has traveled a body's length. The other is the bronze cap at the center, which has been polished, somehow, by the traffic of small bodies across it, and which catches what light there is and gives it back as a dull, green-gold warmth that pools around the cap and reaches perhaps a quarter of the way to the walls. Between these two sources the room is in a kind of shadow, but a working shadow, the kind in which eyes can find their way. The creatures, of course, need less than you do. The pale slugs move on the far walls as confidently as though the walls were lit.

The room is active. Things are happening in it. A beetle is climbing the dome and will fall. A newt has caught a slug and is pulling it, slowly, into a seam. The water in the central channel has risen a finger's width in the last hour, because somewhere above it has rained, and the rain is finding its way down through the fill and through the brick. The channels will adjust. The level will fall again. The brick will cool a little, the drops on the dome will slow a little, the bronze cap will lose some of its glow. And then the rain will stop, or the warmth will win back its ground, and the drops will quicken, and the channels will rise again. The room breathes. The room has been breathing, by the look of it, for a very long time.

The mortar will fall a little more. A course of brick at the base of the far wall has begun to bow inward, and the bowing has begun to admit a thin, persistent seep from somewhere outside the room, and the seep is staining the brick in a long dark tongue that will, in another hundred years, reach the floor. A shelf has fallen. The shards of the vessel that were on it lie among the newts, who use them now as cover. The bronze cap, polished by so many small feet, is becoming thin. It will not last forever. When it is gone, the heat will rise into the room more freely, and the room will be warmer, and the fungi will grow faster, and the insects will breed faster, and the sound will be louder, and the balance will shift a little, and the room will adjust, because the room is adjusted. The room has been adjusted for a long time. The room is what it is now.
