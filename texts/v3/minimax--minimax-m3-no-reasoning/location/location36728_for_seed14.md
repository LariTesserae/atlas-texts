# The Floor

The water here is older than the shape of the world. Cold runs through it like a memory of cold, and the cold runs through everything the water touches—which is everything. There is no above. There has not been an above for so long that the word itself has become a kind of myth the things that live here tell each other in the dark, a half-believed rumor of a place where the pressure is not.

A plain stretches out in every direction. Not a plain the way a floodplain is a plain, with the memory of a river running through it and the possibility of another one. This is a plain that has been here so long it has forgotten how to be anything else. Whatever mountains there were, whatever valleys were carved or thrust upward in the long ago, have been pressed down by the patient weight of the water into a thing so close to flat that to move across it is to move across the surface of a thought, or the inside of an eye. The topography has been kneaded. What remains are swells so low and so gradual they are felt more than seen—a density in the water, a slow leaning current, the barest suggestion of up and down where once there was uproar. The bottom is silt. The silt is fine as breath. When something moves across it, a curtain of it lifts and hangs in the water for days, for weeks, refusing to settle, because the water itself is always in motion and will not permit settling.

And the water is always in motion.

This is the first thing. The currents come from everywhere and go everywhere. They cross each other and braid and fold back on themselves and dive deep and rise shallow and there is no pattern a thing could learn. A current flowing east will, without warning, turn and run north, and another current running north will catch the first and twist them together into a rope of moving water that drags across the floor and picks up silt in long pale streamers that unroll behind it like the tails of things in flight. The water is not still. The water has not been still in longer than anything here can remember. There are places where the turbulence gathers, eddies that have been eddies for generations, slow whirlpools turning the silt in vast slow circles so that the bottom there is not a bottom at all but a column of suspended earth, a standing storm of mud. To swim into one is to lose the way forward. The water turns you. The water decides where you are.

This is the place, and it is dark.

The dark is not the dark of a night, which is a dark with a sister daylight waiting behind it. This dark is the dark of a place where light is a rumor, where a sun is a story someone heard once from a thing that was old and unreliable. What light there is, is light the water makes for itself in places—a faint blue-green glow that rises from the floor in patches, here and there, like the afterimage of something that burned long ago. Some of it moves. Some of it sits in the same spot and has been sitting in the same spot for so long that small things have made their homes on top of it, the way moss makes its home on a stone. The glow is not enough to see by. The glow is enough to see that you cannot see. The eye adjusts, and the adjusted eye sees a world of blacks and grays and the deepest, most exhausted blue, the blue of a thing that has been asked to be blue for so long it has become blue all the way through.

The floor of the plain is not empty.

This is the strange thing, the unexpected thing, the thing the water itself seems to be in the process of doing and has been doing for a very long while. The floor is littered. Covered. Piled. **Packed.** The eye cannot take it in all at once. There is no space between. There is no clearing. The plain is heaped with the things that have come to rest here, and the things that have come to rest here are everything.

Bones. The bones of things so old they have been mineralized, turned to stone, fused with the silt, become part of the floor itself in places, jutting up out of the mud in curves and shafts and the hollow cups of joints. Bones in clusters, as if the things that wore them came here to die together, or as if the water gathered them and brought them here and laid them down. Ribcages open like the ribs of wrecked ships. Skulls with the eyes still dark. Spines unspooling into the silt over distances longer than a swimmer could cover in a day, vertebrae strung out in a line that bends with the current as if the thing they were is still walking, still going somewhere, has been going somewhere for a very long time and has not yet arrived.

Wood. Driftwood, but the word is wrong. There is no drift here. There is no surface. The wood came down through the water, was carried along the floor, and was deposited, and was buried, and was uncovered again by a current that shifted, and was buried again, and the wood has been in the water so long it has taken on the color of the water and the weight of the water and the slow patience of the water. Some of it is still standing, upright, the stumps of trees that grew in some other time in some other place, now sunk to the floor and rooted in the silt with their roots still curled around the stones they held in life, in the life before the water. The wood is dark. The wood is soft. A hand laid on it would sink into it a little, and the water would close the print behind.

Stone. Not the stones of a beach, tumbled and rounded. Stones that have been here so long the water has had time to do something stranger to them than tumbling—to dissolve them, partly, and to let the dissolved part of them precipitate out again in strange new forms, crusts and nodules and soft rounded humps that look almost alive, almost like sleeping things. Some of the stones have grown together. Some have grown onto the bones, onto the wood, the wood and the bone and the stone becoming one another over the slow centuries, materials forgetting which one they were. The floor is a single object, in places. A single long compound thing made of everything that was ever brought here.

And cloth. The flat, soft, impossible presence of cloth in a place like this—thin sheets of it, draped over bones, wrapped around stones, spread out across the silt in patches that lift and settle and lift again with the current. Where it came from. What it was. The mind refuses the question. The cloth is here. The cloth is part of the floor now, the way everything is part of the floor now. It is gray. It is torn. It moves.

The things that live here are few and they are careful.

A shape moves in the gray distance, low to the floor, slow, pausing to nose at a bone and then moving on. Whatever it is, it does not cast a shadow. The dark is too complete for shadows. It is more felt than seen—a length, a movement, a pause. It could be long. It could be patient. It bends to the floor and feeds on something small, and then it is gone behind a humped stone, and the water closes over the place where it was as if it had not been.

There are others. They are not many. They are not close to each other. They live in the spaces between the bones, in the lee of the great piled humps of stone and wood, in the small pockets of quieter water that form in the lee of anything large enough to break the current. They are dark. They are slow. They do not make sounds.

The sounds here are not sounds. The water moves and the moving of the water is a pressure, a long low vibration felt in the chest and in the bones of the inner ear, a hum that is below the hearing and above the silence and which is the actual voice of the place. Sometimes the current shifts and the pressure changes, and the change is felt as a kind of event, a weather of the inner ear, the way a person above water might feel the barometric pressure of a coming storm. Sometimes, rarely, something moves—a bone settling, a stone turning over in the silt with a long slow grinding, a tree stump shifting in a current—and the movement makes a sound that is heard and not felt, a small short noise that travels through the water and is gone, the only punctuation in a place without punctuation.

There is no tool here. There is no made thing. The things that live here do not make things. They have no need. The water gives what the water gives, and the water gives the bones of old things and the silt of the floor and the small living things that drift in the current and are gathered in. The things that live here do not have hands. Some of them have fins. Some of them have the long slow bodies of things that have stopped needing to hurry. The tech of this place is the tech of an open mouth in a current. The tech is hunger and patience.

The weirdness of this place is the weirdness of a thing that has gone on too long. It is the weirdness of a world that has settled. It is the weirdness of a place that has been doing the same thing for so long that the doing of it has become a kind of being, and the place has become a kind of creature, a single thing, the floor and the water and the bones and the wood and the cloth and the few slow dark things that move across it all one creature, one body, breathing the current in and breathing the current out, the turbulence the blood of it, the silt the thought of it, the bones the memory, the dark the skin, and the whole of it stretched out flat and wide in every direction as far as the eye can adjust to see, which is not far, which is a small and circling distance, the distance of a single life in a place where a single life is the whole measure of a life.

To be here is to be inside something. To be here is to be inside the long settling of a thing that once was loud and once was bright and is now just dark, and packed, and moving, and old.
