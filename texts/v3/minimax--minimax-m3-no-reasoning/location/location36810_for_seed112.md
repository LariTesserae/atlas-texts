# The Loomfield

A green so total it has stopped being a color and become an atmosphere.

You stand—what does *stand* mean here?—at the surface of a world that has forgotten water but never forgotten the word for it. The sky is a pale, parched platinum, sun-bleached and ringed at the horizon with a halo of dust so fine it could be light made solid. The light is everywhere, sourceless, the kind of brightness that makes shadows thin and ashamed. The sun—if it is a sun, if that term even applies—hangs in a permanent washed-out glare, three times the angular size of any star has a right to be, and does not move. Has not moved, perhaps, for geological ages. The whole high plateau is a thing made of *having been lit once and never dimming since.*

And yet the field is deafening.

This is the first contradiction. The second is what you are standing on.

---

**THE SUBSTRATE**

The ground is not ground. It is *stem.*

A trillion trillion stalks rise vertically from a depth that no probe has ever returned from, a packed mat of living columns so dense they have, over their uncounted generations, fused into a continental crust of photosynthetic tissue. Walk, and you walk on a fibrous corduroy the color of old jade and new bruises. The stalks are each as thick as a human thigh, a little thicker toward the base, ribbed with a bark that flakes like birch but smells of neither birch nor anything aromatic—smells instead of *ozone*, sharply, the way a thunderstorm smells, the way a wound on a charged machine smells. The surface gives, slightly, under your weight, and rebounds. It breathes. A slow metronome of pressure passes through it, as if the whole plateau were a single bellows being squeezed and released by something below.

There is no soil. There are no roots in any sense you would recognize. The plant *is* the geology. It has eaten the dirt long ago, and now is its own bedrock, its own aquifer (though no aqua), its own mineral record. Slice a column and you find, beneath the green rind, a pale pith layered with veins of pure metallic copper, threads of silicate-glass, and an amber sap that is not sap but a heavy, slow-moving glycol that flashes when exposed to air. The geologists—if there were any—would call it a *living pegmatite.*

You are walking on the planet's nervous system.

---

**THE VOID-WATER**

You are thirsty. You will be thirsty forever.

The 0.5 of water here is not the 0.0 of the Moon. It is a *just-barely* — a memory of moisture held in the chemistry of the stalks, in the glycol that perfuses them, in the air itself, which is dry but not desiccated. The atmosphere is thick. It carries weight. It is, in fact, the second reason you can hear anything at all, and the reason you cannot hear yourself think.

The air is not nitrogen-oxygen. The air is *spore.*

You cannot see them individually — they are too small, and there are too many of them, in a permanent visible haze that is not quite fog and not quite dust. Inhale and you taste green, taste rust, taste a faint electric sweetness like licking a battery. The spores are the planet's broadcast, the medium of its metabolism, and they are *loud*. Each one, a tiny resonance chamber. Each one, vibrating at a frequency tuned to the planet's own fundamental note. Together they are the planet's breath made audible, and the planet is panting.

The wind is the spores moving, and the wind is constant. It does not gust. It *sustains* — a sixty-kilometer-an-hour whisper that never stops, that has not stopped in a hundred thousand years, that abrades your face and dries your eyes and pours into your ears until the roar of it becomes the silence of it becomes the roar of it again. You shout and your voice is gone before it leaves your mouth. You listen and there is no listening. There is only the wind, and the wind is everywhere, and the wind is alive.

---

**THE FABRIC**

Now look up.

The stalks rise.

They rise to ten meters, to twenty, to fifty, and at fifty they begin, very gradually, to lean. The high plateau is *high* — two and a half kilometers above the mean planetary datum — and at this elevation the air is thin enough that the stalks, freed from the drag of denser atmosphere below, begin to arch. They bend in the sustained wind, they have always bent in the sustained wind, and they have bent so long that their growth now incorporates the curve. The whole field is a comb of green bent sideways, a planetary perm, all the stalks leaning east-northeast at an angle of perhaps forty degrees from vertical, their crowns interleaving, their branches (do they have branches? yes — but only at the crown, only after the lean, only in the high thin air) braiding into one another into a continuous canopy miles above your head.

But it is not a closed canopy. The light gets through. The light gets through because the leaves — the *panels*, and you will think of them as panels, they are panels, they are too flat and too regular not to be panels — the panels are *moving.* Individually. By the billion.

Each panel is a broad square of glossy green-black, half a meter on a side, hinged at one corner to a slender petiole that grows out of the crown-branches like a flag from a pole. And each panel rotates. *Pitches. Yaws. Twitches.* They track the light source with a mechanical patience, a stutter-step servo-motion, and the sound of a billion panels doing this in loose synchrony is the third layer of the roar — a dry clatter, like a hundred thousand tin roofs in a hard rain, like the sea heard from inside the hull of a wooden ship. *Clack. Clack-clack. Whirrrrr. Clack.* When the wind shifts by a single degree, the whole field reorients, and the clatter swells, and you feel the noise in your cheekbones.

The light, when it falls on the open panels, is *captured* — visibly captured, you can see it traveling, a bright bloom that races across the field in slow waves as panels commit to a new angle. From a height, the field would look like a screen the size of a continent, displaying a low-resolution image of the sun, refreshed twenty times a second.

This is not a forest. This is a *machine,* and the machine is running.

---

**THE RESIDENTS**

The fauna are here. They have to be. Nothing in the parameters is impossible without them, but with them — *with* them the place becomes legible.

The fauna are not animals. Not exactly. They are *consumers* — small, blunt, segmented bodies a meter long, vaguely arthropod, vaguely grub, with six legs and a cluster of infrared pits where eyes would go. They are the color of the pith inside the stalks: bone, cream, faintly luminous. They have no mouths. They have, instead, a ventral groove lined with capillary hairs that they press against a stalk and *suck the glycol*. This is how the planet's interior becomes available. They are the planet's platelets. They move in herds, dozens of them together, pressing their grooves to the corduroy, the green and the cream against each other, and the sound they make is a soft perpetual lip-smack, a *tck-tck-tck*, lost in the roar, audible only if you put your ear to the ground.

The fauna are not predators. Nothing here is a predator.

But the fauna are not the point. The point is what they don't do: they don't *eat the plant.* They only drink it. They don't break the stalks. They don't tear the panels. They are tolerated, even welcomed, because the glycol they excrete — a thin, oily, iridescent waste — is the *industrial feedstock* of the plateau, and the plateau is, in its slow way, *industrial.*

---

**THE FACTORY**

Watch, now. This is the part no one believes.

When a panel reaches the end of its service life — when its photosynthetic pigments have bleached out and it can no longer hold a charge — it does not fall. It is *ejected.* A small abscission seam, pre-weakened, gives way, and the panel detaches and tumbles. The wind catches it. The wind has always caught it. The wind carries it east-northeast, along the prevailing, and the panel *sails* — yes, sails, on the perpetual sixty-kilometer current, a green-black sail — and it sails for days, weeks, until the wind fails it at the eastern escarpment, and it tumbles down a two-thousand-meter cliff of exposed stem-tissue into a basin below.

The basin is the foundry.

Down there, where the spore-haze is thicker and the roar is a physical pressure, the spent panels accumulate. They are pressed by their own weight into the organic mass of older panels, and the older panels, and the older panels still, in layers kilometers thick, in a deposit that has been accumulating for as long as the wind has blown east-northeast, which is to say forever. The lower layers, under the pressure, under the residual heat, undergo something that is not geology and not biology but is *both*: a slow polymerization, a *coking* of the plant-tissue into a hard, glossy, stratified material that is fibrous like wood, conducts electricity like metal, and burns, when exposed to sufficient oxygen, with a pale blue flame that does not go out.

It is the only fire this world has ever known. It is *geological* fire. It is the planet's own lightning, preserved in its own corpse.

And the field *mines* it.

See, here and there, on the high plateau, a panel does the opposite of detaching. It does not sail. It *descends.* It is one of the lower panels, at the junction of crown and main-stem, and it is rotating the wrong way, against the wind, against the light, and the reason is that it is *paying out* — extruding, from a gland at its hinge, a thin bright thread of the polymerized blue-burning material, drawn up from below through the stalk's copper veins. The thread is fine. The thread is *strong.* The thread is being woven, by a hundred thousand panels working in concert across a hundred thousand stems, into a cable. The cable runs from panel to panel, panel to stem, stem to stem, a three-dimensional net that hangs in the air beneath the canopy, in the wind, in the roar, in the clatter, in the *industrial* dark where the panels cannot reach the light.

This is the grid. This is the *transmission.* This is how a plant does not use electricity, but *is* electricity, is a continent-spanning dynamo driven by the differential between the high cool plateau and the low hot foundry, the wind and the burning, the panel and the polymer, the living and the dead.

You are inside a power plant. You have been inside it since you arrived.

---

**THE DYNAMIC**

And it is *running.*

The parameters said turbulent, and turbulent is the right word, but it is the turbulence of a *working engine,* not a failing one. The field is a system in violent equilibrium, in the way a hurricane is in equilibrium, in the way a star is in equilibrium. The panels clatter and re-clatter. The wind sustains. The spore-haze thickens and thins in slow planetary breaths. The little cream-colored fauna *tck-tck-tck* against the stalks, and where they cluster, the stalks respond — locally, sympathetically — by increasing the flow of glycol, by darkening their rind, by a faint glow that runs up from the base and is visible for an instant in the gloom under the canopy. Trails of bioluminescence, like slow matches, like the planet thinking.

From a height, the field would look like this: a green-black plain tilted slightly east-northeast, a corduroy the size of a subcontinent, and on its surface, constantly, *races* of brightness — a panel here committing, a herd of fauna there, a stalk darkening, a cable paying out — all in the constant clatter and the constant wind and the constant bright sourceless light. It is busy. It is a workshop. It is an anthill with a sky.

The *weird* of it is the *sameness* of it. There is no variation. The temperature is the temperature it has always been. The light is the light it has always been. The wind is the wind. The erosion parameter is 2.2, *established* — and established here means *in a closed loop,* the wind blowing the panels east, the panels burning in the east, the burned material being drawn up through the copper veins, the cables being woven, the new panels being grown, the new panels sailing east, the new panels burning in the east. A closed cycle. A perfect engine. A thing that will continue until the sun, whatever it is, finally, after all this time, *does* move, and ends it.

---

**AT THE EDGE**

You walk, and the corduroy gives, and the wind roars, and the panels clack, and the cream-colored fauna *tck* against the green, and the cables hum in their net beneath the canopy, and somewhere far below your feet, a polymer seam catches a spark and burns with a pale blue light that takes a hundred thousand years to reach the surface, by which time it is already the wind.

You are thirsty. You will be thirsty forever.

The light is everywhere. The sound is everything. The plant is the world, and the world is a loom, and the loom is weaving itself, and it has been weaving itself since before you were born, and it will be weaving itself after, and the cloth it makes is the only thing in the universe that does not need you to look at it to continue existing.

The plateau hums. The plateau *runs.* The plateau is vast and cool and deafening and bright and it is made entirely of one green thing, doing one green thing, forever.

You stand on it. You cannot stand on it. The wind does not permit standing. You lean into it, at forty degrees, like the stalks, like everything here, and you become, for a moment, part of the loom.
