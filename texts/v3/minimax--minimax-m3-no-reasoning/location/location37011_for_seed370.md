The place is high.

You can tell because the air that leaks through the narrow openings far above has the thinness of altitude — that dry coolness, almost a taste — though the air inside is warm, the kind of warmth that stone holds long after the sun has stopped touching it, the kind of warmth a kiln holds long after the firing is done. The warmth is the place's own. It does not come from anywhere else.

It is a small place. The word *small* does not fit it, and neither does the word *large*. It is the size of a hall that has been packed until the packing became the hall. You could cross it in a hundred paces. You could not cross it at all if you stopped to look at what was between the paces, because there is no place between the paces that is not occupied. The floor is occupied. The walls are occupied. The ceiling is occupied. The columns that hold the ceiling up are occupied. Even the air feels occupied, as if it has been pressed into the same density as everything else.

Everything in it was made.

There is no natural surface in this place. There is no bedrock, no untended stone, no part of it that was ever just itself. The floor is laid from hand-shaped tiles of a dark fired clay, fitted so tightly that the joins between them have been rubbed away across an uncountable stretch of time. The walls are stone, but stone worked so many times — chiseled, hammered, ground, polished, and worked again — that it has forgotten it was ever stone. It is the memory of stone. It is stone that remembers only that it was worked. The columns are bronze, but the bronze is old in the way bronze can only be old after centuries of being touched by air that has no water in it: green-black, smooth, fused with the stone around it so that you cannot say where one ends and the other begins. The ceiling is carved from a single block, or it was poured in place, or it grew — you cannot tell, and the place cannot tell you, because it has forgotten the difference.

The carvings cover every surface. They were made by hand, and you can see the handwork if you come close — the slight unevenness of a chisel struck by someone who had learned the chisel but not yet learned to forget it. There are figures, hundreds of them, thousands of them, packed shoulder to shoulder across the walls in rows that march from the floor up to the ceiling and then continue across the ceiling and down the other side. They are not all the same. Some are human, or human-shaped, or what was once human-shaped before the working-down of centuries reduced them to gestures. Some are animals, or what were animals, though the shapes have been worn so smooth you cannot say what kind. Some are just forms — spirals, lattices, repeating geometries — that were never meant to be anything in particular. They were all made by hands that learned from hands that learned from hands, across a span of time that is no longer recorded anywhere, because nothing that recorded it has survived except the carvings themselves.

There has never been water here. The clay of the floor tiles was fired dry. The bronze of the columns was cast dry. The air is the air of a sealed jar that has been sealed so long that the seal has become the jar. You do not feel thirsty here. You do not feel anything that requires water. Thirst has been sealed out along with the water.

Nothing has ever grown here. The figures carved into the walls are not depictions of anything that lived. They are not commemorations, not memorials. They are just figures, made because hands were here and there was material to make them from and the making was what the hands did. The way water, in another place, would have flowed.

The light is dim. It comes from somewhere — from the narrow openings far above, from polished chips of stone inset into the eyes of the bronze figures, from the slight gleam of mica or quartz in the worked walls. It is enough to see the shapes of things. It is not enough to see things clearly. After a while you stop trying. After a while you see in the dim way the place sees in, which is the way it has always seen.

Then there is the sound.

The sound is what the place is.

It is a single tone, held without break, without waver, without breath. It fills the small space so completely that it has become a kind of architecture of its own — you could walk into it the way you walk into water, except it is not water, it is sound, and it is so constant and so total that it has stopped being sound and become a state of being. You feel it in your chest before you hear it. You feel it in the bones of your jaw, in the small bones of your ear, in the long bones of your arms. After a while you stop hearing it. After a while you are it.

It does not come from any one place. It comes from all of them. It comes from the shapes hanging from the ceiling — bells, perhaps, or carved forms, or just shapes — and from the carvings in the walls, and from the angles of the room itself: the curves, the hollows, the small projections, all shaped across uncountable years of working into the body of an instrument. The room is a throat. The room has been tuned by hand into an instrument that plays itself.

It has been playing for so long that it has finished playing. It is at the note it will always be at. The hands that tuned it are part of the room now. Their intention is the note. Their memory is the resonance.

Nothing changes.

The note does not shift. The light does not brighten or fade. The warmth does not rise or fall. The figures in the walls do not move. The shapes hanging from the ceiling do not sway. The place is in equilibrium. The place has been in equilibrium so long that equilibrium is no longer a state it is in — it is what it is. A sealed instrument, tuned and sealed. Playing itself, for no one, in a small warm dim packed room built by hand on a high place, by hands that are now part of the stone and the bronze and the fired clay.

You stand in it. The note fills you. The dim light catches the polished eyes of the bronze figures and they look at you without seeing you, because they are bronze, because they have no eyes, because there is no one to see.

You are the only thing in this place that is not finished.

You are the only thing in this place that is still in the process of becoming. You stand in the warmth and the tone and the packed stillness, and you wait for the note to change. It does not change. It will not change. The place has reached the end of what it was made to do, and the end of what it was made to do is this: to keep sounding this one note, forever, in a room that was made by hand, in a room that was made to be a room, in a room that has forgotten how to be anything else.
