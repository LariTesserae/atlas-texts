# The Plateau of Korrun

What you notice first, approaching from the switchback road that has been climbing for half a day, is the silence. Not the silence of absence — the silence of saturation. The valley below has its sounds: a river you can see but not hear, the lowing of unseen cattle in meadow. Here on the high plateau, the air is too thin and too cold to carry. Sounds fall a few paces from their source and die, like stones dropped into deep water.

The town sits on a shelf of stone that did not exist a hundred thousand years ago and may not exist a hundred thousand more. The mountains here are young. They have not yet learned to be gentle with themselves. The peaks to the west are fluted with vertical ridges that look chiseled; their faces are a pale gray-green stone that splits in clean planes when it fractures. The town has built itself in the lee of one of these faces, on the only flat ground for miles, where a former glacier carved out a basin and then retreated up into the high cirques, leaving behind a scatter of erratics — boulders the size of houses resting in improbable positions, casting long shadows at dawn.

The plateau itself is perhaps three miles across, perhaps four. The town's outer wall runs along its southern lip, and beyond that lip the land drops away in a long series of faulted benches toward warmer country. North, the young mountains climb. East and west, the plateau ends in cliffs that fall into mist. From any point inside the town you can walk ten minutes and reach open ground where nothing is built.

The stone of the buildings is the same stone as the mountains. Pale, sharp-grained, easily worked with iron tools and copper chisels but resistant to weather. The builders here are not wealthy. There is no marble. There is no fired brick — the kilns would consume too much fuel at this altitude, and the wind would steal the heat. Instead they cut the local stone into rough oblongs and stack them, chinked with moss and a mortar of beaten lime and tallow. Roofs are slate, hand-split, laid in overlapping rows that ring faintly when the wind passes through. They are steep, because snow comes, even though snow rarely stays.

It is cool here. Not bitter — the sun, when it emerges, has warmth — but cool in the way that shade is cool, even when there is no shade. The dampness is the defining quality of the air. Rain is rare. What falls instead is condensation. Every surface, by midmorning, has wept. The slate roofs glisten, then drip into the gutters, then into the cisterns that line every courtyard. The streets, paved with the same local stone but worn smooth by feet and hooves, are slick with a thin film of moisture that does not puddle but does not dry either. The lichens on the north walls of the houses are brilliantly green, fat with perpetual damp, and they creep up between the courses of stonework as if they had been invited.

The vegetation is, in this damp coolness, extravagantly itself. The plateau is not forested — the elevation is too high and the soil too thin — but it is overgrown with a low, dense, almost obstinate mat of growth. Mosses in pale greens and a deep almost-black green. Dwarf willow, no taller than a man's knee, with leaves like small coins. Heath, the purple kind, that flowers in late summer and stains the open ground with color visible from the walls. Sedums and saxifrages that have crept up from the cracks and now colonize entire roof slopes. Ferns in the gullies where runoff collects, unfurling their fiddleheads in the brief warm season and dying back into brown fists for the rest of the year. In the crevices of the boulders and in the shelter of the erratic stones, isolated rowans have taken hold, twisted and stunted but flowering every spring, their red berries picked by the children who climb for them with bare feet on the wet stone.

The town is built around these erratics rather than removing them. Some of the larger ones have been incorporated into walls. One, in the central square, has been carved with steps on one side so people can climb it and look out over the plateau. Another has a shrine built into its lee. A third, balanced precariously on a slight rise at the town's northern edge, has simply been left where it is, and the townspeople walk around it without comment, as if it had always been there.

The buildings are organized in a way that suggests planning, though the plan is older than memory. Three main streets run roughly north-south, following the long axis of the glacier basin. They are not quite parallel — they bend slightly to accommodate a stream that surfaces briefly in the town's middle section, runs through a stone-lined channel for perhaps two hundred paces, then disappears back underground into the same gravel it emerged from. The cross streets are narrower, some barely wide enough for a laden mule. Where the cross streets meet the long streets there are small irregular plazas, and in each plaza there is a cistern and a stone bench and a lantern post — iron, unlit except on feast days.

The houses are two and three stories, with the upper stories projecting slightly over the lower in the old way, so that neighbors across a narrow street can almost touch hands from their windows. The ground floors are workshop and stable. The upper floors are living quarters, with small windows of oiled paper or, in the wealthier houses, of thin slabs of mica split from a deposit somewhere in the eastern mountains. The doors are heavy, plank-built, hung on iron hinges that have been greased with tallow and have not rusted, because the air is too dry for rust and too damp for dust.

The people are not many. Perhaps a thousand, perhaps a little fewer. They move through their streets without haste. They speak quietly. Their tools are iron and copper and bone. They have no mechanical clocks — they tell time by the position of the sun when it is visible, and by the dripping of the cisterns when it is not. They plow with oxen. They shear sheep. They quarry the slate and the building stone from a pit on the eastern cliff that has been worked, by their reckoning, for a hundred generations. They have no writing that anyone uses daily, though there are scratched records in a back room of the long building near the cistern plaza, and an old woman who can read them, who is asked to consult them perhaps twice a year.

There are more animals than you would expect. The plateau is rich in them, in a way that feels deliberate, though no one has arranged it. Sheep in small flocks on the open ground, tended by children and dogs. Goats in the rocks. A few cattle, kept in byres through the long damp winters. Chickens in every yard. Pigeons on every roof, in such numbers that the gutters must be cleaned monthly, and their droppings whiten the slate. The wild fauna is denser still. Small deer — the size of large dogs, with rough gray-brown coats — move in herds of eight or ten across the plateau, never far from the cover of the dwarf willow. There are foxes, red-furred, hunting the mice that live under the erratics. There are hares. There are birds in extraordinary variety: small brown things that flock in the heather, larger things with hooked beaks that nest on the cliffs, a black bird with a long tail that sits on the weathervane of the long building and sings a phrase of three notes, repeated, in the early morning and at dusk.

The sounds that do exist — because the air is quiet but not silent — are mostly small and close. The drip of condensation from the eaves. The shuffle of feet on wet stone. The muffled complaints of the pigeons. The occasional clatter of a copper pot. The wind in the dwarf willow makes a sound like silk being torn. When the wind is strong, the slate roofs ring faintly, a high pure note that is felt more than heard. At night, the deer bark in the distance, and once in a while, very rarely, something larger makes a sound from the high cirques that no one can identify, and no one goes to look.

What is strange about the place — and it is strange, you can feel it — is not any single thing but the way everything holds still. The lichens do not grow. The mosses do not spread. The slate does not weather. The iron does not rust. The children's feet do not wear down the paving stones. The weathervane does not loosen on its spindle. The same black bird sits on the same weathervane at the same hour and sings the same three notes. The same old woman walks the same path to the same back room and opens the same ledger and consults the same records. The water in the cisterns does not fall or rise. The lichen on the north walls is the same lichen that was there when you arrived, and will be there when you leave, and was there before you came.

The townspeople do not age. This is the strangest thing, and they themselves do not remark on it. Children grow to a certain point and stop. Adults reach an age and hold it. The same faces, in the same clothes, doing the same work, year after year after year. Visitors who stay long enough begin to notice it. Visitors who do not stay long enough do not. The people are unfailingly polite about this. They do not press you to stay. They do not press you to leave. They say, when you ask, that time here moves differently, or that it does not move at all, or that the question is not well-formed. They smile, and pour you another cup of the bitter herbal tea they drink in the damp evenings, and change the subject to the weather, which is always the same.

The sun, when it comes out, lies on the wet stone in long sheets of pale gold. The shadows of the erratics point the same way they pointed yesterday, and will point tomorrow. The mist that fills the valley below rises in the morning and falls in the evening and rises and falls in the morning and evening and morning and evening, and the pattern is so reliable that the bell in the long building is rung when the mist reaches a certain height, and the children are called in from the plateau, and the dogs come home, and the doors are shut, and the lanterns are lit, and the small bright windows of mica glow in the dusk like eyes looking out at the dark.
