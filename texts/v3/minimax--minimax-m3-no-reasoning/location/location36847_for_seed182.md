# The Condensation Warrens

---

## Descent

You find the entrance at the back of an abandoned boiler station, where a rusted service ladder drops through a hatch that no longer seals. The climb is short. Then the ladder ends, and the city begins.

---

## The Shape of the Place

This is a hollow space. A vast, roughly oval chamber carved — no, *grown* — into the limestone beneath the river delta. The ceiling hangs low, perhaps twelve meters at its highest point, sloping down to scraping-touch distance at the edges where the walls curve inward. Pillars of original rock remain, unmoved columns that the city built itself around. They number perhaps forty, irregularly spaced, each one thick as a house and worn smooth at shoulder height by a century of passing bodies and passing carts.

The floor is uneven. Channels run through it in straight lines — man-made, brick-lined, narrow as a forearm and deep as a knee. Water moves through them constantly. Not fast. Not slow. The rate of condensation sliding down a windowpane, multiplied by ten thousand.

The light is bad. It comes from a few sources: glass spheres of preserved fish-oil mounted on iron arms at intersections, small chemical lanterns that hiss faintly where the air is dampest, and the pale green growth that climbs the upper portions of the pillars. The green does not truly glow. It only refuses to be dark.

---

## Air and Moisture

The air is wet the way a mouth is wet. It coats the skin. It gathers on every metal surface as a fine mist within minutes of cleaning. Breathing feels like drinking.

Heat rises from somewhere below — from the deep earth, or from buried pipes, or from some slow industry in the lower chambers no one you speak to will describe. The heat meets the wet stone ceiling and becomes the constant, soft, world-filling *drip*. Not rain. Not quite. A distributed precipitation that comes from everywhere at once and falls as fine as breath.

When you first arrive it is unbearable. After a week it becomes invisible. After a month you will miss it elsewhere.

---

## The Architecture

Everything is built from the city that was, repurposed.

The dwellings are pressed against the walls and clustered around the pillars in dense, layered agglomerations — timber frames lashed with wire, sheet metal bent and re-bent, brick scavenged from the surface ruins, canvas sails stretched as ceilings. Some have three levels. Some have none, just a curtain. The gaps between them are corridors, and the corridors are narrow, and the corridors are full of people, and so the city is best understood not as buildings-with-streets-between-them but as **a single textured surface of inhabited matter** with the occasional open space where a channel runs or a pillar stands.

There is one open space larger than the others: the Round, a circular plaza centered on the thickest pillar. Here the channels converge into a small basin, and here the lanterns are most numerous, and here the air smells strongly of rust and warm stone and cooking fat. People gather here to talk, to trade, to argue, to mend things. It is not quiet even here — voices carry in this wet air, bouncing softly off the low ceiling — but it is the closest thing the city has to a public square.

A second-tier walkway of plank and iron encircles the chamber about four meters up, accessed by ladder or stair from various points. It runs continuously around the oval, passing behind and through the upper stories of the dwellings. From this height you can see the green-lit ceiling and understand the city's full shape. It is smaller than you expected. It is also more complete.

---

## Industry

The machines are small and numerous.

In the alcoves between dwellings, in low-ceilinged workshops where a person must crouch to enter, the city's work happens. There are metal-shops where copper wire is drawn and re-drawn until it is fine as hair. There are chemical-shops where salts are dissolved and re-precipitated from the dripping water, yielding powders no one on the surface would recognize. There are engine-shops, but the engines are odd: they are powered by the heat differential between the deep stone and the wet air, and they turn slowly, and they are most of them older than the people who tend them.

Steam rises in thin white threads from dozens of vents. It is the only thing in the city that moves with any speed.

The work produces, principally, *more city*. Wire, sheet, brick, treated timber, lanterns, the green-grow cultures, condensed-water vessels. The city builds itself and repairs itself and extends itself into a warren of additional small chambers branching from the main one, downward and outward, where the heat is greater and the air is thicker and the light is almost gone.

---

## Ecology

There is not much animal life.

Rats, certainly. A pale, slick-furred variety that does well in the damp. They are tolerated. They are, in some seasons, eaten.

Insects: silverfish, a few roaches, something like a tick that lives in the green growth and falls on passersby and is brushed off without comment. Moths, drawn to the few flames. The moths are large and slow.

And the green.

The green is the city's main partner in staying alive. It grows on the upper pillar surfaces, on the ceiling where moisture gathers thickest, on any wood left undisturbed for a season. It is not quite a moss and not quite a lichen and not quite a fungus. It eats minerals from the dripping water. It eats trace heat. It produces, as a byproduct, the faint greenish light and a dry, papery fruit that the city dries and grinds and uses in a thousand small ways — pigment, medicine, paste, incense. The green does not grow where it is disturbed. The green grows wherever it is left alone. This is a rule the city has learned to design around. Many surfaces in the upper regions look furred. Many surfaces are.

---

## The Strange Part

There are the wells.

In five places in the chamber floor, in addition to the drainage channels, there are circular openings cut into the stone — older than the city, older than the warren, older perhaps than the chamber itself. They go down. They go down farther than anyone has descended. They breathe. A slow, regular exhalation of warm, mineral-smelling air rises from each one on a cycle of perhaps nine minutes.

No one knows what is at the bottom.

There are ropes on the oldest well. They have been there longer than the city. They are not rotted. No one has used them in living memory. No one is entirely sure why they are there.

It is considered unlucky to drop objects into the wells. It is considered more unlucky to listen to them for too long. Most people pass by without thinking about them. Some people — and you can tell who they are, they walk with a slight hesitation near the well-mouths — some people think about them all the time.

The wells do not appear on the few maps the city has made of itself.

---

## Sound

The drip. The engines. The hiss of vents. The murmur of voices, which never quite rises to a roar because the air is heavy and damp and absorbs vibration. Footsteps on brick, on plank, on the slick stone of the plaza. The small high calls of children playing in the upper walkways. The occasional clang of a hammer. A low, almost subliminal hum that may be the engines, or may be the wells, or may be the chamber itself — no one agrees.

Nothing is loud. Everything is present. The city sounds like a body sounds from the inside.

---

## Time

Time passes slowly here, the way it does in libraries, or in sickrooms, or in any place where the same small things happen in the same small way for a long time.

The condensation does not stop. The engines do not stop. The green grows. The city grows. A new dwelling appears in a gap. A channel is rerouted. A well-mouth is fitted with a new iron grate. A child learns to walk in the upper walkways, which are the safest place to fall.

Outside, on the surface, the river delta shifts. Buildings collapse in slow motion. The seasons turn. Wars, perhaps, happen and end.

Down here, a person can live an entire life and never see the sun, and be glad, and be bored, and be afraid sometimes — mostly of the wells — and eat the green-fruit cakes and drink the distilled drip-water and listen to the engines and the engines and the engines, and die, and be buried in a small niche in the wall, and become part of the city's mass.

The city continues.

The wells breathe.

The green grows on the grave.
