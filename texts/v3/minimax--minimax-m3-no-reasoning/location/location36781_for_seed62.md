# The Vent at Traben

The valley is not old. You can see this in the rock: the way the ridges come up out of the ground like broken teeth, the way the scree at their feet is still mostly the same color as the cliffs above it, the way the rivers have barely sorted their beds. The mountains here are still arguing with themselves about what shape to be. The uplift is patient—you cannot see it—but the result is a country of sharp shoulders and clean falls, a geometry that hasn't had time to soften. The valley floor is broad and stony, a long flatness trapped between the rising. From up on the side ridges you can see most of it at once: a green and a grey thing, three or four kilometers end to end, with the river drawn through the middle like a single wire.

The light is not like lower light. It is a thin and almost violent light, a light that does not warm what it touches, that flattens shadows into hard black. The sky is a pale, bleached thing, almost white at the zenith, and the air is so clear that the far peaks seem to be cut out of paper and laid against it. You can see, on the cliff faces, every fracture line, every lichen patch, the chalky mineral veins that run like writing through the dark stone. The sun is high even when it is low. Nothing is gold; everything is exposed.

Down in the valley, though, the light changes. It comes in flat between the ridges and lands without ceremony on the wet ground, on the metal, on the backs of whatever is moving. The brightness is constant. There is almost no dusk, almost no dawn. The day simply arrives and then departs, and the shadows do not lengthen so much as sharpen.

Water is here, but it is not a flood. It is a dampness. It condenses on every upright surface in the morning and runs down in slow threads, gathering into a film on the flat ones. It drips from the eaves of the small buildings, from the pipework, from the rims of the open tanks. It comes off the cliffs in veils after a rain, but most of the time the rain is somewhere else and the moisture here is just the valley breathing. The river through the middle is thin and clear and almost too cold to feel like a river; it sounds, when it sounds at all, like a kettle that has just stopped. The air is never dry. Your sleeves are heavy. The rocks sweat. Whatever metal has been here long enough has rusted into a deep maroon under the moss, and the rust is wet to the touch.

The vegetation has made a mistake, or has decided something. It is everywhere. It is the second thing you see, after the brightness. It fills the valley the way a liquid would, climbing the scree, climbing the lower cliffs, climbing the walls of the buildings, climbing the pipework itself where the pipes run low enough to the ground. Most of it is low: cushions of moss so deep you cannot see the rock under them, spongy and pale green, the color of old copper. There are ferns, but they are not tall ferns—they are the kind that grow in tight rosettes, each frond unrolling slow enough that you could mark them with a pin and come back next season. There are lichens in three colors at least, the white ones crusting the high rock and the orange ones eating into the iron and the black ones staining the underside of everything. There is a creeping willow that runs along the ground rather than up, making low mounds you could sit on without knowing you were sitting on a plant. And there is one other thing, a thing you don't immediately place, a tall pale stalk with no leaves that pokes up through the moss here and there, alone, like a finger held up to test the wind. The botanists who used to come called it something; the name didn't stick.

The buildings are scattered. They are not a town. They are more like the punctuation of a sentence that has been left out in the rain. A square concrete block with a sloped roof and a single dark window. A taller thing, four stories, made of riveted plate, with a pipe coming out of its top and going back down into the ground. A row of low cylinders, open at one end, lined up along a disused rail. A long shed with a chimney that has lost its cap. Some are far apart, separated by a hundred meters of moss; some are almost touching, leaning against each other as if for company. They were painted once. The paint is gone in some places, and in others it has gone under, taken over by the lichen, and the buildings appear to be a kind of greenish stone themselves, mineral outcrops, the same as the cliffs. The doors are mostly closed. The windows are mostly dark. There is no smoke.

But the place is not stopped. If you stood on a rise and watched for an hour, you would see, perhaps, a single light wink on inside one of the smaller buildings, and stay on. You would see, very rarely, a plume of vapor come from one of the stacks—not smoke, vapor, white and quickly gone, as if a kettle had sighed. You would see, if you were very patient, a section of pipework begin to vibrate faintly along its length, and then stop. The conveyor that runs along the old rail, the one that looks like a snake that died in the open, does sometimes move. Slowly. So slowly that you might think the wind were doing it, except there is no wind. It carries nothing visible, or it carries what it carries out of sight, down into a building and not back out. The light that winks on in the windows is a small and steady light, the color of a flame seen through fog, and it does not flicker. The plants do not seem to mind it. The plants, in fact, lean toward some of the buildings, as if the buildings were warmer than the rock, which perhaps they are.

The pipes are the strangest thing. They run between the buildings in long, low curves, never buried, never very high. They are bolted at the joints and the bolts are crusted with the orange lichen. The metal is pitted. They make no sound, or such a low sound that you cannot separate it from the ringing in your own ears after a long quiet. The air around them is faintly warmer than the air away from them, and in the cool of the morning they steam, briefly, like horses. They feel, when you put a hand on them, alive. Not hot. Just not cold. Something is moving through them. You can hear it if you press your ear to the metal, the way a child listens to a seashell, and what you hear is a hush and a far-off pulse, like a heart you cannot find.

There are almost no animals. A white moth, sometimes, drawn to the vapor. A pale frog in the runoff from one of the lower tanks, the only color on it a single dark stripe. Nothing in the air. Nothing in the moss that you can see, though the moss moves in small ways when you walk, as if there were things in it that did not wish to be seen. In the old records of the place, before the records stopped, there were goats, and a dog that belonged to someone, and a woman who kept chickens. There is a coop behind the long shed. The door is open. The wire is green with verdigris. Whatever was inside has long been a part of the ground.

The sound of the valley is the sound of a held breath. The river, when you can hear it, is a small flat sound, a sound without edges. The drips from the eaves make no rhythm, or a rhythm so irregular it has the feel of silence. The conveyor, when it moves, makes no sound you can place; it is felt before it is heard. The light itself seems to make no sound, although the eye aches after a while, a low ache, the kind you get from staring at snow. The wind, when it comes, comes through the pass at the upper end of the valley and is so steady and so thin that it is less a sound than a change in pressure on the ear. You can stand in the middle of all of it and feel that you are the loudest thing there.

The slow thing is everywhere. The moss is winning; it has been winning for a long time. It has crept a third of the way up the lower cliffs and shows no sign of stopping. The lichen is in the iron. The willow is in the rail. The ferns have opened in the cracks of the smallest foundations, in the gap between a door and its frame. The paint, where it survives, survives as a memory under the growth, a thing seen only when a stone falls and turns over a patch of ground. The buildings themselves are not sinking, but they are settling, in a way you can measure if you have a long time and a level. The pipes have sagged a little between their supports, an inch perhaps, in a span you could walk in a minute. The conveyor rails are out of true by less than a finger's width. The whole place is, by some small amount each year, becoming the ground it stands on. The vapor that comes from the stacks is no longer quite the vapor that came from them last year, and the light inside the windows, if you could see it from a long enough distance, would be a different color than it was a decade ago. The valley is taking itself back. It is doing this so slowly that to be there is to feel that nothing is happening, and yet to come back, if you did, would be to find something you did not quite remember.

There is a feeling, in the afternoon, when the light is straightest and the damp is thickest, that the valley is not a place but a process. That the rocks and the metal and the moss and the few small animals and you, if you are there, are all part of the same slow thing, the same patient working-through, and that it will not stop when you leave, and did not begin when you came, and that the river at the center of it is older than the rocks and will be there after the rocks are not.

That feeling, too, is mostly silent.
