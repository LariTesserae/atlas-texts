# The Hollow at Iridion's Crown

The first thing that meets you is the sound. It is not a sound so much as a *condition*, a weather of pressure that finds the inner surfaces of your chest and hums against them. Even at the rim, where the lip of the place is still cold and bare against the high wind, you can feel it as a wrongness in the throat — as if the air itself has been pried open and something is singing through it that does not intend to stop. By the time you have taken twenty steps down into the bowl of it, the noise has become geological. You cannot think beneath it. You have to let it think for you.

The bowl is small. A cathedral would not quite fit at the long axis; a modest chapel would drown in it. It is bounded, and unmistakably so — by grey-violet cliffs of compressed mica-ash that climb forty meters on three sides, and on the fourth side by a low broken wall of worked stone that is older than the lichen-lace creeping up its seams. Beyond the wall the slope continues, but you do not look at it for long. The eye keeps getting pulled back into the hollow. The hollow insists.

The cliffs are old. You can see it in the way they have given up verticality in places, in the way seams have been peeled open by seasons no one now remembers. There is *balance* here — that is the right word. A long-ago falling has finished falling. Whatever was going to collapse has collapsed. What remains is what will remain: a saturated, settled thing. The cliffs do not crackle. They croak, faintly, only when the wind draws across their pockets in the right way, and the croak is lost immediately in the louder roar that fills everything else.

There is no water.

You keep waiting for water. The eye insists there should be water. The floor of the bowl is a low haze of pale lavender gas — heavier than air, lying thick as a puddle just above the stone, so that from the rim it looks like a still pond of milk-of-violet. Step down into it and the gas parts around your knees and climbs your shins. It is cold. It is not wet. It does not condense on fabric. It is the residue of some chemistry the rocks are slow-cooking out of themselves at altitude, where the air is thin enough that heavier compounds settle and linger. Locals — if there are any — would never call it water. It does what water does here. It cradles things. It pools low. The whole interior of the bowl is its shallow, dry sea.

And in that sea — *in* that sea — the place is lush.

It is lush in a way that makes the word *lush* feel like a mistranslation. Nothing green. Nothing that drinks. The growths rise from the stone like coral does, like barnacles do — bright, branching, prodigal. They are mostly the color of dried blood and the color of tarnished copper and the color of bone, with accents in a metallic violet that catches the high sun and throws it back as colored afterimage. Some are woody. Some are pulpy. Some have the consistency of wax and bend in the constant wind without breaking. None of them appear to have roots in any meaningful sense. They are fastened to the stone by the slow method of mineral chemistry — they *grow into* the rock over years, and the rock grows into them. There are gnarled clusters the size of cars. There are slender chimneys of amber tissue three meters tall and as stiff as iron. Where the mists pool deepest, in the center of the bowl, the growth is so thick it has knotted itself into ridges and ravines of its own making, a small mountain range in miniature, six feet tall, that you could walk through if the sound permitted thought. From the rim you cannot tell that any of this is alive. From inside it, you cannot tell that any of it is not.

The growth is loud. *That* is what fills the bowl with noise. Every stem, every chimney, every waxy leaf and ridged bulb is a resonator, and the constant wind that pours across the rim and drops down into the basin finds in them the entire range of frequencies it could possibly want to make. The bowl is an organ pipe, the cliffs are the case, the growth is the pipework, and whatever is down here with you is the bellows. The roar is not a single note but a thousand-noted roar — undertones of stone, overtones of glass, a ceaseless shrieking sibilance of small leaves, a deeper flute-hum from the larger trunks, and, weaving through all of it, a thrumming drone at the resonant frequency of the bowl itself that you feel behind your eyes. It changes moment to moment. The wind pivots on its own. The growths grow during the time you stand here — you can watch a new spur come up out of stone in the corner of your eye, no longer than a finger by the time you turn to it, and as it lengthens it adds a new partial to the chord. The chord is never the same chord twice. The bowl is composing itself, against you, with you, for you, all at once.

And between the growths — abundant, *abundant* — the fauna.

It is difficult to say where the fauna ends and the growth begins. Many of the small things are sessile. Many of the moving things are camouflaged out of recognition. But they are everywhere, in the way that ants are everywhere in an anthill — not because they are crowded but because they are *omnipresent* in their craft. There are threaded sprites the size of a hand that crawl upside down along the chimney-trunks, harvesting something the eye cannot see, dripping it into bell-shaped blossoms that nod on over-long stems. There are small darting things with too many wings, really wings only in the way that wind through a fence is wind; they shudder along the lavender mist on rapid pelvic flips, and where they pass, the mist briefly dimples into standing waves. There are heavier things — long-bodied, low to the stone — that work their way through the growth with a glacial deliberation that is somehow more alarming than speed. They are pale. They are the color of the mist. When they stop, you cannot find them again. When the wind tugs at a trunk near one, it ripples its skin in a slow color-shift from cream to ash-blue and you see, for a moment, the shape of a creature that is not quite a slug and not quite a velvet worm and not quite a thought. They move as if they have already arrived. There are predators. You cannot see them. You know they are there because the darting sprites occasionally vanish in a way that a sprite should not vanish — with no flutter, no sound, just a lessening. The lessening itself is the predator, sometimes. The predator is the *removal*. There are also larger shapes that pass at the rim of vision, kept back by the growth or by the mist. You hear them. They are responsible for the undercurrent of low hooting that runs beneath the higher shrieks of the smaller pipes. The hooting is felt in the ribcage. You suspect they are browsing. You suspect they are as tall as you.

The hollow is not still. Nothing in this place is still. The wind is in constant rearrangement. The mists roll and pool in their slow currents. The growths grow. The darting sprites wheel. The predators remove. The hooters hoot and shift at the rim. The chord resolves and then refuses itself. If you stand at one point for ten minutes, the basin is a different basin. It is not the kind of turbulence that moves objects. It is the kind that moves *relationships*. You are standing in a sentence that is still being spoken. The bowl is speaking itself, in sounds, in growths, in movements, in the slow color-shifts of creatures indistinguishable from the stone. Every few minutes the chord pivots into something briefly consonant and then careens away. The consonance is a trap; it lures the ear; it passes.

Sewn through the whole — at exact, even intervals, on a grid so regular it can only be deliberate — are the built things.

They are faint. They are easy to miss. They are not ruins. They are not habitations. They are *markers*. Low pillars, perhaps knee-high, each one capped with a small crystalline bowl that catches the mist and holds it as a luminous violet pool. The pillars are spaced twenty meters apart. They trace what looks like a surveyor's grid across the floor of the bowl, and where their lines intersect, a slightly larger structure stands — a tripod of three slender pylons holding a black ring at chest height, and through the ring, suspended by nothing visible, a small dense knot of grey light turns slowly. The grey light does not illuminate. It absorbs. To look directly at it is to look into a depth that does not belong inside the ring. To look beside it is to see the mists dimpling toward it from a meter away, drawn in.

The pillars are old. The grid has weathered into a pattern that the growths have begun to colonize — knee-high rust-colored caps with bright violet pools in their crowns have themselves become perches, become habitats. The grid is no longer being tended. Whatever tended it has moved on, up, elsewhere, into adjacent possibilities of physics that this bowl can sample but not contain. The pillars remain the way an old astronomical clock remains — not as function, but as a clock. The triplet rings still turn, and the grey knots still turn within them, and the mists still feed into them, and the bowl still speaks, and the chord still speaks, and the creatures still hunt, and the sound is still deafening, and it will still be deafening in a thousand years when the pillars are mounds and the mists have eaten them.

You can stand in one of the squares of the grid and watch the whole system act itself out across twenty meters of rock. The pillars are your colonnade. The chord turns overhead and around and under. The growths whisper and shriek. The darting sprites shuttle. The mist pools. The predators remove. You are inside a cathedral whose congregation does not know it is a congregation. You are inside a survey mark whose purpose has outlived the surveyors. You are inside a basin that has been here long enough to make peace with itself, and has done so at such volume that standing inside the peace is almost the same as being struck by it.

The cool is real. The cool is high-altitude cool — thin, mineral, slightly metallic against the soft palate. The light is honest: a clean diffuse wash off the upper sky, with the lower mists underlighting it in violet whenever you look across the basin at a low angle. The shadows are short and move. You can stay in the basin for as long as your chest can take the empty air and your ears can take the fullness of the sound. Then you climb back up to the rim. The wind at the rim is cold and clean and shapeless and it has no chord at all and after a few breaths of it you realize how loud it had been down below — how thick the chord had been against your head. The lip of the place, when you finally turn from it, still hums faintly in the bones of your inner ear, a tone you will carry with you for hours, that will eventually fade, that the next place you walk into will not replace with anything else, because no other place is doing what that place is doing.

The basin churns behind you. You do not look back. You do not need to. It will be different by the time you would have looked.
