# The Cathode Reaches

Below the surface, then. Far below.

The tunnel unspools in every direction, a hundred kilometers of it, more bore than the mind wants to count. The walls are welded plate, decade on decade on decade — you can read the joins like tree rings if you bring a light close enough, a darker steel laid over a lighter, an alloy nobody names anymore, a rivet pattern that went out of fashion three generations of crews ago. Everything here is *made*. The rock, the grit, the air that moves — all of it arrived on a train. The substrate is the architecture, the architecture is the substrate; the only dirt is the dust of human occupation, ground finer with every passing year.

The temperature holds around four degrees. You wear the coat they issue you, and the coat is not enough, and the second coat is not enough, and you learn that the cold is not the temperature so much as the *wetness of the cold*, the way it finds the seams of every layer and pools against the small of the back. Water, here, is the medium of almost everything. It comes in from above through a thousand drilled weep-holes, gathers in the gutters and the half-pipes and the long sedimentational troughs that run the length of the main drift, and then it is led away by channels that themselves weep, and so it never really leaves. The whole of the Reaches feels like the inside of a lung.

The light is poor. A few strings of the old sodiums still burn in the high arches, casting a jaundice you can almost ignore, and where the new LED ribbons have been strung they give a colder, flatter thing that does not so much illuminate the space as advertise it. Most of the work happens under the headlamp cone. The dark between the lamps is not empty dark but *reservoir* dark, dark with shape just out of register, and you find yourself filling it with whatever you last feared.

The sound is the first thing and the last thing. The drainage, a constant low hiss, like rain you can never get out of. The rails, humming with the passage of a battery locomotive somewhere far down the line. The turbines — six of them, set into the lateral adits, fed by the falling water from above, spinning at a speed that adds a barely-there whine to everything. And the air itself, moved by those turbines, moved by the pumping stations a kilometer east, moved by the long pistons of the ventilation plant that exhale and inhale on their own patient schedule. The pressure of it pushes gently on your eardrums and you learn, after a week, to yawn at the right moments to keep the world in balance.

Vegetation: there is none. There was, once, a project. The fungus terraces in the old research bay, stacked trays of something pale that grew on the moisture and the runoff and a labor of care no one could afford after the second closure. The trays are still there, dry, a brittle felt you can peel up with a fingernail. The only green is the algae that lives in the gutters, a slow scum you do not touch, and the rarer bloom — emerald, startling — that erupts in a few favored cracks where copper pipe sweats onto bare steel. The Reaches are metal and water and the human signal that lives between.

Fauna: rare, and you'd be glad of more of it. A white arthropod, a centimeter long, found sometimes in the new concrete where the water pools warmest, and which the engineers call — without affection — the *concrete mite*. A pale moth, blown in on a draft from somewhere above and which cannot survive the first lamp it settles on. A rat, once a year, if the drainage delivers it from a surface it can no longer be returned to, and which becomes a small event, watched by everyone, given what water it can be made to drink.

The strange thing. The strange thing is the hour. The Reaches do not have a day. There is a shift, and a shift, and a between-shift, and a sleep, and the rest is metric. Yet there is a feeling here, deep in the long drifts, that the *place itself* is keeping time. The turbines pulse. The water finds a different gutter at different hours, because the pumping has a schedule, and the air has a tide, and the LEDs brighten in their zones according to a logic no operator will own to. You find yourself, in the third week, knowing what hour it is without checking. You find yourself, in the third month, unable to remember that this is a thing you have learned.

Activity: it is busy down here, in its slow way. Trains move. Drainage is checked. The cable-tensioning crews inch along the catwalks with their spanners, singing sometimes, off-key, into the cotton of their masks. The load-bearing ribs of the main arch are inspected, and re-inspected, and the data is sent somewhere that the data goes. A new bore is being driven east, into rock that is technically not rock but the compressed tailings of a previous century's industry, and the cutter head goes around and around in a darkness it can never see the end of, and around it, day after day, the spoil is hauled out and the segments are carried in and the tunnel gets a little longer, and the *Reaches* get a little wider, and no one is in charge of this and no one is not.

The cold and the wet and the dim and the manufactured dark and the slow moving of the machines, and the people threading through it all, small and orange-vested and necessary, and the place, vast, unfinished, indifferent, taking them in.
