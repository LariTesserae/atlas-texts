# The Reed Cathedral

You stand in a sun-warmed hollow, and the world is green from floor to ceiling.

The ground beneath your feet is not soil. It is root. A knotted, interwoven mat of living root perhaps eight feet thick, springy as packed sod, warm through the soles of your feet. It gives slightly when you shift your weight, and somewhere far below, deep in the mat, you can feel a faint pulse — not a heartbeat exactly, but the slow peristaltic squeeze of water being drawn upward through a column you cannot see. The roots are the color of old copper, veined with green where they meet the air, and they smell of tannin and warm earth and something almost sweet, like crushed starfruit.

Above you, the light. The light is enormous. It pours in from somewhere overhead — a ragged opening, a natural skylight in what otherwise would be a ceiling of vegetation — and falls in a single thick column onto a circular pool twenty feet across. The pool is the only truly still water you can see. Its surface is mirror-bright, and the column of light, striking it, sets the water glowing a pale impossible green from within, as though the pool is lit from underneath. Around the pool, the floor rises in a gentle ramp of root, and then the walls begin.

The walls are not walls. They are plants.

They are reeds, but no reed that ever grew in a marsh. They stand thirty, forty, fifty feet tall, each one a culm as thick as a man's thigh at the base, tapering to a finger's width at the top, and they crowd the space so closely that you can barely pass between any two of them. Their color at the base is a deep red-violet, like an old bruise, fading through burgundy to a clear emerald green by the time they reach the broken canopy overhead. They are perfectly vertical. They are absolutely still. They are warm to the touch when you press a palm against one, and you can feel the slow pull of water rising through the column, a faint tremor as the vessel walls constrict.

At the tops, the reeds branch. The branches weave together into the canopy — not leaves exactly, but a fine, fibrous mat that filters the sky above into a moving patchwork of yellow-white light. Wherever the mat thins, the light comes through in long dusty bars that angle down into the reed forest and make the warm air visible. You can see the heat, the way you sometimes see it over a summer road. It never goes away. The air is hot and humid and smells of growing things and of ozone, faintly, the way it smells before a storm that never quite arrives.

The wind, when it comes, comes from above. A breath of drier air descends through the skylight and moves across the pool, rippling the surface, and the reeds bend to it in a slow wave that begins near the opening and runs outward through the forest, a visible pulse that takes a full minute to travel from the center to the farthest wall. When the wave passes, the reeds straighten again, and there is a sound — a low, papery rustle, like a hundred umbrellas opening at once — and then silence returns.

---

There are other things in the forest.

Pockets of water gather in the crooks where the roots thicken, some as small as cupped hands, some the size of millponds, and these are populated. In one, a dozen pale insects skate on the surface, leaving long V-wakes behind them, each one the size of your thumb. In another, a small dark shape — something with four legs and a long tail — breaks the surface, considers you with two pinprick eyes, and sinks again without a sound. Once, very far off, a sound like a wooden bell being struck with a wooden mallet, three times, then nothing. You never see what made it.

The water is everywhere, but it is not a flood. It is a *humidity* of water, a dampness woven through everything. Condensation beads on the upright reeds in fine droplets that catch the light and run down in slow silver threads. The root mat underfoot is always faintly wet; you can press a finger into it and water will ooze out, dark and cool. From the ceiling, in a few places, single drops fall at unpredictable intervals — you can stand for ten minutes and hear nothing, and then a drop will strike the pool with a clean, distant *tok* that hangs in the air for a long time. The air itself is almost liquid. Breathing is a weight.

---

Twice you notice structures.

The first is a wall, not far from the pool, made of stones stacked without mortar. It rises only to about your waist and then stops, as though the builder lost interest or was interrupted. The stones are dry, pale, sun-bleached, and they do not belong here — they are the only things in the hollow that are not green or red or wet. Moss has begun to climb them from the base, and a single reed has taken root in the crack between the top two stones and grown up through the heap and into the canopy, indistinguishable now from its neighbors except that it is leaning, drunkenly, against the wall it has replaced. The wall is older than the reed. Or perhaps not. It is hard to say.

The second is a small hut, or the memory of a hut, perhaps sixty feet from the pool. Four corner posts of a dark, petrified-looking wood, set in a square. A doorway. No walls, no roof — only the posts, and the spaces between them, and a long curved shape on the ground inside that might be a sleeping bench or might be a root that has decided to lie horizontal. The wood is very old and very hard and has taken on the color of the surrounding roots, so that it is easy to walk past without seeing it. Someone lived here. Or something that wanted to live here. The shape on the ground is too regular to be entirely natural, and not quite regular enough to be entirely made.

There are no other marks. No paths worn into the root mat, no tools, no cloth, no metal. No firepit, although there is a place near the pool where a circle of reeds has died and gone silver and brittle, as though a heat had touched them once. The hollow has the feel of a place that was visited, briefly, by hands that knew how to make things, and then those hands left, and the forest very politely forgot them.

---

The light does not change.

You stand there for a long time  an hour, two hours, you cannot tell — and the column of light through the skylight does not move. There is no sun to track, no shadow to lengthen, no slow fade toward evening. The brightness is simply constant, a permanent noon poured into the hollow, and the air remains hot and still, and the pool remains mirror-flat, and the reeds remain upright. The only motion in the entire world is the slow upward pulse of water through the living columns, and the occasional descent of a single drop from the canopy, and, very rarely, the breath of wind from above that sends its long wave running outward through the forest. Apart from these, nothing. The hollow is not aging. The hollow is *finished*. It is the place it is going to be, and it is already that place, and it will be that place when you leave and after.

You understand, standing in the heat, that nothing here fears you. The insects do not flee. The dark four-legged thing in the pool has no wariness in its glance. The reeds do not rustle at your approach. The whole cathedral is indifferent to you the way a mountain is indifferent — not unkindly, not cruelly, simply because you are too small and too brief to register. You are a warm thing that has wandered into a hot place, and the hot place will hold you for as long as you stay, and it will not miss you when you go.

Somewhere, very far above, beyond the canopy, a bird makes a sound — a thin, wandering whistle, repeated three times, and then cut off, as though a door closed. You look up. The skylight is a ragged circle of white. You cannot see the sky. You cannot see anything but light, and the light does not answer.

You stay a little longer. The drop falls. *Tok.* The water rings. The reeds hold the sound and let it go.
