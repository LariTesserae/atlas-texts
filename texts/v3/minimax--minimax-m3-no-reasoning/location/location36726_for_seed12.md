The world here is mostly water. Not all water — not yet — but mostly, and the not-yet of it matters. Imagine a sea that has no horizon because the sky is also wet: a low ceiling of vapor, gray-violet, that begins twenty meters up and simply continues into a permanent mist that cannot be called fog because fog implies a clearing elsewhere. There is no clearing. The rain does not fall so much as hang — a sustained, vertical, almost architectural curtain that moves in slow sheets when the wind bothers to think about it. When it touches skin it is the temperature of a bath left too long. The light that reaches the surface of the water is the color of old bruises, lavender and a green so dark it is almost black, and it dies within a meter of depth.

The water itself is warm. Not tropical — that implies a sun, and whatever sun this world once had has been diffused into a permanent fluorescent gloom — but warm the way a held breath is warm, the way body heat is warm, the way a fever is warm. It moves in currents you can feel against your skin even at the surface, long slow rivers within the larger river, and these currents are the closest thing the place has to weather.

Above the water: the high.

There is land here, but it is being born. The erosion parameter sits near zero, and you can read it in the geology — nothing has been worn down, nothing has been smoothed. Stone is the texture of broken teeth. Ridges rise like the back of something still trying to stand. A massif occupies the middle distance, and it is not so much climbed as negotiated, because every handhold is fresh, every edge is sharp in the way only new things can be sharp. The rock is dark. Basaltic, mostly, with veins of something greener that the locals have a name for and you will not learn. The massif is high enough that its upper third escapes the worst of the rain, though not the wet — up there the air is simply a different density of water, a fog you can almost chew.

And on that massif, and on the smaller ridgelines that cluster around its base like offspring still deciding whether to stand, there is a town.

It is not a town the way you are used to towns being. The settlement-scale is real — there are perhaps a thousand souls here, and another few hundred scattered along the causeways — but the *built* quality is strange. The builders have worked with what the geology gave them: slabs of basalt, laid on edge, lashed with vine, roofed with the broad ribbed leaves of a plant that grows nowhere else because nowhere else has this exact regime of warm mist and mineral water. The houses are dark against dark. Smoke rises from some of them, thin and quickly absorbed by the wet air. Paths are cut into the stone itself, polished by feet and water into a low gloss. There are no carts. There are no wheels. There are small boats, many small boats, tied to stone bollards at the waterline, and these are how the town actually moves — by water, between the ridgelines, in a permanent negotiation with the sea.

The tech is old. Bronze, maybe — you can see the dull glint of it on a tool here, a hinge there — but the place has the feel of stone-and-fiber, of bone needles and hand-thrown pottery, of people who have always lived here and never needed to leave. They fish. They harvest something from the deep water, something that glows faintly when brought into air, and they trade it in glass jars stoppered with wax. The jars are their nearest approach to currency. Beyond that, the town is its own small closed economy of tide and gossip.

The vegetation is the surprise. Lush, the parameter said, and lush it is, but *where*. Nothing grows low — nothing at the waterline, nothing in the splash zone, because the splash zone is also the erosion zone and the salt zone and the zone of a hundred small violences. But above the tide mark, where the stone begins, the plants arrive all at once, as if a line had been drawn. Ferns the size of doors. Vines thick as thighs, looped over the ridgelines, dropping long tassels of yellow flowers that nod slowly in the wet air. Moss, everywhere moss, in greens so saturated they look artificial. And one tree — only one species, but it dominates — a tall, buttressed thing with a pale trunk and a crown that is permanently weeping, not from rain but from its own structure, long streamers of foliage that trail in the water and root where they touch. The town has been built in a forest that is half-drowned and that does not mind.

The fauna is thin. You will see, if you are here at the right hour (and the hours here are notional, because the light barely changes), a long dark shape moving between the ridgelines at the waterline — some kind of crocodile thing, or otter thing, it doesn't matter, it is patient and it is fed. There are birds, a few, very loud, with calls like metal struck underwater. There are insects. There is something that lives in the soil of the upper massif, blind and pale, that the people eat and do not discuss. Otherwise the place is mostly quiet, mostly its own sounds: water on water, water on stone, the slow complaint of a vine under its own weight.

The slow is important. Almost nothing here moves fast. The currents. The growth. The light changing, if it changes at all, across hours that feel like days. Even the strangeness of the place is patient strangeness — not the abrupt weird of a hallucination but the accumulated weird of a world that has been doing this one thing for a very long time and has gotten stranger by increments you cannot perceive.

Because it is strange. You can feel it. The light is wrong. The water is too warm. The plants are too certain. The town is too well-placed — as if it had been set down by hand into a geometry that was always going to receive it. There are stories the locals tell, if you can get someone to tell you, about the time before the water, about a world of dry stone and dry wind, and these stories have the cadence of mythology rather than history. They say the sea came up over many generations, or it came down in a single night, depending on who you ask. They say the ridgelines are the backs of things that did not finish drowning. They say the deep water is not empty but is full of a kind of attention, slow and warm, that does not think in our timescales.

You will not, standing here on the slick basalt of the upper path, with the mist in your hair and the dark water below you and the long cry of a metal-bird somewhere in the wet trees, be able to say whether any of this is true. The place will not tell you. It is still in the process of becoming itself, and it does not have the energy for explanations.

It will simply continue.

The water will be warm against the stone. The vine will lower another inch. The town will smoke. The creature in the deep will turn, once, in its long sleep. And you, if you stay, will find that your own breathing has slowed to match the pace of the place, and that this, too, is a kind of answer.
