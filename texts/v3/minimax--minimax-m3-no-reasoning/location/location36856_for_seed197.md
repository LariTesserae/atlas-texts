# The Verdant Hollow

You stand in a cathedral that has forgotten it was ever built.

The ceiling—if it can be called that—is a vaulted mass of living canopy, fifty meters overhead, woven from trunks and boughs so old their bark has gone the color of old bone. Gaps in the foliage let down columns of greenish light, each one visibly thick with suspended spores and pollen, and where these columns strike the floor they pool like liquid. The light moves, but not with any sun you can locate; it migrates across the chamber as if the canopy itself were the source, breathing illumination outward in slow tidal pulses.

Beneath you: a floor that is not quite floor. Roots. Roots upon roots upon roots, interwoven into a kind of felted mat so dense your boots only sink an inch before meeting a hidden lattice of older wood. The mat breathes. You can feel it through your soles—a low, regular rise and fall, perhaps the work of water table, perhaps the work of the whole place inhaling. In places the root-mat has parted, opening onto shallow basins of black water that reflect nothing, that seem to drink the light rather than return it. These are the damp places. Everywhere else, condensation beads on every surface, and the air tastes of wet bark and something sweeter, overripe, almost floral, on the edge of rot.

There is no sound. There is *nothing but* sound.

The roar is the first thing that should have deafened you, but it has been going so long that your brain has simply built a wall around it, the way the body walls off chronic pain. Beneath the roar is a chord. Beneath the chord, individual voices. You can pick out, if you listen past the white-noise wash: a percussion of wood on wood, somewhere distant and rhythmic, like a drummer with a great many drums. A high thin whine, almost metallic, that swells and recedes in eight-second cycles. A low sustained vibration felt more in the chest than heard, the kind of sound that lives in the same register as a faraway avalanche or a whale's complaint. And threading through it all, a chittering, chirring, clicking chorus—millions of small wet mouths, all speaking at once, none waiting for another to finish.

The fauna. The fauna is impossible. The fauna is not what the word *fauna* was designed to hold.

Something the size of a small cat moves along a branch thirty meters up. It has too many legs. It has what might be wings, folded. It has a throat pouch that inflates with each of its calls, and its calls comprise roughly a quarter of the lower register. It is, you understand dimly, only a kind of sparrow here, only a city bird, only the sort of thing that lives in gutters. Below it, in a tangle of aerial roots, a slow pale shape breathes with the patience of a sleeping child. A line of luminous ants traverses a vine in procession, each carrying a fragment of leaf larger than itself, and their collective footsteps produce the percussion. From somewhere behind a column of buttress-root, a wet rhythmic sound: flesh on flesh, scales on bark, the unhurried industry of a thing that has no predators and all the time in the world.

The chamber itself—if chamber is the word—extends in every direction, and yet you sense its edges. Perhaps three hundred meters across. Perhaps less. The geometry refuses to be measured; distances are suggestions. You came in through an arch of fused trunks that you could swear was not there when you turned to look at it again. There is a quality to the space that makes you suspect the dimensions are *negotiated* rather than fixed, that the cathedral agrees to be this big only while you are looking, and would be some other size for some other eye.

The architecture is a city and a city is a forest and a forest is a single vast slow organism, and the question of where one thing ends and another begins has been settled, here, in the negative. Trunks serve as pillars. Pillars serve as trunks. Canopies serve as ceilings. Ceilings serve as canopies. Lianas serve as plumbing. Somewhere in the greenish murk above, water is being lifted against its own inclination by capillary action and then released, drop by careful drop, into stone basins that overflow into channels that irrigate the roots that hold up the floor. The engineering is exquisite and the engineers are blind and have no names.

The dew is constant. It is on your hands, your collar, your eyelashes. The air is warm the way bathwater is warm—not hot, not cool, but *inhabited*, temperature as a medium rather than a condition. Moths the length of your hand drift through the light columns, and the dust they disturb glows faintly, as if the spores have a small bioluminescent opinion of their own.

The sound does not stop. The sound has not stopped. Somewhere in the cathedral a creature you have not seen is producing a note that is not a note, a sustained pressure-wave that your inner ear has begun to interpret as color, and the color is the green of new leaves, and you cannot tell whether the green is making the sound or the sound is making the green. The pillars breathe. The light migrates. The roots underfoot shift a quarter-inch and resettle. The city of trees and the city of things-that-live-in-trees goes about its business of being more alive than the world you came from, and the business produces, as its principal byproduct, a sound that fills every cubic inch of available space and leaves no room for silence, and you will not hear quiet again for as long as you stay within these walls, and you are not certain, looking up through the slow green rain of spores into a canopy that may or may not have a face, that you want to leave.
