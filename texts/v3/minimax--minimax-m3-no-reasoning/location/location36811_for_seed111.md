# The Crease

The approach is up. Always up. A path that should not exist, on a mountain that has not existed long enough to be smoothed. The rocks are sharp at the ankle and the air at the shoulder and the cold settles into the chest like water finding its level. The whole spine of the range is recent—still gritting its teeth, still breaking off in fresh slabs that clatter down and lie unweathered at the foot of things. The lichen is what came first, and even the lichen is thin.

The Crease is a pocket in the upper wall of a shoulder of one of these new peaks. From a distance, in the right light, you might take it for a shadow—a vertical shadow, a wound of dark. The opening is narrow and tall, wider at the top than the bottom, as if the mountain inhaled and held it. Inside, the space is cathedral-scaled: forty paces across, perhaps seventy deep, walled in by rock that has not yet learned to be soft. The walls go up. The walls go up further than you can comfortably see.

Light enters as a single pale column that moves across the floor through the day, but for most hours the Crease is in gloom. The rock holds shadow the way wood holds grain. The pool at the center—fed only by what drips from the walls and trickles down from a snowpatch that has wedged itself into a back crack and will not, this year or any year soon, finish melting—reflects what little there is. The water is black, then grey, then briefly silver, then black again as the light shifts on its own slow calendar.

It is damp. Always damp. The walls weep. The floor is slick. A man who has spent a night here will find his sleeping hide heavier in the morning than it was the night before, and his boots will have grown a fine moustache of condensation at the eyelets. There is a smell of wet stone and another smell beneath it, heavier, sweetish, the smell of a great many living things breathing in a small room. The air is cool the year through—cool even when the sun outside is bleaching the scree—and it tastes of iron and of something older.

The stone is sharp because the Crease is young. A block fell, or slid, or was thrust up and cracked through; the clean faces of the cleft are those of a fracture that has had only decades to consider itself. The grain of the rock is vertical in places and horizontal in others, the way a broken thing is vertical and horizontal, and where the water has traveled longest along these faces it has laid down a stain the color of dried blood, in long runs and branching fans. The floor is not a floor in any human sense but a tumbled geometry of slabs and wedges that have come to rest against one another at the angles of recent letting-go. The snowpatch at the back glows faintly through its own thickness, blue-white, the only really bright thing in the place.

The vegetation is almost a rumor. Lichen, in pale roundels on the upper walls where the light sometimes catches. A scatter of dark moss in the cracks that hold water longest, the kind of moss that looks like a small animal pressed flat. Two or three cushion plants clinging to a ledge near the opening, gray-green, tough as old rope, with a single dried flower stalk from some forgotten summer. At the back, near the snowpatch, a strange thing grows: a fungus, or a lichen, or something between—pale, frilled, smelling of nothing, fed by the constant moisture and the constant dark. It is the only living thing in the Crease that does not seem to be afraid.

The fauna is the place.

They are in the walls. They are in the cracks. They are on the floor, in the water, on the snowpatch, hanging from the ledges in soft clusters. There are thousands. There may be tens of thousands. The walls, the ledges, every horizontal seam and sloped face is furred with them, a living wallpaper of small, pale, thick-bodied things. They are about the length of a man's hand. They have soft, almost translucent fur, the color of old tallow. Their eyes are large and black and wet. They have small, clever forepaws and wide, soft mouths and they make, continuously and without apparent pause, a low chittering sound that is the baseline note of the Crease: a sound like rain on thatch, like many small hard things being shaken in a leather bag, like the inside of a hive. It does not stop. When you first hear it you think it is the wind in the cleft. When you understand it is not the wind, the place becomes a different place.

They are not quite mammals. They are not quite anything else. They have too many teeth. Their young are born furred and walking, which is a thing you have to see once and then cannot stop thinking about. They do not fly, though they have a flap of skin from foreleg to flank that they spread when they are alarmed or when they court. They cluster because they are warm and because the Crease is warm, in its way—warmed by their bodies, by the trace heat that comes from somewhere deep in the rock, by the snowpatch's slow exhalation. They feed on the moss and the lichen and the small blind insects that live in the guano and on one another, when a weaker one fails. They are, for the most part, calm. They have not learned to fear the things that come into their cleft, because nothing in the cleft has ever, in their long collective memory, hunted them in earnest.

And yet.

Above the chitter, layered through it, comes the other sound. A higher, thinner call, almost a whistle, that lifts and falls in a slow, patient arc. One of them does this sometimes. Sometimes several, in turn. It is not clear what the call is for. It does not seem to be mating, because they mate at any time. It does not seem to be alarm, because nothing alarms. It is a kind of asking. The Crease is loud with them. The Crease is loud with them the way a forest is loud with insects: you can stand in the middle of it and feel the sound against your skin, in your teeth, in the bones of your skull, and after a while you stop hearing it and start hearing through it.

A man has been here.

He is not here now, but he has been here, in this season or the last. There are toeholds cut into the wall of the cleft, on the left as you come in, in a line that climbs fifteen paces and then ends at a small ledge where a man could sit with his back to the stone and his legs dangling. The holds are simple—a notch here, a notch there, a place chipped away for the ball of the foot. The chips at the base of each hold are still pale, the color of fresh-fractured rock, the color of things not yet oxidized. The tool that cut them was stone. Maybe antler. Something that was not metal.

There is a pile of small bones on the ledge, neatly arranged in a row, all of them from the forepaws of the creatures. A line of offerings, or a tally, or a prayer. Beside them, a scrap of cured hide and a stone knife, the blade of which is the same dark stone as the wall. The man returns in the summer, when the snow on the approach is firm enough to walk on and soft enough to kick steps into. He comes for the creatures. He takes what he can carry. He leaves the bones. He has been doing this for longer than his father's father's father remembered being told about. There is no village, no woman, no child, no name that he has given the place or that the place has given him. The Crease is not a home. The Crease is where the eating is.

There are other marks of him, older. A row of pictographs on the back wall, behind the snowpatch, painted in ochre and what may be blood, of the kind that does not quite wash off. They show the cleft, shown from outside, with the small dark mouths of the creatures on the walls. They show a figure with upraised arms, which may be the painter or may be something else. They show, in the last of them, a thing that is not a man and not one of the creatures but is shaped a little like both, and that is looking out from the cleft. The figure has no eyes. The ochre of this last pictograph is thicker than the others, as if more hands worked on it, or as if it was redone, again and again, across many seasons.

The place is slow.

The water moves. The stain grows, by a finger's width in a human year. The lichen creeps. The creatures are born and die and are eaten and their bones fall to the floor and are ground into the guano that is, by depth, the true floor of the Crease—a black, soft, layered thing, a foot thick in places, smelling of ammonia and time. The snowpatch retreats by an inch each summer, advances by an inch each winter, and in this way is almost the same as it was when the first man climbed the holds. The stain on the back wall is slightly darker than it was when the pictographs were painted. The fungus by the snowpatch has spread another palm's width. The Crease is not in a hurry. The Crease is, in the long way of such things, becoming more itself.

And once in a long while—so rarely that the man who comes has never seen it, and his father's father only spoke of it once, and the speaking was at the end of a long silence—something in the walls exhales. A breath of warmer air from somewhere below, carrying a faint mineral sweetness, a faint wrongness. The creatures go quiet for a moment. The pool stills. The man, if he is there, feels the hair on his arms lift and does not know why. Then the breath is gone. The chitter resumes. The light moves another degree across the floor.

The Crease is open. The Crease is always open. The mountain is young, the cleft is young, the man is old, the creatures are older, and the thing at the back of the cleft, the thing without eyes, the thing the man has never seen and paints anyway, is older still, and patient, and will wait.
