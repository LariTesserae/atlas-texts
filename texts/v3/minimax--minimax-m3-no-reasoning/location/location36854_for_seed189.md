# The Lung

You find it by following a sound.

Not a sound, exactly — more the absence of one. A place where the wind that has been moving through the upper rock strata for as long as anyone can remember simply… stops. A node of silence. And from inside that silence, something else: a low, wet, rhythmic throb, like a heart the size of a cathedral heard through twenty feet of limestone. The kind of sound you feel in your sternum before your ears parse it. So when the elders send you down with a stone hand-axe and a clay lamp and tell you to bring back a piece of the *thing that hums*, you know, somewhere beneath the simple obedience, that you are not coming back unchanged.

The descent takes most of a day. The passage is narrow in places, ancient-water-carved, and the limestone still holds the sharp ribbing of its recent cutting — fresh erosion, the bones of the mountain still exposed and unworn, a place carved in the last hundred thousand years by water that has since gone elsewhere. You have to squeeze sideways at one point, and again at another. Your lamp throws your shadow long against walls that sweat a thin clear moisture; the rock is damp here in a way that isn't quite dripping, more *breathed upon*, as if the stone itself is exhaling. The air is warm. Warmer than it should be at this depth. Warm like a closed room with a body still in it.

Then the passage opens, and you step out onto a ledge, and you understand why no one comes back unchanged.

---

The space is — wrong. Not wrong the way a ruin is wrong, or a corpse. Wrong the way a word is wrong when it means too much.

It is not large. It is not vast. It is *contained* — a single chamber, a pocket, a hidden room the size of a village square, with a low arched ceiling that presses down like a held breath. You can see the far wall. You can see every wall. The space is *comprehensible*, and that is the first mercy. But.

The chamber is *empty*. That is the second thing. Vastly, overwhelmingly empty. The air between things is so generous it feels like a substance. You could throw a stone from where you stand and watch it tumble through nothing for a long time before it lands.

And what is in that nothing — the few presences that exist — are *enormous*.

There is, you realize after a moment, a plant.

There is a *plant*.

It is not *in* the chamber. The chamber is *in it*. Or: the chamber and the plant are the same thing. Or: there is no chamber. There is only plant, and the chamber is a hollow it grew around, the way a gall grows around the thing that made it. You cannot tell which sentence is true because the plant does not commit to one or the other. It is built into the walls. It is the wall, in places. Long pale roots, each as thick as a man's torso, descend from the ceiling and plunge into the floor without visible connection to any growing tip, and they curve in directions that roots should not curve — they bend sideways, they bend *backwards*, they form loops and Möbius-strips that your eyes slide off of. Some of them pass through each other. Some of them pass through *themselves*. The geometry down here is a suggestion, not a rule. You learn not to look too long at any single root, or you begin to feel that you are also a suggestion.

The plant is not green. Nothing here is green. The roots are the color of bone and the color of wax and the color of milk held up to a lamp, and in places they are translucent, and you can see fluid moving inside them — slow, sluggish, dark — and when the fluid passes a knot in the root it makes a sound, a deep resonant *thock*, and that is the sound you heard from outside. The whole structure pulses with it. *Thock. Thock. Thock.* Not fast. Not slow. The pace of something that has been doing this for a long time and intends to go on.

The fluid is water. The roots are full of it. When a knot pulses, a thin mist exhales from somewhere — from a pore, from a crack in the limestone, from a place where two roots have grown *around* each other and left a small hollow between them. The mist drifts into the chamber and becomes the dampness on the stone, becomes the condensation on your lamp-glass, becomes the slick under your boots. The plant is breathing the room. You are standing inside its respiration.

---

There is light. You do not know where the light is coming from.

Your clay lamp is small and dim and its orange flicker does not reach the far wall. But the far wall is visible. The ceiling is visible. The roots are visible — every translucent vein, every pale curving surface — in a soft, sourceless illumination the color of overcast morning, the color of light filtered through a membrane. It is light, and it is normal light, and you cannot find its origin. The roots themselves do not glow. The stone does not glow. You lift your lamp and shade it with your hand and the room does not darken. You cup your hand to a root and look for light inside it, and the light is *there*, but it is also *in your eye*, and it is *between your eye and the root*, and you cannot extract it. After a while you stop trying, because trying makes the light behave more strangely, and you have been told not to bring back a mind that has come unmoored. Only a piece of the thing that hums.

The light does not flicker. It does not pulse in time with the *thock*. It is simply, steadily present, like the light in a room you have lived in your whole life, and it is this normalcy — this *familiarity* — that is the most unsettling thing about it, because the room you have lived in your whole life was not like this.

---

The plant moves.

Not quickly. Not in any way your hunting-eye would call motion. But if you stand on the ledge long enough — and you will stand on the ledge long enough, because the ledge is as far as you are willing to go today — you see a root in the far corner where there was no root before. And you see a root that was there when you arrived has grown, in the time you have been looking, a handspan. And the fluid inside the near veins has moved a foot along its slow course. The chamber is not static. It is growing. It is *becoming*. It is doing this slowly enough that you could deny it, if you wanted to, if denying it would let you sleep tonight. But you are not a fool. You are the one they sent because you do not lie to yourself about what you see.

And what you see: a thing that fills the space without filling it. A thing that is, in some sense that the elders will not let you explain plainly, *most* of what is here. Every surface, examined closely, has plant in it — fine white fibers threaded through the stone, pale threads emerging from hairline cracks and joining roots, joining veins, joining the slow dark river. The plant is not *growing on* the rock. The plant is *in conversation with* the rock. Where they meet, you cannot tell which is which. The stone is damp because the plant is damp. The plant breathes because the stone lets it. The chamber was not carved by water. The chamber was *grown*. It is young. It is freshly grown. It is the newest thing in this mountain, and the mountain is very old, and this should not be possible, and yet here you are.

The plant is not a single organism. Or it is. You cannot tell. The roots that pass through each other are the same root. The roots that loop and join their own earlier selves are the same root. There is a region in the center of the chamber where the roots are so dense they form a kind of forest — a small wood of vertical pale columns, each one the thickness of your arm, each one flexing gently in a wind that does not exist — and you understand that this is a *lung*. The chamber is a lung. The plant is the tissue of a lung. The room breathes in and the room breathes out. The mist comes. The mist goes. The *thock* is the heartbeat. You are standing inside a breath.

---

There is no animal here.

There are no insects. There are no spiders. There are no bats clinging to the ceiling, no eyeless fish in the standing pools that gather in the low places of the floor, and there are pools — small ones, no bigger than your palm, dark water that has dripped from some root-tip and gathered in a limestone depression. You kneel and look into one. The water is clear and still and there is nothing alive in it. But the water is not *still*. It moves in time with the *thock*. It rises a hair's width and falls a hair's width, and you realize it is not a pool. It is a drop. A single drop, balancing on a surface that does not quite let it fall. It has been balancing for a long time. It will balance for a long time. You do not touch it.

There should be life. A chamber this warm, this damp, this full of slow organic movement — there should be beetles, grubs, worms, *something*. There is nothing. The space is too new, perhaps. The plant is too recently grown. The rock has not yet learned to feed anything but itself. Or — and this is the thought that comes unbidden and will not leave — *the plant has eaten everything else*. The space is empty of fauna because the space is *full* of plant, and the plant has claimed all the water, all the minerals, all the available surface, and there is nothing left over for anything that is not itself. The space is a closed system. The space is a single hungry thing digesting itself slowly, and you are standing in its stomach, and you are the only other thing in the room.

You are not sure this is true. You are not sure it is false. You sit on the ledge for a long time and listen.

---

The sound is louder down here. Much louder. The *thock* is everywhere — in the floor, in the roots, in the water, in your teeth. But the *thock* is not the only sound.

There is a high, thin, almost-not-there keening that comes from somewhere near the ceiling. You look up. The roots near the ceiling are frayed — split into hundreds of fine pale filaments that wave gently, slowly, like hair under water. Each filament vibrates. Each filament has its own tiny note, and the notes drift and clash and harmonize in patterns that are almost musical, the way wind through a forest is almost musical. The filaments are *breathing out*. The mist you have been breathing in is passing through them on its way out, and as it passes it pulls a sound from them — a high wet hiss, a flute-note, a struck-glass ringing, and the sound moves through the chamber in slow spirals because the chamber is shaped to make sounds move in slow spirals. You did not notice this earlier. You notice it now. The chamber is shaped. Not by water, not by the plant — by *something*. The ceiling curves in a way that focuses the high sounds down toward the center. The floor is dished slightly, so the low sounds gather and roll toward the edges. You are standing in an instrument. The plant is playing itself. The mist is the breath, the filaments are the reeds, the chamber is the body, and the *thock* is the drumbeat that keeps the rhythm.

And there is another sound. A sound that does not come from the plant.

It is your own breath.

You have been breathing the plant's exhalation and it has been entering your lungs and you have been exhaling it back and the chamber is *listening*. The filaments near the ceiling bend slightly toward you when you breathe out. The mist thickens. The high keening shifts pitch, rises a half-tone, holds. The chamber has heard you. The chamber has answered you. You are part of the instrument now. You did not mean to be part of the instrument. You are part of the instrument.

You stop breathing for a moment. The chamber waits. The filaments drift. The mist settles. The *thock* continues, patient. You breathe again, and the chamber answers again, and you understand that this will go on for as long as you stay, and that you are not staying.

---

You take out your hand-axe.

It is a simple thing — a flat stone, chipped on one end to a dull edge, lashed to a bent wooden handle with strips of wet hide. It is the only tool you have ever owned. You have used it to dig roots, to skin small game, to crack open the soft yellow nodules that grow in the upper caves and that the elders say are good to eat if you boil them long enough. It is not a weapon. It is not meant for this.

But the elders said to bring back a piece of the thing that hums.

You step off the ledge.

The floor is wet under your boots but not slippery. The roots part around your feet as you walk — not quickly, not dramatically, but they part, and they part with a kind of *attention*, and you feel them registering your weight. The small pools of standing water you step near do not ripple. They *lean* toward you, the surface of each one tilting in your direction as if drawn, and the *thock* deepens a half-step and then resumes. You are being weighed. You are being measured. You are being decided.

You reach the central forest of pale columns. They are warm to the touch. Not stone-warm, not wood-warm. *Body*-warm. The warmth of something that has a circulation. You put your palm flat against one and you can feel the slow pulse of fluid inside, and the fluid is moving faster now, you are sure of it, *because of you*, because you touched it, and the *thock* is a fraction quicker, and the mist from the ceiling is a fraction thicker, and the filaments are bending toward you from above like a hundred pale fingers reaching.

You raise the axe.

You do not know what to cut. You do not know what is *yours to take*. The plant is not yours. The chamber is not yours. The elders sent you because they are afraid of this place and they want a piece of it to study, to understand, to *name*, because named things are smaller than unnamed things. But there is nothing small here. There is nothing you can hold in one hand that is the *thing that hums*. The thing that hums is the chamber. The thing that hums is the mist. The thing that hums is the slow dark river in the veins and the patient breathing and the geometry that does not commit to itself and the sound that rises a half-tone when you exhale. You cannot bring any of that back in your hand.

You lower the axe.

You sit down in the forest of pale columns and you listen.

The chamber breathes. The filaments sing. The *thock* comes and comes and comes.

You sit for a long time.

---

When you climb back up the narrow passage, you are carrying nothing in your hands. The elders will be angry. The elders will send someone else. The elders will not believe you when you tell them that the place is not a *thing* but a *place*, not an object but a room, not a hum but a breath, and that you cannot bring a room back in a clay pot, and that the room is still growing, and that the room heard you, and that the room answered, and that you are not sure, now, which of you is the instrument and which is the player.

You carry that back.

It fits in your chest. It will live there for the rest of your life.

Sometimes at night, when the wind stops, you hear it. Faintly. Far away. The *thock* of something patient and damp and warm, breathing in a chamber it grew for itself in the dark of the mountain, getting larger, getting older, waiting for no one, missing nothing, asking nothing, the newest thing in the world, and the only thing down there, and content.
