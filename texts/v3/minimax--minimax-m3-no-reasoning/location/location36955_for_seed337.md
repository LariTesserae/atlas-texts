# The Undercroft

The world has not yet finished being made.

I begin in a corridor carved from the raw and unfinished stone of a place that is still, geologically speaking, deciding what it wants to be. The walls here are damp. Not wet — never running with it — but the moisture is everywhere, a thin skin of condensation that catches the lamplight and makes every surface glisten. I touch one and my fingertip comes away cool and faintly slick. The air holds its breath. Water has soaked into these walls for so long that the stone itself seems to remember rain it will never again see.

The corridor descends. I am below the surface — not deeply, not in any mountain's guts, but below. The city above me is the one I was expecting, the one I was promised, and I will return to it. For now I am down here, threading between pillars that have not yet decided whether they are columns or still the stone they were cut from. Some of them are squared. Some of them are only roughly blocked out, their corners soft, their geometry still arguing with itself. Chisel-marks everywhere. The whole undercroft has the air of a thing that stopped being carved yesterday, or that will resume carving tomorrow. A chisel lies abandoned on a step. Its iron has not yet rusted. Beside it, a curl of stone the color of old cheese.

I count the lamps. Someone has hung them in iron cradles at every fourth pillar, and they are burning with an oil that smells of tallow and something else — a faint mineral scent, almost like heated clay. Their light is not bright, but it is enough. It pushes the darkness back to a respectful distance. Beyond the lamplight, the unfinished parts of the corridor lose themselves in shadow, and the shadows move only because the flames move.

The sound here is a kind of organized quiet. I can hear the lamps, a small domestic hiss. I can hear water, somewhere — not falling, not running, but gathering, the way water gathers when it is looking for a path. I can hear, if I am still, the faint and irregular percussion of stonework in progress from somewhere ahead. Chisel on stone. Pause. Chisel on stone. Someone is working down here, though I have not yet seen them.

I turn a corner and the undercroft opens.

It is a city. Of course it is a city.

I had thought, somehow, that the undercroft would be smaller than the city above. I was wrong. It is the city, in some sense, or it is the city's other self. The streets here are wide enough for a cart but not for a cart and a cart. The buildings crowd close. They are built, every one of them, of the same unfinished stone as the corridor — squared enough for habitation, rough enough that the mason's marks are still visible in the walls. Plaster has been applied in some places. In other places it has not, and there you can see the grain of the stone, the way it was split from the quarry, the places where a flaw made the cutter start again. The buildings rise two stories, sometimes three. I look up and the ceilings are low. I look down and the floors are flagged with stones that have been worn, in the doorways and thresholds, to a polish.

It is damp. The dampness is the city's atmosphere. It beads on every wall. It darkens the plaster where the plaster exists and darkens the stone where it does not. The air is cool — the coolness of a cellar, of a place that never sees the sun. I cannot see my breath, but if I were to stand still long enough, I think I would begin to.

The streets are not empty.

The first thing I notice is that there is no one in them, and then I notice that there is no *one* in them, and these are not the same observation. The streets are full of a kind of occupancy that is not people. Barrels, stacked in threes and fours, line one wall. A handcart, its single wheel chocked with a wedge of wood, has been left across the mouth of an alley. Coils of rope hang from hooks. A pile of grain sacks — not many, maybe eight — has been set against a door, and the door is closed, and the grain is beginning to fuzz at its edges with a pale and uncertain mold. There is a chair outside one building. There is no one in the chair. There is a cup on the chair's arm. The cup is empty and dry.

A cat crosses the far end of the street. It moves with the particular self-possession of a cat that owns a place and is reminding me of the fact. It does not look at me. It disappears around a corner. I do not hear its paws.

The city is packed. I mean that everything in it is here for a reason and the reasons are pressing. There is no space left over. The buildings have been built as close together as the law — or the lack of a law — allows. The alleys are not alleys; they are the leftover distance between two walls that could not be built any closer without becoming the same wall. I can stand in one and touch both sides at once. From above, I imagine, the city would look like a hand of clenched fingers, the gaps between them barely wide enough to see through.

The light is the lamps' light. It is not bright. It is, in fact, the only light, and I do not know, as I walk, whether the ceiling above me is low or whether it is simply dark. The lamps cast overlapping pools of yellow, and between the pools it is the brown of strong tea, and beyond the brown it is black. I stay in the yellow. I keep to the middles of streets.

The architecture is not strange. It is the architecture of a town that has been built by people who knew what towns looked like and who had, at their disposal, only the labor of their hands and the patience of the unfinished. The doorframes are squared. The lintels are true. The windows are small, because the windows are not for looking out of, and the people who built them understood that a small window holds warmth. The roofs are timbered and tiled, where they are finished, and the tiles are of fired clay, the same clay-smell I noticed in the lamps. The unfinished roofs show their rafters. I see, in one place, a building whose upper story is only a frame — the beams squared and pegged, the walls not yet raised between them, and through the empty rectangle of an absent wall I can see the lamplight of the next street over, and the shadow of a woman walking through it, and then she is past and I did not see her face and I will not.

The technology is what I would have called, a hundred years ago, the technology of my time. I see iron. I see iron used without shame, in hinges and nails and the bands that reinforce the wheels of the handcart. I see no steel, or I do not recognize it as steel. I see wood worked with edge-tools that have left their grain in the wood. I see fired clay in pots and lamps and roof-tiles. I see leather, in straps and a discarded sandal in a doorway. I see a forge, somewhere — I smell it before I see it, the hot-iron smell threading through the damp — and when I round the corner I see the building, low and stone, its doorway spilling a faint and pulsing orange. I do not go in. I hear the bellows breathe. I hear a hammer fall, once, and not again.

The dampness is not rain. I think, at first, that it is. The sound of water somewhere is so constant, so much a part of the silence, that I had been hearing it without knowing I was hearing it. Now I listen for it. It is the sound of water falling into water, somewhere below the city, somewhere in the unfinished stone, and the city is built above the place where the water gathers, and the water gathers because the stone beneath it is not yet finished being shaped, and the shaping will go on, and the water will go on gathering, and the lamps will go on burning, and the dampness will go on being the city's breath.

I walk. I come to a square. The square is small — only ten paces across — and it is paved with stones that have been laid, here, with care. The lamps are more numerous here, hung in a ring, and the light is almost generous. In the center of the square there is a well, and the well has a wooden curb and a wooden cover, and the cover is shut, and on the cover there is a stone to keep it shut, and beside the stone there is a cup, and beside the cup there is a pair of gloves, and the gloves are damp, and I understand that someone was here, recently, and drew water, and has gone.

The well is not the source of the dampness. The well is, I think, the city's response to the dampness — a way of taking what the stone gives and putting it to use. I do not lift the cover. The dampness on the gloves is enough.

I have not yet decided whether the city is inhabited. I have not seen a child. I have not seen an old person. The cat. The woman in the lamplight. The chisel-worker somewhere ahead, working in the stone. The smith, breathing his iron. There are signs of many more. A pair of boots outside a door. A broom leaning against a wall. A window with a shutter half-open, and through the half-open shutter the sound of someone breathing in sleep. I do not look in. I do not need to look in. The breathing is enough.

The undercroft is not the only thing being built. The city is being built, in the city, by the city, as the undercroft is being built beneath it. New walls are going up in gaps where old walls have come down. A scaffolding of poles and planks leans against a façade, and the façade is being repointed, and the lime of the new mortar is paler than the stone it joins. I see, on a corner, a man on a ladder. He is holding a trowel. He is singing, very quietly, a song I do not know, in a language I do not recognize. He does not see me. He is intent on his work.

I think, then, about the erosion that is not happening. The stones here are not yet worn. The streets are not yet cobbled smooth. The buildings are not yet blackened with the smoke of the years. I am walking through a city that is still being decided. The chisel-marks are fresh in the walls. The mortar is not yet fully cured. The cat that crossed the street had clean paws. The woman in the lamplight walked without hurry because there was no hurry to walk with. This is a city that has been here, perhaps, for years. It has not yet been here for centuries. I do not know what it will be when it is older. I do not think it knows, either.

I sit on the chair outside the empty cup's building. The chair is dry. I am glad. The dampness has not reached it. The light of the nearest lamp falls across my knees. The sound of water, below, goes on. The sound of the chisel, ahead, goes on. The sound of the bellows, in the forge, breathes and breathes. The cat, somewhere, is not making a sound at all.

I am a visitor here. The city knows I am a visitor. It is patient with me, as it is patient with itself. It is still becoming. It will go on becoming for some time. The dampness will go on. The lamps will go on. The stone will, eventually, forget the chisel that shaped it, and the mortar will, eventually, forget the trowel that spread it, and the well will, eventually, forget the cup that was set beside it on the cover. But not yet. Not yet.

I stand up. The chair does not creak. I have been very still. The dampness is already beginning to find me.

I go on.
