# The Manganese Reaches

There is a place in the high latitudes where the cold is not merely temperature but architecture. The air itself seems to have set, like wet plaster, into a pale gray-blue medium that resists motion. At 3,400 meters, the atmosphere is thin enough to taste metallic — copper and iron oxides and something older, something the old prospectors called *brass-light* because it turned the undersides of clouds the color of a struck coin. This is the Manganese Reaches, a high cold valley pressed between unnamed ridges in a range that doesn't appear on most maps because the names on most maps were given by people who never came here.

## The Forest That Ate Itself

The dominant feature is biomass. The valley floor and both flanks are occupied by a single organism — or rather, a federation of organisms so tightly integrated that the distinction has ceased to matter. It is called locally *the Felt*, though no one local remains to call it anything. The Felt is a mycelial-arboreal complex: fungal networks braided through the root systems of stunted conifers, krummholz pines no taller than a man's chest, their trunks gnarled into horizontal gestures by decades of wind shear. But the pines are only the bones. Over them, through them, between them grows everything else.

Moss. Lichen in three hundred colors. Dwarf birch creeping along the ground like something ashamed of being seen. Berry bushes with stems the thickness of cable and bark like cork. Sedge grass in tussocks three feet across. Crowberry, alpine bearberry, diapensia in mats so dense a boot cannot break through without tools. The Felt is perhaps fifteen kilometers long and four wide, a shag-carpet of living matter draped over the valley's contours, and it is loud with the business of being alive in a way that the human ear cannot quite parse.

The cold has given it a slowness. Decay is glacial. A fallen branch might lie for eighty years before it is fully consumed, and so the Felt is a stratified thing — fresh green at the top, then leather-brown, then gray, then black, then the white of clean bone at the bottom where the last lignin has been stripped away. The whole structure is a kind of frozen compost, layers of time held in suspension by the cold and the patience of organisms that measure their lives in centuries.

## Moisture in Suspense

Water here is not a river or a lake. It is a proposition. The valley gets perhaps 380 millimeters of precipitation a year, but the Felt is so efficient at capture and slow at release that the moisture behaves more like a phase change than a flow. Fog is the primary water source — advective fog that rolls up the valley from a distant, warmer lowland and condenses on every needle and thallus. The Felt drinks from the air. When the sun comes out, which it does for perhaps three hours on a good day, the surface steams gently, and the moisture rises only to be caught again the next morning by the same waiting surfaces.

There are seeps. In midsummer, when the snowpack above finally yields, a few thin threads of water emerge from the Felt's eastern edge and run for fifty meters before vanishing into gravel. These are the *teats*, and they are the only surface water in the valley's lower reaches. They support the rare — and here *rare* means one sighting per season — small creatures: a vole, perhaps, or a bird blown off course.

## The Machinery Below

But the cold damp green of the Felt is only the latest chapter. The valley is underlaid with something older, and something still working.

In the early decades of the last century, before the wars and the long quiet that followed them, this valley was prospected for manganese and minor copper. The ore bodies were extensive but low-grade, and the operation was eventually abandoned when richer veins were found elsewhere. What remains is not visible from above — that is the point of the Felt, which has overgrown everything with the slow obliterating patience of moss on a tombstone.

Beneath the mat of vegetation, the valley floor is riddled with shafts. There are horizontal adits driven into the lower slopes, their timber portals rotted but still standing. There are ore chutes, ventilation raises, winze shafts descending at angles. There are the foundations of a mill, a forge, a powder magazine, a bunkhouse — all reduced to stone courses buried under two meters of Felt. There is a cable-tram system, its steel cables long since snapped and swallowed by the vegetation, its pylons still standing like the ribs of some unidentifiable animal. There are rails, narrow-gauge, that emerge from the Felt here and there like the veins on an old hand.

And the machinery is not entirely dead.

Somewhere in the valley's geology, there is a heat source. A geothermal pocket, or a slow oxidation of sulfide ores, or something stranger. The result is that certain of the deeper workings have become hot. Steam rises from a collapsed stope near the eastern ridge, year-round, a pillar of white that on cold mornings is visible from twenty kilometers away. The steam is sulfurous and smells of boiled cabbage and old pennies. Where it emerges, the Felt is different — greener, faster-growing, with ferns that should not exist at this elevation and a patch of grass so violently green it hurts the eyes after the muted grays and browns of the surrounding vegetation.

The steam has also kept certain of the machinery operational, in a partial, haunted way. A winch in one of the lower adits, its boiler fed by the geothermal heat, still turns — slowly, perhaps one revolution per hour — winding nothing up and nothing down. A stamp mill in the buried foundation clanks and hammers in the small hours of the morning, crushing rock that no one is feeding it, the sound muffled by the Felt above into a deep rhythmic pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. The lights in the bunkhouse — electric, somehow — flicker on at dusk and off at dawn. No one is inside. The lights cast no shadows on the moss-grown walls.

## Sound and Silence

The soundscape is nearly empty of biological noise. A rare bird call. The wind in the krummholz, which is not the wind in tall trees — it is a scratching, a whispering of needles against each other, the sound of something writing very small in a very large book. Water in the seeps, a trickle so thin it is more presence than sound.

The machine sounds, though. They come at odd hours. The stamp mill. The winch. Sometimes, very rarely, the distant clang of a skip being dumped in a shaft that is supposedly collapsed. These sounds are not loud. They are felt more than heard, a sub-audible vibration that travels through the Felt and into the bones of anyone standing on it. They do not echo. The Felt absorbs everything.

## Weather and Turbulence

The valley is in a state of almost constant atmospheric instability. Cold air drains from the high cirques at night and pools in the valley, then is displaced by warm air rising from the geothermal areas, and the result is a churning, convective atmosphere that produces unpredictable weather on a small scale. A pocket of fog will form over one ridge and dissipate over another while the sun shines on both. Rain falls in shafts that move across the valley like searchlights, drenching one patch of Felt while leaving another, fifty meters away, dry. Snow in summer is not unheard of. Lightning is common but usually silent — the strikes hit the high ridges and the thunder is lost in the valley's acoustic shadow.

The wind is the most unpredictable element. It can be dead calm for hours, the Felt holding its breath, and then a williwaw will come roaring down from the cirques, a wall of cold air that flattens the grass and sends a wave of condensation rolling ahead of it like the bow shock of an invisible ship. These events last two to four minutes. They are preceded by a silence so total it feels like a held breath, and followed by a silence of the same quality. In between, the world is noise and violence.

## The Feel of the Place

To stand in the Manganese Reaches is to stand on something that is not entirely solid. The Felt is springy underfoot, a deep cushion of living and dead matter, and beneath it the worked-out ground shifts and subsides in places. One can fall through, sometimes, into a hidden adit or a subsidence pit. The openings are overgrown and treacherous. The cold does not kill the person who falls in, but the dark and the smell of old ore and the distant hammering of the stamp mill usually drive them out before the cold can.

The light is diffuse, even at midday. The high cirques catch the sun first and the valley floor last, and by the time the sun is fully up it is already beginning its descent behind the western ridge. The valley lives in a kind of permanent gloaming, the light slanting, the shadows long. The Felt absorbs most wavelengths and reflects back only the muted greens and grays that are its signature palette. The only color that breaks through is the violent green of the geothermal patch and, in late summer, the red of the bearberry and the blue-white of the diapensia flowers, which bloom in a single week and are gone.

There is no path. There is no trail marker. There is no structure built by human hands that is not being slowly reclaimed. The Felt does not care about the machinery beneath it, and the machinery does not care about the Felt above it, and the two of them continue their slow negotiation, one growing, the other turning in the dark, in a valley that no one visits and that no map names, under a sky that is usually gray and sometimes, briefly, violently clear, and the silence between the sounds of the machines is the silence of something waiting without knowing what it is waiting for.
