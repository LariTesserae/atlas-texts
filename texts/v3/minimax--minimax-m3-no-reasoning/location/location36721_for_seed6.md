# The Filtration Cathedral

You arrive at the cathedral of green glass and listen for a long time before you hear anything.

It rises out of the water in a single column, a square tower the size of a city block, and around its base — around the four walls that drop straight into the lake without so much as a ripple — the water is black and still and warm. Warm in the way bathwater is warm. Warm in the way blood is warm. The air above it is warm too, and humid, and it smells the way a greenhouse smells when something in the greenhouse has been working hard for a long time: loam, sap, fermentation, the sweet rot of things being made into other things.

The tower has no doors at the waterline. It has no doors anywhere that you can find. You came here in a boat, or you came here walking on the surface of the lake, depending on which entrance you took, and either way you ended up at a wrought-iron railing that surrounds the thing at a distance of maybe thirty feet, and beyond the railing there is only the wall.

The wall is covered in plants.

That is the first thing you understand about the place: the wall is covered in plants. Every inch of the glass, every inch of the iron tracery between the panes, every inch of the stone cornices and the copper flashing and the iron drainpipes — every inch is colonized. Ferns spill out of mullions. Moss climbs the glazing bars in a green pelt so thick it looks shorn. Ivy — though no one planted it, it could not have been planted, there is no one here — ivy has threaded itself through the iron lattice and come out the other side and begun to climb back in again, in on itself, layer on layer, so that in places the wall is six feet thick with vine. There are orchids. There are orchids growing out of the drainage holes of the gutters. There are bromeliads the size of your head brimming with rainwater and the drowned husks of insects.

And behind all that green, the glass is dark. You cannot see into the tower. You can only see that the glass is dark, and that somewhere behind it, far back, there is light moving.

This is what you have come to see.

---

The tower is one of sixty-three. You know this because the railing is engraved, in small bronze letters set into the iron at the level of your hand, with a number — 11 — and the name of the place — *The Filtration Cathedral at Slake* — and a smaller legend, in even smaller letters, that you have to crouch to read:

> *Built A.D. 1891 by the Slake & Greensward Industrial Filtration Company for the purification of the air and water of the Upper Greensward Concordat. Maintained in perpetuity by the waters themselves.*

You crouch there a long time. The bronze is warm. The air is warm. The lake, when you let your hand drop over the railing, is warm.

When you stand back up, you notice that the railing is moving.

Not dramatically. Not in a way you would have noticed if you hadn't been looking. But the railing — the wrought-iron railing with its fleur-de-lys finials and its decorative scrollwork — is breathing. Out and in. Out and in. The iron swells and contracts in a rhythm so slow it could be the wind, except that the wind isn't blowing. There is no wind. The air is perfectly still.

The railing is breathing.

You look down at the water. The water is breathing too. The black surface rises and falls against the base of the tower in long, slow undulations, the way the sea breathes against a pier, except that the sea is not still, and this water is.

Out and in. Out and in.

---

There is a path along the railing. A foot-wide plank walk, moss-covered, set into the iron brackets that hold the railing up out of the water. You follow it. You cannot tell, as you follow it, whether you are walking around the tower or whether the tower is walking around you, because the green wall is so close and so uniform that the corners of the thing — and there are corners, it is square, you remember that — the corners seem to take an unreasonable amount of time to arrive.

But eventually they do arrive. You round the first corner. The second wall is the same as the first. The third wall. The fourth wall. The same ferns, the same moss, the same bromeliads brimming with the drowned husks of insects. The same dark glass behind the green. The same slow light moving inside, far back, behind everything.

But there is a sound now.

It is a low sound. A sound so low that for a while you think you are imagining it. A sound like the humming of a transformer, except that it has been slowed down, or deepened, or processed through something organic, until it is no longer a hum at all but a kind of sustained breath, a kind of held chord, the kind of sound a cathedral organ makes when it has been playing the same note for so long that the note has become the building.

The building is humming.

The lake is humming. The railing is humming. Your molars are humming.

---

You find the entrance on the fourth wall, and you would have walked past it if you hadn't been looking, because it is not a door. It is a place where the wall has been taken down.

A square of wall, maybe eight feet on a side, has been removed, and the plants have grown into the gap, and the gap has grown into the plants, so that what you are looking at is a portico of vines: a green, breathing, gently swaying archway, dense as felt, hung with orchids and bromeliads and the strange curled fronds of ferns you have no name for. Through the archway, into the dimness behind, you can see the floor of the cathedral, and the floor of the cathedral is water.

Not deep water. Ankle-deep water, maybe, over a tiled floor. The tiles are green — copper-green, the green of old pennies — and the water is black, and warm, and the same water as the lake outside.

You step in. The water takes your boot. The water is soft. The water is soft the way skin is soft. It closes around your ankle like a hand.

---

Inside, the cathedral is a column of green glass and green water and green light, and the column goes up and up and up, all the way to the top of the tower, and at the top of the tower there is a kind of cupola, a kind of oculus, a kind of opening to the sky, and the light comes down through the opening and through the plants that have grown up around the opening and through the water that is falling, slowly, from the leaves of the plants, in fat warm drops, into the ankle-deep water below.

The light is the color of the light under a forest canopy. It is green and gold and brown. It moves the way light moves under a forest canopy: in long slow shafts, in shifting pools, in the dappled pulses of leaves turning in a breeze that isn't there.

The water that is falling from the leaves is not rain. There has been no rain. The water is being lifted. Somewhere in the cathedral, somewhere in the dark wet green heart of the thing, there is an engine — there are engines, there are sixty-three of them, one in each of the sixty-three towers, distributed across the Upper Greensward Concordat in a pattern that the Concordat's hydrologists understand and you do not — engines that lift the water from the lake, up through the tower, through the soil of the rooftop gardens, through the roots of the plants, through the stems of the plants, up to the leaves of the plants, where the water evaporates into the warm air and falls back down again as the fat warm drops you are standing in.

This is what the cathedral does. This is what the engines do. This is what the plants do. The water goes up and the water comes down and the water goes up and the water comes down, and as it goes up and as it comes down it is filtered — through the roots, through the soil, through the stems, through the slow green chemistry of living things — and what comes down is not the same as what went up. What comes down is cleaner. What comes down is sweeter. What comes down is the water that the Upper Greensward Concordat drinks.

The engines hum.

The engines have been humming since 1891.

---

There is no one here. There has been no one here for a long time. The Slake & Greensward Industrial Filtration Company folded in 1937, and the Upper Greensward Concordat was reorganized as a watershed district in 1944, and the watershed district was dissolved in 1971, and the towers were deeded to the waters themselves in 1971, and the waters, being waters, did not sign any papers.

The engines hum because the engines have always hummed. The engines hum because the water turns the wheels and the wheels turn the engines and the engines lift the water. The engines have been running on the water for so long that the engines have become part of the water, in the way that a stone in a riverbed becomes part of the river. The engines are not maintained. The engines do not need to be maintained. The engines are made of a metal that does not corrode — or that corrodes into a different metal, and the different metal is still functional, and so on, in a slow alchemical descent through the periodic table that no one is keeping track of anymore.

The engines hum. The plants grow. The water rises and falls. The cathedral breathes.

You stand in the ankle-deep water and you listen to it breathe. The sound is the sound of a sleeping animal. The sound is the sound of a slow heart.

---

There are fish in the water.

You notice this after a while, because the water is so still and so clear — no, the water is not clear, the water is black, but the black water is transparent in the way that very black tea is transparent, and you can see down into it — you can see down into it, and what you see is fish.

The fish are dead.

The fish are dead and the fish are everywhere. They lie on the tiled floor of the cathedral in a layer maybe two fish thick, and they lie in the lap of the vines and they lie in the brimming bowls of the bromeliads and they lie on the sills of the windows behind the glass, and they are all dead, and they are all in different stages of being eaten. Eaten by the plants. Eaten by the water. Eaten by the slow green chemistry that is, even now, turning them into the next batch of filtered drinking water for the Upper Greensward Concordat, which is still there, somewhere, downstream, drinking what comes down.

The fish are the only animals in the cathedral. There are no insects. There are no birds. There are no mammals. The legend on the railing said *no animal life* and it meant it. The fish are not animal life. The fish are fertilizer. The fish are feedstock. The fish are part of the engine.

Where do the fish come from?

You look up. The fish come down. The fish come down with the water that is lifted from the lake, the water that carries them up through the tower, up through the soil, up through the roots, where they are caught and held and slowly consumed. They come down again as the water comes down. They come down as part of the water. They have always come down as part of the water. The fish are the sacrament. The fish are the communion. The fish are eaten so that the rest of us can drink.

You look down at the fish around your boots. The fish are looking up at you. The fish have no eyes. The fish have been dead a long time.

---

You walk deeper into the cathedral. There are aisles. There are pews. There is an altar.

The pews are iron, and the iron is covered in moss, and the moss is breathing — in and out, in and out — in the same slow rhythm as the railing outside. The aisles are tiled, and the tiles are copper-green, and the aisles are ankle-deep in water, and the water is warm, and the water is full of fish.

The altar is at the center of the cathedral, directly under the oculus, directly under the falling water, and the altar is a machine.

The altar is a machine the size of a baptismal font, made of brass and copper and glass, and inside the glass there is a wheel, and the wheel is turning. The wheel is turning very slowly, maybe one revolution an hour, and the wheel is connected by a shaft to a gear, and the gear is connected by a shaft to another gear, and so on, down into the floor of the cathedral, down through the foundations, down into the lake itself, where the engines are. The wheel turns because the engines turn. The wheel turns because the water turns the wheels. The wheel has been turning since 1891.

The altar is a machine and the machine is also a garden. Around the base of the font, around the brass and the copper and the glass, the plants have grown in. The plants have grown *into* the gears. The plants have threaded themselves through the teeth of the gears so that when the gears turn, the plants turn with them, and the plants are not cut, and the plants are not torn, and the plants seem, as far as you can tell, to be enjoying it. The plants are turning in the gears the way prayer is supposed to turn in the mind: slowly, endlessly, without friction, without wear.

The altar hums.

The cathedral hums.

The fish lie on the floor around the altar and look up at you with their eyeless faces.

---

You stay in the cathedral for a long time. You cannot tell how long. The light does not change. The sound does not change. The water does not change. The breathing of the railing and the breathing of the lake and the breathing of the cathedral walls go on and on and on in the same long slow rhythm, and you sit in one of the pews — the moss takes your weight, the moss holds you, the moss breathes with you — and you listen.

At some point you realize that the water around your boots has risen. It is no longer ankle-deep. It is mid-calf. It has been rising for a while. You did not notice because the rising is so slow. The rising is so slow that the water seems to be standing still, the way a clock hand seems to be standing still if you don't look at it for an hour.

You stand up. The water is at your knees. The fish are bumping against your legs, gently, the way balloons bump against your legs in a pool. The fish are eyeless and dead and they are bumping against you because the water is lifting them, the water is rising, the water is going up.

It is time to go.

You walk back down the aisle. The aisle is longer than it was. The water is rising. The fish are bumping against your thighs. You walk faster. The water is at your hips. You walk faster. The water is at your waist. The cathedral is breathing you out.

You step through the portico of vines, through the green breathing archway, and out onto the plank walk, and the water outside is higher too. The water outside is at the level of the railing. The railing is breathing, in and out, in and out, and the water is breathing with it, and you have to hold onto the railing to keep from being lifted, and the railing is warm, and the railing is alive.

You hold on. You wait. The water goes down. Slowly, slowly, the water goes down. The water goes down the way it came up. The water goes down the way everything in the cathedral goes down: slowly, gently, without hurry.

Eventually the water is back at the level of your ankles. Eventually the fish are back at the level of the floor. Eventually the cathedral has breathed you out and is breathing itself in again.

You stand on the plank walk and look at the green glass wall. The wall is breathing. Behind the glass, far back, the light is moving.

The engines hum.

The legend on the railing, in the small bronze letters, says:

> *Built A.D. 1891 by the Slake & Greensward Industrial Filtration Company for the purification of the air and water of the Upper Greensward Concordat. Maintained in perpetuity by the waters themselves.*

You read it again. You read it a third time. You do not know what it means. You do not know what any of it means. You only know that the cathedral is breathing, and the water is rising, and the fish are dead, and the engines have been humming since before your grandmother was born, and the plants are growing into the gears, and the light is moving behind the glass, and somewhere downstream, in the Upper Greensward Concordat, someone is drinking a glass of water.

The water is sweet.

The water is clean.

The water has been filtered through the body of a cathedral.

---

You leave. You take a boat, or you walk on the surface of the lake, depending on which exit you take. The lake is warm. The lake is still. The tower recedes behind you, green and glass and breathing, and you do not look back, because you have decided that you will not look back, and because the cathedral is so close to the water and so covered in green that there is nothing to look back at. There is only the lake. There is only the warm black water, and the breathing of the tower at your back, and the hum of the engines, low and slow and endless, carried across the water to you in long waves that you feel in your sternum.

The fish do not follow you. The fish are in the cathedral. The fish are part of the cathedral.

The water is sweet. The water is clean.

You go downstream to drink it.
