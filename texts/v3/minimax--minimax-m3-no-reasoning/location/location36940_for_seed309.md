# The Condensery

You stand in a place that does not want to be walked through. That is the first thing you understand, before you understand anything else: the vegetation presses in a way that is less *around* you and more *into* you, intimate, assuming, and the architecture agrees with it.

The ceiling is low. Not low in the way a basement is low — measured and dry and human — but low in the way a held breath is low. It is the underside of a forest floor that has been lifted somehow, turned over, and laid down flat on top of a cavity. Roots descend through it in long pale ropes, each one ringed with dark moisture, and where the roots touch the cavity's true ceiling — a layered shelf of old concrete, cracked in long meandering lines that follow the pressure of something buried — they swell into nodes, into bulbs, into clusters of small pale fruit you cannot name. The fruit sweats. Everything sweats. That is the second thing.

The space is roughly the size of a chapel. Roughly. The walls bow inward at intervals where the original brickwork has been overtaken by a kind of fig-tree architecture — but it is not fig, you realize after looking: the leaves are wrong, lobed in threes, and they have the dull sheen of something that has never seen direct sun, which is true and which is why they gleam. Behind and within the leaves, in the wet dark, the brick itself is *digesting*. It is going soft. It is becoming loam in slow motion, century by century, and where it has already gone, what is left is a soil-paste that gives off the smell of a butcher's shop in summer. You try not to think about that. The plants do not mind.

There are machines here.

The machines are in three states, and the three states are arranged in a kind of progression as you move inward from where you entered (a rusted spiral stair descending from a hatch you almost could not find). Nearest the stair, the machines are *almost* machines: outlines in rust, frames with nothing left but their ribs, fans whose blades have been replaced by squat rubbery fungi in concentric rings, like the calendar of some other species. You cannot tell whether they were ruined or never built. There are plaques on them but the plaques are etched in a script that uses numerals in groups of five and the rest is curves. You leave them.

The middle state is the loud one.

There is a row of them — a grove, almost a small wood — running down the center of the cavity. They are tall, taller than you, and they have the silhouettes of looms or printing presses but enlarged, with the proportions of an upright piano and then a cathedral organ and then something else entirely. They are running. The sound they make is a thick mechanical heartbeat, layered: a lower register of clack and shudder, a middle register of friction, a high thin almost-animal whine that makes the inside of your skull itch. They are not being tended. There is no one near them. They are operating in the way an ecosystem operates — running because the condition of running is the condition of their being. Tendrils enter them from above. Tubes of pale green plant-matter are threaded through their gears, and where the tubes pass through the metal they are crushed and dark juice runs down and pools on the concrete floor, and from each pool, where the light hits it, more pale tubes emerge and begin their slow re-ascent. The machines are eating the plants. The plants are eating the machines. You understand this is what has kept everything here for a long time. Maybe a hundred years. Maybe more.

The light in the center is what little comes down through the stair hatch, through a long crooked chimney in the cavity's far wall (which terminates, you never find out where), and through the joints between panels of the old ceiling. That amount of light would, above ground, be nothing. Here, it is enough. It falls in long pale shafts onto the machines and onto the floor and onto the things that move between the machines.

The fauna here is the room's third architecture.

You had thought at first the shadows between the presses were condensation, or the movement of the deeper stems. They are not. There are shapes moving in the spaces between, low shapes, longer than a dog and lower to the ground, and they have too many places where a body would normally have one thing. They are light-colored — pinkish-white, like a peeled root — and their surfaces are slick and faintly translucent, and where the light catches them you can see a vague network of darker lines inside, like veins or rivers. They do not look at you. They seem disinclined to look at anything. They flow. They go where the floor is warm. The floor is warm because of the machines, and where the machines have run longest the floor tiles have darkened to a deep amber and the creatures lie on them in long ribbons, basking, and the air above the tiles is so humid that their shape is *telescoped* in the rising moisture — you see them shimmying and doubling against the ceiling, faint apparitions gliding in the wrong direction. They eat the tubes. They eat the rust. They are eaten, you realize after watching a long moment, by something smaller and much more numerous: by grey-black beetles with long antennae, hundreds of them in a slow procession along the floor, each one carrying a fragment of pale flesh larger than itself, proceeding with great patience toward the base of the walls where the brick is becoming soil and the soil is becoming the next generation of the plants that fed the machine that fed the creature that fed the beetle. There is no beginning and no end of this. You understand the place has not had a beginning or end for a long time.

The sound is constant. The rhythm of the presses has settled into a kind of weather. You stop hearing it as a sound and start hearing it as a temperature. The machines are not loud the way a factory is loud. They are loud the way a river is loud: ambient, structural, total. There are other sounds layered under them. The creatures, when they move quickly, make a sound like wet canvas. The plants, breathing, make a sound like a slow crumpling. The water — and there is water, dripping from the ceiling in many fine points, a kind of permanent rain that is only indoors, that is only this room — makes a sound like static or sand. You cannot tell if it is raining outside.

You walk to the far end, where the light is dimmest. There, the machinery is in its third state: sprouting. The great frames have become host structures, their metal bodies now lattice for a violent green. Things have pushed through. The pistons are barked over. The flywheels support whole crowns of yellow-flowered something — the flowers turn toward you as you pass and you try not to think about that, either. The machines here are not running. They have given way. They are the past tense of the middle machines and the future tense of the rusted frames by the stair. You are at the deep end of a system, a kind of metabolic hallway, and everything in this room has been running a long exchange toward this end: the brick becomes soil, the soil feeds the plants, the plants feed the machine, the machine's heat warms the floor, the floor warms the creatures, the creatures become beetles, the beetles enter the soil. And the water drips. And the presses clack. And the flowers turn, slowly, toward whatever moves.

You are leaving. You are climbing back up the spiral stair with your hands on a rail that feels almost alive. Above you, through the hatch, there is daylight or the memory of it. You understand as your eyes adjust that this place will not remember you, exactly, but will remember you the way a forest remembers a hiker — by the disturbance of a path, by the warmth of a footprint soon cooled. You understand that if you come back in a year the floor will be different by an inch. In ten years the stair will not open. In a hundred years the hatch will be a swelling in the earth and a person standing there, if any person stands there, will not know what is under it.

You come out. You have dirt under your nails and an image in your head of long pink ribbons lying on warm amber stone in a dark wet mechanical cathedral, breathing slowly, breathing slowly.

You do not look back.

But the place has looked at you.
