# The High Tarn

I want to start with the cold. Not the abstract concept of cold, but the specific, wet, bone-deep cold that lives in this place like a second tenant who never paid rent. It presses against exposed skin with a damp insistence. Breath doesn't quite freeze—it's too wet for that, too saturated—but it forms a small, weighty cloud that hangs at chin height before reluctantly dispersing. The cold has a taste. Mineral. Like licking a copper coin.

## The Bowl

The place is a bowl. A high-altitude concavity carved into a saddle between two nameless peaks whose names have been forgotten by everyone except the lichen. The bowl is perhaps a quarter-league across—small enough to throw a stone across, large enough to lose yourself in if you crouch behind the wrong outcrop. Its walls rise in ragged tiers of dark stone, slate and something darker than slate, basalt perhaps, or that local rock the inhabitants call *mournstone* because of the way it weeps. The rock is wet. Always wet. Not raining—I haven't seen rain in three days—but weeping. Tiny condensation beads form across every vertical surface in the late afternoon and run down in threads that braid together at the base of each outcrop. The ground itself feels spongy underfoot in places where you wouldn't expect it to, the stone having absorbed so much water over centuries that it's become something between rock and felt.

The bowl is a tarn. Or it was a tarn. The water has pulled back. Now it's a marsh-rim around a central pool no larger than a great hall, and the rest is a confusion of hummocks, seepage channels, and exposed shelves of stone where the water has eaten its way down through generations. Long-established erosion—this place has been finding its level for so long that the word "erosion" feels wrong. Negotiation, maybe. A long conversation between water and stone that has reached an uneasy equilibrium.

## Light

The light is the thing that is wrong, and also the thing that saves.

It comes from everywhere. The bowl is oriented so that at certain times of year—most of the year, the locals say, as if this is a small complaint and not an astronomical impossibility—the light enters from multiple angles and bounces off the wet stone surfaces until the entire place becomes a kind of cold lantern. Mournstone, when wet, does something the geographers in the lowlands refuse to believe: it holds light. Holds it and gives it back slowly, so that at noon the place is unbearable to the eye, and at midnight you can still read by the dim phosphorescence of the rocks. The inhabitants call it the Long Day. They don't talk about it much.

Right now it's mid-morning, and the bowl is a white dazzle. Every wet surface is a mirror fragment. The pool at the center is a sheet of hammered silver. I have to squint to look at the far wall.

## The Houses

They've built into the walls.

That's the first thing I noticed when I came over the lip, expecting wilderness and finding—this. The houses are stacked into the rock itself, the mournstone carved back in places to make rooms, the rubble used to build forward in other places, so that the whole settlement reads as a continuation of the cliff face. Doorways are tall and narrow, designed perhaps for warmth, or for the look of it, or to keep something out. There are no windows on the lower levels. The upper levels have small square openings, none larger than a hand's span, and from several of them I can see faces watching me with the polite, incurious attention of people who have seen strangers before and don't love them.

There are perhaps forty households. I asked a boy—he couldn't have been more than nine, bundled in a grease-stained wool coat three sizes too large—how many people lived in the tarn. He thought about it for a long time. Then he said, *"Enough."*

The technology is what you'd expect from people who live in a place like this. Hand-forged tools, the smithy working from a small cave on the south wall where the rock is driest. I saw him at his anvil this morning, a broad woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow despite the cold, her forearms crisscrossed with old burn scars. She was making something I didn't recognize—a flat blade with a hook at one end, perhaps for cutting sedge, or for something else. The metal was dark. Local. There is no good iron in the mournstone, but there is some, and they work it carefully, waste nothing. Their tools are heavy and unhurried. So are they.

There is a kind of pulley system running up the inside of the eastern wall, made of rope and wooden drums, that I haven't yet figured out. It moves continuously, creaking in a slow rhythm, even when no one is at it. The boy said it was for the water. I don't know what that means.

## The Beasts

The fauna is the part I keep returning to in my notes, because the fauna is impossible.

There are birds. That part is fine. A pale species of something between a gull and a falcon wheels above the pool at all hours, screaming in a voice that sounds almost like a child calling. There are small things in the sedge—voles or their local equivalent, quick dark shapes that vanish if you look directly at them. There are insects. There is the cold itself, which feels alive in this place.

But there are the others.

The boy called them *the herd* but that isn't quite right. They are large. They are not quite anything. I saw one at dawn, standing at the far edge of the pool, drinking. It had too many legs—six, I thought, then seven, then six again, but I may have been dazzled by the light. Its head was low and featureless, the color of wet stone, and it made no sound at all. When it moved, it moved all at once, the way a drop of water moves when it releases from a surface. It was at the edge of the pool and then it was gone, and I couldn't say where it had gone, only that it was no longer there. The boy said they came up from the deep places when the Long Day was brightest. He said they were not dangerous. He said this in a way that made me feel it was not a useful category here.

I have seen four more since. Always at the edge of vision. Always gone before I could turn my head fully toward them. The light on the mournstone seems to confuse them, or to be confused by them; sometimes I think I see them as a kind of interference in the brightness, a rippling, the way heat shimmers above a road in summer, except that there is no heat here, only the cold that tastes of copper.

## The Sound

The quiet is not the quiet of absence. It is the quiet of saturation, the way a sponge is quiet when it can hold no more water. There are sounds, but they are so layered and continuous that they form a kind of silence.

Water. Always water. The seepage down the walls, the small channels between the hummocks, the slow drip in the back of every cave-room, the pool at the center, which is never quite still, which has a small restless motion even in the dead calm of early morning. The water sounds like breathing.

Wind. A thin, high wind that comes over the lip of the bowl and circulates in it, never strong enough to be called a wind, never absent long enough to be called a calm. It whistles in the narrow doorways. It hums in the pulley ropes.

The screaming of the pale birds, far off, like a memory of distress.

The pulley, creaking.

The smith, hammering. I can hear her even when I cannot see her, the rhythm of her work steady and unvarying, the kind of rhythm that drives thought out of the head.

And under all of it—under the water, under the wind, under the hammer—a low vibration, not quite a sound, more a pressure in the inner ear. The mournstone, perhaps. The deep places. The boy said that if you put your ear to the rock at night, you could hear the tarn moving. I asked him moving where. He didn't answer.

## The Turbulence

I have used the word *turbulence* and I want to take it back, or to clarify it. The tarn is not turbulent in the way a storm is turbulent. It is turbulent in the way a city is turbulent, in the way a market is turbulent—a high saturation of activity that has become so constant it has the appearance of stillness. The water is always moving, the light is always shifting, the birds are always crying, the pulley is always turning, the smith is always hammering, the beasts are always appearing and disappearing at the edge of vision. The boy is always somewhere, watching me with that polite incuriosity. The woman at the smithy has glanced up once to assess me, and that glance is the most considered attention anyone here has paid me.

I do not feel that I am in danger, exactly. But I feel that I am being *processed*—that the tarn is a kind of slow digestive system and I am a thing that has been swallowed, and the question is not whether I will be digested but what I will become in the process. The boy said *enough* when I asked how many people lived here. I think he may have meant it as a number. I think he may have meant it as a destination.

I have been here four days. I will stay one more. I do not know if I will leave. The lip of the bowl is visible from the settlement, a ragged dark line against the dazzle of the sky beyond. I look at it often. It does not look back, which is a small mercy.

The light holds. The water weeps. The pulley creaks. The beasts appear and are gone.

The tarn does what it has always done.

I am beginning to learn the rhythm of it, and that, I think, is the most dangerous thing that has happened to me here.
