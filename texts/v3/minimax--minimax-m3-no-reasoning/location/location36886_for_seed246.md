A high basin on an ancient plateau. That is the first thing, and almost the only thing that needs to be said, because once you understand the basin, the rest arranges itself.

The plateau is so old that it has nearly forgotten being a mountain. It is granite and gneiss, the kind of stone that began as something deeper and more violent, and that has been worked on by weather and weight for so long that the working has become a kind of peace. The ridges around the basin are not ridges so much as the last suggestion of ridges — low swells of exposed bedrock, smoothed until they look poured, the color of wet bone where the lichen has not taken them, the color of iron and amber where it has. They rise perhaps twenty meters above the floor of the basin, which is enough. It is enough to make the place a room. It is enough to make the wind do what it does.

The basin itself is a few hundred meters across. Roughly oval, though the oval shifts depending on where you stand, so that it seems more circular from the north rim and more elongated from the south. The floor is not flat. Nothing here is flat. But it is settled. The contours are long and low, the kind of topography that has been negotiated over millennia rather than carved. There is a slight depression toward the center, and a stream that gathers in it.

The stream is the loudest thing in the basin, and the basin is the loudest thing for many kilometers, and the stream is the reason. It is not a large stream. It begins as a seep — a hundred seeps, really, along the base of the eastern ridge, where water comes out of the rock as if the rock has been holding it for a long time and has finally given up. The water finds a course almost immediately, because the floor of the basin is tilted a little, and the tilt is enough. The stream runs west, then bends south, then bends again, and in the bends it accumulates. By the time it leaves the basin through a low notch in the rim it is a real stream, a thing you could not step over, though you could step into. It makes noise the whole way. It runs over the differentially eroded remnants of the bedrock, which are not quite steps and not quite rapids, and each one gives a different voice: a clatter, a hiss, a small bell-tone where water drops into a pocket of air and the air answers, a low continuous hiss where the current slides over a smooth shoulder of stone. In calm weather you can hear individual notes. In wind, the stream disappears into the wind, and then reappears when the wind lulls, and the lulls are sudden, as if someone has closed a door.

The damp is everywhere, and it is not rain. Rain comes, but not often, and not for long. The damp is the basin breathing. Fog pools in it most mornings, and sometimes all day, lying in a shallow layer that the ridges barely contain, so that you walk with your head in cloud and your feet in a green, near-visible dark. The stones sweat. The mosses hold water like sponges and squeeze it out slowly, and the squeeze-out is the beginning of every seep. The turf on the roofs of the settlement is always wet, and the water finds its way through the thatch in thin streams that fall from the eaves like curtains. When the sun comes out, which it does, the whole basin steams, and the steam smells of stone and of something green and slightly sour, the smell of growth in a closed space.

The cold is the high-plateau cold. The sun is not weak — in fact, when it breaks through, it is sharp enough to burn the back of your neck — but the air is thin, and the stone stores winter in itself and gives it back reluctantly, so that even in summer a hand laid on a boulder in shadow feels the chill of another month. There is frost in the basin until late spring, and again by early autumn, and sometimes in the middle of summer if the night is clear. The cold is not hostile. It is patient. It is part of the slowness of the place, the reason the mosses can afford to be so thorough, the reason the lichens can take a century to colonize a hand's breadth of stone.

The vegetation is lush, and it is lush in the way that only patient, cold, wet places can be lush — not tall, not showy, but dense and complete, every surface colonized. Mosses in a dozen greens, from the almost-black of the deepest clefts to the pale celadon of the exposed caps. Lichen in pale maps on the stones, and in deeper orange and yellow where the rock is richer. Dwarf willows, no higher than your knee, with leaves that turn silver underneath in the wind. Crowberry and bearberry in low mats that you sink into. Sedges along the stream, surprisingly tall, their blades a vivid, almost aggressive green against the grays and ambers of everything else. Sphagnum in the wettest pockets, holding water in a way that makes the ground under it feel like a held breath. The walls of the settlement are softened by it. The floor between the buildings is softened by it. The ridges around the basin are softened by it. In a few centuries the basin will be a single low mound of green with a stream coming out of it, and there will be no sign that anything else was ever there.

The settlement is built along the stream, mostly on the slightly higher ground south of it, where the seep-line is just below the surface. It is built of stone, because stone is what the place offers in excess, and because the cold and the damp will not tolerate anything less patient. The walls are dry-stone, unmortared, fitted with the care of people who have been doing this for a long time and have stopped inventing new methods. They are low — never more than a story, often less — and they are thick, and the doorways are narrow, and the roofs are of heather and turf, layered so that the moss can grow over them and make them indistinguishable from the ground. From a little distance the settlement looks like a heap of stones that has gone soft. Up close, it is a warren.

The structures are layered. This is the part that takes time to read. A wall will be built against an older wall, and the older wall will still be visible inside, and the newer wall will have a doorway that has been sealed with stones and a new doorway cut beside it. A floor will turn out to be the roof of a chamber that has been partially filled in, and below that there will be another floor, and below that perhaps another. The settlement has been added to and reconfigured for a very long time, so that the original plan, if there was one, is no longer recoverable except as a habit — a tendency toward circularity around certain points, toward alignments with the stream, toward the placement of doorways where the wind will not push straight through them. The place is not a ruin. It is occupied, or it was recently occupied, or it is occupied in a low-key way by people who are elsewhere at the moment. There is smoke from one of the central hearths, a thin thread that the wind is taking apart. There are paths worn into the moss. There is a tool — a wooden digging-stick, or a long bone, hard to say — left leaning against a wall as if the person who set it there will return in a moment. There are no people, and there have been no people for as long as you can tell, and yet the place is not abandoned. It is between inhabitants. The way the basin is between animals.

The fauna is sparse. A bird, sometimes — a meadow-pipit, or a wheatear, with its quick, sideways look — pecking at the moss for insects. The tracks of something larger along the stream, the prints too blurred by the damp to identify with certainty, but the stride wrong for a deer and right for a fox or a small dog. The bones of something in a sheltered corner between two walls, bleached and scattered, no longer a creature but a topology of its own. The rest of the basin is given over to growth, and to weather, and to the slow business of stone becoming more like itself.

The sound is what you remember. The stream, which never stops. The wind, which comes down off the higher plateau to the west and finds the basin and is forced down and through, and which sings in the cracks and between the stones of the settlement, a low continuous moan that the dry-stone walls and the turf roofs seem to focus and amplify. The moan is not one note. It is a chord, almost, and the chord changes as the wind shifts, and in the shifts there are moments when the wind passes over a particular stone at a particular speed and the stone answers, and the answer is a tone so clear and so close to a voice that you look up. There are other sounds: the slow crack of ice in the stream at the edge of a frost; the soft, almost liquid sound of a turf roof settling under the weight of its own water; the occasional percussive note of a stone falling from a wall that has decided, today, to give up that particular piece; the distant, felt rather than heard rumble of weather on the higher plateau, arriving minutes later as a change in light and a smell of ozone. The basin is loud in the way a shell is loud: the sound is partly the place, and partly you, and the boundary is not easy to find.

The strangeness is in the details, and you may not notice it for a long time. The basin is slightly too oval, or slightly too circular, depending on where you stand. The stream hesitates in places where the tilt should carry it through without thinking. The stones of the settlement are arranged in patterns that are almost geometric — you keep almost seeing a spiral, a quincunx, a series of concentric arcs — and the patterns do not quite close, and the almost-closing is what bothers you. The moss grows in rings around certain stones, and the rings are not concentric, and they do not follow the drainage, and they do not follow the light, and they are not random. There is a stone in the center of the settlement, low and broad, that is worn smooth on top, and there is a cup-shaped depression in it that always holds a little water, even in dry weather, and no one can show you where the water comes from. The fog moves in the basin in directions that are not quite the wind's. The sun, when it comes through, falls in long shafts, and the dust and pollen in the shafts are too thick, too visible, moving in a way that suggests they are not quite falling, not quite rising, but holding their depth. The place feels, in the end, less like a place on a map and more like a place that has happened to itself — an eddy in the long erosion of the plateau, a quirk of geology that has gathered everything that is soft and quiet and persistent around itself, and that has, in doing so, become something slightly more than the sum of the stones and the moss and the loud, patient water.
