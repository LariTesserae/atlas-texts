Not room the way a human means it. Room the way a lung means it — a chamber inside something larger, tucked into the flank of a submerged ridge beneath a hundred meters of frigid lake. The fissure is the only door. You found it because the locals — the few who know — call it the Calcicle, and they only whisper the name in winter, when the lake above freezes thick enough to walk on and the muffled percussion of ice settling sounds, faintly, down through the water column like a heart you can barely hear.

The chamber is roughly ovoid. Perhaps forty meters long, twenty across, eight meters at its highest point. The walls are not rock, or not exactly. They were rock once, and they are still mostly mineral, but they have been, over what feels like a long time but may not have been — the structures here do not measure time the way you do — re-clothed in layer after layer of biologically precipitated architecture. It is the city's only building material, the only building material that has ever existed here. Calcium carbonate, in long pale ribbons and stacked arcs and upright shafts that branch like petrified trees but with the regularity of something load-bearing. The walls are no longer walls. They are façade. They have been faced.

And the facing is still going on.

You see it happening at the edges, where the architecture thins and resolves back into raw basalt. Pale lobulate creatures — not coral, though the resemblance is enough to trick you at first — secrete the mineral from their soft bodies in slow, deliberate courses, building along invisible templates, following stresses and geometries that are not visible to anything but themselves. The new work is rougher than the old. It has the unfinished look of scaffolding, of courses laid but not yet capped, of lintels placed between uprights with empty space waiting to be filled. The city is under construction. The city is always under construction. There has never been a moment, as far as you can tell, when the Calcicle was finished.
