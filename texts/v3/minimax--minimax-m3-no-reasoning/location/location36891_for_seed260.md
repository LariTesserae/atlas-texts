What I want you to do first is just look. Don't think about it. Don't try to make it mean anything. Just let your eyes adjust to what's in front of you, and let the place be in front of you, and let yourself be the kind of creature who can stand inside a place like this without flinching.

It takes a while.

The air here has no name for water. There has never been a name for water here. The closest thing is the slow, mineral breath that comes off the high places when the light shifts — a thin, pale exhalation, like something dreaming of being fog. If you cupped your hands and held them out, nothing would condense on your palms. Your palms would simply become drier than they already were, and then drier than that, and then you would stop noticing.

You are standing at altitude. The altitude is not kind. The altitude is the kind that makes your thoughts arrange themselves into thinner and thinner layers, like pages of a book that has been used so many times the print shows through to the other side. Above you, the sky is the color of tarnished pewter, and the pewter is older than pewter. It has been pewter for so long that it has forgotten what blue was. Lower down, near the horizons, the sky thickens into something the color of bruised slate, but a slate you would not want to touch.

The ground beneath your feet is not ground. It is the unfinished business of ground. You are standing on the early drafts of geology, on the rough sketches that continents make when they are only half-sure they want to exist. The stone is new. Not young-new, the way a kitten is young-new; old-new, the way a word is old-new the first time anyone says it. It has not had time to remember itself. When you press your boot into it, the surface gives a little, but not the way soil gives. It gives the way a thought gives when you have almost had it. You can see your footprint afterwards, but you cannot be sure it is a footprint. It might be a fissure that was going to happen anyway.

The rock is mostly a deep, unsaturated ochre, the color of dried blood that has been dried for a very long time. Veins of something darker run through it in long, patient lines, like rivers drawn by a cartographer who has never seen water and is working from rumor. There is also a stone here that is almost green — not the green of leaves or moss or anything that has ever been alive, but the green of a bruise that has decided to become permanent. It catches what little light there is and holds it in its teeth. If you kneel and look closely you can see the green stone is faintly damp, but the dampness does not come from anywhere. It is the stone's own memory of a dampness it has never had. The dampness is a kind of faith.

To your left, the land rises. It rises the way a sentence rises toward a question mark — slowly, with gathering intention. By the time it has risen a thousand feet it has become a ridge. By the time it has risen two thousand it has become a wall. By the time it has risen three thousand it has become a thing that is no longer geology but syntax, a kind of grammatical feature of the landscape, and you cannot tell anymore whether you are looking at a mountain or at a long, slow word that someone has carved into the face of the world in the hope that the world will eventually pronounce it. The peak, when you finally see it, is not sharp. It is blunt, the way a fist is blunt. It is the color of the sky, which is the color of the stone, which is the color of everything here.

And yet.

There is life.

Not the kind of life you are used to. Not the kind that begins with water and ends with water and in between is mostly water rearranged. The life here is a different arrangement. It is a life that has decided, or been decided, to do without.

The first thing you see is the color. From a distance it looks like a stain. Up close it looks like a mistake. It is neither. It is a small, low, dense growth that hugs the stone in mats and rosettes and slow, deliberate curls. The color is difficult to describe. It is somewhere between the green of old copper and the green of a bruise that has decided to become hopeful. The texture is somewhere between felt and velvet and the underside of a leaf that has been left in a book for a hundred years. When you brush it with your finger it is cool, and the coolness feels like an opinion.

There are no roots. You dig with the heel of your boot, and there are no roots, and yet the growth clings. It clings the way a thought clings. It clings by being continuous with the stone. It is not anchored to the rock. It is the rock, in a different mood. The boundary between mineral and vegetable here is a rumor, and the rumor has been going around for a long time, and no one is willing to be the first to deny it.

The growth does not spread so much as insist. It advances by millimeters per year. It advances with the confidence of something that knows it has nowhere else to be. A lichen, you might say, but the word lichen assumes a water cycle, and there is no water cycle here. A moss, you might say, but the word moss assumes a rain, and there is no rain here. So the growth goes on without a name, and the lack of a name does not bother it, and the lack of a name will continue not to bother it long after you are gone.

There are taller things. Not trees — the word tree assumes a trunk, and the word trunk assumes a vertical ambition that this place does not reward. But there are vertical things, sparse and widely spaced, that rise from the mats of growth like exclamation points in a long, quiet paragraph. They are the color of dried bone, with a faint undertone of the same bruised green as the ground cover. They are smooth. They are not wood. They are not stone. They are something else that the world is trying out, and has not yet decided whether to commit to. When the wind comes — and the wind comes, here, it comes, it is one of the few things that comes — they vibrate at a frequency that is just below hearing, and the vibration is the closest thing to a heartbeat this place has.

The wind.

The wind is the loudest thing here, and the wind is loud the way a thought is loud when you cannot stop thinking it. It does not howl. There is not enough atmosphere for howling. It hisses. It keens. It carries with it a fine, pale powder that is not quite dust and not quite ash and not quite the powdered bones of something that died. The powder gets into the corners of your eyes and the corners of your thoughts. After a while you stop noticing it. After a while you realize you have stopped noticing it, and you wonder what else you have stopped noticing.

The wind is loud, but it is not the loudest thing here.

The loudest thing here is the sky.

You do not hear the sky with your ears. You hear it with the rest of you. The sky here is not silent. The sky here is full of a low, sustained, tonal something that is not quite a hum and not quite a moan and not quite the sound a planet makes when it turns in its sleep. It is a frequency. It is a pressure. It is the sound of reality being slightly unsure of itself. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in the back of your teeth. You feel it in the place where your name used to be.

This is the weirdness, if you want to call it that. It does not feel like weirdness from the inside. It feels like the only possible shape of things.

Above you, the pewter sky occasionally thickens into bands. The bands are not clouds. There are no clouds here; clouds assume water, and water is a rumor that has not yet reached this altitude. The bands are more like creases. They are the places where the sky has been folded too many times and has begun to remember the folds. They move, but they move slowly, the way a thought moves across the face of someone who is deciding not to say something. They are the color of a bruise that has been a bruise for a thousand years and has become, in that time, almost beautiful.

The light is dim. The light here is dim the way a candle is dim in a cathedral — it is not that there is not enough light, it is that there is so much space that the light cannot find all of it. The shadows are long and the shadows are blue and the shadows are older than the stone they fall on. When the shadows fall across the growth, the growth seems to lean into them, the way a plant leans toward a sun that it has never seen but has heard about in the family.

There are no animals. There are no insects. There is no buzz and no hum and no distant lowing of anything that has a larynx. The absence is so total that after a while you begin to hear your own blood, and the hearing of your own blood is so loud that you begin to suspect it is the only animal left, and that it has, without your permission, begun to count you as its habitat.

There is a structure.

Not a building. Buildings assume walls, and walls assume an interior, and interiors assume a difference between inside and outside that this place does not concede. The structure is more like a gesture. It is a low ring of the bone-colored vertical material, arranged in a circle about thirty feet across. The pieces are not fitted together. They are placed together, the way words are placed together in a sentence that the speaker has not yet finished. In the center of the ring, the ground cover is thicker, and greener, and it rises in a low, soft mound that is almost the shape of a sleeping animal, but is not. There is nothing inside the ring. There is everything inside the ring. The ring is old, or the ring is new, or the ring is both, because the stone here does not quite understand the difference.

There is another ring, smaller, half a mile away, oriented to no celestial body you can identify. There is another, smaller still, on a ridge you can see but cannot reach before the light fails. There are probably others. The rings are not a village. They are not a monument. They are a punctuation. They are the places where someone, or some thing, or some version of someone, paused in the long, slow work of being here, and marked the pause, and went on.

There is no metal here. There are no tools that would survive being called tools. The vertical growths can be broken, and the broken edges are sharp, and the sharp edges can be lashed to other broken edges with strips of the mat-growth, which is stronger than it looks. The lashings hold. The lashings hold the way agreements hold between people who have been neighbors for a very long time. With this, you can cut. With this, you can scrape. With this, you can make a mark on the stone, and the mark will outlast you, and the mark will outlast the stone, and the mark will outlast the stone's idea of being stone.

The marks that have been made are few. They are mostly on the inside of the rings. They are mostly vertical lines, or horizontal lines, or a single, repeated shape that might be a letter in an alphabet that never caught on, or might be a tally, or might be a small, patient argument with the dark. You do not read them. You cannot read them. But you can feel that they were made by a hand, and the hand was attached to someone, and the someone was here, and the being-here was, for a while, enough.

The temperature is cool. The coolness is not the coolness of a spring morning, which is a coolness that is planning to leave. It is the coolness of a stone that has been in shadow for a long time. It is a coolness with permanence. It settles into your joints and into the small, soft places between your ribs. It does not make you shiver. It makes you quiet. After a while, you realize you have been quiet for a long time, and the quietness feels like an inheritance.

The land is still becoming. You can feel it. The erosion is at the beginning of erosion. The rain that has never fallen here is about to fall. Or the equivalent of rain is about to happen. You can see, on a distant slope, a long, pale line that could be a channel, but is not yet a channel. It is the sketch of a channel. It is the place where water would run if water ran. It is the river that will exist in ten thousand years, or in a hundred thousand, or in the next sentence, if the next sentence decides to include it.

The growth advances toward the line. The growth does not know it is advancing. The growth does not know there is a line. The growth simply leans, by a millimeter a year, in the direction of least resistance, and the direction of least resistance happens to be down.

The wind keens. The powder drifts. The sky creases and uncreases itself in its slow, geological way. The rings stand in their patient circles. The marks inside the rings wait, the way all marks wait, for someone to come back and read them, or for someone to come and fail to read them, which is also a kind of reading.

You are standing in a place that is not finished.

You are standing in a place that may never be finished.

You are standing in a place where the difference between those two statements is the only weather there is.

The bruise-green holds the light in its teeth.

The sky hums its long, uncertain hum.

The wind, which is the loudest animal, goes on being the only one.

You stay a while.

You do not know yet that you are staying forever.

You will find out.
