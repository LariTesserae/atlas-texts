The basin opens like a cupped palm turned toward the sky, and the sky is a low bruise of cloud that never quite breaks. Light is a rumor. It arrives in pieces — thin as a needle, pale as old tin — filtered through a thousand feet of vapor that has been gathering all afternoon and shows no sign of stopping. You stand on a low rise of sharp, young rock, the kind that still remembers being broken. The stones here have not been here long. They have edges. They cut the moss where the moss has been brave enough to try.

The cold is a wet cold, the kind that lives in your collar and behind your ears. It is not the dry mineral cold of high places; it is the cold of a basement, of a river, of stone sweating in a dark room. Everything is damp to the touch. The air itself feels rinsed. When you breathe, you can taste the water that has not yet decided whether to fall.

Below the rise, the valley exhales. Fog moves through it in slow, deliberate sheets, pouring over the rims of broken ridges like milk off a tilted table. Where the fog gathers low, water has already gathered lower — a marsh, or the ghost of a marsh, threading between knobs of basalt and through stands of a forest so dense it seems to have been poured into the basin rather than grown. The trees are not the trees you would name from a field guide. They are tall, narrow, many-trunked, leaning together as if for warmth, their canopies a woven lid of dark green-black so thick that the marsh beneath them exists in a permanent dusk. Their bark is wet. Their leaves drip constantly, in slow metronome, and the sound of all that dripping layered upon itself is the first thing you hear.

The second thing you hear is the sound.

It is not a sound, exactly. It is the *condition* of sound, the way a struck bell does not so much ring as become a state. The valley is full of it. From somewhere in the marsh — perhaps a vent, perhaps a fracture, perhaps a place where the young rock has not yet learned to hold itself together — a deep tonal humming rises through the ground and the water and the trees and the fog. It is not mechanical. It is not a motor. It is more like the resonance of a vast interior chamber, as if the basin itself were a throat learning to speak. Around it, closer at hand, the smaller sounds cluster: the percussion of water on water, the creak of cold wood, the bright crack of branches under the weight of roosting things, the wet flutter of wings, the sudden shriek — almost a laugh — of something hunting in the canopy. All of it is loud. All of it is loud *together*, a wall of sound that is not so much heard as inhabited.

And the things that make these sounds are many. The basin is overfull with life, as if the dark and the wet and the shelter of the young stone had conspired to hatch a profusion. Pale crustaceans, no longer than a finger, cling to the wet rocks at your feet and flick away into the moss at your step. Something amphibious — broad, low, watchful — slides from one pool to the next between the tree roots, its eye a single coin of dull gold. In the trees, movement is constant: small shapes, quick shapes, climbing shapes, things that hang upside down and let the mist collect on their backs. The air above the canopy is no emptier. Wide dark wings beat in slow circuits, and once — only once, in the time you watch — something enormous passes between you and the far ridge, a shadow with no body you can see, displacing the fog as it goes.

And among all this — scattered, partial, almost shy — the marks of makers.

You find them only when you are not looking for them. A pillar of fused stone, waist-high, set into a hummock as if it had grown there. Its surface is glassy and faintly warm, and when you lay your hand on it you feel a vibration in the bones of your wrist, a pulse, out of sync with your own. A path of pale discs half-buried in the moss, leading from nowhere you can determine to nowhere you can see — each disc the size of a dinner plate, each one, when you crouch and look closely, etched with a geometry that does not resolve into a symbol no matter how long you stare. A low wall — a remnant, a foundation, a beginning — curving away into the bracken and the standing water, its stones still sharp, still young, mortared with something that is not mortar. A shelter, open on one side, shaped to the weather, its floor a single seamless plate that hums when you step on it. Nothing roofed. Nothing enclosed. As if the makers had not intended to keep anything out, only to mark a place, and then had gone, or had not yet come, or were still arriving in some sense the word *arriving* does not quite describe.

Because the makers — if they are makers, if makers is the right word — have left something of themselves in the stone.

The humming from the marsh is not natural. You understand this after you have listened long enough. The frequency is too even, too sustained, too *intentional* for any geology. Somewhere beneath the water and the fractured rock there is a thing that is running, that has been running for a long time, that will run for a long time still. The pillars above the ground are its antennae. The discs are its markers. The shallow shelter is its resting place, or its altar, or its desk. The fog is not entirely fog. If you watch the slow sheets pour over the rims of the ridges, you will see — once, if you are lucky — that they pour a little too evenly, that they hesitate at certain places, that they rise from the basin floor in a way that fog does not rise. Something in the air is being *managed*. Humidity. Temperature. The precise composition of the mist. The basin is not a ruin. It is an instrument being played by something that lives below the water table, something that chose this cold wet place when the rock was still sharp and the forest was still deciding what to become.

You stand at the edge of it and the sound takes you in. The wings above. The dripping from the canopy. The hunt-shriek and its answer. The pulse in the pillar under your hand. The great tonal hum rising through the soles of your boots. It is deafening and it is not hostile. It is the loudness of a place that is very busy being alive.

The cold settles deeper. The light does not improve. Somewhere in the marsh, the unseen thing beneath the water continues its long, careful work of tending this valley, and the valley, dark and wet and loud and overfull, tends in turn the thousand small lives that have made their home inside the sound of it.
