The air goes heavy before you arrive at it. You feel the place first as a change in the climate against your skin — a thickening, a warm weight, the way breath becomes visible not as fog but as a kind of pressure. Then the light thins, as though the day were already half-finished even when it is not. The stone of the path darkens with moisture, and you hear, very faintly, the patient murmur of water that has nowhere to go.

The terrace sits at the foot of a low rise. It is small — you could walk its perimeter in two slow minutes — and it is sunken a little below the surrounding ground, so that the fitted stone walls come up nearly to your shoulder. The walls are of a single dense rock, basalt or something close to it, dark grey in the dimness, veined with paler mineral seams that weep continuously. Every seam is wet. Every joint has its thin bright thread of water, and where two stones meet there is often a thicker bead of it that gathers, holds, hangs, and falls in a slow rhythm into a basin below. The basins are everywhere. They are carved directly into the paved floor — shallow rectangular troughs, deeper round pools, long channels that run between the larger forms and carry their overflow from one to the next. The water in them is warm and still and faintly cloudy, and a faint white rim of mineral deposit marks each high-water line as clearly as a painted stripe.

There is no plant life. Nothing green, nothing soft, nothing that grows. The stones are bare of moss and lichen; whatever the water carries, it is not the chemistry of life. The walls look older than any living thing should be allowed to be near — not crumbled, not falling, just established, as if the place had stopped accumulating change a long time ago and would now go on being exactly this until something external broke the arrangement. The wear on the surfaces is the wear of long habit rather than ongoing violence. Edges that should be sharp have been rounded by the same drip, the same trickle, the same patient water for so long that the rounding itself has become the shape, and the water no longer bothers them.

The terrace is packed. There is almost no empty floor. Around the basins are arranged low stone benches, some single blocks, some formed of two or three fitted pieces. There are small altars — squared platforms the height of a knee, with shallow bowls carved into their tops that always hold an inch of water. There are figures: a seated one near the back wall, too worn now to be certain of its original posture, the face reduced to a smooth suggestion; a standing one at the corner of the larger central pool, arms folded across its chest in the old way, the stone around its feet darkened by a permanent damp. There are tools left where they were used. A pestle rests in a mortar at the edge of a trough. A blade of chipped flint lies on a ledge, its working edge still bright against the dark of its matrix. A bronze vessel — small, two-handled, the patina of long wet contact turning it the color of verdigris — sits on the bottom of the deepest basin, half full, never emptied. Woven mats, soaked through and rigid now, lie folded on one of the benches, their fibers mineralized into something closer to a stiff cloth of stone than to anything that was once plaited. The arrangement of these objects is not chaotic. Someone placed them where they belong. You can tell because each thing rests in a slight hollow worn into the surface beneath it, as though it had been sitting in that one spot for longer than the spot had existed.

The light is dim and warm-grey, the color of an overcast afternoon near the sea. There is no direct sun on the terrace, only the even, filtered glow that comes in from the open side and over the walls. The shadows are soft. The water holds a dim gleam rather than a shine. The metal of the bronze vessel catches what little light there is and returns it as a dull green flash. The standing figure looks darker on one side than the other, but the difference is slight.

The sound is mostly quiet. What there is, is water: the thin hiss of a sheet of it running down a wall, the plop of a drop falling from a seam into a basin, the lower continuous murmur of the deeper pool where the inflow is steady. No wind moves here. The walls are high enough and the surrounding ground low enough that the air is undisturbed. A breath, a footfall, a single word spoken aloud would sound louder than any of the water, and would linger against the stone.

The living things here are few. You might see, after a few minutes of looking, a single large snail moving along one of the upper channels, its pace glacial, its trail silver. A small pale fish or eel-shaped thing hangs in the deeper pool, drifting slowly against the constant gentle current of the inflow, never leaving. Somewhere in the damp a faint hum, possibly an insect, possibly not. You do not see them so much as sense the few that are present, and the rest of the place reads as genuinely empty of animal life — not hostile to it, simply arranged so that none has found reason to stay.

The place is warm. The walls hold heat and the water releases it and the air is close. You feel it on your skin almost immediately, and after a short time you feel that you have been here longer than you have. The clocks that matter here are not hours. They are the slow ones: the rate at which a particular seam weeps, the rate at which the white mineral line inches up the wall of a basin, the rate at which the figure in the corner is becoming, by imperceptible degrees, more of a smooth stone and less of a figure. All of these rates are very small, and all of them are exactly equal to one another, and have been for longer than anyone could measure. Nothing is being added. Nothing is being lost. The water comes in at the upper end and flows out at the lower end and the total in the basins is the same now as it was a thousand years ago, and will be the same a thousand years from now. The pestle in the mortar is in the position of a pestle that has just been lifted and will shortly be set down again. The bronze vessel is in the position of a vessel that has just been filled. The standing figure has just turned its head, and is about to turn it back. Nothing has happened here for a very long time, and nothing will happen here for a very long time, and yet nothing in the place is unfinished. It is held, exactly as it is.
