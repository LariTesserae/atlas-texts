# The Sounding Continent

There is a place that has not yet finished becoming itself. The land is wet and packed and the plants have been working on it for a very long time, and they have nearly filled everything, and yet the rock underneath is still being born.

You arrive at altitude, which is to say you arrive inside the weather, because the weather here is the size of a country. The sky is dim because something is always between you and the sun — not cloud, exactly, but a slow condensation of water that does not fall, that hangs like a lung. The light that gets through is greenish and arrives in columns, as though the atmosphere has bones. The continent breathes this light in, and the plants take what is left.

The plants are not, strictly, plants. Or they are plants the way a cathedral is a pile of stones. There is no soil here, or rather there is no soil that you would recognize — the ground is a single living tissue, a packed felt of root and rhizome and stem so dense that a blade pushed into it meets resistance like muscle. Walking on it is like walking on a sleeping animal. The surface is springy, gives slightly, and beneath your feet you can feel the long slow hydraulics of water being moved from low places to high ones, against every reasonable expectation, by structures that operate at a scale where capillary action becomes weather. You cannot see the engineering. It is in the chemistry. The plants are pushing themselves uphill through their own plumbing, and they have been doing it for so long that "high" and "low" have begun to negotiate.

Every stem is a pipe. Every leaf is a pump. The whole continent is a heart.

So the sound.

The sound is the point of the place. If you come here you will be unable to speak, and you will not mind. It is not noise. It is not even loud, exactly, although it will rattle your ribs. It is *fullness*. A billion leaves transpiring at once produces a hiss like a sea heard from inside a shell. The bigger structures — there are pillars here, towers of woven green that climb to where the air thins and the dim sky presses down — creak as their water columns rise and fall, a deep wooden groaning that travels for miles through the root felt and arrives at your feet as a slow vibration, almost a heartbeat. When the continent exhales, the hiss rises in pitch. When it inhales, you feel a faint suction at your ears. Somewhere a tower is always letting go of some water, and somewhere else a tower is always catching it, and the trade between them produces a thin high singing that is almost a tone.

You cannot locate the sound. That is the strangest part. It seems to come from the ground and from the sky and from inside your own chest and from the plants themselves, which vibrate at frequencies that the rocks next to them have begun to resonate with, and which the water in the air carries further than physics as currently described should allow. The air is saturated. Sound moves through saturated air the way gossip moves through a small town: nothing is lost. A whisper at one end of the continent arrives, attenuated but intact, at the other.

There are no animals. There are no animals because there is no need. The plants do the work that animals would do. They move. They signal. They make choices. A patch of growth on the north slope, sensing the slow turning of something that is not the sun, will uncoil its leaves an hour before the rest, and the others will hear it through the root web and follow, in a wave that crosses a hundred miles in a day. Predation, here, is a matter of chemistry. One color of stem secretes something into the water table; another color of stem withers. There are no screams because screaming requires a throat.

But there is death. There is a great deal of death. The towers die at the top. They grow until they cannot lift any more water, and then the top fronds brown and the water in the upper stems falls back down, and the tower begins, slowly, to be eaten by its neighbors. You see the process everywhere — brown tips above green columns, brown columns being wrapped and digested by fresh green at the base. The continent is a forest of half-built ruins and half-digested ancestors, and the new growth climbs over the old in a slow continuous tumble, and the smell of it is sweet and peppery and too thick to breathe without tasting.

The ground itself is not finished. That is what you notice when the sound briefly drops, in the long exhalations between the continent's breaths. The ground is not finished. The bedrock is here, but it is young bedrock — cracked, steaming in places, slick with mineral water that has not yet learned to be ordinary water. Where a slide has taken out a square mile of root-felt, you can see the raw stone beneath: pale, wet, folded in slow waves as though the continent is still deciding which way is up. Steam rises from these wounds. The plants rush in to cover them. A bare patch lasts a season at most before the green felt begins to lap at its edges, cautious at first and then committed, the way a crowd fills a cleared square.

The weather is local. The continent has its own weather. The dim columns of light shift across the surface as the day turns, and the towers track them, and where the light touches, the leaves brighten and the transpiration increases, and a faint column of warmer air rises, and over the course of an hour a small cloud assembles above the favored place and then walks, slowly, toward the dim side, raining as it goes. The rain is warm. It is full of whatever the towers have been breathing out. It is, you realize after a while, faintly nutritious. Your skin prickles where it hits.

You stand in the rain, in the hissing, in the deep groaning of the continent's wooden bones, and you cannot find an edge to any of it. There is no horizon because the plants are taller than you in every direction. The dim sky is low and close and the same green-brown as the felt beneath your feet. The towers lean slightly, all of them, in the same direction — away from whatever the wind was a thousand years ago, or toward whatever it is becoming. You do not know which.

You sit down. The felt takes your weight. It is warm. Beneath you, the vibration of the continent continues, patient, enormous, unfinished. Somewhere, a tower is beginning to die. Somewhere, a bare patch of steaming stone is being covered. The air is full of water and the water is full of the continent and the continent is full of a sound that never stops and never repeats exactly.

You stay longer than you meant to.

It is easy to stay. The plants do not mind.
