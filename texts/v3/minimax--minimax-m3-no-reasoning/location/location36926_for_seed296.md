The mountains here are tired. Worn down across epochs into something that no longer wants for the name *mountain* — these are the ruins of mountains, the leftover bones, the geological equivalent of an old laborer's hands. Granite shoulders humped under a sky that rarely commits to clarity. Glacial scoops from a colder age still hold the shape of the ice that made them, but the ice is gone, replaced by moss, by slow water, by terraces of old stone fitted to the slope as if grown there.

The city — and it is a city, though *growth* would be the more honest word — sits in the scoops and climbs the shoulders. Built up over centuries in a way that no one planned and no one has ever finished mapping. The lowest levels are the oldest: stone on stone, tight-fitted, mortared with something that has long since surrendered to the damp. Above that, brick. Above that, the rusted iron and soot-stained timber of the industrial expansions. Above that, newer construction, but already being eaten by the moss. The whole thing is so dense that the mountain beneath has become rumor.

Water comes from the sky and not from the sky. Clouds settle on the shoulders of the worn range and simply stay. The air is saturated. Condensation runs down the walls in the morning and again at night. Old stone sweats. Moss colonizes every horizontal surface that isn't moving, and several that are. The city is half-stone, half-vegetation, and no one is sure which is winning. In the deepest alleys, the brick is more root than brick. The ferns have nested in the gutter-spouts. Where the smoke comes out of the chimneys, the lichens grow orange and grey, fed by the same breath.

Industry is here, but it is an industry that has been here long enough to forget what the hurry was. The factories are squat, dark, windowed with thick glass yellowed by age. Steam rises from them in plumes that never quite clear the rooftop line before the damp drags them down. The sound of the engines is felt more than heard — a low vibration that lives in the floor of the valley and the bones of anyone who stands still long enough. The wheels turn. The gears engage. Something is always being made, but the product is rarely named, and the workers — what few remain — move through the dimness like sleepwalkers with no appointment to keep.

The light is a problem. Not darkness, exactly — a refusal of brightness. The sky is white, the white of a held breath, and lets down a light that has no source, no shadow, no direction. The stone walls hold it and give nothing back. The moss drinks it. By afternoon the streets are lit by the occasional gas lamp, itself yellowed, itself dim, itself half-swallowed by the moss climbing the pole. By evening the lamps are the only definite light, and they make a constellation of low orange smears across the dark stone.

Few animals. A dog, sometimes, in the lower terraces. Cats in numbers no one has counted — the cats own the city in a way the people do not. A bird, occasionally, blown up from the valleys below. Something that might be a rat in the cellars, larger than it should be. But for the most part, the city has been emptied of fauna, or the fauna of the city, and the only moving things are the people, the water, the mist, and the slow green.

The sound is mostly the sound of absence. Water, somewhere, dripping. A creak in a beam that has been creaking for a hundred years. A pipe somewhere letting out a breath of steam at intervals so regular they become a kind of pulse. Footsteps, sometimes, on wet cobble, fading. The hum of the engines, far off, so constant it has stopped being heard. Once in a while, from somewhere high, the clatter of a shutter in a wind that never quite arrives at street level.

But the place is active, in its way, ferociously so. The water is always moving — down the walls, into the gutters, into the cisterns, out again through pipes that groan. The moss is always growing, slow but relentless, the patient conqueror. The clouds move, even when the eye cannot see them; the light shifts, dims, brightens, dims again. The engines turn. The chimneys breathe. Something is being processed, refined, made. The city is a body, and like all bodies, it is never still. The water in the pipes is the blood. The steam in the vents is the breath. The engines are the digestion of something — coal, ore, time itself. The place has been eating the mountain for so long that no one is sure which is the eater and which is eaten.

And there is a strangeness. Not a magical strangeness, not the strangeness of the impossible — just the strangeness of accumulation, of layered time, of a place that has had too much happen in it and has lost the ability to be simple. The streets do not connect the way the maps say they do. A stair climbs and arrives somewhere to the side of where you started. A door in a wall opens onto a roof three stories above the street. The city has a kind of drunken geometry, as if it tilted slightly on its axis at some point in its history and has been quietly falling ever since, in a direction no one has agreed upon. The cats seem to know the way. The people have stopped trying.

At the very top, where the shoulders of the old mountains flatten out into something almost like a plain, there is a fog that never quite leaves. You can stand up there and see the city below as a smear of dark stone and orange lichen and the slow pulse of chimneys, and the fog will move through you, and the world will narrow to the few feet around you, and you will be inside the weather.

Below that, the city. Below that, the worn rock. Below that, the bones of the bones of mountains. Below that, time.
