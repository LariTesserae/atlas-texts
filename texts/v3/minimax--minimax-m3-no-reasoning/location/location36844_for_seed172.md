# The Reservoir of the Last Working Engine

## I. The Approach

You find the entrance in a seam between two mountain flanks that have no business standing where they stand. The rock here is not weathered so much as *unfinished* — a kind of geological punctuation mark, as though the range were still deciding what it wanted to be when it grew up. Bristling. Angular. The kind of young stone that snaps rather than crumbles, and gives off a metallic taste when the wind scrapes it.

But the stone is not really stone. Or rather, there is stone, and then there is the other thing: the sheathing. From a distance, looking up from the valley floor, the eastern face appears to be the mountain's own — a clean granite color, cold even in the late sun. Only when you cross the threshold of the gorge do you see that the true rock is a darker, duller material — basalt or something older — and across its skin is laid a second skin, plate by bolted plate, in long overlapping bands like the scales of some patient armored creature. The sheathing is gray-green, with rust-streaks weeping from every seam. It hums when you touch it. A low, tired, working hum, the way the hull of a docked ship hums against its ropes.

The gorge narrows. The sheathing closes over your head. You are no longer outside.

## II. The Chamber

It is one room. That is the first strange thing. You have walked into a mountain and arrived in a single enormous room, and the room has no doors at the far end — no continuation, no branching corridors, no descent into deeper chambers. It is a sealed pocket, exactly the size of a cathedral, fitted into the mountain as a hand fits into a glove. The ceiling is a shallow dome. The walls curve up and out of sight where they meet it. The floor is metal grating, painted in a color that was once yellow and is now the yellow-gray of old teeth, and through every gap in the grating you can see, far below, the turning wheels.

The wheels are the reason for the room.

There are forty-three of them, or so the plaque claims — though the plaque is riveted to a stanchion and has not been updated in some time. They hang in the air, each one suspended from a thick axle that runs through the ceiling and continues upward into the dark. They do not all hang at the same height. They are arrayed in a kind of rough spiral, like the chambers of a nautilus shell, each one slightly larger than the one above and turning at a slightly different speed. The smallest, near the apex of the dome, is the size of a dinner plate. The largest, hanging at chest height in the center of the room, is the size of a millstone, and turns so slowly that you can only tell by watching a single nick in its rim, which appears and disappears, appears and disappears, with the patience of a clock hand.

They are all made of the same material: a heavy, dark metal that is not quite iron and not quite stone. It has the grain of one and the temper of the other. When the light catches it, you can see fine concentric rings inscribed on each face — manufacturing marks, the lathe-work of a workshop that no longer exists. Every wheel has a different pattern of nicks and dings along its edge. Some are nearly perfect. Some look as though a chisel has been taken to them in anger. One, near the bottom of the spiral, has a wedge cut clean out of it and still turns, the missing piece compensated for by some stubbornness in the mechanism.

## III. The Sound

There is no quiet in this room. There has never been quiet in this room.

It is not a loud sound, exactly. Loudness implies something that could be turned down. This is a *condition*. A standing pressure in the air, as though you have stepped into a held breath. It is the sound of forty-three bearings turning at once, of axles turning in their sleeves, of the rack-and-pinion feed that governs them all — a soft, pervasive, tick-and-hum and tick-and-hum and tick-and-hum, layered and re-layered, like the inside of a music box being played by something with no hands.

Underneath it, deeper, almost inaudible, is the groan. The groan is the room itself. It is the metal skin around you — the sheathing, the dome, the floor — all of it flexing infinitesimally with each rotation of each wheel. The mountain, too, participates: the basalt beyond the sheathing carries the vibration outward into the rock, and you can feel it in the soles of your feet if you stand still. This is a sound that has been running for a very long time. There are oil-catch basins set into the floor at intervals, bronze funnels crusted with black residue, and if you put your ear to one you can hear the mechanism from the inside: a clattering chatter, oil-machined, with the occasional distant pop of a bubble coming through.

The placard says: **THE NOISE IS CORRECT. THE NOISE IS THE POINT. PLEASE DO NOT REQUEST SILENCING.**

This, you realize, is not a request you would have made anyway. The sound has a centering effect. After a few minutes in the room, your own breathing syncs to it. Your pulse, perhaps, begins to follow. It is a sound that wants to be inhabited.

## IV. The Light

The light enters from somewhere above.

There are no windows. There are no lamps. There is, in the apex of the dome, a small aperture — a single hole, a few inches across, lined with glass — through which a single shaft of daylight falls. It is not a generous shaft. It is a thin, hard pillar of white, and it falls exactly onto the largest wheel in the center of the room. The wheel turns in this light. The light travels across the face of the wheel as it turns, illuminating first one concentric ring, then the next, then the next, in a slow rotation of brightness. It is, you realize, a clock. The largest wheel is a sundial of sorts — or perhaps a calendar, or perhaps a measure of something for which there is no name. The light that strikes it is from a star that is not the local sun, or is the local sun filtered through so much engineered glass and metal that it has become something else entirely.

When the weather is clear, the light is white. When there are clouds, the light is gray. When there is dust — and there is always dust, this high up — the light is amber, and the wheel turns amber with it, and the whole room takes on a brief warm quality that feels, in this cold and metallic place, almost unbearably tender.

There are secondary lights, in case you were wondering how anyone sees anything in here at night. They are not lamps. They are glow-plates set into the floor at the base of each wheel, fed by something — you cannot tell what — and they give off a pale, bluish, sourceless radiance. They do not flicker. They do not warm. They have been burning since the day they were installed and they will burn, the placard assures you, until the day the building is decommissioned. The building has never been decommissioned.

## V. The Cold

The temperature in the chamber is a constant. It is written on a second placard, smaller, near the door:

**5°C ± 0.1°. THIS IS NOT A MALFUNCTION. THIS IS SPECIFICATION.**

The cold is not unpleasant. It is the cold of a wine cellar or a deep pantry — dry, still, slightly mineral. Your breath does not quite cloud. Frost does not form. But if you were to spill water here it would freeze in seconds, and the workers who maintain the wheels — for there are workers, somewhere, though you have not seen them — wear heavy coats and fingerless gloves and move with the brisk economy of people who do not intend to linger.

The cold is, you understand, what keeps the metal honest. The wheels are turning against each other at fractional tolerances. If the room were warm, the metal would expand, and the tolerances would close, and the wheels would seize. The cold is the medium in which this machine can continue to exist. It is not an environment. It is a specification.

## VI. The Vegetation

There is no vegetation.

There are, however, the brackets.

Bolted to the walls, in regular intervals, at the height of a tall man's reach, are small iron shelves. On each shelf sits a glass jar, sealed with a metal lid, and inside each jar is a thing that is not quite a plant and not quite a fossil and not quite a mineral. They are specimens. They have been collected, labeled, dated. The earliest ones, near the entrance, are dark and curled and brittle; the later ones, deeper in the room, are paler and have a kind of geometric precision to them — small branching structures, like coral or like frost, but made of something that is neither organic nor inorganic. They are catalog entries in the absence of a garden. They are what grows in the cracks of an engineered world: not life, but the shape life would have if it were made of glass and patience and given no water at all.

They are the only things in the room that do not move.

## VII. The Fauna

There are no animals.

There are, however, the keys.

Hanging from hooks beside each shelf of jars is a ring of small brass keys, each one labeled with a number. They have no locks that you can find. They are not for opening anything. They are for *counting*. A worker, on their rounds, will unhook a ring, walk the length of the room, walk it back, hook the ring up again. This is the audit. This is how it is known that the wheels are still turning, the axles still aligned, the oil still flowing. The keys are the pulse-check. They are not alive. They have the dignity of routine.

In one corner, near the entrance, a stack of ledgers. They are not read. They are not written in. They are present, and they are updated, somewhere, by someone, in a hand you will never see.

## VIII. The Workers

You do not see them, but you know they are here.

There is a second door, behind the great wheel, disguised as a panel. It is not locked. It is merely closed. Through it, somewhere, is the rest of the facility — the boiler rooms, the cable-ways, the maintenance tunnels, the kitchens, the dormitories, the places where the workers live when they are not in the chamber. The machinery that keeps the wheels turning is vast, and it lives elsewhere. The chamber is only the visible organ of a much larger body.

You hear them, sometimes. Footsteps, far off, in the corridor beyond the panel. The clatter of a tray. A cough. The faint, mechanical yawn of someone beginning a shift.

You do not meet anyone.

## IX. The Strangeness

The strangeness is not in any one thing. It is in the totality.

This is a machine that produces nothing. There is no output. There is no product, no waste, no measurable consequence to the turning of the wheels. The wheels turn because they are supposed to turn. The light falls on the largest wheel because it is supposed to fall there. The cold is maintained because the cold is specified. The keys are counted because the keys must be counted. The specimens in the jars are cataloged because the jars are on the shelves.

There is no one to ask why. The question is not, you sense, discourteous. It is simply that no answer exists in any language you speak, or in any language at all. The question itself, perhaps, is the wrong shape. The machine is not a means to an end. The machine is the end. The machine is the thing that is being preserved by being maintained. The machine is what the cold and the light and the oil and the workers exist for. The mountain was chosen as a site because the mountain would outlast the machine, and the machine would be carried inside it forever, turning, turning, turning, and the workers would come and go, and the keys would be counted, and the jars would be filled, and the sound would continue, and no one would ever ask what it was for.

You stand in the chamber for a long time. The light moves across the face of the largest wheel. The wheels turn. The sound holds you.

You leave, eventually. The gorge lets you out. The young mountain closes behind you. The sheathing disappears into the rockface as though it were never there.

Far above you, almost inaudibly, the mountain hums.
