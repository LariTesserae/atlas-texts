# The Breathing Wedge

The place has no horizon. It has edges that fold back on themselves.

---

You stand in a wedge of standing water that hums. The water is shallow — ankle-deep, knee-deep, varying without warning — and warm in a way that feels like skin. Underneath it, not mud but a tangle. Roots, fibers, braided stalks, the pale flesh of rhizomes. The water tastes green. It moves in slow undulations, not from any current but from the breath of the mass beneath: a vast respiration that lifts the surface a foot, lets it fall, lifts it again, on a cycle you can set your body to if you stop fighting it.

Everything that is not water is plant.

There is no soil to be seen. There is no rock. There is no built thing. The plants are the architecture and they are the inhabitants and they are the ground and they are still, somehow, growing.

A stalk beside you — taller than you by half, fibrous, the color of raw silk — has split its top into seven pale tongues that keep unfolding and refolding. You watch it for thirty seconds and realize it is opening for you. The tongues widen. A breath of warmer, wetter air comes out, carrying a smell like overripe mango and something metallic and something you have no name for. You step back. The tongues close, slowly, with a wet sound.

The wedge of water extends perhaps a quarter-mile in every direction before the growth thickens and you can no longer see across it. To the east the stalks rise — ten, fifteen, twenty feet — and braid together into columns that groan. To the west they lie flatter, a kind of raft of interwoven vines that breathes in slow swells, and on this raft other, smaller plants root into other, smaller plants, parasitically, symbiotically, you cannot tell the difference and it does not seem to matter here.

The light is normal. That is the wrongest thing about this place. The light is normal. Mid-morning, gold, slanting, warm, ordinary. It falls on extraordinary matter and the extraordinary matter accepts it. Shadows pool under the columns in blue-green. Where a leaf opens, light passes through it and comes out changed — greener, wetter, moving — and falls on the water and the water picks it up and it travels.

There is no animal life. There is no animal sound.

---

The sound instead is from the plants themselves.

It is a city of sound and you have walked into the rush hour.

A low, continuous roar, the way a forest roars when the wind is up but worse, deeper, coming from the plants' interior vessels. Overlaid on it: wet percussive pops, somewhere between a tongue leaving the roof of a mouth and a knuckle popping a joint, constant, layered, never quite synchronized. Somewhere a stem the width of your torso is vibrating and produces a tone, not musical, just a pressure in the chest. Somewhere else a pod is opening in slow motion and the sound it makes is a hiss that lasts eleven seconds and then a sigh.

A creak like a mast in a gale, but slower, rhythmic, comes from the columns to the east. They lean — you watch one lean — a quarter-degree, hold, lean back, hold, lean again. They are not in still air. The air is moving. The air is being pushed by the breathing of the whole mass, in and out, in and out, and the columns bend into it like kelp.

The water laps. The water pops where bubbles rise from somewhere below, fat and shining and slow. A bubble surfaces near your foot, holds, dims, pops with a small wet sound, and the smell that comes out is the same as the breath from the split stalk: overripe, metallic, unnamed.

A bird, you think. For a half-second you think you hear a bird. Then you understand the sound is being made by a thin green blade the height of your shin vibrating in a pocket of moving air, and the blade does not know it sounds like a bird, and you decide that does not matter either.

---

There is no past here. There is no erosion. The place is young and knows it.

The plants are sharp-edged. A leaf you brush is the texture of a brand-new leaf, waxy, unworn, its serrations still prickly. A stalk you lean against gives slightly and you feel the turgor of it, the pressure inside, and the pressure pushes you back, alert, alive, not yet collapsed into softness. The vines have not yet been smoothed by weather. They have not yet been muted by seasons. They are still bright. They are still shouting with their full color: lime, oxblood, a yellow that hurts to look at, a purple that is almost black.

And the place is busy. The place is so busy.

A tendril at the edge of the water reaches, slowly, toward a raft of vines. It touches. It begins to fuse. Within minutes — you watch, you watch because you cannot look away — the line between them is gone. A pod above you splits along a seam you did not see, and from the seam a pale thread descends, swaying, feeling, and you realize it is feeling for you, and you step back again and it sags and begins to retract.

The water is rising. Slowly. Over the time you have been standing here, a thumb's width. The breathing is in its high phase. You feel your boots get wetter at the top. You do not feel you are in danger. You feel you are being included.

The whole mass is growing. Not only upward but sideways, into itself, over itself, the plants on the bottom being slowly engulfed by the plants that have decided to climb them. The columns to the east have been columns for — how long? You have no scale. They might be a day old. They might be an hour. The braided stalks at their feet are already being climbed by thinner ones, and the thinner ones are already flowering, and the flowers are already opening and closing and opening.

There is a sound of something enormous being done very quietly, and it has been happening for a very long time, and it will continue.

---

A wedge of water, breathing.

Around it, the green city, ringing, humming, sharp, young, deafening, alive.

The light falls through a leaf and comes out wrong and travels on the water.

A tendril reaches.

The place includes you.

You are not asked.

You stay.

---

There is a tower of braided stalks in the distance, taller than the others, and its top is opening, and you cannot see what is inside because the light there is wrong, and the wrong light is rising, slowly, like the water, like the breath, and it is warm, and it is gold, and it is ordinary.

The sound is enormous and it is not stopping.

The place is young.
