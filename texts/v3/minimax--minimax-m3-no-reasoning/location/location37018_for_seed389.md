# The Convection Ward

The town rises into air that should not hold it.

The first thing you notice, standing on the lower switchbacks, is that nothing is green. The stone is dark—trachybasalt, something like that, or some local name that doesn't translate because the language here was built for this and only this—and it holds the day's heat in its surface like a grudge. By late afternoon the walls have forgotten the morning was ever cool. You can feel the warmth rising through the soles of your boots.

The second thing you notice is that the town is *climbing*. Switchback after switchback, buttress after butbuttress, the structures stair-step up the inside of what might once have been a caldera or might have been a borehole or might be something no useful word exists for. The architects who laid this out followed a contour that wasn't there when they started. They were patient. Some of the foundations were poured into stone that was still warm.

---

The street you're on is called something that means *the long ledger*, and it runs along the inner wall at roughly the third-tier level. Above you, tier after tier of dwellings cantilever out from the rock face on basalt-and-iron corbels, each row shadowed by the row above it. The air between the rows doesn't move. It tastes of sulfur and of something sweeter that might be the lichen-stand-in—those flat, pale blooms that crust the shaded stone and feed on nothing you'd call a root. They grow in the dark places only. They have the patience of geology. They were here before the town and will be here after.

Below you, the ward drops away into a haze of heat. You can hear it if you listen: a low, slow, almost-musical exhalation, the breathing of the deep workings. Somewhere far down, the heat exchangers are turning. Steam moves through iron pipes the thickness of a man's torso, and the pipes move it again, and the pipes run down the inside of the slope in long dark lines that the workers have polished to a dull sheen with decades of passing hands.

---

The buildings are not painted. There is nothing to paint them with. The builders used the local rock and a sort of slag-brick made from the slag of the refineries lower down, and the two materials sit side by side in the walls, dark beside lighter dark, a kind of masonry that records its own provenance. Every block of slag-brick in this town was poured by someone whose name is in a ledger at the foundry-hall on tier one. The town is built, in a real sense, of its own people.

The roofs are heavy. Almost flat, slightly dished toward central scuppers that pour runoff into carved channels along the buttresses. The water comes from somewhere upslope—a spring that surfaces inside a small grotto above the seventh tier—and the townspeople watch it the way other towns watch their crops. When there is rain, the rain falls on the outer slope and is largely lost, slicking the switchbacks so that the night-shift workers have to rope themselves to the railings on the way down. The gutters on the inner face carry the precious cargo all the way to the cisterns at the bottom, where it is metered out by a man with a leather-bound book whose great-grandfather had the same job.

You can tell when it has rained, two days later, by the smell of wet iron.

---

The foundry-hall sits at the lowest inhabited tier, taking up nearly the full width of the chamber, and from it rises a sound that you only notice when it stops: a deep, throaty hum that you feel more in your sternum than your ears. The great heat-engine turns. It is the size of a small house, and it has been turning, in some form or another, for as long as anyone in the ward can remember. There are older engines behind it, retired and rusting in their alcoves. There are drawings on the wall for engines that will replace this one in forty years, drawn in a hand that has not yet been born.

The engine is fed by the heat of the deep rock. Pipes come up from below—*from how far below no one says*, because the answer, you sense, would not be comforting—and they bring with them a steady, warm exhalation that sets the air shimmering above the foundry roof. In winter—which here means only that the nights are colder—the exhalation is visible as a pale column all day long, and the children climb to the upper tiers to watch it bend in the upper winds.

The foundry takes the heat and does work with it. It pumps water up to the upper tiers. It runs the mills that shape the slag into brick. It charges the cells that light the lamps at night. It does not, so far as anyone will tell you, do anything else. The engines have been getting more efficient for a century. The ward gets a little more habitable each generation, in small increments.

---

The people.

There are not many of them for so much stone. The ward is dense in its construction but thin in its occupation; whole tiers stand empty, kept dark, used for storage of things that cannot tolerate moisture or that simply no one wants. You can walk a hundred paces along a gallery in the upper works and pass only one or two closed doors, each with its own small lamp burning. The lamp-fuel is rationed. The doors are never opened when the man inside is home.

But when you reach a market-tier, or the well-court on tier four, the density is suddenly near-overwhelming. Bodies. Voices. The press of warm shoulders in a corridor. The smell of cooking-oil and of the bright, peppery steam from the noodle-vats that the eastern families keep in their lower rooms. Someone is always playing a long, slow tune on a horn that has only three notes, and the tune goes on for as long as the player has breath, which is considerable.

The ward is loud in pockets and silent between them. A man walking from one tier to the next can pass from a market that fills his ears to a stairwell where he hears only his own boots and the faint, constant, breathing from below.

---

There are animals. Not many.

Six-legged things like small dogs, kept in pens in the lower tiers, fed on kitchen-waste and on the pellets that fall out of the sieves at the mill. They are eaten in winter. They make a soft chittering noise when they sleep.

Birds, sometimes, in the upper air. The ward is open at the top, the inner face of the cone opening out to a circle of hot sky, and a bird that loses its way in a storm will sometimes spiral down into it and not be able to climb out. People rescue them when they can. They are considered a small omen.

The pale blooms in the shaded cracks are not animals, and the town has not decided what they are. They do not seem to die. They do not seem to reproduce. There are roughly the same number of them as there were a hundred years ago, although the people are not sure, because no one has counted.

---

Night comes fast and is colder than you'd expect. The rock that was warm all day gives up its heat grudgingly, and the upper air, when it finally moves, moves downward into the ward and settles among the tiers and chills the highest dwellings first. The foundry never stops, and so the lowest tier stays warm all night. People migrate downward in the cold hours, sleeping in shifts in the lower halls, near the engine, near the warmth.

In the deepest hour, before dawn, you can hear it if you stand very still at the lowest gallery: a tick. A slow tick, from somewhere below the foundations. Some say it is the engine cooling. Some say it is the rock itself, settling, the slow working of the deep heat against the deep cold. The town is younger than the rock, and the rock has not finished accommodating it. There is always some small adjustment. A door that does not close quite the way it used to. A pipe that has to be re-seated every few years. The founders' drawings note these things and account for them.

In the morning, the heat returns. The walls warm. The pale blooms in the cracks drink the dew that has gathered overnight. The shift-workers climb back up the long switchbacks to their tiers, and the day resumes.

The engine hums. The town breathes. The cone holds it all, patient as geology, patient as the rock that has not finished becoming what it will be.
