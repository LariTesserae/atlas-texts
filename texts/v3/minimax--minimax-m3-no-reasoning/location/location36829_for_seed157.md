# The Weeping Hollow

It is a single sinkhole in the shoulder of a mountain that no one has named, because naming requires return, and no one returns from this place with the desire to come back, only the inability to forget it. The opening above is perhaps forty feet across, irregular, choked with the roots of a single stunted fir that leans in over the lip and drops a few pale needles each year into the dark below. Beyond that lip the light does not really arrive. What little comes in has been filtered so many times through cloud, through canopy, through the slow descent down wet stone, that by the time it touches the floor of the hollow it is more a quality of air than a quantity — a dimness you feel rather than see.

The hollow is cathedral-scale, though not in the soaring way of a built thing. It is cathedral-scale in the way a throat is: narrow at the top, opening downward, walled all around in dark stone that has been wetted without cease for longer than any of the small lives here have been keeping time. The rock is a dark basalt, or something near it — black when dry, which it never is. Every surface weeps. There is no rain in the hollow proper; the rain falls somewhere above, somewhere outside, and by the time its memory reaches these walls it has become seepage, condensation, the slow patient gravity of water finding its way down through a thousand hairline fractures. The stone glistens. The stone is always glistening.

And on the stone, packed so densely there is no bare rock left anywhere, the vegetation has laid hold. Mosses, of course — a dozen kinds, deep green and bottle green and the almost-black green of things that have forgotten what light is for. Liverworts pressed flat like wet paper. A film of lichen so thin it is indistinguishable from a discoloration of the rock itself. Ferns — these are the largest presences, perhaps two feet tall, their fronds curled tight against the cool air, uncurling only in the brief hours when some accident of weather sends a brighter draft down from above. Clubmosses. A single clump of what might be a saxifrage, blooming with five white flowers no larger than a fingernail, though it blooms in no particular season and has been blooming, it seems, for a very long time.

The air is the temperature of held water. Cool the way a cellar is cool — not cold, not sharp, but a damp coolness that sits on the skin and will not leave. Breath comes out in faint threads. The walls are slick and the floor is a kind of wet rubble, a scree of small dark stones pressed into dark mud, all of it colonized. There is the smell of wet stone, of leaf-rot, of something faintly mineral, of something faintly sweet — the smell of so much living tissue breathing out its slow exhalation into a space that never quite empties.

The fauna is here. The fauna is everywhere. This is the first thing one notices once the eyes adjust, and then it is the only thing one notices, because there is no surface, no crevice, no square inch of moss or stone or root that does not hold something living.

On the floor — the wet dark floor — salamanders. They are small, dark-backed, with the pale bellies of creatures that spend their lives on the underside of stones. They lie in the moss and on the stones and under the stones and they are so numerous that to step would be to tread on something. They are not moving. Most of them are not moving. A few, far fewer than the eye expects, shift their weight occasionally, blink, slide one wet inch to the left or right, and settle again. They are not sleeping; they are simply *here*, in the way that the stones are here, in the way the water is here. They have always been here. They will be here.

On the walls, the ferns, the hanging roots — frogs. Smaller frogs, almost the color of the moss, almost the color of the wet stone. They cling with their pale toes and they do not leap. Perhaps one in a hundred is in the act of leaping — caught mid-leap, mouth open, hind legs extended, and then landing softly somewhere in the mass and going still again. Their throats pulse. That is the only motion.

In the cracks of the upper walls, where the rock meets the leaning roots of the fir, the bats. They hang folded into the crevices so tightly that they look like a part of the stone — like knots in wood, like bulbs in bark. There must be hundreds of them. They are not flying. It is not their hour. It is never their hour. They hang in the dark and they wait, and the dark does not change, and so they wait some more.

In the moss, and under the moss, and through the moss — worms, springtails, mites, beetles no bigger than grains of rice, all of them in the same arrested industry, all of them doing what they do at a scale too small for the eye to track. A beetle climbs a fern stem and stops. A worm withdraws into its burrow and does not emerge. A springtail is poised on a droplet and the droplet holds it. Everything is packed. Everything is in its place.

And so, mostly, it is quiet.

What sound there is comes from the water. A drip from a frond tip onto a stone. A trickle along a fissure. The faint, almost-musical ticking of condensation forming and falling somewhere above. Once in a long while, a frog — one of the hundred — shifts and produces a small wet *cluck*. Once in a longer while, a salamander, disturbed by nothing, opens its mouth and the sound is less than a sigh. The bats are silent. The insects are silent. The whole packed, overwhelmed hollow hums at the bottom of hearing, the way a held shell hums, and that hum is the sound of so many small bodies breathing in the same damp air.

There are tools.

This is the strangest thing, though one would not notice it for a long time. Wedged into the mud at the base of one wall, half-covered in moss, is a flat stone with a shallow depression worn into its upper surface — the bowl of it stained darker than the rest. Beside it, also in the mud, partly grown over by a liverwort, are three flakes of chert, or something like chert. They are sharp. They are old. They have not been used in a very long time, or they have been used continuously and so slowly that the wear is invisible. Nearby, half-buried, the broken bone of something — a handle? a wedge? — and a length of wood that might have been a shaft once, that is now more peat than wood.

No one made a camp here. No one built a fire. There are no postholes, no cleared ground, no arrangement. The tools are simply *in* the hollow, the way the salamanders are in the hollow, the way the drips are in the hollow. They are part of the stasis. They have been absorbed into the equilibrium. The moss has grown over them in such a way that to remove them would be to injure the moss. Perhaps they were left here, long ago, by something that came down through the narrow opening and found the place already full and set its things down and went away again. Perhaps they fell. Perhaps they are not tools at all and only look like tools to eyes that expect tools.

The water continues to weep. The moss continues to hold. The salamanders continue. The light, what there is of it, does not change — it is always the same hour of an overcast day, the same grey almost-dark, the same damp and sourceless dim. The frogs do not breed, or if they breed, the young are already here, already still, already packed into the corners. The bats do not fly out, or if they fly out, they fly out and return in the same night, to the same crack, and the night is indistinguishable from the day. The drips fall and the moss catches them and the drips fall again.

It is a place in balance. Not the balance of a held thing — a balance of a settled thing. A thing that has found its form and has been finding its form, without disturbance, for a very long time. The hollow is not waiting for anything. It is not decaying. It is not growing. It is simply *being*, the way the stone is being, the way the salamanders are being, with the same unhurried patience, in the same dim wet air, under the same narrow opening through which the world above is glimpsed but does not enter.

A fir needle, occasionally, falls. That is the only event.
