# The Cane Yard

A square of low sky, framed. The walls around it are not walls but the inner faces of a living perimeter — a fence of bamboo, sixty feet tall and as old as anything here, that grew from a single experimental planting in some year the landscape has forgotten. Each culm is the diameter of a man's chest, their nodes swollen like knuckles, their skin the color of polished bone, and they press so close together that a child could not pass between them. Where one culm has died it has not been removed; it has been absorbed. The living bamboo has grown over the dead, around the dead, fused with the dead, so that the perimeter is one continuous organism of wood and hollow, the dead feeding the living the way dead leaves feed a tree.

Inside this square, the air is cool and damp and tastes of chlorophyll and the iron tang of old rain. The sky above is a small, flat, pearl-colored disc — the bamboo holds the clouds at a distance, or perhaps the clouds are simply uninterested in what happens here. Light falls in long green columns, diffused through the canopy sixty feet up, where the culms lean together and interlock into a vault. The light that reaches the floor is not light exactly. It is more like a green memory of light, the kind of illumination that does not cast shadows so much as thicken the air.

The floor is concrete. It is the only floor that has ever been here. Poured in some long-ago spring, it has cracked since into irregular polygons, and from every crack has come the bamboo's revenge: a thicket of shoots, each as thick as a wrist, that have been left to grow because no one has ever come to cut them. Some of these shoots are twenty feet tall now, still slender, still the bright green of new wood, leaning at the angles their cracks taught them. They make the concrete look like a dry seabed from which a strange kelp has risen. Between the shoots, you can still see the original floor, gray and permanent, and the painted lines — white, faded to the color of old teeth — of the old yard's geometry. A center line. A circle at the midpoint. Numbers. A loading bay marked in letters two feet high, the words themselves unreadable now because the bamboo has eaten the middle of each one.

This is the place. The factory floor that has become a forest floor, a building that has become an organism, a place that was made and has been unmade and is in the slow process of being made again into something that does not have a name.

The bamboo is ancient. The bamboo is the oldest thing here. The concrete is younger than the bamboo now, in the patient arithmetic of such places. The bamboo remembers when the floor was new. The bamboo remembers when the machines were here, though the machines are gone.

The machines. What remains of them is in the corners of the yard, in the bays where the loading doors were bricked up decades ago. There are three of them, or the ghosts of three of them, or perhaps they are still partly themselves. They were looms, or they were presses, or they were something for which there is no longer a word. They are large. Each one fills a bay, each one is forty feet long, each one was painted a color that has now been eaten down to bare metal and then to orange and then to a black that is the color of old blood. They have not moved in living memory. They are bolted to the concrete. They are bolted to the concrete that the bamboo has not yet reached. Around their bases the shoots grow thick and strange, the culms bending in toward the machines as if curious, as if the machines are a kind of weather.

The machines still make a sound. This is the strangest thing about the place. In the deep of the cool damp air, among the creak of the bamboo and the small constant dripping of condensation from the vault sixty feet up, the machines hum. Not loudly. They hum the way a transformer hums on a hot day, the way a refrigerator hums in a kitchen at night. They have not been plugged in for longer than anyone can say. The sound is residual, residual in the metal, residual in the foundations, the sound that a machine makes when it has been making the same sound for so long that the sound has become a property of the metal, like the color of the metal, like the weight of it. The loom, the press, the unnamed thing, they hum. The hum is a single note, somewhere below the range of conversation, and it fills the yard the way a held chord fills a cathedral. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. The bamboo has grown around it. The concrete has not cracked against it. The machines and the bamboo have come to a kind of agreement, the way two old neighbors come to an agreement about the property line: the machines stay where they are, the bamboo grows where it wants, the hum continues.

The yard is loud with the hum. The yard is loud with the small percussion of condensation falling from culm to culm, the slow tick of water gathering weight and letting go, sixty feet up, a constant soft rain of distilled water that never reaches the floor because the canopy catches it and it runs down the culms and gathers at the nodes and falls again. The yard is loud with the creak of the bamboo as it sways, but the bamboo does not sway, the bamboo is in equilibrium, the bamboo is in its final form, the bamboo has reached the age when it no longer moves in the wind. The creak is the creak of the bamboo growing. You can hear it if you stand in the right place. A low groan, the sound a ship makes in a harbor. The bamboo is still growing, very slowly, thickening at the base, the culms pressing against each other with a force that is patient and absolute.

There is no wind. There is no sun. There is no rain except the internal rain of the condensation. The yard is its own climate. The yard has been its own climate since the doors were bricked up and the roof was grown over and the ventilation was sealed with living wood. The temperature is the temperature of a cellar in late autumn. The humidity is the humidity of a greenhouse. The air moves only in the slow circulation of the canopy's breath, the rising of warm wet air from the concrete, the falling of cool dry air from the vault. The yard is its own weather.

The animals are here. They came in through the gaps, or they were always here, or they were brought in when the machines were brought in and they stayed when the machines were left. The animals are pigeons, the kind of pigeon that lives in machine sheds and railway stations, the fat gray pigeons with the iridescent necks. There are hundreds of them. They roost in the canopy, in the crooks of the culms, and they come down in the morning to walk the concrete between the bamboo shoots, bobbing their heads, cooing in the particular pigeon way that sounds like a conversation held just out of earshot. The pigeons own the floor. The pigeons are the inheritors of the yard. They eat the bamboo seeds, the few that the bamboo throws down in late summer, and they drink the condensation from the leaves, and they nest in the eaves of the bays where the machines are. Their droppings are the only fertilizer the bamboo needs. The bamboo grows the leaves. The leaves make the condensation. The condensation makes the water. The water is for the pigeons and the bamboo. The cycle is complete. The pigeons are part of the machine.

There are also, in the upper canopy, things that move faster. Lizards, perhaps, or the small green frogs that live in bamboo forests where there are no longer any bamboo forests. They move in the culm crooks. They are not often seen. They are heard as a small rustle, a small shift, a small correction in the background hum. There are spiders, the large ones, the ones that build the wheel-shaped webs that span the gaps between culms, and the webs are everywhere in the upper canopy, layered, the new ones built across the old ones, the old ones sagging with the weight of what they have caught. The flies are caught in the webs. The flies are the food for the spiders. The spiders are the food for the lizards. The lizards are the food for whatever comes next, and there is nothing that comes next, and so the lizards live a long time.

There is no one here. The yard has not been a yard in the working sense for longer than the bamboo has been a fence. The last workers, if there were last workers, are a memory the concrete holds and does not give up. The painted lines on the floor are their signatures. The numbers are their counting. The circle at the midpoint is where something stood, or where someone stood to watch something else. The workers are gone. The machines remain. The bamboo has come.

A person who came here, if a person could come here, would find the gate. The gate is a gap in the bamboo wall where two culms have died at the same time and the gap has not yet been filled. The gate is on the north side, or the side that was north when there were directions. The gate is narrow. The gate is the width of a person sideways, the way cattle gaps are. Beyond the gate is another yard, or the edge of another yard, and beyond that the next bamboo fence, and beyond that the next, and the next, and the next, the whole of the old industrial estate given over to the cane, the whole of the district now a forest of bamboo, the whole of the city in time, perhaps, a single organism, a single grove, a single slow thought.

But inside the gate, inside this yard, the light is green. The sound is the hum. The smell is the iron and the chlorophyll. The floor is the concrete and the shoots. The machines are humming their single note. The pigeons are walking. The bamboo is growing.

The bamboo is always growing.

The bamboo has been growing for longer than the machines have been humming.

The machines and the bamboo have come to an agreement.
