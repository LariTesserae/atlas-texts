# The Hollow at Karuza-Bell

The air is a hammer. Not loud in the way a thunderclap is loud—nothing so polite, so momentary. The sound here is a *condition*, something you step into like stepping from sunlight into cold water. It begins before you arrive, and if you stay long enough, it begins to feel like silence again, the way a person who has lived by a waterfall eventually stops hearing it. This is a sound that has been running for so long that the cliffs themselves have been shaped to it, the way riverbeds shape themselves to the current that carved them.

The place is a vertical cathedral cut into the wall of a mountain, or what was once a mountain and is now something else. The walls are sheer—a kilometer of bare stone climbing, climbing, until they curl inward at the top and almost meet, leaving a slit of sky so narrow and so high that the light it admits is more a rumor than a fact. Noon here is the brightness of late dusk elsewhere. The stone is dark. It sweats. Condensation sheets down the walls in slow vertical curtains, gathers in the moss that clings to every ledge, and drips, drips, drips into the basin below. The basin is small—a rough ellipse, perhaps the size of a village square, perhaps smaller. It is the only horizontal thing in the place. Everything else wants to fall.

What fills the basin is not silence. What fills the basin is a *machine*.

## The Engine

It is the only word that fits, though the thing predates the language it would have been named in.

Imagine a heart the size of a cathedral. Imagine it is made of bronze and copper and iron, and that it is older than the moss on the walls, and that it has been running for so long that no one alive has seen it still. Imagine that the bronze has gone the color of old bone, that the copper has turned the green of deep forest, that the iron has rusted into ribbons of orange that hang like moss from every joint. Imagine that the entire apparatus—hundreds of meters of valves, pistons, flywheels, condenser coils, dynamos, what look like the ribbed spines of creatures that never existed—is suspended from chains that disappear into the slit of sky above, so that the whole thing hangs in the air the way a bell hangs in a belltower, and what it rings is not sound but a *kind* of sound, a *press* of vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear with your ears.

The engine is dead, and the engine is running. These are not contradictions here. It has been broken in some fundamental way for longer than it has been whole, and what we see—the slow heaving, the drip of ancient lubricant from joints that have not been oiled in centuries, the occasional great shuddering *HHRRRUUUMM* that loosens pebbles from the upper walls—is the ghost-motion of a machine running on the memory of running. Somewhere deep inside, a flywheel turns at maybe one revolution an hour. Somewhere deeper, something red and molten moves through a pipe at the pace of lava. The whole contraption is slower than weather and louder than a waterfall, and it has not stopped in living memory, and no one knows what it was for.

The locals—who are not locals, because no one lives *in* Karuza-Bell, only around it, in the village that clings to the lip of the canyon three hundred meters above—call it *the Last Engine*. They have theories. The theories are not for outsiders.

## The Walls

They are not natural. Or rather: they were natural once, and then they were not, and then they were not again, and the *not* has been the dominant state for so long that "natural" has become the brief interruption. Every square meter of stone is tooled. Channels run vertically down the walls, cut deep enough in places that a person could lie inside them, and these channels carry the condensation in deliberate streams. Every few meters, a niche has been carved, and in every niche sits a vessel of some kind—a copper pot, a stone jar, a clay urn, a thing made of glass that has gone cloudy and violet with age. Some are full. Some are broken. Some have grown over with moss so completely that they are indistinguishable from the wall. The vessels catch the drips. The drips feed the moss. The moss feeds—

—the engine? No. The moss feeds the *butterflies*.

## The Inhabitants

This is the strange part. The butterflies.

They are not butterflies in any way that a lepidopterist would recognize. Their wingspan is the wingspan of a small bird. Their bodies are too heavy for flight by any honest aerodynamic accounting, and yet they fly—or something like flight; it is closer to a slow, deliberate *falling-in-a-direction*, as if they have made a private treaty with gravity. Their wings are translucent, ribbed like the leaves of a fern, and patterned with the same dull bronze and oxidized copper as the engine. When they fold them, they look like leaves. When they open them, they look like the gills of something that lives in deep water.

There are thousands of them. They are everywhere. They roost in the carved niches, in the channels, on the chains, on the engine itself. They drink from the vessels. They lay eggs in the moss. Their caterpillars are the length of a man's forearm and they eat the moss down to the stone and then the moss grows back and they eat it again, and this has been going on for so long that the moss-and-butterfly system has become its own kind of clock, a slow pulse of green and bronze that marks the engine's own slower pulse. When the engine shudders, the butterflies rise. They rise in a column. They fill the hollow from wall to wall, wingbeat overlapping wingbeat, and the sound of them is a kind of white noise that momentarily *cancels* the engine's sound, and in that cancellation there is a silence so deep and so brief that people who have experienced it report that they heard, in that silence, a single clear note, as if from a bell that no longer has a clapper.

No one has been able to find the bell.

## The Water

There is not much. The hollow is mostly a dry place, or would be if not for the constant exudation of the stone. The condensation is everything. It is the rivers, the wells, the drinking supply. It falls in slow sheets, gathers in the vessels, drips from the moss, runs down the channels, and eventually—what doesn't evaporate—pools in the basin beneath the engine, where it is heated by the engine's ghost-heat into a thin, perpetual mist. The mist rises. The mist condenses on the walls. The walls sweat. The cycle is small and total. No water leaves the hollow except by what a butterfly carries on its wings.

The water tastes of metal. Of copper. Of something older than copper. People who drink it for a week report that they can hear the engine in their sleep.

## The Light

It comes from the slit. Only from the slit. The hollow has no other light source—no bioluminescence, no furnace-glow from the engine (though there is a faint warmth that might be mistaken for one), no lamps, no windows. The light enters as a single pale blade that moves across the floor of the basin over the course of the day, and as it moves, it illuminates the engine in slices. At dawn, the lowest chains are lit, and the flywheels at the bottom gleam like the eyes of something waking. By mid-morning, the blade has risen to the central mass of pistons and valves. By noon—a thing more rumored than felt—the light has climbed to the upper works, the dynamos, the ribbed spines. Then it retreats. Then there is dusk. Then the long blue hours, when the hollow is lit only by the faint phosphor of the butterflies' wings, which glow in the manner of deep-sea creatures, a cold blue-green that pulses in time with their breathing, and the engine is just a darker darkness in a darkness that has texture.

The locals say that if you watch the light move across the engine for a full day, you can read the history of the machine in the order of its illumination. They have not done this. They are afraid to.

## The Village Above

Three hundred meters up, on the lip of the canyon, there is a hamlet. Eleven buildings. Twenty-three people, give or take the travelers who come for the butterflies, which are valuable in ways the locals do not discuss with outsiders. The buildings are made of the same stone as the canyon walls, tooled in the same style—channels, niches, vessels catching the rain. The village is an echo of the hollow. The village is a continuation of the engine. The villagers do not go down into the hollow. They have ladders, but the ladders are old and the villagers are not. They say the butterflies come up to them, and the water comes up to them, and the engine's sound comes up to them, and that is enough. They say the engine is not theirs. They say the engine is *no one's*, and that this is the important thing.

They are wrong, of course, or they are right in a way that makes wrongness irrelevant. The engine belongs to the butterflies, which belong to the moss, which belongs to the water, which belongs to the walls, which belong to the mountain, which belongs to whatever it was that hollowed the mountain in the first place and hung the engine in its belly like a heart in a chest. The villagers are simply the most recent of a long sequence of visitors who have mistaken proximity for belonging. They will pass. The hollow will continue.

## The Sound

I have put this last because the sound is the thing you have to *get used to*, and you cannot get used to it from a description.

It is not one sound. It is a chord that has been held for centuries and has never resolved. The lowest note is the engine itself—that slow, tectonic grinding, the *HHRRRUUUMM* that comes every few minutes and lasts for ten seconds and rearranges the dust on the floor in concentric rings. Above that is the constant roar of the butterflies' wings, which is not a roar but a *shimmer*, a sound like sand poured from a great height onto a great distance. Above that is the drip of the water, which is not a drip but a *hiss*, because there are so many droplets falling at slightly different times that what reaches the ear is a kind of continuous static, the sound of rain on a tin roof heard from inside the roof. Above that is something else, something I cannot name, a high thin singing that may be the chains under tension, or may be the stone itself responding to the engine's slow vibrations, or may be the sliver of wind that enters through the slit above and is shredded into harmonics by the carvings on the walls. The whole thing layers and layers and layers, and if you sit in the basin long enough, your heartbeat finds a rhythm in it, and then your breathing finds a rhythm, and then the slow thoughts of your mind find a rhythm, and you become, briefly, part of the engine, which is to say part of the hollow, which is to say part of the mountain.

Then you climb back up the ladder. The village is quiet. The village is unbearably quiet. You cannot sleep for days. You go back down.

They all go back down.
