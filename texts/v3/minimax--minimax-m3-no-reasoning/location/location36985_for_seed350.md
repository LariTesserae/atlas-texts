The water is the first thing, and after that everything else is in it.

It comes up to a level that doesn't quite reach the surface of the world, or it does, or it doesn't — the surface keeps being a problem. There is a haze above the water that may be fog, may be fine rain, may be the water itself deciding to lift off and become a thicker thing. The light comes through this haze in a flat, sourceless wash, the color of weak tea held up to an overcast window, and the result is that nothing has a shadow. Everything is rendered in the same dull jade, the same tarnished brass, the same algae-stained cream. The air is warm and tastes of brine and iron and a kind of green rot that hasn't quite committed to rotting. The water is warm. You can feel it working, even from above, the way a body of water is felt through the air above it. It moves in slow eddies around the bases of the things that stick up out of it — pipes, posts, the corners of half-submerged buildings. The water has been here long enough to be familiar with these things, but not so long that it has worn them down. The edges are still sharp. The rust is fresh in places, red as a wound. The concrete still has its rough grain. This is recent. This happened, and the happening is still in the room.

The sound comes next, or first, or always. It is the most solid thing here. It presses against you, and it does not let up. It is the roar of water moving through pipes, the high whine of turbines that have not been turned off, the slow groan of metal structures that were not built to be what they are now built to be. There is a clatter that might be conveyor belts running empty. There is a hiss that might be steam, although the water is warm enough that there should be no steam. There is a low, constant rumble, and on top of that a higher, keening note, and on top of that a kind of white-noise roar that has no source, that seems to come from the water itself, or from the air, or from the way the world is holding itself together here. The sound is deafening but it is also the only way to know the world is real. Without it the place would be a still life. With it, the place is a machinery. The place is an engine that has not been told to stop.

The machines are scattered. There is no pattern to them. A pump here, a length of pipe there, a tank, a stack of drums, a building that is more pipe than wall. Some of them are recognizable — that is a boiler, that is a gearbox, that is the spine of a conveyor. Some of them are not recognizable, or are recognizable in the way that a face is recognizable when the lighting is bad. They are industrial, scaled up and scaled down and scaled to nothing in particular, scaled to fit a place that has not been measured. They are running. They have been running. They are running.

The water goes through them. The pipes have cracked, or the pumps are over-pressurizing, or the water has simply found the inside of the machinery and made itself at home. Water comes out of places water should not come out of. Water goes in through seams that were not made for water. The result is a kind of symbiosis that the original engineers did not design and would not have approved of. The machines and the water have agreed to be each other's problems. The machines keep cycling. The water keeps moving. The sound they make together is most of what you hear.

The structures are the partial remains of something. Walls, mostly, in the early stages of going under. The roofs are still above the water on some of them, and on some of them the roofs are not. The windows are dark, or are full of water, or are full of the same dim haze as the air. There is a faint smell of paint somewhere and a sharper smell of fuel in one of them. They are the bones of a town, or of a single town, or of a district, and the town is being taken into the water without ceremony. Foundations that were not meant to hold water are holding water. Basements are full to the brim. The water comes up to the sills and stops, or doesn't stop. The buildings tilt a little, in some cases, because the ground beneath them is soft and the ground is being undermined by water and by roots. The buildings are not falling down. They are being absorbed. They are being made into a different kind of building, a building in which the floor is two feet below the surface of a warm jade sea, and the walls hold the water in instead of out.

The vegetation is what the place has done with itself in the time since the water came. It is lush, and fast, and indeterminate. It grows on everything. It grows on the pipes and along the pipes and through the pipes. It grows on the buildings, up the walls, in through the windows, out again, around the corners, up onto the roofs. It grows in the water, in long strands and floating mats, in dense clumps around the bases of the things that stick up. It grows on the machines, in ways that make the machines look as if they are sprouting. The vegetation is a kind of green and a kind of brown and a kind of black, and it has a sheen to it, a waxy wet look, and it smells green — the smell of a thing that is growing, the smell of a thing that is in a hurry to be here.

The vegetation is not what you'd expect. It has the texture of plant, the slow patience of plant, but it also has a kind of speed. It moves faster than it should. It is in the act of growing. It is in the act of being here. It is not the kind of vegetation that has been here a long time. It is the kind that has been here a short time and means to be here a long time. It will be here when the machines have stopped, if they ever stop. It is more patient than the machines. It is less patient than the water. It is in between.

The rules of the place begin to make themselves known.

The water, for instance, has not finished deciding what to do. It goes up. It goes down. It flows in directions that do not quite make sense — into a pipe that is supposed to flow out, out of a pipe that is supposed to flow in, over a wall that is supposed to contain it, under a wall that is supposed to keep it out. The water is not finished. It is still in the act of becoming the place. The place is still in the act of becoming underwater. The water has not decided if it is a flood, or a sea, or a permanent condition, or a temporary one. It is doing all of these at once.

Time is also unfinished. The buildings are being built and unbuilt. A wall goes up, brick by brick, while another wall, identical to it, comes down, brick by brick, into the water. The machinery is running forward and backward. The conveyor belt moves things to the left and, at the same time, to the right, and the things on the belt — a kind of shapeless material, perhaps ore, perhaps a kind of broken plant, perhaps a kind of broken building — are getting simultaneously more and less themselves. The vegetation is growing and dying in the same motion. A shoot puts out a leaf; the leaf withers; the shoot puts out another leaf; the leaf withers; this is one plant, or many plants, or a single plant experiencing all its seasons at once. The sound is the sound of a place that is being both demolished and constructed, that is both rising and sinking, that is both starting and ending.

The light does not help. The light is dim, sourceless, the same color from every direction. It does not indicate the time of day, because there is no time of day. It does not indicate the weather, because there is no weather, or there is only weather, and the weather is the same. The light is the light of an industrial interior, a workshop, a mill. It is the light of a thing that is being made, in a room without windows, in a place without sun.

There are no animals. The place has not allowed for them. There are machines, and plants, and water, and buildings, and a kind of vague protoplasmic matter that has not decided what it is, and the sound. There is no room, in the rules of this place, for an animal. An animal would imply a watcher, a hunter, a thing that moves with intent, a thing that eats, a thing that dies of something other than dissolution. An animal would imply a chain of being that the place has decided to do without. The machines, the plants, the water, the structures — they are all in their own way alive, and they are all in their own way not. They are a single substance in different states. The animal is a substance the place has no use for. An animal would be a witness, and the place is not yet finished, and the place does not wish to be witnessed.

The sound continues. It is the only thing that is not changing, or the only thing that is changing slowly enough to seem unchanged. The roar of water. The whine of turbines. The clatter of empty conveyor belts. The hiss of steam in a place that is too warm for steam. The low groan of metal, the high whine of stressed bolts, the deep thrum of engines that are running and have always been running and will always be running. The sound is the heartbeat of the place. It is the only way to know that the place is somewhere. It is also, perhaps, the way the place knows that it is being heard.

The place is dense. There is a lot of it in a small area. There is a lot of it in a large area. It extends past the immediate, past the reach of a hand, past the reach of an eye, into a haze that is the same color as the place itself, so that the place does not have an edge. The place has features that go on. A pipe that emerges from the water here goes on under the water there, emerges again, goes on. A building that is half-submerged here is fully submerged a little further on. A stand of vegetation that is waist-high here is head-high there, and trees-high somewhere else, and somewhere else it has become something that is not quite a tree and not quite a pipe and not quite a wall, but has the properties of all three. The place is a continuum. The place is one substance in many forms.

The reality of the place is the place itself. The place is what it is. It is a low-lying region being made into something else. It is a region of warm water, dim light, deafening sound, scattered machinery, and fast vegetation. It is a region in which the boundaries between things are not firm. The water is in the metal. The metal is in the water. The plants are in the metal. The metal is in the plants. The buildings are in the water. The water is in the buildings. The sound is in the water. The water is in the sound. The light is in the water. The water is in the light. The place has not decided what to be, and is being many things, and is in the act of being all of them, and is deafeningly loud about it.

It goes on. It is going on. It will be going on. It is the act of being made, and the act is loud, and warm, and dim, and dense, and full of vegetation, and full of machines, and full of water, and full of itself, and there is no animal in it, and there is no quiet in it, and there is no end in it, and there is no edge to it, and it is being made right now, and it is being made of itself, and the sound of it is the sound of the place being the place.
