# The Deepstack

The city does not sit on its substrate. The substrate sits inside the city. That is the first thing to understand, and even after fifty years inside it, Marn-8 still found herself correcting the orientation in her head when she woke.

The floor of her quarters was a tectonic plate. A small one — only a few kilometers on a side — but genuine, with its own granitic composition, its own internal heat, its own imperceptible drift. It was one of perhaps twenty thousand such plates that comprised the horizontal plane of the city, packed together edge to edge like scales, each one a fragment of some older, simpler geology. Beneath the plates there was no land. Beneath the plates there was only water, and beneath the water there were the vents, and beneath the vents there was the city's reason for being here at all.

She stood up. Her boots found the floor with the small familiar click of the magnetic plates in their soles engaging. Outside the window — and she had to use the word *outside* deliberately, because the window faced inward, into the gap between her plate and the next — the gap was three meters wide and fell, by the local geometry, into the sky.

She had grown up in the upper stacks. She had not been down here before.

---

The Deepstack was a city built on water that was built on heat that was built on something else.

The first humans — if they had been humans; the histories were ambiguous on this point — had found the warm ocean of Kelth-3 and understood, almost immediately, that it had no floor. The crust was thin. The mantle was close. There was a kind of permanent thermal spring across the entire planetary surface, a planetary condition rather than a feature, and the water above it was, accordingly, *warm*, the way a bathtub is warm: not hot, not cold, just everywhere and always the same blood-temperature.

They could not build on the water.

They built on the heat instead.

The vents were the substrate. Each vent was a chimney of precipitated minerals, basaltic and silica-laden, rising from the seabed to a meter or two above the waterline. They grew. They had been growing for geological time. They grew fast enough that the original builders had seen opportunity: the vents were already a forest of stone, a saturated, packed, mineral world waiting for someone to climb on it.

Someone had climbed.

Now, after however many centuries, the chimneys were kilometers tall. They had been fused, bricked, mortared, welded, and grown into one another until the gaps between them had become streets, and the streets had become districts, and the districts had become the Deepstack. The city was, formally, a single continuous structure that rose from the seabed and continued upward for seven vertical kilometers, occupying the entire space between ocean floor and low stratosphere. Its horizontal extent — measured along the curving surface of the planet — was continental.

It rained here. That was the strange part.

Rain, in the conventional sense, fell from above. In the Deepstack, rain fell from *within*. The thermal spring was not just heat: it was evaporation. The warm ocean gave off water vapor in enormous quantities, and the vapor rose, and as it rose through the layered, packed city it condensed on every available surface — on the underside of the plate districts, on the inward-facing walls of the chimneys, on the bridges and struts and the bodies of the inhabitants themselves. There was no dry surface anywhere in the Deepstack. There was only the constant slow descent of distilled water from above, and the constant slow ascent of new vapor from below.

The result was a city that was perpetually weeping. Every horizontal surface was wet. Every vertical surface wept. The undersides of the upper plates were slimed with condensation that gathered into beads and fell as a fine, steady, cold rain. The bridges that spanned the gaps between plates were crossed in weather that was, locally, indistinguishable from a storm. The inhabitants wore slickers, or had skin adapted for it, or simply didn't care.

The *sound* — and the sound was the second thing to understand, the second thing Marn-8 had to learn in the lower stacks — was deafening.

Every drop of water that fell, fell onto another surface, and every surface gave up its heat to the new water, and every new water, in falling, displaced air, and the displaced air moved through the narrow packed corridors of the city at speeds that produced a continuous roar. It was the sound of a river, but it was not coming from a river. It was the sound of the city itself, breathing, in and out, the slow respiration of a continental organism inhaling vapor and exhaling rain. In the upper stacks, where the gaps between plates were wider and the rain had time to fall freely before hitting something, it was a steady drumming, almost musical. In the lower stacks — where Marn-8 was now, where the chimneys were young and close together and the architecture was dense — the roar was a wall, a physical presence, a thing you leaned against when you walked.

You had to communicate by gesture, or by the small bone-conduction transducers that every Deepstack citizen wore in the mastoid bone behind the ear. The transducer received a coded vibration and translated it into a sensation that the auditory cortex interpreted as speech. You thought your words, and the other person heard them, inside their skull, in your voice.

It was like thinking together.

---

The plates that made up the upper floors of the Deepstack were not, despite their appearance, geological in origin. They had been *grown*. There was a material that the Deepstack called *worked basalt*, which had been the enabling technology of everything that had come after, and which current physics did not entirely explain. It was stone, but it was stone that had been persuaded, at the molecular level, to do things stone did not do. It was denser than steel and lighter than balsa. It was warm to the touch when you wanted it warm, cool when you wanted it cool. It accepted a load without deforming, and it accepted a voltage without resisting, and it had a faint grain that you could see if you held a piece of it up to the light, like wood, as if it remembered having once been alive.

It had been alive. That was the theory. That the builders — the first builders, whoever they had been — had found the vents already warm, already wet, already saturated with the chemistry of something that was almost life, and had *finished the job*. Had taught the stone to grow.

The Deepstack was, in this reading, a crop.

Marn-8 did not know what to make of this. She worked in maintenance. Her job, on the lower stacks, was to inspect the seams between plates, and the seams between chimneys, and the various small interstices where the worked basalt met its own kind or met the older natural basalt of the original vents. The seams wept more than the rest of the city. They were where the rain first condensed, where the water first pooled, where the small animals of the lower stacks came to drink.

---

The animals.

This was the third thing she had had to learn.

In the upper stacks, the fauna were the conventional fauna of warm wet cities anywhere: insects, birds, the occasional rat-sized vertebrate. In the lower stacks, where the thermal vents were still active, where the water at street level was lukewarm and the air was thick with vapor, the fauna were different.

They were not, strictly, animals.

They were the chimneys' own patients. They were the fauna that had evolved, or been grown, or had simply appeared, in the particular chemistry of warm stone and warm water and constant wet. They were small — the largest was the size of a thumb — and they were abundant, and they were, in their fashion, busy. They moved through the seams. They fed on the constant rain of organic matter that descended from the upper stacks. They did things to the basalt that were not fully understood: they grazed it, they broke it down, they processed it, they excreted a finer basalt that the worked-stone growers could re-use.

They were the city's small intestine.

They were deaf, like her, in the roar.

---

Marn-8 finished her morning check. The plate-edge she had been assigned was within tolerance: the seam was weeping at the expected rate, the basalt was warm but not overheating, the condensation was draining properly into the channel that carried it down to the next level. She stood and looked out across the gap.

Across the gap was the next plate. Across the gap was the next kilometer of city, rising into the falling rain. Across the gap were more chimneys, more plates, more gaps, more chimneys, more plates, packed in every direction until the curvature of the planet intervened and the city dissolved into haze.

The haze was permanent. The rain fell through it. The sound filled it. The fauna moved in it, blind and busy.

She thought, into her transducer, to the colleague who was waiting at the next inspection point: *Coming*.

The thought arrived in her colleague's skull, in her colleague's voice.

She stepped off her plate, onto the bridge that spanned the gap, and began to cross.

The rain hit her slicker. The roar pressed against her. The chimneys of the lower stacks rose around her, fused, packed, weeping.

She had walked across gaps like this ten thousand times.

She walked across this one again.
