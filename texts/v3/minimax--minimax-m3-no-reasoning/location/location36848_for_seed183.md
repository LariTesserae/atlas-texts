You reach the place by going up for a long time. The path, such as it is, loses definition gradually: flagged stones set into pale rock, then only the rock, then only the pale rock for as far as you can see in any direction. The air thins. The light intensifies. By the time you arrive the sky has become a thing you can almost feel against your skin — close, bright, faintly humming.

It is a plateau of sorts, though the word suggests a flatness this place refuses. It is more like a vast shoulder of stone, ridged and terraced and creased, sloping off in long facets toward edges you cannot quite see because the constant mist erases them. The scale is regional: you could walk for a day in any direction and remain inside the place. It holds a valley's worth of features, but it is not a valley. It is a face — the face of something enormous turned up toward the sky.

## The Stone

The substrate is mineral, almost entirely. Travertine, sinter, basalt, occasional veins of something darker and glassier that the light does not enter. The terraces rise in pale curtains, layer upon layer, like the work of a slow and patient architect who had nothing but time and hot water. The colors are not colors you would name — creams and ash-grays and the faintest rust, the off-white of bone that has been in the sun, the dull green-gray of water-smoothed copper. Everything is wet. Everything has been wet for so long that dry is not a state the stone remembers.

The rock is dense with feature. A square meter of slope may hold a tufa rim, a shallow pool, a crystalline efflorescence, a darker band of basalt, a fossil steam-vent now sealed over with a knob of sinter, a place where the surface is stippled with tiny knobs the size of fingertips, a place where the surface is glass. There is no empty ground. Every inch is in some process or another, and the processes have been running long enough that they have layered over each other, palimpsest upon palimpsest, so that the same square meter might be a cooling flow from one era and a precipitated crust from another and a place where a hot seep is currently pushing upward and has not yet broken through.

The erosion is settled. Nothing here is actively wearing away; everything is being added to, or being very slowly re-dissolved and re-precipitated somewhere else. The place has reached an internal equilibrium with its own water and heat and light, and it tends toward itself.

## The Water

There is no rain to speak of, or only rarely — once or twice a season a storm comes through and the mist becomes a downpour and the terraces run full, and then it is over and the mist returns. The rest of the time, the wetness comes from below.

Fumaroles vent along the higher ridges. Hot seepages weep from cliff faces. Somewhere under the stone, heat and water are still talking to each other, and what they say comes up as steam and as warm trickles that run across surfaces and pool in basins and spill over edges in pale ribbons. The steam rises into the cold upper air and condenses, and falls back as a fine mist that wets every surface equally. The rocks sweat. The terraced rims glisten. The pools sit still and full, and their surfaces are almost never quite still — they breathe, very slightly, with the pulse of whatever is beneath.

The water runs in directions that are mostly downhill but not always. Some flows follow paths that were cut when the underlying stone was hotter and have since been re-sealed with mineral deposition, so that the water climbs a centimeter or two to get around an obstruction. Some pools overflow on the side that is higher, as if they have quietly forgotten about gravity. The cascades are small — no more than a hand's breadth, often less — but they are persistent, and they make a sound that is part of the place's voice.

The water is hot where it emerges, cool where it pools. Somewhere in the middle it crosses through a band of temperature where, if there were any life, it would flourish. There is no life. Or only just.

## The Almost-Life

Vegetation, in any recognizable sense, is absent. There is nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that roots or seeds or spreads by any means a botanist would recognize. But the place is not lifeless, only non-alive in the way we mean.

In the spray-zones of the hottest vents, crystalline tufts push up from the rock: clusters of fine needles, pale and translucent, that grow a millimeter in a week and dissolve again when the vent shifts. They look like frost, but they are warm. On wet surfaces, white crusts bloom overnight and disappear by midday, following some rhythm of saturation and drying that has nothing to do with the sun. Glassy flowstones creep sideways across flat basalt in thin sheets, leaving trails like the tracks of something that walked very slowly. There are films on the pools — not organic film, but mineral precipitates forming at the surface tension — that wrinkle and shift with the slightest change in the air.

If you took a broad view of the processes — heat rising, water falling, minerals precipitating, crystals growing, surfaces being etched and re-coated, the whole slow churning of stone through solution and back — you might call it a metabolism. The place digests its own rock and excretes it elsewhere. It is patient. It is also, in its own terms, thriving.

## The Light

The sun at this elevation, filtered through the constant mist, produces a brightness that has no shadows.

You expect shadows. You look for them. They are not there. ## The Remains

The place was walked through, once, by hands that held hammers and abrading stones and perhaps small pieces of soft metal. They left almost nothing.

Cairns mark the tops of terraces — waist-high, rough-stacked from local stone, some of them tumbled and re-stacked and tumbled again, as if this has happened before. A few dark uprights stand at intersections where flagged paths suggest themselves, the stones half-buried now in fresh sinter, the paths more memory than way. They left marks that the terraces are slowly covering over, and will in time cover completely, and the place will not be different for their absence.

No one has come back in a long time. The cairns have not been maintained. The paths are not paths. Whatever brought hands here has either ended or gone elsewhere, and the place has resumed its own long projects.

## The Sound

The wind is constant and very loud. It moves across the plateau in a way that seems to come from more than one direction at once — a crosswind, a falling wind, a wind that comes up from below through the steam-vents as if the whole ridge is breathing out. It carries sound strangely. A rockfall on the far side of the plateau will arrive at your ear twice, from two different angles, slightly out of phase, so that for a moment you think there were two rockfalls and then you think there was one and then you are not sure. Your own footsteps return to you a half-beat late and at a slightly different pitch, as if someone else is walking just behind you, matching your pace.

The fumaroles add a low continuous hiss. The cascades add a higher tone, dozens of them, slightly out of tune with each other, so that the whole slope produces a kind of shifting chord. And somewhere in the range between — not loud, not quite, but persistent — there is a sound that does not have a source. A harmonic that builds and fades. A resonance that seems to come from the stone itself, or from the air deciding to vibrate in sympathy with the steam, or from nowhere you can locate. You can tune it out, after a while. You can also tune back in, and it has not changed, and you cannot tell whether that is reassuring or not.

## The Wrongness

The geometry is wrong. Once you notice, you cannot stop noticing.

The terraces that appear to be a hundred meters away, when you walk toward them, turn out to be either two hundred meters away or already beside you. A cairn you can see from here has a twin on the next ridge over, identical, down to the tilt of the topmost stone, and you could swear neither is the original. Distances on the flat stones in front of you are unreliable: a shallow bright puddle a step away turns out to be waist-deep and cold; a depression you walked around turns out, when seen from the other side, to be a rise you have already climbed. The upright stones lean at angles that, when you look at them again, have changed by a few degrees.

It is not that the place is unstable. It is that it has stabilized around a different set of rules, and you are the only thing here that is still using the old ones. The terraces are where they are. The cairns stay put. The light does not flicker. It is a settled strangeness, a long-established wrongness, in perfect balance with itself. You could mark your way back by cairns if you trusted them to be where you left them, which is itself a question you will be asking all day.

In the pools, if you look long enough without blinking, you see things that are not reflections. Not faces. Not shapes. More a sense of motion beneath the surface — a slow rotation of the water that does not match any current you can find, or a depth that suggests something is down there, very far down, in water that is only a meter deep. Sometimes a single tone emerges from a pool that does not match the steam-hiss: held, pure, then gone.

## The Seasons

The place has a kind of year.

In the colder months the steam rises higher and thicker; the mist becomes a low cloud that sits on the plateau for days; the terraces freeze at their rims and the cascades slow to a drip. Sound travels differently in the cold — flatter, more muffled, the harmonic from nowhere almost inaudible. The crystal tufts in the spray-zones pause their growth. The place goes quiet in its own way.

In the warmer months the vents put out more steam, more water emerges from below, more mineral is deposited, more crystal grows. The terraces extend themselves by millimeters. The flows creep further. The sound rises. The place wakes up, if wakefulness is the right word for a process that never quite stops.

It is in constant, slow motion, and the motion is its own kind of life.
