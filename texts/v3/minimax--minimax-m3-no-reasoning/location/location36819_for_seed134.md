# The Cold Engine Cathedral

The place is approximately the size of a small cathedral, and it hums.

You arrive — if "arrive" is the right word for materializing inside a flooded chamber — at the bottom of a vertical shaft. The shaft is roughly forty meters across and perhaps sixty deep, and every centimeter of it is filled with seawater. The water is cold. Not arctic, not lethal, but the kind of cold that settles into your joints and reminds you, patiently, that you are a warm thing in a cold medium, and the medium does not care. Somewhere far above, a circular opening admits a column of pale light. The light falls through the water in a slightly diffused, slightly greenish way, illuminating motes — enormous numbers of motes — that drift in slow spirals down the shaft like dust in a sunbeam, except the beam is pointed down, and the dust is alive.

The motes are mostly small. Pale-shelled things. Crustaceans, mostly, with two species of translucent shrimp in the billions and a slower, larger copepod that drifts like a thought. They school in long ribbons that wrap around the shaft in helical paths, their collective motion producing a faint, continuous sound — not a roar, not a whisper, but something like sand pouring, like rain on a hard surface, like static. The shrimp are the white noise of this place. They are everywhere. They are the medium as much as the water is.

The shaft itself is not natural.

The walls are dark basalt, but the basalt has been *cut*. Cut into facets. Cut into regularities. The geometry is industrial in a way that feels old, or at least patient. There are no inscriptions, no decorative carvings, no tools left behind, no makers to name. But the facets are too even, the corners too consistent, the angles too repeated across too many meters of stone to be anything but the output of intent. Whoever cut this shaft — whatever cut this shaft  worked in straight lines and right angles, and worked at a scale that suggests either great patience or great machines. The rock surfaces are fresh. They have not yet been softened by time. There are still sharp edges where the facets meet, and the edges cast sharp shadows, and the shadows shift as the light column above shifts, which it does, slowly, across the day, tracing slow ellipses on the faceted walls as if the sun were a slow animal pacing above the opening.

There is machinery. The shaft is lined with it.

Pipes, mostly. Thick black pipes of some metal that does not corrode — the water has been here long enough, and the shrimp have been here longer, and nothing is fouled, nothing is encrusted, nothing is rusting. The pipes run vertically along the walls, in clusters of three or seven or twelve, disappearing into the rock above and below. Some of the pipes are pulsing. A faint throb moves through them in slow waves, and where the waves meet certain fittings — certain nodes that are bigger than the pipes, that have a knuckle-like geometry, that look almost like joints — the water around them vibrates differently. The vibrations are too low to hear, sometimes. They are felt, in the teeth, in the chest. The shrimp avoid the nodes. They give them a wide berth, schooling in clean ovals around the silent machinery, and the avoidance itself contributes to the architecture of the schools: every node is a hole in the lacework of the swarm.

Some of the pipes are not pulsing. Some are still, and cold, and the shrimp pass close to them. Some are hot — a few, here and there, radiate a gentle warmth into the water, and around those pipes, life is different. The shrimp cluster thickest near the warm pipes, not in frantic feeding swarms but in languid, slow aggregations, and among them, larger things move. Things with more mass. A pale flatfish, perhaps, or something that functions as a flatfish in this system, hanging in the water column above a warm pipe with a patience that is almost mechanical. Jellyfish, small ones, pulsing their bells in slow-motion, drifting down and up and down again. A long, ribbon-thin thing that might be an eel or might be a manufactured cable, except it moves with intention, coiling once around a pipe, then unwinding and swimming to the next.

The light from above is sufficient. It reaches the bottom of the shaft, barely, as a faint glow that lets the eye distinguish shapes but not colors. Everything at this depth is in grayscale, or in the greens and blues of refracted seawater. The water itself is clear. There is no silt, no algal bloom, no murk. The clarity is itself a kind of statement: nothing has fallen into this water that wasn't supposed to. The column of light catches the shrimp and makes them glitter, catches the pipes and makes them gleam, catches the faceted walls and breaks into planar reflections that move and move and never settle. The whole shaft, at the right angle, the right moment, is a slowly rotating lantern.

The sound is the shrimp and the machinery, together. The shrimp provide the high end — the chitter, the rustle, the soft continuous crackle of carapace against carapace in the dense swarms. The machinery provides the low end — a hum that is felt more than heard, a pulse that arrives and recedes, the suggestion of cycles that are longer than a heartbeat, longer than a breath, longer than an afternoon. Between these two, the acoustics of the shaft do their work: the stone walls focus the sound, the water carries it, the geometry of the cavity creates standing waves that make certain frequencies louder at certain depths, so that as you rise or fall through the shaft, the sound changes character. Near the top, near the light, the hum dominates. Near the bottom, near the deepest pipes, the chitter dominates. In the middle, they blend into something like a chord.

The pipes are doing something. They are not decorative. They are not inert. The pulses that move through them have direction — some downward, some upward, some lateral where branching fittings send them sideways into the rock — and the pulses have timing, regular but not metronomic, with a slight variation that suggests they are responding to conditions rather than running on a fixed schedule. The pipes are part of a system. A system that is operating. A system that has been operating for a long time, perhaps, and will operate for longer, in this flooded shaft, with its shrimp and its warm pipes and its pale light from above.

Nothing here was built for the shrimp. The shrimp came, or were brought, or simply arrived, as shrimp do, and found a cathedral, and filled it. The machinery does not acknowledge them. The faceted walls do not acknowledge them. The shaft itself, with its patient industrial geometry, predates them, or outlasts them, or operates on a scale that does not include their comings and goings. They are tenants. They are the medium's own music. And the music plays on, in the cold water, in the green light, in the slow and unhurried way of all things that are not in a hurry.

The place is dense with life and dense with mechanism, and the two are layered over each other without ever quite touching, like two transparencies on the same light. The shrimp school around the pipes. The flatfish drifts above its warm pipe. The ribbon-thing coils and uncoils. The light moves its slow ellipses on the faceted walls. The hum goes on. The chitter goes on. The water is cold, and clear, and full, and the shaft is small, and old in the way that unfinished things are old, and the whole thing is, in some sense that resists language, a kind of engine — or a kind of prayer — that has not yet figured out which.

You do not have to figure it out either. You can simply be in the water, in the shaft, in the light, and let the place do its slow work around you.
