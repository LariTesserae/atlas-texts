# The Girdlewood

Listen first, before you try to see it.

The roar is the first thing. Not wind, not rain — none of those exist here — but the **chorus**: ten thousand paired clappers of hollow wood striking each other in overlapping polyrhythm, the deep **tok-tok-tok** of root knuckles drumming against the limestone crust, and above all of it, a continuous shrieking whistle, like a kettle that has never been taken off the flame, that rises and falls but never stops. The place has no quiet moments. The place has never had a quiet moment.

What you are standing in, if you have the misfortune to stand in it, is a basin. A shallow sink some eight leagues across, sunk into the rind of a world that has forgotten water. The walls of the basin are raw, pale, almost cream-colored — a soft mineral that flakes under a thumbnail, the bedrock of a planet whose name you could not pronounce. It is warm to the touch, that stone. Not baking, but the warmth of an animal's flank, of bread fresh from an oven, the warmth of something that is metabolizing. The basin floor is not stone at all. It is **rootplate**: a single vast lattice of woody material, pressed and felted into something almost fabric-like, that extends from wall to wall. When you step on it, it gives — not spongy, not pulpy, but **supple**, like stepping on the back of a resting ox. It is the world's only floor, and it breathes in a slow tidal rhythm that you only notice after you stop panicking about everything else.

From this floor, the vegetation rises.

---

It rises wrong. That is the second thing you notice, after the sound.

There is no water here — none has fallen in living memory, perhaps none has ever fallen — but the basin is **green** beyond the capacity of green. Not the dusty olive of a desert shrub. Not the silver of a succulent. The color of pond scum, of frogs, of the underside of a leaf in August. A thick, wet-looking green that should be impossible in a place with no apparent moisture, and which is, if you cut into it, exactly as wet as it looks. The plants of the Girdlewood have solved the problem of thirst by **becoming their own weather**. Each stem sweats. Each leaf transpires. Each root-tip dribbles a thin clear liquid down into the felted rootplate, and the rootplate sweats it back up to the stems, and the stems respire it back out into the air of the basin, where it hangs as a permanent low haze, a fog you can chew, that never quite condenses into rain because the basin is too warm for it to fall.

It is a closed system. A terrarium the size of a county. The plants water themselves.

And they are **everywhere**.

---

The architecture of the place is botanical. There is no other word for it, and no other language for what they do. Stems as thick as temple columns rise twenty, thirty, forty feet, and instead of branching into boughs they **reweave** — twisting back into each other, fusing, splitting, fusing again, until the upper stories of the basin become a living cathedral ceiling of braided wood. The weave is so dense in places that the light comes through in green coins, in shafts that move only when the wind in the canopy moves, and the wind in the canopy moves constantly because of the fauna.

Under the cathedral floor — under the rootplate, in the rootcellar of the world — are the **bulbs**. That is what the locals call them, though the locals are not locals in any sense you would recognize. The bulbs are great swelling nodules where a stem has chosen, for reasons of its own incomprehensible botany, to thicken into a chamber. Some are the size of a hut. Some are the size of a house. A few, near the center of the basin, are the size of a longhall, and these are the **drums**, the great resonating knots of wood whose hollow chambers, when the climate is right (it is always right), fill with a thin warm gas that the plant secretes from its inner lining, and which then vents through calibrated slits in the bulb's hide, producing the tok-tok-tok that is the bass note of the Girdlewood's roar. The plants drum to themselves. The plants drum to each other. The plants drum because that is what they do, and the drumming is loud enough that you feel it in your anklebones if you stand still long enough.

In between the bulbs, in the dimmer regions of the canopy, the **parasols** spread — enormous round leaves, some as broad as a wagon, held aloft on flexible petioles that rock and tremble with every gust. They are not photosynthetic. There is barely any sunlight at floor level, and what there is has been drunk green by the canopy above. They are **condensers**. Each parasol is dished slightly upward, and the underside is stippled with thousands of tiny pores that exhale moisture into the space between leaf and rootplate, where it is caught by the rootplate and recycled. They are the clouds of the Girdlewood. They do not drift. They do not need to.

Between the parasols, threading the canopy in every direction, are the **rattles** — long whip-like extensions of stem that end in hollow bulbs of brittle shell. They swing. They swing constantly, because the fauna swings them, because the wind they make by swinging is part of the weather, because the breeze of the basin is generated as much by the percussion of the rattles against each other as by any thermal effect of the warm stone. When a strong gust passes through, the entire canopy rings like a million shaken bells. This is the kettle-whistle you hear above the drumming: the wind made by wind, the sound of a forest hitting itself.

And it is warm. Always warm. The stone radiates. The bulbs radiate. The respiration of ten thousand tons of vegetation radiates. The Girdlewood is its own stove, and the air is the temperature of a bathhouse, and the moisture is the moisture of a bathhouse, and you cannot tell, after a while, whether you are in a forest or in a steam room that has been built out of living wood.

---

The fauna is what makes the place **inhuman**.

There is a great deal of fauna. There is more fauna than vegetation, by mass, which is a sentence that should not be possible to write, but is. The fauna has eaten the vegetation, has become the vegetation, has married itself into the wood until you can no longer tell which is which. The creatures of the Girdlewood are **insect** in the broad sense — jointed, chitinous, driven by strange blood — but they are insect the way a cathedral is a stone. Some are the size of your thumb. Some are the size of your arm. The **legions**, the small ones, swarm in rivers along the upper surface of the rootplate, in columns a foot wide and miles long, each one carrying a fragment of leaf or a chip of bulb-husk back to a **node**, a place where the columns converge.

The nodes are the cities of the Girdlewood. There are dozens of them, scattered across the basin like villages on a plain. Each node is a heaped mound of worked plant material — pulped, regimented, cemented with a resin the insects secrete — in which the openings are many, the chambers are deep, and the activity is **continuous**. From a node you will hear, even above the omnipresent roar, a thinner sharper sound: the **chitter** of a million jaws working. The nodes are not nests. They are **stomachs**. The insects of the Girdlewood feed the bulbs, and the bulbs feed the insects, and the arrangement is so intimate that neither partner can survive more than a few hundred yards from the other. The wood eats them. They eat the wood. The boundary has softened past recognition.

The great fauna are the **hornbearers**. They are the size of ponies. They have six legs and a horn — a single forward-curving horn of black chitin, two feet long, that they use to gouge the bulbs open when the gas inside has built up to the point of needing release. The hornbearer **knows** which bulbs to open. The hornbearer **knows** when. When a hornbearer opens a bulb, the pent-up gas vents with a scream that drops the pitch from a kettle-whistle to a foghorn, and the entire canopy rocks in the shockwave, and every parasol for a hundred yards around shivers, and you, if you are standing in the basin floor, feel it as a sudden warm gust at your ankles and a sudden ringing in your ears. The hornbearers do this constantly. They are the percussionists. They are the **orchestra**.

The hornbearers are tended. You did not expect that sentence, but it is true. At the nodes, certain of the insects — larger, slower, with strange swollen abdomens — are kept in shallow pits of rootplate, and they produce, from glands at the joints of their legs, a thick amber liquid that the hornbearers will lick from a keeper's outstretched palm. The amber liquid keeps them calm. The amber liquid makes them musical. The hornbearers, when they are not gassing the bulbs, spend their time wandering the rootplate in long processions, swinging their horns in slow arcs, and the horn strikes the rattles as they pass, producing the syncopated clatter that runs above the tok-tok-tok of the drums. **Tok-tok. Tak-tak-tok. Tok.** A polyrhythm. A canon. A percussion symphony performed by a herd of six-legged animals in a forest that is also a steam bath.

It is deafening. You cannot hear yourself think. You cannot hear yourself speak. If you want to speak to someone in the Girdlewood, you press your mouth to their ear, or you write in the dust of churned rootplate, or you wait until a hornbearer lets off a particularly long blast and the rest of the orchestra pauses to refill. There is a pause every few minutes. It is the only quiet the place affords.

---

There are people here.

A few. Not many. They live at the **rim** of the basin, where the rootplate thins and the raw warm stone shows through. They have learned — over how many generations, no one remembers — to live above the floor. Their houses are built into the bulb-chambers of the giant stems: hollowed-out rooms with windows cut in the woody walls, ladders of peeled rattan, doors of hung leaf. They are a small and a quiet people, for obvious reasons. They communicate with each other across the basin by means of **fire-signals** in the daytime and by **drum-codes** at night, tapping on the inside walls of their own bulbs in patterns that carry, because the wood of the Girdlewood is an excellent conductor of vibration. They farm the **amber**. They tend the hornbearers in small ways — leading them to the right bulbs, sealing the wrong ones with plugs of resin — and in return the hornbearers tolerate them as moving furniture. They have iron. They have cloth. They have not, in any recorded memory, climbed to the top of the canopy. There is no reason to. There is everything up there.

The people here believe — those who still believe anything — that the basin is **waking up**. That the Girdlewood is not yet finished. That the bulbs are still thickening, the parasols still widening, the rattles still extending. They point to the **new growth** at the basin's edges, where pale stems no thicker than your wrist push up through cracks in the warm stone in the night, and by morning have woven themselves into a lattice, and by the next night have pushed up new stems from that lattice, and by the week after are a young forest a yard tall. They point to the slow creep of the rootplate outward, up the basin walls, year by year. They say: *it is coming over the rim*. They say: *when it comes over the rim, we will have to move*. They say: *we have always said this*. They say it with the resignation of people whose doom is also their livelihood.

They do not say what will happen to the hornbearers when the wood overruns the rim. They do not say what will happen to the orchestra.

---

And so: the warm dark green deafening breathing world.

A forest that is a factory. A factory that is a forest. A place where the boundary between the living and the built has been worn away by generations of mutual digestion. Where the rain is the sweat of leaves. Where the thunder is the percussion of insects on wood. Where the wind is made by the things the wind moves. Where the floor is a single organism and you are standing on its back, and it does not mind, and it does not know, and the drumming does not stop, and the drumming will not stop, because the Girdlewood is a closed system, and a closed system can only **continue**.

If you stand very still — which is difficult, because the hornbearer processions come close, and the small insects run over your feet like a tickling carpet, and the floor is breathing under you, and the air is thick and warm and wet — if you stand very still, and you close your eyes, and you let the roar wash over you until it stops being a roar and becomes a single tone, you can feel, through the soles of your boots, the slow tidal pulse of the rootplate. A systole. A diastole. The heartbeat of a forest that has forgotten it is a forest and has remembered that it is an animal.

It is not a kind place.

It is not an unkind place.

It is a place that is **busy**, in the way that a beehive is busy, in the way that a storm is busy, in the way that the inside of a living cell is busy. It does not know you are there. It will continue whether you are there or not. It will drum, and sweat, and swing its rattles, and open its bulbs, and pour its amber, and creep another inch up the warm stone rim, and it will do this for as long as the stone stays warm, which may be forever.

The light, where it reaches, is green.

The air, where you breathe it, is wet.

The sound, where you stand, is everything.

This is the Girdlewood.
