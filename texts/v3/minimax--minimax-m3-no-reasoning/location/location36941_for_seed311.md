# The Casing

It begins where you'd never think to look: inside a length of hollow cable that fell off a barge hauling decommissioned server farms across the Yelesvar Shelf, and that cable has lain here — at this depth, in this cold, in this dark, for what the operating logs of the long-dead barge would have called *seventeen months and four days* before anyone, before any*thing*, noticed what was growing inside it.

You cannot stand here. There is no up.

The inside of the cable is a tunnel. The tunnel is full. The tunnel is the world.

---

**The geometry is simple and monstrous.** Imagine a polyethylene sheath the diameter of a subway car, lying on its side in the black silty bottom of a shallow sea, one end crushed flat by the weight of silt, the other end sealed by a flange of corroded copper that has bloomed, over the months, into a perfect hood of pale mineral. Inside the cable, in the absence of current, in the presence of pressure and a steady trickle of seawater through a hairline crack halfway along its length, a city has installed itself. The water is everywhere — it is the air, it is the weather, it is the medium of every transaction. It moves in a slow endless exhalation through the crack at one end, threading the entire length of the tunnel, and pools in the deepest sag of the cable's belly where the floor has, against all expectation, become *ground*.

Everything grows out of the cable's original lining. The white polymer has been colonized so completely by a coralline crust of engineered material that none of the original surface remains visible. The crust is a city. The crust is alive in the way that cities are alive — which is to say, mostly not, but convincingly.

---

**The buildings** are lamellar plates of calcium-aluminate excreted by a strain of self-assembling nanite that someone, somewhere, once designed for undersea foundation work, and that has, since the loss of the barge, been working very hard at a job it was not given. The plates stand on edge from the tunnel walls, packed shoulder to shoulder, leaning at the angles that optimize load-bearing against the inward pressure of the sea. They are thin as paper and some are taller than you. Between them, in a darkness that is not quite darkness, the water hums. Sound is everywhere. The sea, forced through the hairline crack, becomes a pressurized jet at the far end of the tunnel, and that jet, ricocheting off the mineral hood, sets up a standing wave that runs the length of the cable and back. The frequency is low. The frequency is a sound you feel in the plates of your skull. The frequency has, in seventeen months, taught the nanites where to put their next plate: always in the node of the standing wave that reinforces it, never in the antinode that would damp it. The city is shaped by acoustics. The city is a sound organ. The city is *tuned*.

You would not call the plates *architecture* exactly, because nothing built them on purpose — but you would not call them *coral* either, because the pattern is too obviously metropolitan. They have the quality of housing in a port city: built right up against one another, no space wasted, no light admitted, the population of every plate so tightly stacked with its neighbors that to pass between them you'd have to go sideways and hold your breath. Which is what the water does. The water threads between them, finding every gap, every capillary seam, the way a crowd threads itself through a market street at noon.

---

**Above the plates** — if *above* means *toward the crack*, *toward the source of the slow current* — a second layer of structure has begun to form. It is younger. It is messier. The nanites, having solved the problem of *where to put a wall*, are now solving the problem of *what to do with the current*. They are building vanes. The vanes are not quite fins and not quite chimneys. They are curled sheets of mineral, like cabbage leaves, like the pages of a book left out in the rain, each one angled so that the water passing it sets it vibrating, and the vibration of a thousand vanes at a thousand frequencies produces a constant, pervasive, almost musical wash of sound that fills the tunnel the way traffic fills a city street — omnipresent, directionless, belonging to no one. This is the loudness. This is the *sound*. It is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud. It is loud in the way a city is loud: in aggregate, in accumulation, in the sheer fact of so many small things insisting at once.

Sometimes — and you have to be here at the right moment, and you have to know to listen — one of the vanes will shatter. It is a small event. A flake of calcium-aluminate the size of a fingernail, suddenly released from the structure, spinning into the current, carried back through the city, through the market-streets between the plates, out through the crack in the cable's skin, and into the open sea. In its place, in a day, a new vane grows. The city maintains itself by losing things.

---

**Life** is sparse in the conventional sense. There is no fauna. There is nothing here with a nervous system, nothing with a face. But on the inner surface of the mineral hood at the far end, where the pressure of the incoming jet first breaks, there is a slick of something that is not quite a plant and not quite a fungus and not quite a colony of bacteria. It is the color of a bruise: violet-black, faintly iridescent. It is the only colored thing in the tunnel. The mineral plates are bone-white, the water is a green so dark it is almost black, the silt at the bottom of the cable's sag is grey — but this slick is *purple*, and when the standing wave passes through it, it phosphoresces. A dim, cold, lavender glow, lasting a quarter of a second, repeated every few seconds when the wave crests. It is the only light in the city. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to navigate by, if you are the kind of thing that lives here, and nothing here is the kind of thing that lives here, and yet the glow persists. A lighthouse with no ships. A clock with no readers. A heartbeat in a body that has forgotten it has one.

The slick grows. The slick is eaten — slowly, mineral-by-mineral — by the nanites at the hood's edge, who incorporate it into their plates. The slick grows back. This has been going on for seventeen months. There is no reason to think it will stop.

---

**The cool** is the cool of deep water that has never been warm. It does not change. It enters the tunnel at four degrees and exits at four and a fraction degrees, having warmed only against the faint metabolism of the nanites, which is to say, against the entire city. The city is, in aggregate, the temperature of a sleeping mammal. You could, in principle, be warmed here. You could not, in practice, be warm.

The **light** is the light of fourteen meters of water above you, which is to say, the light of late dusk in late autumn, in a place where the sun is low and the weather is bad and the sea is turbid. A grey-green suggestion of illumination falls through the crack and dies within a meter of entering. The city is not lit. The city does not need to be lit. The city is for the water, and the water does not see.

---

**What is strange about it** — and it is strange; you feel it the moment you imagine yourself inside — is the *intimacy*. The scale says: hidden. The scale says: pocket, room, the inside of a thing. And it is. It is the inside of a cable. It is a city the size of a cathedral, and it is inside a single length of industrial waste, and you could pick the whole thing up if you had a hand big enough and a crane strong enough, and you would not, because no one knows it is here. It is invisible from above. It is invisible from the outside. The cable lies on the seabed and looks like a cable. Open the cable, and the cable is already open: it has opened itself, it has turned itself inside-out, it has become the building and the building has become the city and the city is the inside of a thing that was never meant to have an inside.

The nanites do not know they are a city. The water does not know it is a sky. The slime on the hood does not know it is a clock. But the standing wave knows where the plates should be. And the plates know how to support the water. And the water knows how to find the crack and leave. And the city persists, the way cities persist, by being better at being a city than anything else that could be here.

The current moves through it. The sound moves through it. The cool moves through it.

The cable lies on the shelf. The shelf lies under the sea. The sea lies under the sky. The sky does not know.
