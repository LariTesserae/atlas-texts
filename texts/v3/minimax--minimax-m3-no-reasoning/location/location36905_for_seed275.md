# The Root Vault of Verre-Brûlé

Beneath the basalt cap, in the negative space where water once pooled and never returned, there is a city. It is not a city of stone.

## The Substrate

Everything that holds weight here is alive. The streets are root-mat, pressed and interwoven by three centuries of growth, each segment as thick as a human torso, plated in a corky bark that crunches under a boot. Above the streets rise the walls — not walls, exactly, but rather continuous vertical gardens of a single hybrid species the colonists call *verre-brûlé*, "burnt glass," because its heartwood shines with a vitreous luster when cut. The trunks are columnar, three to nine meters thick, rising fifteen meters before they fork into the canopy that has replaced the sky. Between the trunks, the spaces that in any other city would be windows and doors are bridged by living cambium lattices that the inhabitants keep trimmed into arches, lintels, thresholds. Lintels grow back. Doorways migrate, season to season, as the city's master-arborists guide the living architecture toward new configurations. Last year's customs house is this year's granary. The granary is already becoming a chapel.

The roots, when you follow them down, plunge through the basalt into mineral strata that have not seen liquid in geological memory. The plant does not need water. It eats stone.

## The Chemistry

This is the strangest thing, and the settlers have only half-understood it. *Verre-brûlé* does not photosynthesize. It does not transpire. Its pale, almost translucent leaves — clustered only at the canopy, fifteen meters up, where a scattering of luminous mineral dust suspended in the air provides just enough radiational flux — are vestigial, ornamental. The real work happens below. Hair-fine root tendrils extend through micro-fractures in the basalt and into the underlying geochemistry, where they harvest reduced iron, sulfur compounds, trace carbides left by some primordial carbonaceous layer no one's drilled down to confirm. The tree is, in the patient way of trees, a refinery. Its respiration is non-aqueous. It breathes through its bark in slow mineral cycles that take weeks where our breaths take seconds. From a distance, on a still evening, you can hear the city's metabolism, a vast and unhurried tectonic suspiration that the inhabitants find comforting.

It is cool here. Fourteen Celsius, year-round, with perhaps two degrees of seasonal drift. The basalt cap insulates the vault from the surface, where midday ground temperatures must sear. The colonists speak of *dehors* — "outside" — as a place of violence and dryness, visited only by the salt-carts that trade with the caravan cities of the surface. They go up through three vertical shafts, also drilled and grown, into a light that they say is too loud.

## The Light

Light, here, is a question of taste. The vault is not dark. A slow phosphorescence lives in the heartwood of every mature *verre-brûlé* trunk — a deep amber, almost but not quite orange — and it intensifies as the roots draw deeper. The newest buildings, grown from grafts only just rooted into the basalt, glow like blown sugar. The oldest, the cathedrals and the original settlement ring, burn steady and low like banked hearths. Between them, the streets are lit by the combined glow of every wall. There is no night. There is only the diurnal migration of the dust: a slow rain of luminous particulate that settles through the vault during what passes for the sleep-cycle, and is stirred upward by the thermal draft of the city's mass during the waking hours. It catches in the canopy leaves and makes them glitter like a frozen sea seen from below. Children jump to shake the branches and bring it down.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet. The basalt absorbs. The wood absorbs. The dust absorbs. What sound remains is the city's own: the soft tectonic suspiration of the forest's breath, the occasional creak of a trunk settling, the deeper rumble of a particularly large limb moving under its own weight. Footsteps are muffled. Voices do not carry. Intimate conversation happens in the streets as easily as indoors, and the colonists have forgotten the concept of whispering — here, everyone whispers whether they mean to or not.

But there is one other sound. Twice a day, at the long transitions, the canopies sing. The dust, descending or rising, passes through the leaf lattices of the canopy, and each leaf — each tiny, translucent, hollow leaf — resonates at its own pitch. The canopy is a wind-chime the size of a city. The colonists time their rest to it. The first down-chord, in the long evening, is the signal to put the cookfires out. The first up-chord, before waking, is the signal to begin the day's graft-work on the buildings. Between the chords, the canopy is silent, and the streets are pools of amber light, and the city's slow breath is the only sound.

## The Fauna

The vault is not silent of animals, but the animals are quiet and mostly small. The dust itself supports them. Tiny hexapods — surface-descended, probably — graze the luminous particulate where it settles on the upper surfaces of the leaves, and these in turn support climbing vertebrates the colonists call *mites-faucons*, "falcon-mites," for their habit of dropping from the canopy on silk threads to take the hexapods. There are also the *échos*, blind and pale, that live in the root-mat and eat dead cambium. They are the size of dogs, they are entirely silent, and they are the city's principal scavengers. They have been here longer than the colonists. They are tolerated.

There are larger things. The colonists speak of *les longues*, the long ones, that move through the trunk-forests in the deeper, less-inhabited districts. They are rarely seen. Their passages through a district are marked, in the morning, by a hundred meters of recently grown trunk — new wood, fresh, pale — where the night before there had been a regular street. Whatever they are, the trees grow around them. The trees seem to prefer it.

## The Industry

The vault is industrial. Not in the smoke-and-iron sense; there is no iron here that anyone uses. But the city's metabolism is industrial in scale. The *verre-brûlé* forests are harvested in rotation, a district at a time, the trunks felled (the verb is "to thank") and the heartwood shipped up the shafts to the surface caravans, where it is the most prized building material in the dry world above. In exchange, the vault receives what it cannot make: metals, ceramics, certain salts, and — once a year, with great ceremony — the seeds of new species the master-arborists will attempt to graft into the substrate. Most fail. Some take.

The city's other industry is the management of its own growth. Every colonist above the age of twelve is, in some formal capacity, an arborist. The Graft-Guild is the largest. The Dust-Guild manages the luminous particulate and the canopies it falls through. The Breath-Guild — the oldest, the most respected — tends the roots, the slow mineral respiration, the long and patient negotiations with the basalt. To fail a Breath-Guild apprentice is to be sent above, into the dry loud light, to join the caravans. It is a serious disgrace. Most recover it.

## The Air

The air is breathable, slightly sweet, faintly mineral. It moves, but slowly. There are drafts that follow the shafts, drafts that follow the kiln-districts where green heartwood is being slowly cured for surface trade. There are also the great slow exhalations of the forest itself — exhalations that are not breath, not quite, but the venting of metabolic gases from below the root-line, where the trees are doing their slow geology. The colonists have learned which drafts to avoid. The deepest drafts smell of sulfur, and a long breath of a deep draft will make you sleepy for a day.

## The Mood

It is not a happy city, exactly, and not an unhappy one. It is a city with the patience of geology and the busyness of a port. There is commerce, there is craft, there is gossip, there is the long and unending conversation between the colonists and the substrate they have learned to live within. The substrate is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is alive, and vast, and old, and it will continue its slow work long after the colonists have gone up or down or out. The colonists know this. They graft themselves into it as best they can.

They have not named the vault. They call it *ici* — "here."

I, too, would call it here.
