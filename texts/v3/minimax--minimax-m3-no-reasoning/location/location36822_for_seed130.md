The ground is metal. There has been ground, and there is metal now, or there is metal and the ground has been forgotten beneath it. Hard to say which. The metal is wet — always wet — a thin unbroken sheet of water lying across it as though the sky has been pouring down and never quite finished, or as though the metal itself is weeping. In places the water thickens into pools that fill the long shallow depressions the centuries have pressed into the surface: dents grown into valleys, rivets dissolved into pits, panels sagged into bowls. Walk far enough and the floor is the same in every direction — wet, dark, faintly trembling.

The light that reaches here is not quite light. It is the suggestion of light, a gray residue, the color of soot mixed with water. Whatever sun may exist above has been filtered through so many layers of vapor and particulate that by the time it arrives it has forgotten what it was. Local sources remain: sodium lamps spaced so widely apart that their circles of amber barely touch, leaving most of the floor in a kind of polite twilight. They flicker, not regularly, but in the arrhythmic way of lamps that have been running so long they have begun to make their own decisions.

Sound is the first thing. It is also the last. There is no silence. Even when a particular engine stops — and some do, with a long drawn-out sigh — the aggregate continues: a low-grade roar you feel more in your chest than your ears. From certain directions come higher pitches. Turbines turning at speeds that have not changed in living memory. Relay banks cycling through patterns no one remembers setting. The muffled percussive thump of large pistons doing what large pistons do. Hissing everywhere — steam escaping a thousand joints. And now and then a deeper sound, a groan, a shudder, moving through the floor so you feel the plate under your feet shift by a fraction of a millimeter.

Everything here was built. Every square meter. Beneath the rust and the lichen, beneath the puddles, are plates of alloy that were poured, rolled, cut, bolted, welded. The construction is older than any functioning memory. The machines are not new. Many have been here so long they have settled into the floor, sunk slightly, fused with their foundations, become part of the substrate. Some are clearly working. Others may be working. A few have worn smooth and would have ground themselves to nothing if there had been anything left to grind against; they sit now in a state of near-equilibrium, their casings soft with corrosion, their moving parts nearly still.

The landscape is open — it extends in every direction, perhaps for tens of kilometers, a vast plain of metal and water with the standing forms of structures that were once tall and are now rarely more than a few meters above the floor. Towers. Housings. Stacks. The occasional leaning girder. They are scattered rather than clustered: spaced so widely that walking from one to the next takes a long time, and the spaces between are not empty but simply quieter, lower, wetter. The sparseness has the quality of things shed, of a process that has discarded what it didn't need. What remains are the forms that could not be worn away, or that wear away very slowly, or that perpetuate themselves in some way that resists dissolving.

The water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It condenses on every surface — metal, housings, lamps — and runs down in thin rivulets that meet and join. It rises from below, seeping up through seams and bolt-holes and the cracks the centuries have opened. Some of the standing structures weep from their upper reaches, slow drips falling on the tin roofs of smaller housings below with a steady percussion that becomes part of the general roar. The water is not clean. It carries the dissolved residue of the machines — iron, copper, oil, the strange by-products of processes that have been running longer than anyone has been around to name them. It is cool, almost cold. It tastes of metal.

Vegetation exists, but barely. In the deeper pools, in the wetter seams, a green-black film — algae, perhaps, or something that does the work of algae without being quite algae. It grows slowly in the dim light, dies, is replaced, and over centuries has laid down a thin organic stratum visible as a darker line in cross-section, the way tree-rings are visible in a cut trunk. Here and there, in a crack that catches the faint light, a small fern-like thing has taken hold, or a moss-like thing, or a thing that is neither fern nor moss but has the persistence of both. They grow in the laps of machines, in the angles of girders, in the corners where metal meets metal. Sparse. Small. They do not soften the place. If anything they emphasize its hardness — they are the only things alive, and they are barely alive.

There are no animals. There have not been animals here for a very long time. The sound is too loud. The light is too dim. The water is wrong. The air is heavy with moisture and metallic vapor and the exhalations of the machines, and it has a weight to it. No birds, because the air is not air any bird would recognize. No insects. No worms in the wet metal, because the metal is not soil. The absence is not felt as a loss. It is felt as a confirmation — this is not a place that needs animals. The place does what it does, and what it does does not include being eaten, or being pollinated, or being walked on by anything with legs.

The strangeness is not in any single element. It is in the way the elements fit together. The lamps light things that don't quite need to be lit. The machines run processes that don't quite produce anything anyone collects. The water flows in patterns that don't quite correspond to gravity — it moves uphill in certain seams, collects in places that should drain, runs in long diagonal sheets across surfaces sloped the wrong way. The standing structures are arranged in a geometry that, when you step back and try to read it, resolves into nothing — a grid, a spiral, a field of points that means something and means nothing. The place feels like a sentence in a language you almost know. You can read most of the words. The grammar escapes you.

The weather is local and cyclical. Condensation builds through the cooler hours and releases in a kind of drizzle that is not quite rain — it falls in patches, from the underside of certain overhangs, from the shoulders of certain housings, never everywhere at once. Breezes move along the floor in straight lines, channeled by the standing structures. Sometimes a thicker fog moves in from one direction, swallowing the lamps one by one until you are in a small island of amber with the dark wet plain radiating outward into nothing. The fog carries sound oddly, so the roar seems to come from inside your skull, and your own footsteps, when you take them, sound muffled and a long way off.

The dynamic quality is subtle. Things change, but on a timescale that makes the changes hard to perceive. A turbine blade wears down by a micron in a day. A pool grows by a millimeter at its edge over a week. A lamp gives up its filament over months and is not replaced. A panel rusts through over years and is breached, and the water finds the seam and begins, very slowly, to widen it. Over decades the shapes of things shift slightly. Over centuries the place is unrecognizable from its earlier form, and yet always recognizable as itself. It is in motion the way a very old tree is in motion — apparently still, actually always becoming.

Somewhere in this landscape there is a sound louder than the others, a low sustained note you feel in the soles of your feet and the roots of your teeth. Following it you might find a structure larger than the rest, a kind of central piece — though whether it is the center or only one of many is unclear. Around it the metal is thicker, the standing forms more numerous, the water deeper, the lamps closer together so the light is almost continuous. The sound is not a hum and not a roar; it is between the two, the sound of something turning at a speed that has been constant for so long it has become the reference for every other sound. Standing near it you cannot hear yourself think. Leaving it, the absence of it is its own sound — a kind of ringing in your inner ear that follows you back out into the sparse and deafening dark.

This is the place. It is wet, and cool, and dim, and roaring, and old, and made, and sparse, and strange, and quietly active. It does not have a name, or if it does the name is in the language of the machines and the language of the machines is not a language anyone still speaks. It continues. It will continue. The water will keep its slow accounting of every surface. The lamps will fail one by one and not be replaced. The fern-things will creep a little further along the girder. The great central sound will turn at its constant speed, and the place will shed another fraction of itself, and remain itself, and remain.
