The plateau does not announce itself. There is no horizon at which it begins. You are already on it, or you have been on it for some time, and it has not registered your arrival, because nothing here registers. The place is the same with you in it as without you.

The air is hot and very wet. It is the heat of a body — not a sun-blast but a contained warmth, the warmth of something alive or recently alive, a warmth that holds even in the shade, even at altitude. Breathing takes effort, because the air is thin. The combination should not hold — thin air at this height should be cool, should be biting — and yet the heat holds, and the wetness holds, and the only explanation is that the place has decided to be this way, and will not be talked out of it.

You are very high. The sky, when you can see it through the haze and the steam, is white and low, a ceiling rather than a vault. The sun, when it appears, is a pale disc without heat or direction, more an arrangement of light than a source. But the air around you is warm, and the stone beneath your feet is warm, and the water running down the walls of the buildings is warm, and you understand that this place has its own climate, the way a body has its own temperature.

The plateau is large — a few miles across, perhaps, though the edges of it are unreliable. You walk to what you believe is the edge and find a chasm, and beyond the chasm another plateau, and beyond that another chasm, and the pattern recurses downward (or upward, or sideways) as far as you can bring yourself to look. The plateau you stand on is one tier of a structure with no top, no bottom, and no clear reason to exist at all.

The city covers the plateau. It is dense in a way that cities are dense — building against building, terrace above terrace, narrow lanes threading between, occasionally opening into small plazas where shallow pools gather and steam lifts off them in slow columns. The buildings are tall, narrow, often five or six stories, with setbacks and balconies and overhangs. They are made of a dark mineral that is not quite stone and not quite coral. It is layered, accreted, like the work of water depositing calcium over centuries. The walls are ribbed with this layering, and they look as if they were grown in place by some patient secretion, and indeed they were. There are no tool marks. There is no mortar. There are no joints. The buildings are not constructed. They are precipitated, the way travertine builds at the mouth of a hot spring, the way a reef builds in warm water, the way a thing builds itself when given enough time and the right minerals.

The urban shape — the repetition, the alignment, the way the buildings face each other across narrow streets — is the work of the water and the plants. It is unclear which came first. It does not matter. The order is settled.

The plants are everywhere. They erupt from every ledge, every roof, every crack in the dark stone. They are huge. Ferns with fronds the length of a room. Vines as thick as an arm. Leaves broad enough to stand on. They layer over the buildings until, in many places, you cannot see the mineral beneath at all. The plants are dense, and they are wet, and they are not moving. They are fully grown, fully formed, and they hold that form with the patience of things that have nowhere else to go. No leaf turns. No vine swings. Nothing sways. They exist in their final state and they have been in it for a long time, and they will be in it for a long time.

The sound is enormous.

It is everywhere, and it is constant, and it is the only thing that is happening. It is the sound of water in freefall. Sheets of water come off every roof, off every terrace, off every ledge, off every broad leaf — a continuous precipitation that never reaches the ground, or reaches it too slowly to be seen falling. The water hangs. It descends at a rate the eye cannot catch, or it is held in place by some property of the air. Droplets are suspended. Sheets are suspended. And the sound they make — the roaring hiss, the percussive clatter, the white static of a million impacts — is constant and unchanging and so loud it has weight. You feel it in your chest. You feel it in the bones of your skull. You cannot hear a voice. You cannot hear your own footsteps. The sound is the city, or the city is the sound, and it has been this loud for as long as the city has existed.

There is a wind. It does not change. It blows through the narrow lanes at a constant speed, and where it meets the openings of the buildings — the doors, the windows, the gaps where walls have not quite closed — it sings, low and steady, a tone that does not waver. The sound of the water and the sound of the wind and the sound of the leaves vibrating in that wind all run together into one continuous roar, and the roar is the place.

There are no animals. There are no insects, no birds, no fish in the pools, no worms in the wet stone. The plants grow, but nothing eats them. Nothing pollinates them. They grow anyway, in the dense wet heat, in the impossible conditions, and they reach their final form, and they stop. There is no food chain. There is no decay. A frond that falls — and none fall, because nothing moves — would not rot, because nothing here changes.

The geometry of the city is wrong in a settled way. Streets meet at angles that are not ninety degrees and not anything else. A lane that goes straight will, if you follow it long enough, return you to where you began, but the turning happens at a place you cannot identify, and you are not certain you have curved. Stairs descend and you arrive higher than you began. A door opens onto a wall. A window opens onto the same room you are standing in. You can stand at an intersection and see down three lanes, and the lanes converge in the distance in a way that should not be possible — they meet, and at the meeting there is a building, and the building is also down the third lane, halfway along, and the building is the same building, or is not.

These contradictions are not experienced as disorienting, because nothing here moves. The wrongness is held in place. The buildings stand in their wrong configurations. The water runs down the wrong walls. The shadows — what shadows there are — fall in the wrong direction, sometimes upward, sometimes sideways, sometimes toward the light source. None of this changes. None of this is a problem. It is simply the way the place is.

The light is white and flat. The pale sun gives no direction. There is also light from below — from the pools, from the steam, from some quality of the air — so that things are visible from all sides at once. There is no chiaroscuro. There is only presence, the uniform illumination of a place that has agreed to be seen equally from every angle.

The city is old. It has the settled-form of a thing that has been in its own state for so long that the state has become its nature. There is no ruin, because there is nothing to ruin toward. There is no wear, because nothing moves against anything. The buildings are as whole as the day they finished precipitating. The plants are as lush as the day they finished growing. The water is as constant. This is not because the place is maintained. It is because the place is in equilibrium — the closed-system balance of heat and water and mineral and growth that has found its form and will hold it, the way a crystal holds its form, the way a stopped clock holds its form.

You cannot tell if the place was ever inhabited. There are no bodies, no bones, no worn steps, no fouled pools. The doors are not smoothed by hands. The plazas are not packed by feet. It is as if the city was always like this — a city without citizens, an urban form that emerged from the long patient work of water and plants into something that resembles a settlement because repetition, over time, becomes settlement. Or it is as if whoever was here has been gone so long that the place has finished forgetting them, and what remains is the form of inhabitation without the fact of it.

You stand in a small plaza. The water comes off the roof of the building beside you in a sheet, but the sheet does not arrive at the ground — it thins into steam before it lands, and the steam rises into the unchanging wind and is the air, and the air is the steam, and the place is a closed system of heat and water and dark mineral and wet green growth that has found itself and will not change.

The sound is so loud you cannot think. The sound is so constant you stop trying to. After a while, you do not hear it. You are simply inside it. You are simply in the place, and the place is in its state, and the state is the equilibrium of a thing that was once, perhaps, a settlement, and is now only itself — a hot wet high plateau where the water falls and does not fall, where the plants grow and do not move, where the sound roars and does not change, where the geometry is wrong and is not corrected, where there are no animals because the place does not require them, where there is no technology because the place has no need of devices, where the light is white and comes from many directions and lights nothing in particular.

You stand in the plaza. The water descends in a sheet beside you. The sheet is loud. The sheet is the same as it has always been. The place is in its state.
