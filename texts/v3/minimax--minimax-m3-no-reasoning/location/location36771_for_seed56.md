# The Submerging Garden

You come to it through a fracture in the bedrock — a fissure that didn't exist last century, and may not exist next — and then down. The first thing that reaches you is not a sight but a pressure in the ears, the gentle, insistent change of depth. You descend a long ramp of wet stone, the rock itself granular, soft-looking, as though it were a thing still deciding whether to be stone at all. Water beads on every surface in pearls the size of grapes. The light that filters down here is not the light of the surface world; it is older, slower, a kind of phosphorescence that the rock itself is giving back.

The chamber opens beneath you the way a lung opens around a breath.

---

## The Floor That Is a Throat

The floor of the chamber is not floor. It is the upper surface of a living tissue — call it moss, call it mycelium, call it the underside of something dreaming of being a forest — that has been quietly filling the available rock for the last several thousand years. Where you stand, the green-black mat compresses under your weight and a slow, patient seep of groundwater wells up around your foot and is greedily, immediately drunk. The whole floor breathes. It inhales on a cycle of perhaps nine minutes; you can feel the change in the air, a faint cooling, a faint movement toward you, and then the slow long exhale, which carries a warmth and a smell like the inside of a fistful of rotted leaves held to the face.

There is no cliff here, no horizon, no sense of being inside a thing that has a name. There is only the sense of being *within*.

---

## The Things That Have Made This Place

The chamber is roughly the size of a small cathedral — say a hundred paces across, perhaps forty high at the apex of its domed ceiling — but the architecture is not architectural. The chamber was excavated, or grown, or *remembered into existence* by the root-machinery of the plants that fill it. They have been here longer than the chamber has. They were here first. They ate the stone into a shape they preferred and then they kept eating it, slowly, the way a stream keeps eating its own bed.

And what fills the space is plant, plant, plant, plant, plant, plant.

The dominant organism is something between a fern and a nerve. It rises from the floor in pale, almost colorless stalks, each as thick as a man's thigh at the base, branching higher and higher into finer and finer whips that eventually dissolve into a fog of fine rootlets and cilia. At the crown, where the chamber's ceiling should be, the stalks have fused into a kind of false sky — a mat of pale, knotted, breathing tissue from which more roots descend, and from which, here and there, the great pendant structures hang down that the locals, if there were locals, would probably call the chimes.

The chimes are the size of children. They are translucent. They are full of water.

Inside each one, visible as a soft warm shape, something is suspended — a smaller chime, a fetus-chime, already growing its own internal organs of liquid. The chimes are not decorations. They are the reproductive bodies of the chamber, and they are *very slowly*, on a cycle measured in human generations, reaching maturity, ripening, and at last releasing their internal water — a slow rupture, a long, gentle, days-long weeping — which falls to the floor and is drunk by the mat below, and which carries with it the new chime's first cargo of seeds, or spores, or whatever these things are that the floor will accept into itself and begin to grow.

The falling water is what makes the sound. The whole chamber rings with it, gently, a long, low, continuous chime, never quite the same note, because the chimes are at different stages of ripeness and their internal waters are at different densities. Some are almost a tone. Some are a chord. The sound is just below the threshold of language; it does not feel like music, it feels like the world quietly speaking its own name to itself.

---

## The Air

The air is wet. Not foggy — wetter than fog. It is wet the way the inside of your mouth is wet. You are breathing water. Every breath is a small visible plume, a ghost that hangs and is then reabsorbed into the general damp.

And it is cold. The cold here is not the cold of a winter morning; it is the cold of being *underneath*, the cold of stones, the cold of things that have not seen the sun in geological time. It is patient. It is not trying to do anything to you. It will simply outlast you, whichever of you outlasts the other.

But the air is not still. The breathing of the floor sets it in slow, almost imperceptible motion. There is almost always a faint draft against the skin of your neck, a directionless movement, and on it, occasionally, a heavier breath that carries the smell of the deeper places — of clay, of iron, of the strange sweet rot of things being slowly digested by other things.

---

## The Things That Live Here

The chamber is not silent and it is not empty. The fauna here is small, pale, almost transparent in places, and very numerous.

Things like pale newts the length of your hand cling to the stalks of the great ferns, motionless for hours, then moving in sudden darts. They eat the cilia. They are eaten, in turn, by the things that look like wasps but are not wasps — they have too many wings, the wings are wet, and the wings make no sound. They drift through the air on those wet wings and they hunt by echo, and the chamber is so loud with the chime of the pendant chimes that their echolocation must be a private language heard only by themselves.

There are things that hang in the pendant chimes themselves, swimming in the slow internal waters — small, slow creatures the color of weak tea, almost motionless, their entire lives spent in a single chime, eating the nutrients suspended in the fluid, breeding, dying, and being slowly incorporated into the chime's ripening body. They are the chime's livestock. They do not know they are livestock. They do not, perhaps, know anything at all.

Higher, in the false sky of fused stalks, things the size of small bats roost by the thousand, packed shoulder to shoulder, heads down, wrapped in their own wings. They are not bats. They are, possibly, a stage in the life cycle of the chimes. They do not move during the day. They will, perhaps, all at once, on some slow cue, release their grip and fall — and from the fall, something else will be born. The chamber has not yet told you what.

---

## The Dimness

There is not much light. What there is comes from two sources.

The first is the faint phosphorescence of the rock itself, a slow, green-gold glow from mineral bacteria that live in the wet stone and that are themselves being very slowly eaten by the rootlets of the great ferns. The rock is, in a real sense, a stomach lining. The glow comes from inside the digestion.

The second is the chimes. Each one, when it is near ripeness, develops a soft internal warmth and a faint internal light — a light the color of a sunset seen through a cataract, rose and amber, that pulses once every several minutes. The whole false sky of the chamber, on a quiet evening, is a constellation of these slow pulses. The light is not enough to read by. It is enough to see the next ten feet of your own body by, and to see, at the edge of vision, the vast pale architecture of the fern-forest dimly lifting into the dimness.

You do not have the sense of being underground. You have the sense of being *in* something. The chamber does not feel like a place you are inside. It feels like the inside of a thing that is also, somehow, inside you.

---

## The Almost-Built

There are no walls. There is no door, no threshold, no ruins. And yet there is a sense, here, of something almost — *almost* — arranged.

The chimes, in their pendulous rows, hang at strikingly regular intervals. The great ferns rise in something that can only be called a colonnade, and they are spaced with a regularity that the eye wants to call deliberate. The floor, where it has not yet been eaten, is divided by faint, regular seams that suggest a tiling, or a pavement, or the ghost of a pavement — a grid of some long-vanished intention that the living mat has not yet quite managed to digest.

The chamber is, you begin to feel, the ruin of a ruin. There was something here before the plants. The plants came, and very slowly, very patiently, ate it. What the plants are now is, in some dim, unconscious, *structural* way, the building that was here before, but rebuilt out of themselves. The colonnade is the colonnade the plants remember from the colonnade that was here first. The chimes are the lamps the plants remember from the lamps that were here first. The floor is the floor the plants remember, but a floor that is alive and that is, very slowly, digesting the memory.

---

## The Strange

It is not a place for a human being. You know this without being told.

The water is drinkable, and if you drink it you will be quietly altered — not in any way you can name immediately, but you will find, over the following days, that your sleep is a little deeper, that your dreams are a little stranger, that you are not quite as interested in the surface of things as you used to be. You will begin to prefer the underneath. The plants, which are not intelligent in any way you can recognize, are nonetheless *attentive*, and they have been known to adjust the growth of their tissues around a sleeping visitor in ways that are, in retrospect, disturbingly comfortable. They are not malevolent. They are not benevolent. They are *interested*.

The technology that is here is not technology in any sense you have a name for. The plants are doing things that are not, as far as current physics can describe, possible — the way they fold time, the way the chimes seem to remember things that have not yet happened, the way the small newts can, occasionally, be seen walking up the walls and across the ceiling and back down again with a casual ease that the geometry of the chamber does not, strictly speaking, permit. You do not see this often. You see it the way you see a shooting star — once, by accident, and then you are not sure you saw it at all.

The engineering is *old*. It is older than the chamber. It is older than the rock. The plants are not using it. They are *made of* it. They are the technology, and the technology is the plants, and neither of them remembers, anymore, which came first.

---

## The Long, Slow Sentence

The chamber is not finished.

The ferns are still eating the rock. The chimes are still ripening. The floor is still breathing. The pale newts are still hunting. The wasp-things are still hunting the pale newts. The bats-that-may-not-be-bats are still hanging in the false sky, waiting for the cue. The water is still falling, very slowly, from chime to floor, from chime to floor, in a long quiet continuous chord.

A human being standing in this chamber for the first time has the strong, uncomfortable sense of having walked into the middle of a sentence that has been in progress for a very long time, and that will continue, in this same patient, dim, wet, breathing way, long after the human being has gone. The sentence does not need the human being to finish. The sentence will finish itself, in its own time, in its own language, with or without witnesses.

You stand in the dimness. The chimes ring. The floor breathes. Somewhere above you, a thousand miles above you, a sun you have begun to forget is shining on a world that is, perhaps, no longer quite the world you came from.

You are, you realize, not lost. You are simply *understood*.
