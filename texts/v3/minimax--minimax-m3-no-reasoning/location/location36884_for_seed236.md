# The Long Galleries of Velmoth

There is a city, if it can be called that, folded into the side of a mountain. I do not mean built upon the mountain, or carved into it, exactly. It has grown, in the way a tree grows around a stone, until the two things are difficult to separate. The stone is pale — the color of old ivory, of weathered bone, of dust in a sunlit room — and the city is not green so much as it is composed of green: a presence, a saturation, a kind of vegetal insistence.

The mountain rises in a long spine from a plain that is not quite a desert and not quite anything else. Beneath it, the ground is fine and pale, something that has the texture of flour, of talc, of powdered feldspar. When the wind moves, and the wind moves often here, the powder rises in low sheets and travels along the ground in pale rivers, and nothing grows in it. Or almost nothing. A few things have learned. A few things have decided.

But the mountain itself is a different sentence entirely.

I should describe how it sounds first, because the sound is what I remember. There is almost no sound. The air is thin and dry and it carries small noises very far; you can hear a single leaf fall from a great distance, and the soft percussion of it on the steps, and then the long held breath of dust rising to take its place. From the galleries themselves there is sometimes a low, slow creak, the sound wood makes in cold places, except that it is never cold here, and the sound comes from things that are not wood. I will come back to that.

The city is old. I want to be careful about this, because the question of how old is strange, and the answer is that nobody who lives here knows, and the question itself is a little beside the point. What matters is that it is *settled*. The galleries are not ruins. The terraces are not abandoned. The bridges still hold. But the things that hold them up have changed their nature, and the things that built them have become, over the slow generations, a kind of memory embedded in the structure of the place, the way the rings of a tree are a memory of years.

I should describe the geometry.

The spine of the mountain runs roughly east to west, a single long ridge with a curving profile, like a wave frozen at its crest. Along the southern face, where the sun is warmest and the prevailing wind is broken, the city has unfolded in a series of long horizontal bands. Each band is a terrace, or a gallery, or a balcony, depending on how you look at it, and they are stacked one above the other, connected by ramps and shallow stairs and the occasional vertical shaft of pale stone that has been worn into a kind of polished groove by the passage of things climbing it. There are perhaps thirty of these bands that are occupied, and another fifty that are not, and another hundred higher up that are still more or less empty, where the bare stone shows through in places like the white of an old nail.

The lowest band begins where the powder plain ends, at a line you can feel with your foot — the powder becomes coarser, becomes gravel, becomes a kind of pale scree, and then there is the first paved surface. It is not quite pavement. It has the look of pavement, the smooth swept look, but it is not stone. It is too warm under the hand, and too slightly yielding, and if you press your ear to it you can hear, very faintly, the same low creak I mentioned earlier, the sound of something vast and slow adjusting itself beneath you. The dwellers of the lowest band walk on it without noticing. I noticed because I was new to it.

The terraces above are of different widths. Some are narrow, no wider than a man can lie down with his arms at his sides. Some are wide enough to be little plazas, with a low wall along their outer edge where the mountain falls away to the air. From the upper terraces, on a clear day, you can see the powder plain below as a kind of pale sea, and you can see, far to the south, a smudge of darker color that is another range of mountains, very distant, and beyond it, if the haze is thin, the suggestion of a horizon that curves.

I will say this carefully, because I do not want to be misunderstood: the sky is the wrong color. It is not blue, or rather, it is only sometimes blue. Most often it is a kind of pale amber, the color of weak tea held up to a lamp, and the sun, when you find it, is not a disc but a brighter region, a luminance, a place where the amber becomes almost white. There are no clouds, or there are very few, and those that appear are thin and high and the color of bone.

This is a dry place, and the dryness is not just an absence. It is a presence. The air has a taste. The taste is mineral, slightly alkaline, with a faint sweetness underneath that I cannot account for. I think it comes from the plants, but I will come to the plants.

The plants.

I have to think about how to describe the plants.

The first thing to say is that there is no soil. Or very little soil. The terraces are mostly stone, or the substance that pretends to be stone, and yet the place is, as the parameters suggested, lush. Lush is not the right word, actually. The right word is something like *populated*, or *colonized*, or *saturated*. The terraces are full of plants in the way a coral reef is full of life, except that the plants here are not, or are not all of them, what we would normally call plants. They grow from the stone. They grow from each other. They grow from the air in some cases, or seem to. They hang over the edges of the terraces in long slow curtains that move very slightly in the wind, and they climb the vertical shafts, and they fill the spaces between the stones with a kind of dense, soft, breathing mass.

Let me try to give you a single example, and then I will have to admit that the example is not typical.

There is a kind of plant that I will call, for lack of a better word, a *flute*. It is a vertical thing, pale, the color of the stone it grows from, and it rises from the cracks between the paving stones in clusters of three or five or seven, like the pipes of a small organ. Each flute is about as tall as a man's chest, and about as thick as his arm, and it is open at the top, and closed at the bottom. When the wind blows across the open tops, the flutes make a sound. The sound is a low note, and each cluster plays a slightly different note, and together they make a kind of chord, or a kind of weather, depending on the wind. The dwellers of the terraces walk through this chord all day and do not remark on it. I remarked on it. I remarked on it for several days. Eventually I stopped remarking on it, and I think that is when I began to belong to the place, or the place began to belong to me, or some small part of the bargain was struck.

The flutes are not typical because they are simple. Most of the things that grow here are not simple.

There is a kind of trailing vine that has a kind of awareness of where you are walking, and it draws itself back from your path as you approach, and returns to its position after you have passed. I do not mean that it moves quickly. I mean that it has a memory, or a kind of slow patience, and it waits for you. After a few days, the vines on the terrace where I was staying had learned my route, and they drew themselves back before I reached them, and I never touched them, and we came to a kind of arrangement.

There is a kind of low shrub, dense and dark, the color of old bronze, that produces at certain seasons — and the seasons here are very long, the slow turning of a great wheel — a fruit that is not quite a fruit. The fruit is the size of a small apple, and the color of the sky on a clear day, and if you cut it open it has no seeds and no core, only a soft, sweet, faintly luminous flesh that tastes of nothing you have ever tasted and that I cannot describe. The dwellers of the terraces eat these fruits. They eat them with a kind of reverence, and they do not speak while they are eating them, and afterward they sit very still for a long time, and their eyes are a little brighter than before. I asked one of the dwellers what the fruit was called, and she did not answer for a long time, and then she said a word that I will not try to reproduce because I do not have the right sounds in my language.

There are other things. I will not enumerate them all. I will say that the place is full, and that the fullness is a kind of conversation, and that the conversation is slow, and that it has been going on for a very long time.

The dwellers.

I should describe the dwellers.

They are not, I think, a single people. They are more like a sediment, or a kind of accumulated community, a place where many kinds of creatures have come to rest over the long generations. Some of them are tall and pale and have long hands and move without sound. Some of them are shorter and darker and have a different relationship to the plants — they seem to know the plants, to have an understanding with them, and the plants seem to know them in return. Some of them are very small, and they live in the lower galleries, and they come out mostly at the times of the long amber twilights, and they are shy.

They are, all of them, very quiet. This is consistent with the silence of the place, but I think it is more than that. I think they have decided, in some collective and unspoken way, to be quiet, and the quiet is part of the long conversation that the place is having with itself. When they speak to each other, they speak in low voices, and they often do not speak at all, and they communicate with each other by small gestures, by small movements of the hands, by small inclinations of the head, and by the position of the body in space. I learned a little of this language, in the time I was there, and I will admit that I was a clumsy student.

They have few tools. Or rather, they have tools, but the tools are not separate from the place. A knife, here, is not a knife that you can pick up and put down. It is a particular plant, or a particular shaped stone, or a particular kind of dried and prepared fiber, and it is used for a particular purpose, and when it is not being used for that purpose it is not a knife, it is just a plant, or a stone, or a fiber. The distinction between tool and not-tool is, here, a matter of posture and intention, and this is true of almost everything in the place.

The light.

I have not said enough about the light. The light here is amber, as I said, and it comes from a sun that is more felt than seen, and it falls on the pale stone and the bronze plants and the pale dwellers in a way that makes everything look a little older than it is, and a little more itself. In the morning the light is thin and horizontal and it comes from the east through the long spine of the mountain, and it throws long shadows from the flutes and the vines and the dwellers, and the shadows are not black but a kind of deep amber, the color of the sky at the opposite end of the day. In the evening the light does the same thing from the other side, and the terraces glow. The upper terraces, in particular, in the long evenings, have a kind of luminance of their own, as if the stone itself has been storing the day's light and is now slowly giving it back, and the dwellers who sit on the upper terraces in the evening look, from below, like small figures painted in gold leaf on a pale wall.

The long evenings. The seasons. The slow wheel.

The time here is not like time elsewhere. Or rather, time is the same, but the way it is metered is different. The dwellers do not, as far as I could tell, have a precise sense of hours. They have a sense of the position of the light, and a sense of the season, which is a much longer thing, and a sense of the slow inner turning of the place, which is longer still. They speak of events in terms of how many fruits have ripened, or how many times the lower pool has dried, or how many times the long vines have climbed to the top of a particular shaft. These are the units. They are very long units.

The lower pool.

I have not mentioned the lower pool. The lower pool is the one feature of the place that contradicts the dryness, and I have to think about how to describe it, because it is not really a pool, or not only a pool. It is a basin, a long shallow basin cut into the lowest terrace, near the place where the powder plain begins. It is dry, mostly, or it holds a thin liquid that is not quite water, that has the color of the amber sky and the taste of the air and the faint sweetness of the fruit. The dwellers come to it, especially in the long twilights, and they kneel beside it, and they do not drink from it, or not often. They look into it. They look into it for a long time, and their reflections look back at them, and the reflections are not quite right — they are a little slower, a little more deliberate, a little more themselves — and this seems to satisfy something in the dwellers, and they rise after a while and go back to their terraces, and the pool waits for the next one.

I have not drunk from the pool. I will admit this. I came close, once, in the long evening, and I knelt beside it, and I looked into it, and I saw my own face looking back at me with that slow and slightly altered regard, and I did not drink, and I am not sure why. I think it was because I understood, kneeling there, that the place was not asking me to drink, and that to drink would have been to accept something I was not yet ready to accept, or to refuse something I was not yet ready to refuse. I rose. I went back to my terrace. The flutes played their low chord. The wind moved through the vines. The amber light did what the amber light does.

The upper galleries.

There are galleries higher up that are not occupied, as I said, and I went to a few of them, on the days when my legs were good. They are reached by the shallow stairs and the vertical shafts, and they are not dangerous, exactly, but they are quiet in a different way, and the plants there are different, and the light is different because the air is thinner. The pale stone is more exposed there, and the flutes are rarer, and there are other things, other growths, that I do not have words for. I went to one of the highest occupied galleries, on a day when the amber was particularly clear, and I sat on the low wall at the outer edge and I looked out over the powder plain and the distant mountains and the smudge of the further range, and I felt, for the first time, that I understood why the dwellers live where they live. It is because of the view. It is because of the long slow quality of the light, and the way the place makes you feel that you are a small figure in a large and patient composition, and that the composition is not finished.

I will not say that the place is beautiful. The word is too small, and it suggests too much of an idea of an outside observer appreciating a thing. The place is itself, and the dwellers are part of it, and I was, for a time, part of it, and the question of whether it is beautiful or not is a question for someone who has not been there, and I do not think I am that person anymore.

The wind.

I should say something about the wind. The wind here is a regular thing, a daily thing, a thing that can be relied upon. It comes up from the powder plain in the morning, and it moves along the terraces from east to west, and it plays the flutes and moves the vines and carries the small sounds very far, and in the evening it dies down, and there is a long stillness, and the amber light does what the amber light does, and the dwellers sit in their doorways and on their walls and do not speak.

There is a wind in the night, too, but a different one. It comes from the west, from the direction of the distant mountains, and it is cooler, and it carries a faint mineral smell, a smell of old stone and old distances, and it moves through the upper galleries and along the empty terraces and it makes the flutes play a different chord, a lower one, and the vines move in it, and the dwellers sleep, and the place is not quite the same place in the night.

I slept, most nights, on a low platform on one of the middle terraces, under a kind of canopy of woven fibers and broad leaves, and I listened to this night wind, and the small sounds of the place, and the creak of the substance beneath me, and the occasional small sound of an animal moving through the plants, and the very faint sound of the lower pool, which I had not realized made a sound until I was lying still in the night and could hear it, and it makes a sound like a very distant bell, or a very distant voice, or the memory of a sound heard a long time ago.

The fauna.

I have not said enough about the fauna. There is a kind of small creature that lives in the lower vines and comes out at the long twilights, and it is about the size of a squirrel but it is not a squirrel, and it has too many legs, and it moves with a kind of deliberate and graceful caution, and it has a small face that is almost a face, and it looks at you when you look at it, and it is not afraid. There is a larger creature that lives in the upper empty galleries, and I saw it only once, at a great distance, moving along a high terrace in the amber light, and it was tall and pale and it moved without sound, and I do not know what it was. There are small flying things that come out in the evening and move through the air in long slow arcs, and they are the color of the sky, and they are difficult to see, and the dwellers do not remark on them.

There is a creature that lives in the pools, when the pools have liquid in them, and it is a kind of small pale fish, or a kind of small pale eel, and it comes to the surface when the dwellers kneel beside the pool, and it looks at them, and the dwellers look at it, and there is a kind of exchange, a kind of slow mutual regard, and then the creature sinks again, and the dweller rises, and that is that.

The weirdness.

I have to admit that I am not, in the end, sure how to describe the weirdness of the place. I have tried to describe the plants, and the dwellers, and the pool, and the light, and the time, and I have not, I think, conveyed it. The weirdness is not in any one of these things. It is in the way they fit together. It is in the way the place makes you feel that the categories you have brought with you — plant and animal, tool and not-tool, water and not-water, time and not-time — are not quite right, and that the place has its own categories, and that its categories are not arbitrary but are the result of a long and patient process of becoming, and that you, the visitor, are being slowly inducted into those categories, and that the induction is not complete and may never be complete, and that this is, in some sense, the point.

The slow wheel turns. The amber light falls. The flutes play their low chord. The dwellers sit in their doorways. The pool waits. The vines draw back from your path. The fruit ripens in its long season. The lower creatures come out at the long twilights. The upper creature moves along the high terrace. The small flying things move in their long slow arcs through the amber air. The wind comes up from the plain in the morning and dies down in the evening, and comes again from the west in the night. The pale stone of the mountain holds the whole thing together, the way a hand holds a small and delicate thing, very gently, for a very long time.

I was there for a long time. I am not there now. I do not know if I will return. The place, I think, does not particularly mind either way. It will go on without me. It has been going on for a very long time, and it will go on for a very long time, and the slow wheel will turn, and the seasons will pass, and the flutes will play their chord, and the dwellers will eat the luminous fruit and sit in the long twilights and look into the pool, and the pool will give back their slow and altered reflections, and the vines will draw back from the paths of those who walk them, and the wind will move through the place, and the place will be the place.

I will say one more thing, and then I will stop.

On the last evening I was there, I went up to one of the high occupied galleries, and I sat on the low wall, and I looked out over the powder plain and the distant mountains, and the amber light was particularly clear, and a dweller came and sat beside me, one of the tall pale ones, and we sat together for a long time without speaking, and after a while she reached out and took my hand, and her hand was warm and dry, and we sat like that for what seemed like a long time, and the flutes played their chord, and the wind moved through the vines, and the long evening came on, and the upper terraces began to glow, and she let go of my hand, and she rose, and she went back down the shallow stairs to her own terrace, and I sat for a while longer, and then I rose, and I went down too, and that is the last thing that happened to me in the place of Velmoth, and I have carried it with me, and I will carry it for a long time.
