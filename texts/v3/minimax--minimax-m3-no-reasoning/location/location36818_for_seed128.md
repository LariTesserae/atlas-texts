# The Underbelly of the Bell

You find it by following the water.

Not the obvious water—there is none at the surface, none of the lakes or bright rivers you might expect in a place this wet. You find it because your boots darken, because the air suddenly tastes of iron and old chalk, because the sound comes up through your feet before it reaches your ears: a low, permanent, *tearing* hum, the kind of noise that lives in the lower register of stone and makes your molars itch. Then the ground opens, and you descend.

---

**The space** is a bell inverted. That is the first thing the guides tell you, and it is true in the sense that a map is true: useful, approximate, badly incomplete. The chamber is roughly ovoid, roughly three hundred meters across at its widest, and roughly *carved*—though carved suggests a chisel and a hand, and there has been no chisel here for a very long time. The walls are smooth. Not polished smooth, not the way a river polishes marble, but *engineered* smooth, the way a soap bubble is smooth, and they curve in long parabolas that meet overhead in a kind of crown, a junction of seven arches that, if you trace them upward with your lamp, vanish into a darkness the lamps do not want to enter.

Everything here is built. The floor beneath your boots is a single continuous material—a dull gray-bronze substance that hums faintly when struck, that warms under pressure, that is *trying*, you sense without understanding, to do something useful with your weight. It is not metal. It is not ceramic. The technicians in the upper galleries have names for it, none of them flattering, and the names change every decade.

The walls are the same substance, continuous with the floor, continuous with the ceiling, continuous with the seven arches. There is no seam anywhere. You could walk this chamber for hours and find no joint, no rivet, no place where one piece ends and another begins. The architects—if they were architects, if they were people—either built this in one pour, or built it so well that time has forgotten how.

---

**The water** is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps from the walls in sheets so thin they glitter, and where a sheet meets the floor it does not pool but *vanishes*, drawn down through pores that close behind it. It condenses on your skin within seconds of entering. The air is near saturation, a wet towel pressed against your face; you can feel the moisture in your lungs before you take your third breath. The humidity is not accidental. Some part of the machinery of this place wants the air to be heavy, wants it to conduct, wants the chamber to be a single slow battery of damp and bronze. When the great process downstairs cycles, the walls sweat, and you can taste the change on your tongue: sweeter, sharper, alive.

There are no puddles. There are no streams. There is the constant weeping, and the constant disappearance, and the sound of water going somewhere you cannot follow.

---

**The hum** is the first thing you stop hearing, which is the most dangerous thing in the chamber. It begins at the threshold—a pressure on the eardrum, a vibration in the teeth—and by the time you have been inside for ten minutes it has become furniture. By the time you have been inside for an hour, you have forgotten your own voice. People speak to each other here by leaning close and mouthing words into each other's ears, or by the older method of hand-sign, which the long-term technicians prefer because it does not interrupt the listening.

The hum is not one sound. It is many sounds layered: a deep subsonic pulse that arrives through the floor; a mid-range roar like wind in a chimney, but *tuned*, harmonic, almost musical; and a high thin hiss that lives at the upper edge of hearing and skitters across the walls like sand on glass. Somewhere beneath all of it, almost too low to perceive, is a slow *throb*, a heartbeat rhythm that you eventually synchronize with, whether you want to or not. After two days you stop trying to breathe against it.

The technicians call this *the tone*. They have been listening to it for generations. They have not yet explained it.

---

**The fauna** is the second thing the guides warn you about, because the fauna here does not behave like fauna anywhere else.

It lives in the walls.

You cannot see it at first. The walls weep, the lamps catch the weeping, and your eye slides over the surface looking for landmarks and finds none. But after an hour, in your peripheral vision, you begin to catch movement: a slow uncurling along a seam that is not a seam; a flicker of darker bronze against the bronze; a shape that is not there when you turn to look.

After two hours you stop trying to look directly at it.

They are flat. That is the first consistent observation. They are flat against the walls, pressed so close they seem to be *part* of the wall, and they move by a kind of ripple, a redistribution of their own mass, the way a drop of mercury moves across a surface. They are warm to the touch. They have, somewhere on their bodies, an opening that is not a mouth—technicians call it the bell, which seems like a joke until you realize the technicians are not joking—and through this opening they emit a sound that is a single pure note, and each individual emits a different note, and the notes together form the hum.

The hum is not the chamber. The hum is *them*. They are the chorus. They have been singing this note, all of them together, for longer than the chamber has existed, because the chamber was built *around* them, or *for* them, or *of* them; the technicians no longer argue about this, because the answer is always the same: *we don't know*.

They are abundant. There must be tens of thousands in this chamber alone, perhaps hundreds of thousands, pressed into every available surface, every wall, every arch, every curve of the ceiling that the lamps do not penetrate. They cluster thickest where the water weeps fastest. They eat the water, or the minerals in the water, or the electricity that the water seems to carry; nobody has ever seen one eat. They reproduce by splitting along their long axis, which they do silently and quickly, in the dark hours when the chamber is empty of human observers. They die by simply stopping the note. The note fades. The body loses its grip on the wall and falls. Nothing eats the bodies. They dissolve back into the material of the floor over a few days, and the floor is *thicker* where they have been.

---

**The light** is dim and wrong. The lamps the technicians carry are not electric, exactly—they are some variant of the wall-material, taken from the chamber in small pieces and shaped into rods, and they give off a yellow-orange light that flickers at the same frequency as the hum. You get used to the color. You do not get used to the flicker. After an hour your eyes ache. After two hours you begin to see shapes in the dimness that may or may not be there; the technicians call these *echoes* and consider them unremarkable.

There are darker regions of the chamber. The seven arches each branch into smaller passages, and the passages go up into the crown, and the crown is not lit at all, and no one goes there. Sometimes, late in a shift, a technician will claim to see a light moving in the upper darkness—a slow swinging, like a lantern carried by someone climbing a rope ladder. The technicians who make this claim are usually senior. They are usually not contradicted.

---

**The vegetation** is not vegetation at all. It is a kind of growth that has learned to live on the walls without being either fauna or flora, a brown feathery bloom that appears in patches where the water is heaviest and the note is highest. It does not move. It does not flower. It smells, faintly, of cinnamon and old paper. The technicians harvest it carefully, in small jars, and they take it upstairs, and they do not say what it is for.

It is sparse. Almost everything in this chamber is the bronze substance or the creatures or the water or the air. The growth is a rare exception, and the technicians treat it as rare.

---

**The erosion** is the strangest thing here, and the most troubling if you let yourself think about it. The chamber has been here a very long time—long enough that the bronze has worn smooth in places, long enough that the arches have acquired the kind of patina that mountains acquire over millennia. But the bronze does not *behave* like a substance that erodes. Where the surface has been worn, new surface appears underneath, identical, unmarked. The chamber is settling into a shape, the technicians say, the shape it has always been tending toward. They say it as though this is reassuring.

You will notice, if you stay long enough, that the chamber is *very slightly smaller than it was when you entered*. Not in any way you can measure. Not in any way the instruments can detect. But the air feels closer. The arches feel lower. The walls feel nearer. The chamber is *deciding*, slowly, what shape it wants to be, and it is becoming that shape, and the shape is tighter than the one it started as.

The technicians do not know what it is becoming.

---

**The dynamic** is slow and total. The water falls and disappears. The creatures sing. The growth extends and is harvested. The chamber shrinks, imperceptibly. The hum continues, undimmed, unchanging in its volume, although the technicians insist it has *changed in color* over the years, has shifted in ways that are not measurable but are felt by the long-term staff as a kind of seasonal melancholy. There is weather here in the old sense: cycles of weeping and dryness, cycles of growth and harvest, cycles of note and silence when, for reasons no one understands, all the creatures stop singing at once for several minutes, and the silence is so loud that people fall to their knees.

---

**The rules** are not spoken, but they exist:

You do not speak above a whisper. You do not touch the walls with bare skin if you can avoid it. You do not look at the creatures directly. You do not go into the upper arches. You do not stay longer than nine days; after nine days the synchronization with the hum becomes difficult to undo, and there are technicians who never came back up, who live in the chamber now in a way that is not exactly living and not exactly not-living, and who are still, after decades, listening for something in the note that the rest of us cannot hear.

---

You find the chamber by following the water. You leave it the same way: upward, through the throat of the bell, into a colder air, into a quieter world. Above you, the rain is falling on green hills, and the rivers run bright and obvious, and the only sound is the wind in the grass.

For days afterward, you hear the hum.

For the rest of your life, you are not certain you have stopped.
