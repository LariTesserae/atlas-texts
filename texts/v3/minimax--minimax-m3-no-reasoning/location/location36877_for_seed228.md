# The Condensary

The first thing is the roar.

It does not stop. It has not stopped for as long as this place has existed, and the place is older than the species that ever measured such things. The sound pours down from above — a constant, vertical rain of noise, a waterfall of pressure that vibrates in your sternum before your ears even register it. It is the sound of water meeting itself at speed, of steam meeting stone, of something that should not be possible meeting itself and not caring.

You are underground. You know this because there is no sky, only a ceiling of red-gold limestone weeping water in long mineral threads, each one catching the light and turning it into a hanging filament of fire. The light has no source you can find. It is simply *there* — bright as noon, warm as late summer, falling from the rock itself as if the stone had remembered how to burn. Your eyes water. Not from the wet. From the brightness.

The chamber is roughly oval, cathedral-sized, perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest. The walls curve up and in. The floor is shallow water, ankle-deep in most places, chest-deep in others, never more than that — a flooded plain that stretches across the whole interior. You can feel the temperature of it through your boots: warm. Warmer than it should be for water this deep in the earth. The warmth rises from below. Something down there is working.

And the water is *moving*. That is the loud part, in part. The floor of the chamber is not still. Currents cross it in long lines, slow but relentless, carrying warmth and a faint mineral smell — sulfur at the edges, copper where the rocks are green, and underneath it all something cleaner, sharper, like the air after a long storm. Where two currents meet, the water lifts slightly, a standing wave no higher than your shin, and there the surface trembles and a sound comes: a low, thrumming chord, felt in the teeth.

The stone pillars rise from the water at intervals. Not close together — you could throw a stone between them and have it land twice — but they are there, and they have been there. Ten, twelve of them that you can see. They are not natural. The erosion has worked on them for so long that the original shape is almost gone, but you can still see the suggestion of angles where the angles wanted to be, of flat faces where flat faces were once cut. They are built of something that is not stone but has become stone, or has been persuaded to forget it was ever anything else. They are warm to the touch. They hum.

Vegetation, such as it is, clings to the pillars above the waterline. Long, pale roots drape from their crowns like wet hair, trailing down into the pool. They are not quite plants. They have no leaves. They have no flowers. They are fibrous, faintly luminescent where the light catches them — a sickly green-gold, the color of a river seen at night through a lantern. They grow where they grow, and they do not seem to need much. A few have climbed the walls. One has reached the ceiling and is drinking from it, its tip lost in the weeping mineral threads. It hums too, slightly out of tune with the pillars, and where it hums the air smells of ozone and old rain.

The ceiling is moving. Slowly. Continuously. The water seeps down in long threads, gathers in scalloped basins of stone, and pours from each basin into the next in a thousand small waterfalls, none of them more than a finger's width but all of them adding to the roar. The basins refill in seconds. The water that leaves them has gone somewhere, has been used, has been *changed*, and comes back warm. You do not know how the water comes back. You can guess it goes down through the floor, through channels that the pillars were once built to mark, through systems older than the system, and returns. A closed thing. A circuit. The Condensary, if that is what this is, breathes water the way a body breathes air.

There is a structure in the middle.

A single building — that is the wrong word. A single *presence*, set on a low mound of stone at the center of the chamber where the currents meet and the standing wave is highest. It is a ring of black metal — metal that does not rust in the wet air, does not corrode, does not tarnish — perhaps fifteen paces across, perhaps two high, perfectly circular, perfectly smooth on its inner face. The outer face is scribed with marks that do not repeat and do not resolve into language. The marks are deep and old and have begun to grow over with the pale root-fibers, but the fibers cannot quite settle into the incisions. They bridge them. They hum around them. The marks are cooler than the metal around them. You can tell because the water films the metal everywhere except across the scribed lines, and across the scribed lines the metal stays dry, as if the lines themselves are pushing the wet away.

Something is happening inside the ring.

You cannot see it directly. When you try, your eyes slide off, the way your eyes slide off a thing too bright to look at, except this is not bright — it is *indistinct*, as if the air inside the ring is being rubbed by a thumb and is refusing to settle. But you can hear it: a slow, patient sound, like a great wheel turning in honey, like the inside of a bell heard from the wrong side. And you can feel it: when you stand close to the ring, the warmth in the floor rises through your boots and up your legs and meets the cool of the scribed lines on your face and the two temperatures do not agree. They argue, gently, in your skin.

The ring is doing something to the water. The currents bend toward it, faintly, and the pale roots lean toward it, more strongly, and the chamber's hum changes key when you stand at certain points relative to it. You can feel it listening. You can feel it processing. You cannot tell what it is making. It might be making the water warm. It might be making the ceiling weep. It might be making the light. It might be making the roots, or the pillars, or the room. You suspect it has been here longer than the room has.

The dynamic is the thing. The place is not stable in the way a stable place is stable. It is stable the way a vortex is stable — a pattern that holds because it is constantly *running*, constantly being enacted, constantly being lost and remade. If the ring stopped, you suspect the water would stop. The roots would stop. The light would drain from the stone like blood from a cut. The roar would taper to silence and the chamber would become what it appears to be at first glance: a wet hole in the ground with some pillars in it. You do not know this for certain. You know it the way you know that the heart is doing something.

The fauna are rare, and they are shy of the light. You have seen, once, a small pale shape move between two pillars at the far edge of the chamber — a thing with too many legs, perhaps, or with legs that did not divide clearly into pairs, low to the water, quick. It moved from one shadowed patch of wall to another. The shadows here are not dark — they are merely less bright. The thing preferred them. It left no ripple. It made no sound. Whether there are more of them, or whether there was ever more than the one, you cannot tell.

The air is heavy and wet and full. It tastes of mineral and warmth and the faint copper-sulfur of the deep places. It condenses on every surface, including you — your skin, your clothes, the lenses of any instruments you have brought. Within minutes you are beaded with water that is not quite water, that has a faint slickness to it, that smells faintly of the roots. It does not run off. It waits to be wiped. When you wipe it, your skin beneath is pink and warm and somehow cleaner than it was before you came down.

The noise, when you listen for it past the roar of the water, has texture. There is the high hiss of the ceiling falls. There is the lower chord of the meeting currents. There is the almost-subsonic hum of the pillars, layered, slightly out of phase with itself, so that the sound seems to come from a direction that is not where any pillar stands. There is the patient wheel-sound of the ring. There are clicks, occasional, from far up in the roots — a small, dry sound, like a fingertip tapping on glass. You cannot find what makes them. You suspect the roots are talking to each other. You suspect they have been talking to each other for a very long time.

There is no weather down here. There is no day. There is no night. There is only the chamber and its running and its light and its listening, and the slow patient work of something that is older than engineering and more patient than engineering should be, doing something you cannot see to water you can drink and air you can almost breathe. You stand at the edge of it. You are a small warm thing in a small warm room in the middle of a wet bright world that has not paused to notice you.

It will not pause.

You are welcome to stay as long as you can stand the noise.
