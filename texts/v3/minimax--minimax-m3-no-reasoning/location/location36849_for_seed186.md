# The Breath-Gorge of Olethreon

The air is thin enough to taste. Cold enough to crack. And yet green — impossibly, defiantly, almost insultingly green — green as though the gorge had made a wager with some god of altitude and won.

The place is a wound in a mountain, or perhaps a mouth. The walls on either side rise two vertical miles, basalt and greywacke sheeting upward in folding strata, each layer a different mood of stone: rust-red shale near the bottom, then a band of pale limestone that bleaches out under what little light reaches it, then darker and darker rock until the rims vanish into the violet-grey of perpetual overcast. The gorge runs north-south for roughly thirty miles. It is wide enough at the floor that you cannot hear a person shouting from the opposite wall, narrow enough that you always feel the walls. The scale is meant to make you feel both small and unsettled. It succeeds.

---

**Water and motion**

The gorge has a river — the Oleth, the locals would call it, if there were locals. The Oleth does not behave as rivers do. It is fed from above, mostly, by condensation and the perpetual slow weeping of the cliffs: a thousand thin rivulets that bead on the rock and trickle down, joining the main thread along the way. In some places the walls simply *weep* — you can hear them, faintly, the sound of wet stone giving up its moisture. In others, a true pour will appear, a thread of water the width of a wrist falling two thousand feet, never quite reaching the bottom because the wind shatters it. The mist from these ghost-falls hangs in the air in places and never dissipates. You walk through it and come out damp.

The river itself is a steely colour and moves fast but not violently, more like something that has places to be than something angry. It is also, in stretches, absent. There are reaches where the water simply goes underground, sucked down through fissures in the gorge floor, and the bed is dry for half a mile or more, then suddenly the river is back, full, as if it had been continuing its business in another room.

The dampness here is *intermittent but assertive*. You cannot get fully dry. Stone sweats. The mosses that climb thirty feet up the cliff base are always wet, always drinking, and their colour is the colour of this place: a green so saturated it looks black from any distance.

---

**Vegetation, against sense**

At two vertical miles of elevation, the gorge floor is technically above the treeline. There are no trees. There is no business being lush here, and yet.

The vegetation has made its own arrangement. It clings to the cliff base in a thick, ankle-high to knee-high mat of mosses, lichens, dwarf willows no taller than your thumb, alpine sedges, and a few scraggly heath shrubs. This mat is continuous — for thirty miles of gorge, an unbroken carpet of growth along both walls, four to six feet wide, like a green tide-mark. It drinks the wall-weep and the river mist and the condensation that forms when the night cold collapses against the warmer stone.

There are also, inexplicably, fungi. Whole drifts of them — pale, faintly phosphorescent, growing in shelf-form on the damp cliffs and in the cool crevices. They are not large. But in the dimness, especially in the deeper reaches where the gorge narrows, they are enough. The gorge is never quite dark, because the fungi glow, faintly, a cool blue-white, just enough to see your hand before your face. They are the lamps of the place, and they have been burning for a very long time.

And there is the strangeness: scattered along the gorge floor, here and there, a handful of stunted, twisted conifers. They should not be here. They are not native. They look as though they were *placed* — each one alone, each one different, each one leaning toward the river. The wood is pale, almost white. They grow perhaps two feet tall in a century, and they are very old. Nobody planted them. They are not here for any reason anyone has been able to determine. They simply are.

---

**Atmosphere and light**

The light is poor. The gorge is narrow enough that direct sun reaches the floor for perhaps an hour around midday in summer, and never in winter. The rest of the time, the light is the light of overcast skies seen through a slot — diffuse, cool, and weak. By late afternoon the floor is in deep shade. By night, with no moon and the overcast holding, it is near-black, lit only by the fungi and by the river, which catches whatever faint glow there is and wrings it into a dull silver sheen.

The air itself has a quality. It is cold, yes — mean temperature something like 2°C, dropping well below freezing in the long winters — but it is also *still* in a way that is unusual for a gorge. The wind that should scream down such a channel is somehow muffled. It comes in currents, but the currents die at the floor. The walls give off their own cooler air at night, and this pools in the bottom, so the floor is always colder than the rims. Mist, when it forms, lingers for hours. Condensation beads on every surface. Your breath is always visible.

The atmospheric pressure at the floor is roughly sixty percent of sea-level. The first day here, your head aches. The second day, you adjust. The third day, you feel as though you have never been anywhere else.

---

**Fauna**

For a place that feels almost empty, there is a great deal of life.

The gorge floor is home to a species of mountain goat — though calling them goats undersells them. They are pale, almost luminous, with wide-spaced eyes and a strange high-stepping gait, as though the ground were not quite to be trusted. They move in small herds of four to seven, browsing the sedge-mat, and they are extraordinarily quiet. You will be standing ten feet from one before you notice it. Their hooves make no sound on the moss.

There are foxes — small, dark-furred, sharp-eared, with a yip that carries strangely well in the still air. They hunt something, but not anything you can see at first glance.

There are insects. Pale moths, near the size of your thumb, that drift in the fungi-glow at night like sparks. Crane-flies with absurdly long legs, bouncing along the wall. A kind of stone-cricket that sings, very faintly, even in winter, in the crevices. The sound of the gorge, when there is sound, is mostly the moths' near-silent wing-beats, the fox-yips at dusk, and a faint, unplaceable piping from somewhere above.

There are also birds, but only one species: a small, dark, sharp-billed thing that nests in the cliff-crevices and hunts the moths and the crickets. It does not sing. It does not call. It moves in silence between the rocks.

And there is the river. The river has fish in it — pale, blind, tasting their way along the bottom. They surface rarely. When they do, it is a brief flash of silver and back into the dark water.

---

**Industry, and the strange machines**

There are machines here.

This is the part that does not fit, and yet it does.

Along the floor of the gorge, at intervals of perhaps a mile, there are structures — though *structures* is the wrong word. They are arrangements. A series of stone pillars, roughly shaped, roughly spaced. Some of them are aligned with the river; some of them span the gorge at angles no engineer would approve; some of them stand alone, with no apparent function. They are built of the gorge's own stone, but cut, and laid with a precision that the rest of the gorge's wildness contradicts. There is no mortar. They have stood for a very long time.

And, scattered among these pillars, there are *things*. Cylinders of dark metal, perhaps ten feet tall, that hum — faintly, always. Their pitch is just at the edge of hearing, so you feel them more than you hear them, a pressure in the inner ear. They are warm to the touch, even in winter, even in this cold. They have no visible mechanism. They have been running for as long as anyone — anyone at all — has been able to determine.

The cylinders do something. They pull, or push, or generate — something. The river's behaviour, the wall-weep, the fungi's phosphorescence, the way the mist behaves, the way the air lies still at the floor — there is a theory, held by the very few who have ever been here and cared to theorize, that the cylinders *maintain* the gorge. That they are the reason the river weeps from the walls instead of running off. That they are the reason the fungi glow. That they are the reason the conifers, against all reason, persist.

The theory may be wrong. The cylinders may do nothing at all, and the gorge may simply be the gorge. But they hum. And the place is strange. And strangeness often hums.

There are no roads leading to the cylinders. There are no footprints near them. There are no people. No one lives here. No one comes here. The cylinders run, and no one tends them, and no one knows who built them.

---

**Sounds**

The gorge is quiet. This is partly the altitude and the thin air, and partly the still weather, and partly that not much here makes a noise. The river is the loudest thing, and it is not loud — a low, continuous hush, like a long exhalation. After that, the wind against the upper rims, but it is far away and sounds like distant surf. The goats make no sound. The foxes, occasionally. The stone-crickets, almost subliminally. The cylinders, just at the edge of hearing.

When the wind does come down into the gorge proper, it is sudden and disorienting — a cold slap of moving air that lasts seconds and is gone. The mist shifts. The river glints. The fungi-pale flickers in your vision. Then the stillness returns, and you remember you had stopped breathing.

---

**Dynamic, in time**

The gorge has its seasons, faintly, because the rims above take the brunt of the weather. In summer, the mist thickens and the river rises and the moss-mat drinks deep and is greener than you can credit. In winter, the floor is buried in a particular kind of dry, fine, blown snow that sifts down from the rims but does not accumulate deep, and the river shrinks, and the fungi's glow is more visible against the white. Spring and autumn are short and almost indistinguishable — a week or two of slow change, of melt or first frost, and then the gorge settles back into its usual dimness.

There is also a longer cycle. The river, the locals — there are no locals — the *records* (there are no records) say that every nineteen years, in late summer, the river goes away. Not underground, but away. The bed is dry. The mist stops. The fungi dim. The cylinders, witnesses say, drop their pitch by a half-tone and hold it for the duration. This lasts three days. On the third night, the river returns — suddenly, in full, as if a dam had burst somewhere upstream. The moss-mat drinks. The mist resumes. The cylinders return to their pitch. The gorge, briefly alarmed, resumes being itself.

The gorge has done this for as long as anyone has marked it.

---

**The feeling of the place**

You come down into the Breath-Gorge of Olethreon along a path that is not really a path — a tilted shelf of stone, partly natural, partly not, that descends from the south rim. You come down out of the wind. You come down into the cold and the dim and the green. You see the river. You see the pillars. You hear the hum. You walk for an hour and see nothing you expected to see, and the things you see you cannot place.

By the second day, the gorge has become familiar. By the third, you have stopped trying to name it.

By the end of the week, you do not want to leave.

This is, perhaps, what the cylinders do.
