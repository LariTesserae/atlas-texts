The Carpentry of the Green Dark

You stand on a low swell of biomass that might, from a distance, be mistaken for a hill. It is not a hill. The surface beneath your feet gives with the faint fibrous resistance of living wood — old growth, dense-grained, the color of damp walnut left out in cold sun. When you kneel and press your palm flat, you can feel it breathing. Not metaphorically. A slow expansion and contraction, perhaps one cycle per minute, and against your skin the warmth of metabolic heat, like touching the flank of some vast sleeping herbivore.

There is no water here. That is the first thing the place teaches you, and it teaches it gently, over hours. Your mouth does not dry the way it would in a desert. The moisture is held elsewhere — inside the trunks, inside the leaves, inside the architecture that surrounds you for as far as you can see in every direction. The air tastes resinous, faintly sweet, with an undercurrent of something metallic you eventually identify as the ionized tang of working machinery, distant and diffuse.

---

The light is generous and it is wrong. It comes from no single sun. The canopy — if it can be called a canopy — is too high and too broken for that word. The growth rises in columns, in ropes, in braided cables of stem and vine from a common substrate several meters thick, and these climb and weave until they form, perhaps fifty meters up, a kind of cathedral ceiling that is not a ceiling. Gaps between the columns admit a sky that is bright and colorless, the white of overcast, but overcast implies weather and weather here implies water and water is the wrong category. What you see when you look up is the underside of more growth, layer upon layer, and through the lattice of it a luminosity that may be diffuse daylight, may be bioluminescence, may be something else, but is always bright, and is always cool. The temperature does not rise above what you would call a brisk autumn afternoon. It does not fall below what you would call a chilly morning. The plants are not photosynthesizing. They have not been photosynthesizing for a long time.

They have been doing something else.

---

The architecture is total because there is no architecture. The distinction has ceased to apply. The buildings here are made of growth and the growth is made of buildings; you cannot tell where the engineered ends and the biological begins because the engineers stopped distinguishing long ago, if they ever did. A wall is a trunk that was once a beam that was once a living sapling that was trained, braced, inoculated, fed through tubes of copper and rubber that are themselves now encased in bark. A floor is a rootplate. A roof is a span of branches that have been convinced, through decades of selective pruning and graftwork, to grow in an arc.

You pass under what might have been a doorway — two verticals of oak-like timber, scarred with the pale horizontal stripes of removed scaffolding — and you enter a chamber. The chamber is the size of a small cathedral. Its floor is sprung beneath your step. Its walls breathe. High above, the joined canopies of what must be a dozen individual trunks admit the cool white light in shafts that fall like the light through clerestory windows, and in those shafts you see, drifting, slow as snowfall in a still room, motes. Not pollen. Not dust. You watch one for a long time. It is angular. It catches the light in facets. It is, you realize, a seed. One of the engineered seeds, drifting down from the highest stories to germinate in the engineered soil below, completing some cycle of reproduction that has been going on for centuries and which you, in your brief visit, will only ever see one frame of.

There is machinery here. You find a pump — a thing of cast iron and pale ceramic, once polished, now furred with a soft green growth that looks like moss but is not moss. It turns, very slowly, at one revolution every several minutes. You crouch beside it and put your ear near. Through the green fur you hear a deep mechanical pulse, and through the wood of the floor you feel a matching pulse, and the two are synchronized. The pump is moving sap. The sap is moving nutrients. The nutrients are moving through a network of buried pipes and channels and living root conduits that you cannot trace from above, and the whole of this vast living floor is the substrate of a circulation system that has, over generations, been elaborated until it has the redundancy of a river delta. Somewhere, in a chamber you have not found, the pumps labor; somewhere, the nutrients are stripped and recombined; somewhere, the waste is processed. You cannot find these places because the growth has healed over them.

A light comes on. It is pale blue, the color of a welder's arc seen through smoked glass. It comes from a fixture suspended in the joined branches above you, a glass shell the size of your head, and the shell is veined with copper wire and the copper wire is veined with green. The light flickers twice, holds steady, and goes out. A few moments later another comes on, some distance away, and another, and another, and you realize you are watching a chain — a sequence of ignitions that runs along some unseen corridor, perhaps along the entire length of this structure, lighting as it goes, the way a string of lamps lights along a thoroughfare in a city you have never visited. The chain dies. The chamber is lit only by the cool white from above.

---

There is sound, but the sound is the sound of a held breath. The breathing of the substrate. The creak of trunks shifting in some slow, internal sway that you only notice if you stand still for several minutes and wait. From far away — the distance is hard to judge, because there are no echoes, the wood absorbs everything — you hear the occasional percussion of a seed pod falling, or a branch adjusting, or something mechanical engaging and then disengaging with a sigh. You hear, once, a sound like the low note of a struck drum, and you never learn what caused it.

The fauna are rare. You will see, if you are lucky, in your whole time here, perhaps three living things that are not plants. A pale moth with translucent wings, resting on a trunk, fanning itself slowly. A beetle the color of verdigris, moving with great deliberation across a root. And once, late, in a chamber where the light from above came in at a particularly low angle, a small mammal — or what you take for a mammal, something warm-blooded, something that looked back at you with black, wet, considering eyes. It was the size of a squirrel. It did not flee. It watched you, and you watched it, and after a long time it turned and moved away into a hollow in the wood that had not been there the day before, or had been there and had been hidden.

The mammal is the only thing here that is unambiguously not engineered. The moth may be engineered; many of the moths are, their wing patterns carrying pigmented grafts that release scent on certain schedules. The beetle is certainly engineered; you can see the fine seam where its carapace was fitted, and the copper glint of an internal mechanism when it lifts its elytra to adjust. The plants are all engineered. The very soil — if soil is the right word, this dark friable medium that smells of humus and old iron — is engineered, a designed substrate of composted biomass and mineral grits and recycled ceramics.

---

You walk for what may be hours. You do not find the edge. You find chambers that open into corridors that open into chambers. You find a stair, or what was a stair, the treads grown over so that they are now gentle rises, the risers bulged with the slow expansion of trunk. You climb, and the chambers above are smaller, and the light from above is brighter, and the trunks that pass through them are thinner, younger, and you can see in their bark the pale, neat spirals of training wire still embedded. You find a room where the floor is a single unbroken span of polished rootwood, and on the wall there is a panel, and the panel is covered with a fine tracery of veins — green, living, pulsing faintly — and in the panel, set like a tooth in a gum, a disc of dark glass. You put your hand near the disc. It is warm. You cannot read what the veins are saying. You withdraw your hand.

The places that the builders — the gardeners — left empty have been filled. The places they left open have closed. The pipes they laid have been grown around, incorporated, become part of the circulation of the wood. The ducts they cleared have been reoccupied by roots, by fungal threads, by the fine white mycelium that you sometimes see in cross-section where a trunk has been pruned and has healed over. The engineers and the engineered have, over the long centuries, become indistinguishable. You are walking through a building that is also a forest that is also a machine that is also a single organism that has, perhaps, forgotten which of these things it is.

---

At night — and there is a night, or a cycle that serves for night, the light from above dimming through some mechanism you never determine, the pale blue lamps coming on in their slow chains — the place is not silent but is as quiet as a held breath. The breathing of the floor continues. The pumps continue. Somewhere, a seed pod drops, and the sound travels through the wood to you as a faint, felt pulse rather than a noise. The coolness deepens slightly. The motes still drift in the dim air. You sit on the sprung floor and you feel the pulse of the place through your bones, and it is patient, and it is vast, and it is ongoing, and you understand that you will leave and the place will not particularly notice, and that this is not indifference but a kind of attention so scaled up that your departure is, to it, the falling of a single seed.

You sleep. Or you do not sleep. The distinction, here, is less clear than it was.
