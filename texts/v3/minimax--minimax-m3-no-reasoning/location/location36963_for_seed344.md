The basin is small enough that you could circle its rim in two minutes walking. That is the first thing that registers — that nothing here is far from itself. The whole place is contained, sunk into the ground as if it has been scooped, or as if the ground has simply given way around something it could not absorb.

The walls are pale stone, sharp-edged, the kind of sharp that suggests time has not yet had a vote. Where the rock has fractured it shows clean planes, almost geological in their freshness. Water weeps from every surface — not in streams, but in threads, in beads that form and fall and form again. The walls are slick to look at. They breathe moisture out. In the hotter hours they breathe faster; in the cooler hours, not slower exactly, but differently, the beads smaller, more numerous, clinging.

The basin holds water. Of course it does. The water sits shallow and hot and not-quite-still. You can see heat above it — not steam exactly, because the air is already so saturated that steam has nowhere to go. Everything condenses. The metal edges along the pad are varnished in a continuous wet film. The stone, a foot above the waterline, is dark with moisture. Your breath, if you came here with lungs, would bloom thick.

In the center of the basin a thing stands. A pump, or what remains of one. Cylindrical, shoulder-high, bolted to a concrete pad that breaks the surface by a few inches. Pipes emerge from its sides and rise into the air and end. The ends are flanged, four bolts each, the bolts still present, the gaskets still present but blackened and petrified. A pressure gauge is mounted on the pump's flank, glass unbroken, the dial face fogged with interior condensation so the needle is only a suggestion. A wheel-valve, painted green at some point in the past, the green still mostly there, sits at the pad's edge where someone once could have reached it.

The pad is sharp-cornered. Rebar pokes out where a chunk has spalled away. The rebar has not rusted. It has done something else — dulled, grayed, taken on the look of wet slate. The concrete, likewise. No orange. No brown. A different chemistry here, or simply not enough time.

A small shed stands at the basin's near edge, half on stone, half on a lip of packed earth that has not yet eroded back into the basin. Corrugated walls, a door hanging crooked on one hinge, a roof that holds. Inside: the suggestion of equipment. A steel frame where something larger once bolted down. A coil of cable, stiff as rope. A switch panel with most of its toggles still in place, dark with tarnish, the labels long since bled into illegibility. Nothing has fallen. Nothing has collapsed. The shed is not a ruin. The shed is a thing that is simply no longer in use, and has not yet begun the long argument with time that would turn use into ruin.

The water moves. This is the loud thing. From somewhere — a pipe, a vent, an opening in the basin wall above the waterline — water enters under pressure and strikes the surface in a single bright column. The hiss of it is constant. The column fractures against the pool and becomes spray and the spray becomes a low fog that never quite lifts. The water that leaves the basin goes over the lip in a sheet no thicker than paper, and the sheet makes its own small endless sound. Beneath these, deeper, almost not heard but felt in the sternum and the teeth, is a pulse. Mechanical. Patient. As though somewhere, deeper than the basin, something turns at a steady rate and has been turning for a long time and will continue to turn. Or as though the pulse is just what this air does, this heavy wet air, finding every metal surface and resonating against it.

Nothing lives here. No insect skims the water. No bird calls from the unseen walls above. No algae green the surface; the water stays clear, slightly opalescent, with a pale mineral stain along the rocks at the fluctuating waterline. Whatever is dissolved in it does not encourage biology. The place is sterile the way an autoclave is sterile — not dead, but actively inhospitable to anything that tries.

What grows here is sparse and refuses to commit to abundance. A few pale ferns, the kind that like steam, root into cracks in the upper wall. Their fronds are open, never fully curled even when the air is hottest. Three or four reeds stand in a shallows at the basin's far edge, yellow-green, slightly bent as though a permanent wind is passing, though there is no wind. Where the hot water spills over and dries, a white mineral crust has built up — calcium, or silica, or something local — and a fine fuzz of pale growth colonizes the crust. It is not quite lichen. It is not quite moss. It is the kind of thing that requires patience to name, and patience is what the basin rewards.

The light is flat. White. The humidity diffuses it so thoroughly that nothing throws a hard shadow. The corrugated wall of the shed, which would normally stripe the floor with light and dark, appears softly banded, almost painted. The far rim of the basin glows where the wet air catches what little sun filters down. You cannot tell distances well. The walls could be ten feet or thirty.

The dynamics here are glacial. The water level has not changed perceptibly since you arrived. The mineral crusts thicken at a rate that would require years to notice. The paint will outlast you. The pump will outlast the paint. The ferns will lengthen their fronds by a thumb's width across a season. Nothing in this place responds to a visitor. Nothing hurries. Nothing stops. The slow churn is the only clock, and it does not advertise.

You feel watched, in the basin, though not by anything alive. The geometry looks back. The round of the basin. The rectangle of the pad. The straight run of the dead pipes. The plumb of the shed's doorframe. Things that hands once made still hold their lines, and they have the patient, blank regard of objects that do not yet know they have been forgotten. The place is loud, and hot, and small, and wet, and it will be this way tomorrow, and the day after, and for some time yet — not forever, but longer than you.
