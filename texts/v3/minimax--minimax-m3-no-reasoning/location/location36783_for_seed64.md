# The Cold Conservatory

It is a single room. That is the first thing you must accept.

A chamber, roughly the size of a modest chapel, hollowed out from the interior of a mountain that no one ever climbed because there is no mountain — only the suggestion of one, a curving wall of unweathered grey stone that meets itself somewhere overhead in a vault you can feel but not quite see. The ceiling is too high to make out. A dim, sourceless glow clings to the surfaces like a memory of light, enough to find your footing, not enough to dispel the sense that everything here exists at the threshold of being perceived.

The floor is the second impossible thing. It is carpeted.

Not fabric. Not quite. A dense, continuous mat of something vegetative — a felted green-black growth that presses up between your toes when you step, cool and faintly damp, smelling of cedar and cold stone and something metallic, like old pennies left in rain. It is alive. Or it is behaving as though alive. The individual blades, if they are blades, are so fine and so tightly packed that they form a single velvet surface, and they are everywhere. They climb the lower portions of the walls. They fur the underside of a low stone bench that runs along one side. They have crept up the rusted iron legs of the machinery.

Because there is machinery.

---

Three presses stand in a row against the far wall, each one taller than a person, each one the deep brick-red of long-dried blood. They are industrial in scale and absolutely silent. No belts move. No pistons tick. The flywheels are still. And yet — *and yet* — if you press your ear against the cold flank of the nearest one, you can feel a vibration so low it lives more in your teeth than in your ears, a hum that suggests these machines are *waiting*, or *finished*, or have arrived at some state beyond either.

No one has tended them in a very long time. The felted growth has crept up their casings in delicate drifts. Copper pipes overhead have oxidized into a lacework of mint-green and soot-black. A single pressure gauge, mounted on the central press, has its face stopped at a number you cannot quite read in the gloom — the dial is cracked across its middle, and the needle points at four o'clock as though that were a meaningful hour.

A smell of machine oil hangs in the air, but it is old oil, the oil of engines that will not run again. Beneath it, the green smell. Beneath that, stone.

---

The vegetation is the dominant fact. It presses in from every surface. It is not the lushness of a jungle — it is the lushness of a small, sealed thing that has been given unlimited time. Slow growers, dense growers, things that do not need sun. Mosses, liverworts, the felted hypnaceae of perpetually shaded places, but here thickened beyond any natural analog, as though they have been breeding in the dark for a thousand years and have forgotten how to stop. The green is so dark it is almost black. Where the light catches it — and the light is weak, sourceless, coming from nowhere — it gives back a faint wet gleam, the gleam of something that is always just about to be touched by water, though it never actually is.

The air is cool. Exactly cool. Not cold enough to chill you, not warm enough to comfort you. The temperature of an unheated stone room in late spring, except that there is no spring here, and no season. It has always been this cool. It will always be this cool.

There is no water. You search for it. The growth is not wet. There is no condensation on the iron. No drip from the ceiling. The machinery is dry. The moss is dry. Yet nothing has died. Whatever moisture cycle this place runs on is invisible, closed, its own sealed negotiation between plant and stone and metal.

---

And then — the sound.

You almost do not notice it at first, because it is so constant, so even, that it has become the texture of the air. It is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, with peaks and variations. It is loud the way a held note is loud, or the way a transformer substation is loud when you stand inside it.

A hum. A grinding, sustained, almost-musical hum, coming from the presses, coming from the pipes, coming from somewhere in the walls themselves, as though the whole chamber is a single resonant cavity and something inside it has been vibrating at a steady pitch since before you arrived and will continue to vibrate long after you leave. It sits in your molars. It sits in the bones of your wrist. After a few minutes you stop hearing it, and then you cannot stop hearing it.

There is no fauna. You have looked. The only movements in the room are the imperceptible ones — the growth of the moss, the slow oxidation of the copper, the almost-imperceptible settling of the presses into the floor they have pressed against for centuries. Nothing moves. Nothing flies. Nothing rustles. The hum is the only sign that any process is occurring at all.

---

Along the bench, arranged with what might be care or might be accident, are a number of small artifacts. A brass fitting, green at the seams. A glass vial, empty, its stopper sealed with dark wax. A leather glove, collapsed and empty, furred over with the same growth that covers everything. A folded piece of paper that, when you pick it up — carefully, because it is fragile as a dead leaf — turns out to be covered in handwriting so small and so faded that you cannot read it, only see that the lines are even, that the page was filled completely, that someone once sat on this bench in this room and wrote all of this down.

There is no indication of where the paper went afterward. There is no indication of where the writer went. The room simply persists.

---

You sit down on the bench. The felted growth gives softly beneath you. The hum finds your ribs. The presses wait in their eternal stillness. The moss grows at whatever rate it grows. The light neither strengthens nor fades. The temperature neither rises nor falls.

Somewhere, if you listen very carefully, beneath the hum, there is a silence so total that it feels like the negative space of a sound — the silence of a place where nothing has ever spoken, and yet where, for a long time, a great deal was being listened to.

You are in a pocket. A single sealed chamber at the heart of a thing that is not quite a mountain. The machinery has done its work or is doing it or will do it. The moss has grown or is growing. The paper has been written or is being written. The hum is the only clock, and it does not advance.

You sit. The room holds you. Nothing changes.

Nothing has changed for a very long time.
