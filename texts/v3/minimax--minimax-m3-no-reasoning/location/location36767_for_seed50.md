# The Throat of Sky-Mother

You find the place by following the sound. That is the rule of it, and perhaps the only one. The valley doesn't open. It admits.

---

The approach is a switchback of broken stone slabs laid by hands no longer here, set into the flank of a peak so high that the clouds pass *beneath* you rather than around. The slabs are rough-cut, undressed, the color of dried bone; they have no mortar between them, only the patience of whoever set them down. Their edges are softened by water, but the water that softened them was not this water. Older water. They look older than the mountain they rest on, which is impossible and yet the way the stone lies flat against the gray granite says so plainly: *I was here before this rock was cool.*

You climb. The path narrows. The wind stops.

The wind stops, and that is when you understand that the basin is a bowl, and that what you have been hearing for the last hour of climbing — what you took for wind — was not wind at all.

---

The basin is small. Smaller than you were braced for. You could walk its rim in five unhurried minutes if the rim were walkable, which it is not, because the rim is also the water.

The water fills it to the brim and a finger's width above. It is a single body of water, no more than thirty paces across, almost circular, almost perfectly circular, and it is in motion so violent that the surface cannot remember itself for a moment. The water does not flow out. It does not flow in. There is no visible tributary, no channel, no fall. The whole pond simply *works* — a column of churning, vertical, almost orgasmic water that rises from a darkness in the center and slops against the lip of the basin in a continuous rolling cataract of white. It is as if someone has pulled a stopper halfway and the world is hesitating over whether to drain.

The sound is the first thing, and it stays the first thing. It is the size of the place turned up. It is a roar and a hiss and a low chest-felt shudder, all at once, and you can feel the bones of your face receive it. Conversation here is pantomime. You shout only for form.

---

The water is not the color of water. It has the milky blue-gray of glacier melt, but laced through it — most visible at the edges, where the turbulence is briefly tamed — are strands of something darker, almost oily, almost alive. They do not move with the current. They move *across* it, lazy and deliberate, as if the water is a sheet of glass they are crossing. If you watch them long enough you begin to suspect they have a direction, and that the direction is toward you. You stop watching them long enough.

The water is cool. Not cold. You would not want to fall into it, but you would not die of it. The cold is older than the water — the basin has the temperature of stone that has never seen sun, and the water has simply learned to be like the stone.

---

The basin sits at the bottom of a hidden cirque, walled on three sides by a horseshoe of vertical rock no more than fifty feet high. The walls are *new*. You can tell. They have the raw, gritty, granulated look of stone that was uncovered last week or last century; there are places where a fingernail would take a flake off. They have not yet learned to be mountains. They are embarrassed mountains. They have not yet grown their lichen, except in a single down-pointing smear at the north face — a kind of green-black beard, maybe twenty feet long, dripping. Above the cirque, the rest of the peak continues upward, ordinary and indifferent, as if nothing were happening here.

The fourth side of the basin is open: a low notch in the wall, where the lip of stone has been deliberately, primitively, knocked down to make a way out. That is where the path you climbed meets the water. There is a sill of worked stone across the gap, and a low wall on either side, perhaps waist-high, built of the same bone-colored slabs. Not a dam. A *threshold*. They have built a way for the water to *leave* but not all of it. They have built a way for a person to enter but not to approach without kneeling.

---

The basin is otherwise empty. There is almost nothing here. A few mosses, the color of tarnished silver, cling to the rocks near the waterline — only on the west side, where the light lingers. They are the wrong color for moss and the wrong shape; they grow in tight spirals, like the ghost of a fern, and when the spray from the basin hits them they briefly, impossibly, glow. A single bird — small, dun-colored, the kind you cannot name — perches on the eastern wall. It has been here the whole time you have been here. It does not leave. It does not move. It watches the water the way a cat watches a door.

You do not see it fly in. You do not see how it tolerates the sound.

Below the waterline, where the lip of the basin meets the churning, there are a few marks. Not carvings. More like the suggestion of carvings — a chevron, a half-circle, three short parallel lines. They are too worn to read, but they are deliberate. They are oriented toward the dark center of the basin, the place where the water rises from. Someone, sometime, with simple tools — bone, perhaps, or a hard stone — knelt at the lip of this thing and made a sign toward whatever lives at its bottom.

---

What lives at the bottom, you cannot see. The dark in the center is not the dark of depth. It is the dark of a thing that does not want to be looked at. The churning water rises around it like a fist, and within the fist there is a hole, and within the hole there is — you have to look away. You have to look away. The light is normal here, true: it is the cool, gray-white, high-altitude light of a peak at midday, and the shadows are sharp, and the colors of the stone are honest, and yet. *And yet.* When you stare into the center for more than a few seconds, the normal light begins to behave strangely — it gets thinner at the edges, and the dark at the center gets denser, and you understand, with a sensation like a small bell struck somewhere behind your sternum, that the basin is *finishing*. The rock is still coming up out of the water. The rim has not found its final shape. This place is not old. It is *new*. It is being *made*, and the making is not finished, and what is making it has not yet decided what kind of thing it will be when it is done.

You are inside something that is still becoming itself.

You are inside something that knows you are here.

---

The path down the switchback is easier than the path up. You do not look back, but the sound follows you, thinning by degrees, until the wind returns and erases it. By the time you reach the tree line — sparse, twisted, knee-high and bent — the roar has become a hum, and the hum has become a memory of a hum, and you can hear your own footsteps again, ordinary, on the ordinary mountain.

At your back, above the snow and the dwarf pine, a thin plume of mist hangs over the peak. It does not move with the wind. It stays where it is. If you stood here long enough — and you will not — you might notice that the plume, too, has the lazy, deliberate drift of the dark strands you saw in the water.

Something is up there, turning.

Something is almost done.
