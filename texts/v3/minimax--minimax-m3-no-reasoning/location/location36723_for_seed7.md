# The Pump Chamber Beneath the Orchard

There is no sky here, only the underside of one.

The chamber is roughly the size of a small cathedral — say forty meters across, perhaps twelve at its highest point — but it does not feel like a cathedral. It feels like the inside of an engine that someone has forgotten to turn off. The ceiling is corrugated steel decking, original to the construction, rusted into brown plates that bow downward in places as though the weight above has been pressing on them for decades. Whatever sits above this chamber — soil, root systems, the long trunks of an orchard, the stone foundation of something larger and long since demolished — has settled onto the steel slowly, and the steel has accepted it.

You enter, if you enter, through a corridor on the south side that descends at a shallow grade. The corridor is concrete, painted once in a pale blue that has yellowed to the color of old teeth. There are hand-railings along the walls, iron pipes threaded through brackets, and every pipe sweats. Beads of moisture collect along the underside of each rail and slide down in slow spirals, gathering at the lowest point and dropping, one by one, onto the floor. By the time you reach the chamber itself your shoes are dark with water that has no mineral taste and no particular warmth and no origin you can trace. It is condensation from the air. The air is saturated.

The heat is the first thing that meets you, and the first thing you stop noticing after a few minutes, which is the worst part of it. It is not radiant heat, exactly. It is the heat of a greenhouse with all the vents closed in August. It presses on the skin. It makes breathing feel like drinking something thick. It comes up through the floor in slow waves from somewhere lower — a basement of basements, a place no one has charted, where some process still runs — and it carries with it a faint sulfur-and-iron smell, like the inside of a kettle that has been boiled dry a thousand times.

The floor is poured concrete, cracked in places, and where the cracks run there is green. Not grass green. The deeper green of things that have given up on chlorophyll efficiency and simply *are.* Ferns have rooted in the cracks and in the joints where walls meet floor and in the rust-seams of the machinery, and they are absurdly healthy. They are the healthiest things in the room. Their fronds uncurl toward nothing in particular — toward the heat, perhaps, which they have learned to read — and they drip constantly, the way ferns do when the air will not let them dry. Between the ferns, moss. Between the moss, something liverwort-thick and dark, almost black, that grows in rosettes around the bases of the larger plants.

Lianas — real ones, with the gray-brown bark of mature stems — have found their way down through the ventilation shafts and the abandoned cable conduits. They hang from the ceiling in long, motionless ropes and root again where they touch the floor, and they root again where they touch the machinery, and they have begun, very patiently, to pull the machinery apart. You can see where a vine has found the seam between two cast-iron housings and has worked its way in, millimeter by slow millimeter, prying. The seam has not yet given way. It will. The vine is not in a hurry.

The machinery is the reason this room exists, though no one alive remembers that.

There are four pumps in the chamber, arranged around a central column. Each pump is a vertical thing, about two meters tall, with a cylindrical body, a flanged head, a small electric motor on top, and a base bolted to a raised concrete plinth. They were painted, originally, in the same pale blue as the corridor. Now they are the color of an old coin. They have not been serviced. They were never serviced, perhaps. The motors on two of them are gone — taken, or failed and removed, leaving only the mounting flanges and a few severed wires capped with crumbling rubber. The third pump has a motor but no impeller; someone has opened its access plate and the cavity inside is empty, colonized by a single large fern that has anchored itself in the shaft. The fourth pump is running.

It is running now, as you read this. It will be running when you set this down. Its motor hums at a frequency just below the threshold of attention — the kind of hum that lives in your teeth rather than your ears — and once every eleven seconds its impeller, deep inside, moves something. Water, presumably, though there is no water moving visibly through it. There is no pipe leading away that you can see. The pump sits in the middle of the room like a small animal sleeping with its eyes open, and it pulls.

Around the pumps, in the wide spaces between them, are the smaller things. A workbench, wooden, sagging. Three empty oil drums with ferns growing out of them. A metal locker, door hanging open, containing a pair of rubber gloves gone brittle and a folded instruction placard, white, the ink faded to gray, in a language you do not read. A coil of wire on a wooden spool. Two fluorescent light fixtures hanging from chains, both dark; one has a tube still in it, the tube clouded and emptied of its gas, the other has lost its tube entirely and shows only the bare contacts. A wooden stool, one leg shorter than the others because someone has shimmed it with a folded piece of cardboard.

The light in the chamber comes from three sources, none of them intended.

The first is a small fissure in the west wall, where the concrete has shifted against the earth behind it and cracked open a gap of perhaps two centimeters. Through this gap, daylight — the ordinary daylight of the orchard above — leaks in as a thin slanted blade and falls across the floor in a bright trapezoid, and in that trapezoid the ferns grow denser, greener, almost aggressive. The dust motes that would normally be visible in such a beam are absent; the air is too wet for dust. The light is yellow.

The second source is the fourth pump. Its motor is old enough that its housing has loosened at the seams, and through those seams a dim red glow escapes — the color of a stove coil set to its lowest setting. It is the color of a thing that is still working. It throws no useful light. You would not see your hand by it. But it is enough to make the curved metal of the pump housing look like a coal in the dark, and it is enough, when you stand in the right place, to cast the faintest suggestion of your own shadow on the floor.

The third source is a thing you do not at first identify. It is a glow from beneath the central column — a low, pulsing, indeterminate luminescence, blue-white, that comes up through the seam where the column meets the floor. It is not mechanical. It is not electric. It has no visible apparatus. It brightens very slowly over the course of about a minute, dims over the course of about three, and brightens again. It casts no shadow. It does not flicker. If you put your hand over the seam, you can feel no heat from it. If you press your ear to the column, you can hear no sound from it. It is simply there, the way a heartbeat is simply there, and no one installed it.

The chamber is quiet. The pump hums. The water drips. The condensation slides down the rails and gathers and falls. A frond, weighted with moisture, dips low enough to touch the top of the locker and straightens again, and later dips again. Nothing else moves. There are no insects. There are no spiders. There are no bats. There is no reason for any of them to be here — the room is sealed against the world above by everything except the fissure and the conduits through which the vines have entered — and whatever life has come in has come in as seed or spore and has stayed, and nothing that walks or flies has ever found its way down.

The slowness is the deepest quality of the place. The rust is slow. The root pressure is slow. The pump's cycle is slow. The glow pulses on a slow cycle. The vines will pry the seams open in a slow time. The ferns will fill the oil drums in a slow time. The concrete will erode under the moss in a slow time, the moss releasing a faint acid each wet season, the concrete softening in patches, the patches growing. Nothing in this room has been in a hurry for a long time, and nothing will be in a hurry for a long time still.

You stand in the chamber and you are the only thing that is moving quickly. Your breath comes fast. Your heart is fast. Your thoughts chase each other. The room does not mind. It has been here, and will be here. It does not know you are standing in it.

Somewhere far above, through a meter and a half of root-tangled earth, the orchard is in leaf. You cannot hear it. You can hear only the pump, and the water, and the slow, almost inaudible ticking of the central column as it cools and contracts and cools again, and the glow pulses under your feet, and you are alone in the small, hot, wet, dark heart of a machine that has been growing for longer than you have been alive, and will be growing, in its patient and indifferent way, after you leave.
