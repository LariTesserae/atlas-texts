# The Raised

## I. The Air

The first thing to understand is that there is no sky here, only air. The distinction matters. A sky implies distance, a horizon you can approach and never reach; the air here is closer, thicker, the kind of substance you feel on the inside of your lips. It is warm the way a held breath is warm — not the warmth of a sun striking stone, but the warmth of something enclosed, of a body exhaling into itself. The temperature never falls. You do not shiver here. You do not need to.

Above, where the sky should be, there is a ceiling of suspended dust. It is perhaps two kilometers up, perhaps more. Light enters it the way light enters deep water: filtered, slowed, made amber and then amber-grey and then, as your eyes adjust, simply *old*. The dust is not storm. There is no storm here, because storms require weather, and weather requires water. This is older than weather. This is the slow, planetary exhalation of a world still sorting out what it is going to be. The light has the color of an attic in late afternoon, even at what passes for midday, which is also the time that passes for dusk, which is the same as the time that passes for dawn.

You do not cast a sharp shadow. You cast a stain.

## II. The Plateau

The ground beneath you is being made.

You stand on a basaltic shelf that did not exist, in any meaningful sense, eight thousand years ago. Eight thousand years is a long time. It is also nothing. The shelf is approximately one hundred and forty kilometers across at its widest, and it sits at an elevation that would turn human blood to syrup if the air were thinner, but the air here is not thinner, the air here is the same density it has been since you began reading this sentence, and that is the trick of the place: it is high without being cold, dry without being dead, dark without being unlit.

The basalt is young. You can tell because it has not yet learned to be stone. It still has opinions. Underfoot it isometries — small ridges of cooled flow, the ropes and lobes of lavas that set while still moving, the ropy entrails of pahoehoe frozen mid-gesture. Where the wind has had time, and the wind has had very little time, these have been scoured into shallow flutes. Where the wind has not yet reached, they are sharp enough to cut boots. In places the rock is still warm, in the sense that the planet's skin is still warm: you feel it through the soles, a polite heat, the temperature of a cup held too long.

To the north the shelf breaks. There is no cliff, no edge. The rock simply *ceases*, in a long curving line, and what lies beyond the ceasing is not visible because of the dust, but you can hear it: a sound like a slow furnace, like the inside of a kiln, like the world working at something. To the south, the shelf rises. It rises into a ridge. The ridge is not yet a mountain. It is the proposal of a mountain, a draft of one, drawn up out of the plateau by the same forces that built the shelf and have not stopped. Its flanks are striped in colors that have no names in any language you speak: a copper-gold, a near-black, a green that is not a green but a mineral sulking toward green.

There are no trees. There are no shrubs. There are no lichens, even. There are, in places, thin crusts — pale, almost translucent, hugging the underside of overhanging flows. They are not alive. They are what comes before alive. They are mineral patterning that has begun to consider whether it might prefer to be something else. They do not move. They do not grow. But they are not nothing, either.

## III. The Objects

The plateau is not entirely empty of intention.

Walking — and you will walk, because there is nothing to ride and nothing to ride on — you will encounter, at intervals of perhaps three or four kilometers, things that do not belong to the geology. They are made of the geology. They are *part* of the geology. But they have been arranged.

The first is a wall. Three meters tall, eight meters long, basalt blocks fitted without mortar. The blocks are not dressed. They have been chosen, lifted, set. The wall does not enclose anything. It is not the start of anything. It stands in the middle of a flow field with nothing behind it and nothing in front of it except more flow field. On its highest block, balanced precisely on a corner that should not hold it, there is a small flat stone the size of a hand. The wind has not moved it. The wind, which is gentle here, has had eight thousand years to move it, and has not.

The second is a cairn. But the cairn is wrong. It is built downward, into the rock, a tapering shaft that descends through a hole that should not have admitted it, its stones fitted with a tightness that suggests they were placed from the inside. You can lean over and look down. The shaft is dark. The shaft is dark the way the air is dark, which is to say not entirely. From somewhere below, faintly, there is the suggestion of a warmer darkness, a glow that has traveled a long way to be this dim.

The third is harder to describe. It is a low platform, perhaps a meter and a half on a side, made of a material you have not seen elsewhere on the plateau. It is not basalt. It is not the copper-gold stone. It is something that has no business being here, a dark vitreous substance with a faint internal layering, as if it had been poured and cooled many times in many small increments. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding rock. It does not appear to be a tool. It does not appear to be a seat. It is, you will eventually conclude, an *artifact*, though of what, and of whom, and for what, the plateau does not say.

There are others. They are not numerous. A line of small stones, set on edge, curving away from themselves. A slab that has been raised onto three smaller slabs, table-like, in a place where no table is needed. A hole, perfectly circular, descending through a flow and emerging on the other side, the flow above it undisturbed, as if the hole had been there first and the flow had politely arranged itself around it.

There are no animals. There are no footprints other than the ones you are making. The objects have been here longer than you have. They will be here after.

## IV. The Sound

Mostly, the plateau is quiet.

It is quiet the way a held room is quiet. There is the small sound of your own breathing, the small sound of your boots on the loose scoria, the small sound of grit shifting in the wind. There is, occasionally, the sound of the wind itself, which is not a wind you would call wind if you had grown up elsewhere. It is more like the air remembering to move. It carries grit, but gently. It carries heat, but only a little. It has nowhere to go.

The quiet is broken. The quiet is broken with regularity, and this is the part of the place that does not let you settle.

From the north, where the plateau ceases, there is the furnace sound, steady, almost musical at a distance, the sound of magma moving in a chamber far below. From the ridge to the south, there are cracking sounds, occasional, the sound of cooling stone giving up its heat, the sound of a planet exfoliating. From below your feet, intermittently, there is a *thud* — not loud, not sharp, a deep percussive note, as if something heavy has been set down somewhere inside the world. The thud does not repeat at any interval you can predict. It comes when it comes. It is the planet deciding something.

Once — you will hear this once, perhaps twice, if you stay — there is a louder sound. A sustained low roar from somewhere beyond the dust, the sound of a flow emerging, of new basalt being introduced to the air. It lasts perhaps an hour. When it is done, the plateau is larger. The plateau has, by a margin, increased. If you walked to the site you would find fresh rock, still steaming faintly in the dim air, still sharp, still learning its shape.

You will not walk to the site. The plateau is large, and you are one, and the new rock will be incorporated into the geography of the place long before you could reach it.

## V. The Light at the Edge

The dimness is not uniform.

At certain points on the plateau — and you find them by accident, because they are not marked — the dust thins. The reasons for the thinning are local: a vent, a fissure, an upwelling of thermal air that has pushed the haze aside for a few meters. In these places the light arrives more directly, and it is not amber there. It is the actual color of the star, which from this world is a dim red-orange thing, low on the horizon even at its zenith because the planet is far from it, or because the planet turns slowly, or because the planet's axis is angled in a way that keeps the star low and warm and almost touching the dust.

In one such clearing, on an evening that is not really an evening, you sit beside one of the artifacts. The platform of the dark vitreous stone. You do not know what it is for. The plateau does not say.

The light that comes through the cleared dust is enough to see by. It puts a faint shadow behind you, the first sharp shadow you have cast. It warms the side of your face. It is not a strong light. It is not a triumphant light. It is a light that has come a long way, that has been filtered and bent and slowed, that has survived the dust to reach this one small place on this one small plateau on this one still-forming world.

It will be gone in a few minutes. The vent will cool. The dust will return. The shadow will fade back into the general stain of you.

But for those minutes, sitting beside the artifact that you do not understand, on the shelf that did not exist, under the ceiling that is not a sky, you are briefly in a place that is finished. The place is not finished. The place is being made. But the light, in its small arrival, has paused the making, and you are allowed, for a moment, to be in it.

The plateau continues to rise.

The objects continue to stand.

The dust continues to fall.

The star, low and red, continues its slow transit of a sky it is only dimly permitted to enter.
