The plateau is older than memory, and the cold has had time to settle into its bones.

You stand on ground that is almost not ground at all. What was once mountain has been ground down across a span so long that the word "mountain" no longer fits; what was once river-cut valley has been filled and re-filled and smoothed until the surface resembles something between a fist and a forehead. The elevation is high enough that the air thins, but the wetness is so constant that the thinning never feels clean. The sky, when it can be seen at all, is a low pewter lid; when it cannot be seen, which is more often, the mist simply takes over as sky, and the question of where one ends and the other begins has not been settled.

Light arrives, but dimly, as if filtered through the membrane of something enormous. Everything in the foreground is legible; everything past a certain distance gives way to a softness, a blur at the edges, as though the air itself is digesting outlines. The light does not fall so much as bleed, sideways and downward, pooling in the shallow concavities of the land where the water gathers. It is the kind of light that does not produce shadows so much as leaks.

The cold is a fact, not a feeling. It is the temperature of stone that has not been warm in living memory. It is the temperature of water that wants to be ice but cannot quite commit because the mists keep thawing it from above. The breath of the place — if breath is the right word — is hoar and rime, a thin rime that crusts everything black and grey and ochre, that climbs the stems of things and the bones of things and never finishes its work.

And the water. The water is everywhere, almost, but not quite. It does not flow so much as exude. It comes up through the substrate in slow dark tongues. It condenses on every surface and runs down in patient filaments. The rocks weep — actual moisture gathers on their faces and trickles. The turf is saturated to a depth that never drains. When you step, the ground accepts your weight with a soft wet sigh and holds the impression of you for a long time after. There are pools, but the pools are not so much features of the land as places where the land has given up holding the water back; they are shallow and dark and they reflect the dim light back as a thing slightly different from light.

Across this wide wet ground, at distances that vary and that you should not trust, stand the plants.

They are not plants as you would use the word. They are plants in the way that a cathedral is a stone. The substrate is alive; the substrate is vegetal; the substrate is the only architecture. They rise from the boggy ground in solitary trunks or clustered stands, and they are tall — improbably tall for the climate — and they are doing something.

The first thing you notice, after the scale, is the rhythm. There is a rhythm. From each trunk, at irregular intervals, a pulse moves upward and is taken up by the canopy above. The trunks themselves are not smooth: they are ridged, banded, articulated, as though they were assembled from stacked rings of woody material pinned through with darker rods. The rods are not decorative. The rods are functional. They move. They slide within their housings with a slow hydraulic certainty that suggests they have been doing this for a very long time and have worn the channels smooth.

The canopies are not leaf-shapes so much as sieves, lattices, broad dished platforms of interlocking frond that face the dim sky. They tilt. They open and close in sequences that are not quite synchronized, so that the whole forest — for it is a forest, even if it is a sparse one — appears to be breathing at the rate of a sleeping animal, with deep undulations of inhalation and exhalation that take minutes to complete. They are processing something. Air, certainly. Moisture, certainly. But also, you begin to suspect, the mist itself, drawing it down through the lattices and reducing it, fractionating it, sending some component of it downward through the trunk and exhaling a different component upward through vents that run like chimney-flues up the sides of the woody columns. The plants are working.

They are working in a way that produces sound.

The sound is constant and is not far from loud, though the wet air dampens it and gives it a muffled quality, as though heard through a wall. There is a low grinding, the sound of lignified gears meshing and turning under load. There is a hydraulic hiss and creak of the rods being driven through their channels. There is a deeper throb from beneath the bog, where roots of an unimaginable size are turning, churning, working the substrate, mixing the cold peat and the cold water in vast slow-rotating cylinders that are not visible but are inferred by the way the ground itself moves, very slightly, very slowly, in long deep breaths that match the rhythm of the canopies. From somewhere in the mists, intermittently, comes a high thin whine — a resonance, a vibration at the upper edge of hearing — that seems to be produced by the lattice canopies when they tilt to certain angles and the wind passes through them.

It is the sound of industry. It is the sound of a place that is manufacturing something. What it is manufacturing is not clear. Possibly more of itself. Possibly the mist. Possibly the fauna.

The fauna is abundant, though you might not see it for a long time at first, because the density of the place is sparse and the mist is generous with its concealments. When you do see it, it is wrong.

Things move in the boggy ground between the great plants. They are pale — bleached, almost, the color of things that live in mist. They have too many joints, or too few, or joints in the wrong places. Some are low and sinuous and move through the mires like eels, leaving wakes in the saturated turf that fill in slowly behind them. Some are upright and stand on stilts of leg and watch the canopies with heads that are mostly eye. Some are small and quick and swarm along the ridges of the bog where the ground is firmest, and their sound — a chittering, a clicking — is part of the constant sound of the place, woven in with the hydraulic creak.

They feed, you eventually understand, on the mist. Or on what the plants exhale. Or on each other. The food web is not legible from the outside. What is legible is that they are present in numbers — the plateau is thick with them, scattered, hidden, and their distributed movements contribute to the constant low-level turbulence of the place.

And the place is turbulent. Nothing sits still. The mists rearrange themselves in slow banks that come and go. The great plants are not where they were a moment ago — or, more precisely, you are not where you were a moment ago. The space is unreliable.

This is the part that does not obey.

It begins as a feeling of slight unsteadiness, as though you had stepped off a boat and onto a dock and the dock were still moving. You look back the way you came and the way back is not the way you came. The pools have shifted. A plant that was a hundred meters to your left is now closer, or further, or in a different orientation. The mists do not so much drift as propagate, like waves in a slow medium, and you begin to suspect that the medium they propagate through is not air but something more general, something in which the geometry of the plateau is not fixed.

The plants are doing this. The plants, with their slow industrial cycling, their intake of mist and exhalation of processed vapor, are bending something. Not light, exactly. The geometry. The distances. The way one thing is positioned relative to another over time. The rods that slide within their housings are not only moving substance but moving something in the fabric of the locality, and over the long run, across the wide wet ground, the fabric has been worked until it is pliant. You stand on a place that has been kneaded. You stand on a place that has been churned until it no longer quite remembers its own shape.

This is why the rocks are so old and so worn down. This is what "ancient erosion" means here. Not wind, not water, not ice — though all of those are present — but the plants. The plants have been grinding the substrate for longer than the substrate can remember, reducing it to a near-flattened surface, working it down toward a final form, and the final form is one in which the ground is soft and dark and almost level and almost nothing, and the plants stand upon it like the last machines of a factory that is still, against all expectation, running.

The sound goes on. The grinding, the hiss, the throb, the chitter, the thin whine. The mists continue to be processed. The fauna continues to swarm and watch. The cold continues to settle. The light continues to bleed. The geometry continues, very gently, to be unreliable. The plants continue to work, and to grow, and to dissolve, and to be reborn from the dissolved remains of their predecessors, in cycles that take so long that you cannot see a single cycle but only infer it from the gradients of age among the trunks — the youngest, pale and smooth and tight-banded, standing between the elders, whose trunks are dark, gnarled, vented, and leaning under the accumulated weight of their long labor.

It is an industrial ecology. It is a pure-plant ecology. It is ancient, cold, wet, sparse, dim, loud, turbulent, abundant with strange fauna, and quietly, persistently, wrong.

It has been here a long time. It intends to continue.
