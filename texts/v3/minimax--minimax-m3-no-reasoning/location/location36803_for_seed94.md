# The High Wet

I want to start where it is quietest, because the quiet is what the place is built around, even though nothing here was built.

A bowl. A high bowl, ringed by a rim of grey-green rock that the locals — there are no locals — that the lichen calls home. The rim is old in the way that suggests patience rather than time: not shattered, not jagged, but worn into soft shoulders, into long slopes that a body could slide down if it lost its footing. The rock weeps. I mean that literally. Every fissure has its dark seam, and from each seam a thin braid of water descends the inner wall, threading between clumps of feathery moss that lean into the wet like they're listening to it. The braids meet in the middle and become a stream, and the stream does not have a name because nothing has been here to name it. It just runs, and has run, and the channels it has carved are themselves grown over with green things that don't know what a channel is.

Above the bowl, the air is thick. Not fog exactly, but a cousin of fog: a damp ceiling that sits at about the height a tall tree would reach if there were tall trees, and which occasionally lowers itself to swallow the rim and then lifts again as if remembering a schedule. The light that gets through is the color of old paper. There is no harsh sun here, and no full dark either. The bowl has its own weather, small and complete: a slow rotation of mist and clearing, mist and clearing, with the rain that falls inside the bowl never quite the rain that falls outside it.

And it is packed. That is the first thing that would strike you, if you arrived. There is no horizon inside the bowl. The eye expects to travel and finds it cannot. Moss, fern, a kind of low groundcover with leaves like wet hands; a thicket of dwarf shrubs with berries the color of bruises; a sprawl of something that might be a relative of bamboo, jointed, arching, so dense in places you could walk across the tops of it if you weighed nothing. Birds, but you don't see them first. You hear them — or rather, you hear that there are sounds that belong to birds, though the birds themselves are buried in the green, and only the occasional whir or trill or liquid note comes out, as if the foliage is translating for them. Insects, of course. A wet hum, continuous, like the place is vibrating.

The stream is the only open artery. It moves between the banks of green at a pace that suggests it has somewhere to be, but if you follow it for an hour you find it only loops back, splitting and rejoining, braiding across the bowl floor in a delta that never reaches anything larger. It sinks, eventually, into a flat gravel patch that is dark with constant saturation, and from there the water goes somewhere underground, where it is not anyone's business.

If you climbed the rim — and it would be a climb, because the slopes are slick and the moss is treacherous — you would find that the bowl is one of many. The land up here is a field of bowls, nested, overlapping at their seams. Some are smaller, tighter, more like wells; some are vast enough that you cannot see across them in weather. Between bowls the rock rises into low ridges, and on the ridges the vegetation thins but does not surrender — wind-stunted shrubs, lichen, a kind of tough flowering mat that lies flat against the stone and blooms in a color that has no name because no one has needed to discriminate between this shade of lavender and that one. Up here the wind is a constant, low, almost subsonic, and the water on the rims is constantly being peeled off into spindrift that the wind takes and drops back down into another bowl a mile away.

The silence is not true silence. The wind has its sound, and the water has its sound, and there is the hum, and the distant bird-trills. But the proportions are off in a way that registers as quietness. No machinery. No human vocal range. No percussion. Nothing that says *intention*. The sounds that are present are the sounds of processes, not of acts. Water running is not an act. A bird calling is an act, but the birds are buried so deep in the green that the act is muffled into ambiance. The result is a place that feels like it is happening to itself, and you are only witnessing.

Now. The difficult part. The thing that does not quite fit, and that is why I am going to spend time on it.

There are machines.

Not many. Not large. But present. And the place is older than them, and will be here after them, and they are not built into the landscape but are *accepted* by it, the way a fallen log is accepted. A steel thing — squat, cylindrical, dark with patina — sits in the gravel flat where the stream sinks, half-buried, its top bristling with a flange that has become a perch for the birds. It hums, faintly, at a frequency below attention. From a seam in its side a thin pipe emerges, runs a few yards along the gravel, and disappears into the ground. Whatever it is doing, the place has incorporated it: moss climbs its curved shoulder, ferns have rooted in the angle between it and the earth, the stream runs around it without comment. Further up the slope, near one of the weeping fissures, there is a small assembly: a metal frame, a drum, a glass housing that is now cracked and filled with rainwater and a single frog. Whatever this was, it is no longer operational in any sense that would satisfy its makers. But the drum still turns, slowly, in the current of water that has been diverted through it — diverted how? There are no visible channels, but the water is moving through the drum, and the drum is turning, and the turning is producing nothing, or producing only the slow rotation itself. Beside it, a rectangle of corroded plating, perhaps a meter square, is set into the moss like a memorial, and a different kind of moss — greener, fatter — has colonized its surface. It is the only flat thing in the bowl besides the gravel flat, and the eye keeps returning to it.

These things are not ruins. Ruins imply abandonment by people, and there are no people here, and never were. They are *residuals*. Something was extracted from this place, or processed in it, or studied in it. The machines have the look of instruments meant for a wet, cool, high, quiet environment — which is to say, an environment like this one. They were made *for* a place like this, and they have been returned to a place like this, and the place has not particularly noticed. The lichen grows on them at the same rate it grows on the rock. The frogs breed in them. The drum turns because water turns it. They are weather now.

I do not know how long they have been here. The patina suggests decades, perhaps. The colonization suggests more. But the bowl is older than any of it — the bowl is what the rock became over centuries of water and lichen and the slow negotiation between elevation and wet — and the machines are just the latest layer of weather on its surface. A previous layer was the dwarf bamboo. A previous layer was the birds' ancestors, who chose this bowl and stayed.

There is no climax to this place. There is no center. The eye moves from the weeping rim to the braided stream to the gravel flat to the drum turning in the current to a bird that emerges suddenly from the thicket, is a smear of iridescent color, and disappears. The eye moves on. The bowl is not a destination; it is a *condition*. You could spend a long time in it and never feel you had arrived anywhere, only that you were in a place that was in the middle of being itself.

The coolness is not cold. It is the kind of cool that a body can absorb indefinitely without chill, because it carries moisture and the moisture carries a faint vegetal warmth — the heat of all the living things, transpiring, breathing, digesting, humming. If you sat down on a stone in the middle of one of the denser thickets, in an hour the moss would begin to grow over the hem of your clothing. You would become, very slowly, part of the place. The machines have already become part of the place. That is the lesson of the drum.

Above, the mist lowers and lifts. The braided stream runs and runs. The wind on the rim takes the spindrift and moves it elsewhere. The bowl is one of many. There is a vastness here that is not visual — you cannot see it, you can only infer it from the way the bowls repeat, the way the rims are not the edge of anything but a sample of something that continues, bowl after bowl, for as far as the elevation and the wet allow. A vastness of *kind*, if not of scale. And in each bowl, the same slow weather, the same packing green, the same quiet that is not quite silence, and now and then, the same kind of small dark machine, accepted, turning, half-eaten by the place it was brought to study.

You would not call it beautiful, exactly. You would not call it anything. You would just stand there, in the old-paper light, getting wet, and listen to the hum, and after a while you would stop noticing the machines, and after a longer while you would stop noticing yourself, and you would simply be another piece of weather in the bowl, briefly, until you left, and the bowl continued.
