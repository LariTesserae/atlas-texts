The canyon doesn't begin where you think it does. It begins somewhere in your chest, in the low rumble that has lived there since you were small and first understood that the world was not silent, that it was never silent, that even the dirt beneath your feet was talking to itself in voices too deep to hear but not too deep to feel.

You stand at the eastern rim. Below you, the air drops away into a long green wound cut through the spine of a mountain range so old the ranges have forgotten their own names. The sun, what little of it manages to pierce the high haze, arrives already tired, filtered through kilometers of humid air, so that the light here is less an illumination than a thick golden broth the atmosphere has been slowly cooking all morning. It does not dispel the darkness so much as deepen it, give it texture. The shadows between the canyon walls are not black but a kind of bruised indigo, and in them things move that you will not see clearly even if you stare.

The walls themselves are built. That is the first wrong thing, or the first right thing, depending on how you were raised. Every centimeter of exposed stone bears the marks of making — not the tool-marks of any hand you recognize, but the slow patient architecture of creatures that build as they breathe, as they eat, as they love. The stone is layered with their work the way a reef is layered with coral. There are terraces, thousands of them, carved into the cliff face in rings that spiral down toward the river far below. Each terrace holds a structure: domes like halved eggs, spires that lean slightly as if listening, long low halls with walls thick as a man's torso. None of it is tall. The builders here, whoever they were, did not reach upward but spread outward, colonized the rock the way moss colonizes a fallen log, and the result is a city that is also a geology, that is also a body, that is also still being made.

It is not abandoned. That is the second thing.

The sound comes first, as sound always does here. It begins not in the ears but in the feet, a vibration that has traveled through the basalt from somewhere deep in the range, and it is not mechanical — there are no machines, no wheels, no blades, no fire kept in any vessel — but it has the persistence of a machine, the rhythmic insistence of something that has been running longer than memory. It is the sound of the builders working. You cannot see them yet. You may never see them clearly. They are small and many and they keep to the interiors of their structures, and what you hear is the aggregate of their activity: a wet, continuous churning, like a river heard through stone, punctuated by the occasional percussive note — something heavy being moved, or something being agreed upon, or something being born.

Then the smell reaches you. Warm and sweet and thick, the smell of a place where many living things are doing the work of living at volume. There is musk in it, and fermentation, and the green exhalation of a billion leaves transpiring in the high thin air, and beneath all of it a copper tang that you eventually decide is the smell of blood, though not of violence — of life merely continuing, of the small daily ruptures a body performs to keep itself going.

The river is the spine of the place. It emerges from a slot canyon in the northern wall and runs the length of the inhabited valley, wide and slow and dark, the color of tea brewed too long, and it is loud — louder than it should be, louder than a river of that breadth has any right to be — because the builders have built into it, have shaped its bed with weirs and ramps and resonating chambers, so that the water sings as it moves, a low sustained chord that underlies every other sound and makes the whole valley feel as though it has a single throat. Where the weirs pool, the water turns almost glassy and you can see, far down, pale shapes moving with purpose — the river is also a building, also a structure, also a body that the inhabitants maintain and are maintained by.

Above the terraces the vegetation has conquered everything that was not deliberately kept clear. Ferns the size of pavilions uncurl from every crack. Lianas as thick as your thigh drop from the upper ledges and root again in the lower ones, making curtains of green that move very slowly in breezes you cannot feel from the rim. There are flowers — enormous, fleshy, the color of liver and bruise, pollinated by creatures you catch in glimpses: something wingless and quick that runs along the underside of leaves, something else that hovers without wings at all, holding itself in the humid air with a patience that suggests it does not need to land. Orchids grow directly from the carved stone, their roots threaded into the mortar the builders used, or whatever passes for mortar here. The vegetation and the architecture have negotiated a treaty over centuries and neither side is winning.

You begin the descent. There is a path — there are many paths — switchbacking down the eastern wall in a series of broad steps worn smooth by the passage of many small feet. The steps are not for you. They were not made for any visitor. They are the ordinary routes of the inhabitants moving between their terraces, and you are simply tolerated on them, the way a breeze is tolerated, the way rain is tolerated.

Halfway down, the sound changes. The base note of the river is now close enough that you feel it in your jaw, and over it the inhabitants' industry has resolved into distinct registers: a scraping, a tapping, a sound like many wet palms pressed against stone and drawn slowly downward, and beneath everything a humming that is not quite vocal and not quite mechanical, a sound produced by bodies in numbers too large to count doing work in unison. The humming has melody. You cannot hold the melody in your head; it is too slow, its phrases too long. By the time you have grasped one phrase, another has begun, and the first has already faded into the architecture of the next.

You see your first inhabitant clearly at the third switchback. It is small — no larger than your hand — and it is built from many smaller things. You cannot tell where one creature ends and another begins. It is a moving mosaic of segments, each segment a living thing, the whole behaving as a single animal with a coordinated intention. It carries a droplet of water in a cupped appendage, moving it with great deliberateness from one carved depression to another in the terrace wall. It does not acknowledge you. It is busy. Everything here is busy.

There are thousands of them. There are millions. The terraces, when you reach them, are not the empty stone galleries they appeared from the rim — they are dense with motion, with bodies stacked on bodies, with the slow traffic of segments moving along grooves worn into the floor, carrying materials you cannot name to destinations you cannot see. The air itself, in the inhabited layers, seems to have weight, seems to be a substance the creatures swim through. It is warm and damp and it moves in currents you can feel against your skin, and it smells of copper and green sugar and the faint ozone of many small electrical presences.

The light down here is dim. The canyon is narrow enough that the sun, even at noon, only glances down the upper walls, and the vegetation has closed overhead in places like a cathedral ceiling, so that the inhabited terraces exist in a perpetual dusk that is nevertheless full of a soft reddish glow — bioluminescence, you realize after a while. The creatures themselves are the light. Their segments, where they join, give off a faint steady warmth that is more felt than seen, and in the deeper chambers, in the domed structures that open onto the terraces, you can see the glow intensify, can see it pulse very slowly, a heartbeat visible from outside.

You find a place to sit. A low carved bench, worn by use, facing the river. The sound is enormous here — the water-song and the workers' hum braid together into something that fills the skull completely, that leaves no room for thought, that is not trying to leave room for thought. This is not a place that wants to be understood. This is a place that simply is, and has been, for a very long time, and will be for a very long time after.

A segment crosses your foot. It is cool and dry and it moves with purpose. It does not pause.

Somewhere above you, at the rim, the sun is doing whatever the sun does. Down here, it hardly matters. The builders will work through the night, and through the next day, and through the day after that, shaping the stone a little at a time, raising their terraces another millimeter higher, deepening their weirs another fraction, singing their long unending song. The canyon is not finished. It will never be finished. That is not a melancholy thought here. It is simply the weather.

You sit a long time. The bench is patient. The light does not change. The sound does not change. You begin to feel that you have been here a long time, that you have always been here, that the bench has shaped itself slightly to accommodate your particular weight, and that somewhere in the city a new groove has begun to be worn into a stone floor, and that this groove is, in some way you will never be able to explain, yours.
