# The Sun Pit

There is a place where the ground opens, and the sun pours down into it.

The opening above is irregular—not round, not square, but toothed, as though something had bitten through the crust of the world and left ragged edges. From the air, it would look like a wound in the pale grass of the plateau. From below, it looks like the mouth of a furnace, except cold. A column of white daylight falls straight down through it, so bright at its source that you cannot look up into it without your eyes watering. The column thickens as it descends, diffusing against the walls, and where it strikes the floor of the pit it spreads into a pool of brilliance that paints the stone pale gold.

The pit itself is older than anything that lives in it. This much is obvious from the first glance at the walls: they are worn into long, slow curves, the limestone and pale sandstone polished in places to a satin smoothness that catches the light and throws it back in soft flares. Water has done most of the work over the eons—seeping down, dripping, evaporating, returning—and the walls bear the record of every year it has spent here, in the form of dull mineral streaks and the faint scalloping where softer layers have yielded first. The chamber is roughly the size of a modest cathedral: tall enough that a tall tree could stand inside it with room to spare, and broad enough that a small village could hold a meeting in the middle of it without anyone touching elbows. The floor is uneven but mostly flat, sloping gently toward the back where the lowest point lies, and at that lowest point there is water.

Not much water. A shallow, kidney-shaped pool, perhaps four paces across at its widest, with a pebbled margin that emerges and disappears over the weeks as the slow seepage rises and falls. Around its edges, and in the shadowed regions away from the central shaft of light, the stone is dark with persistent moisture. Condensation gathers on the walls in the cooler hours and rolls down in lazy beads, tracing the same paths the seepage has traced for centuries. When a drop falls from high up on the ceiling, the sound is a single small *tok* that echoes softly for a long time afterward, as though the pit is considering whether to say something in reply.

The light is the strangest thing about the place, stranger even than its geography, because of how it behaves. It enters as a single shaft, then breaks against the irregular floor and walls and scatters into a soft, all-pervading glow that fills the chamber almost evenly. There are no true shadows. Every surface, even the deepest crevices, carries some illumination. Things at the back of the pit, far from the opening, are not dark so much as they are silver, lit by light that has been bounced a hundred times before it reaches them. It gives the air itself a milky quality, as though the pit is full of a thin, bright fog.

Vegetation has come in sparingly, through the years, by way of that opening. A few tough ferns have taken root in the cracks where moisture gathers, their fronds a dusty blue-green that curls inward against the dryness of the air. There is moss in the dampest shadows, low and dense, the color of tarnished copper. Near the water's edge, a stand of pale reeds has established itself, growing thin and slow, leaning slightly toward the light as if uncertain which way to face. None of it is lush; all of it is patient. The plants here live on condensation and the rare rain that must blow sideways into the opening during the wet season. They are small, and they grow slowly, and they seem to know that this is not their true climate but a borrowed one.

The animals, though. The animals are another matter.

The chamber is full of them, in the sense that you cannot stand in any one place for long without hearing or seeing one move, and yet the pit is also mostly empty. The walls are not crawling with life. There is no dense carpet of movement. What there is, is presence: scattered individuals, widely spaced, each going about its own business in its own corner. The air, when it stirs, seems to carry small sounds—soft clicks, papery rustlings, the occasional liquid trill that comes from no visible source and stops before you can locate it.

The largest of the creatures are pale and flightless, something between a hen and a lizard, with long necks and small, clever heads that tilt sideways to look at you. They stand motionless in the diffuse light, and their skin is so close to the color of the stone that you almost walk into them before you see them. They eat the moss, and the tender new shoots of the ferns, and they drink from the pool. There are perhaps a dozen of them, spread across the chamber, and they are wary of humans in a way that suggests memory—that humans have been here before, that humans are dangerous or were once dangerous, and that the lesson has been passed down. They move in short darts when they move at all, with a low, hooting call that carries further than you'd think possible in a closed space.

Smaller things live in the cracks and the moss. Long-bodied things, slender and pale, that flow along the walls like spilled water, feeding on the insects that are themselves feeding on the moss. The insects are delicate and almost transparent, with iridescent wings that flash violet and green when the light catches them. They do not fly well here; the air is too still for it. They mostly walk, or hop, or make small, gliding descents from one surface to another. There is a faint hum to the place, beneath the silence, that is made up of all of them.

And there are the things in the pool. You do not see them unless you sit very still at its edge for a long time. They are pale, soft, slow creatures—larger than your hand—with many small limbs and a face that is mostly eyes. They float just below the surface, where the light comes through and warms the shallow water, and they seem to be waiting for something. They wait, and they wait, and occasionally one of them rises a little higher and you can see the pale ridges of its back, and then it sinks again. There is no knowing what they are waiting for. They have been waiting a long time.

There is no sound here except the drips, and the soft hoots of the pale birds, and the almost-inaudible hum of the insects, and the slow sigh of the air as it moves through the opening above and down into the chamber. There is no architecture. No one has carved anything here, or built anything, or made anything more permanent than a footprint. The tools that have touched this place, if any tools have, left no mark. It is, in that sense, an untouched place, a place that has only ever belonged to itself and the slow processes of water and light and the patient creatures that learned to live in the bright hollow under the ground.

If you sit with your back against the warm wall and let the diffused light fall across you, and wait long enough that the birds forget to fear you, and listen past the silence to the silence underneath it, you begin to feel what the pit feels like from the inside: a long, slow inhalation, a held breath, the world gathered into one small bright hollow and held there.

The light does not move much across the day. The opening is high enough that the sun, when it crosses, only shifts the position of the bright pool on the floor by a little. The seasons change the water level, and the moss grows a little thicker or a little thinner, and the birds molt, and the things in the pool drift a little closer to the surface or a little deeper. Nothing happens quickly. Nothing here is in a hurry. The pit has been here a long time, and it expects to be here a long time still.

You, sitting in it, feel very temporary. The stone does not mind this. The birds do not mind this. The light, which has traveled ninety-three million miles to fall here, does not mind at all.

It is the kind of place you leave quietly, and then walk back to, sometimes, in the years after, when you want to remember what silence sounds like when it is full.
