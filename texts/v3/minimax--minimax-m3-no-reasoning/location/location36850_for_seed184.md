# The Settlement Under the Hush

I should start somewhere, so I'll start with the cold. Not the cold of ice — this place is warm, you understand, but the water has a particular way of moving past a body that makes the warmth feel like a thing being applied rather than felt. The warmth of bathwater grown long past comfortable. The warmth of a held hand that has forgotten to let go. Down here, twenty meters below a surface I have not seen in years, the warmth is the atmosphere itself, pressed close.

The settlement is built into a fold of basalt that rises from the silt like a long broken rib. The basalt is older than anything I can name — it has been here when the water was shallow, when it was deeper, when it was this, whatever this is. The rock has settled into its own patience. Crustaceans have threaded their calcium filigree along every joint, so that the stone looks embroidered, looks lived-in, looks like something a careful thing made on purpose over many centuries.

The buildings cling to this spine. They are not tall — nothing here is tall, because tall is a vanity that needs air and a horizon to measure against. They are wide, low, pressed together so thoroughly that a traveler cannot tell where one structure ends and another begins. The walls are poured concrete, pumped down through shafts in better years and now, in the dim years, cast in place by divers who mix the slurry from local sand. The buildings lean against each other like tired people on a train. Their roofs are dome-shaped, which is structural, which is hydrostatic, but which also makes the place feel, from inside, like the inside of many skulls joined end to end.

I should describe the light. The light is the wrong word. There is a glow, and the glow comes from three sources, and none of them are the sun.

The first source is the lamps. Sodium-throated, hung in cages along the basalt ridge, they give a jaundice-colored light that reaches about four meters before surrendering to the murk. The second is the water itself, faintly, the way certain deep waters carry a ghost of bioluminescence even in places nothing should be living — a residue, perhaps, of the old phytoplankton, or something newer that has learned to mimic them. The third is the people, and I will come to the people.

The murk is not total. Visibility on a still day — the days are not days here, the workers keep their own shifts, but a still shift — might be eight meters. On a working shift, with the dredges running, it drops to two, and the lamps become necessary, and the world becomes a corridor of lit bubbles and moving shadows and the particular gloom of fluorescent light filtered through brine. The water carries silt, always. It is never quite clear. It has the color of weak coffee, of old lace, of a cataract.

The sound. The first hour a visitor spends here, they listen for the water to roar. It does not roar. There is a low hum from the industrial works — the pumps, the separators, the anaerobic digesters turning in their slow stomachs — but beneath that, and sometimes louder, is a quiet so complete it has texture. The water absorbs everything. Footsteps, voices, the clink of a tool dropped against a railing: all of it is swallowed within a meter, muffled into a kind of plush silence that presses against the ears. People speak to each other at close range. They develop small voices, careful voices, voices for the next person and no farther. There is no echo. The basalt drinks sound. The concrete drinks sound. The water, generous with everything else, takes the sound too.

I should describe the streets. They are not streets. They are corridors. The settlement is laid out along the basalt spine for about three hundred meters, and the buildings rise on either side, and between them are passages roofed with the same poured concrete, and within those passages the residents move on foot or by small submersible cart. The carts are electric, were electric before the war, will be electric when the war is forgotten, and they creep along the corridors with their pale headlamps picking out the next bend. The air — I keep saying the water, I mean the breathing mixture, the pumped atmosphere kept at one-point-two atmospheres in the habitation shells — the air in the corridors smells of brine and machine oil and, beneath that, faintly, of the cooking. The cooking is rice, mostly, and the small pale fish that the dredges bring up, and a fermented paste of some kind of kelp that I have not yet learned to like.

The vegetation. Yes. This surprised me too, the first time I saw it. The whole fold of the settlement is a kind of farm, although the word farm is too pastoral for what is happening here. Along the basalt, in pockets of crushed shell and recycled organic, the residents grow kelp — long ribbons of it, anchored to the rock with weights, swaying in whatever current finds its way into the settlement's lee. The kelp is the color of weak tea, and it grows toward the lamps in a slow, blind reaching. There is also a rice, a paddy rice grown in sealed trays under the lowest domes, where the air is thickest and the warmth is most held. The rice is a small miracle of engineering; it requires the pumping of fresh water, which the settlement cannot afford to do often, so it is grown in cycles, and the cycles are a kind of religion. There are mushrooms, too, stacked in dark shelves in the wettest corridors, white and patient and smelling of loam. The vegetation, when I first saw it, made me want to cry, and I did, a little, in the privacy of my rented room. The plants are so insistent. They are doing what plants do, here at the bottom of an ocean, in the dim glow of sodium lamps, with a patience I find hard to look at directly.

The fauna is rare, and this is the strange part. I will come to the strange part. But the fauna — there are fish, small pale ones, that come in through the filtration barriers when the dredges disturb the silt. They are edible. They are also, the residents tell me, a sign of poor maintenance; the filters are supposed to keep them out. The residents eat them anyway. There are the crustaceans I mentioned, the ones that embroider the basalt; they are not eaten, they are too slow to catch, and also, I think, the residents have made a kind of peace with them. There is something else. Something I have not yet seen, that the residents speak of in lowered voices, that I have heard referred to as the visitor, or the long one, or, once, by an old woman in a sari, simply *it*. I do not press. I have been here eleven days. There is time.

The industry. The settlement exists to mine the basalt, or to mine what is in the basalt, or to process what was mined from the basalt in some earlier age — I have not yet untangled which. There are digesters, large as houses, that take the organic sludge of the settlement and the swept-up silt and turn it, slowly, into methane and a kind of protein paste that supplements the rice. There are pumps, large and small, that move water in and out of the filtration curtains. There are the dredges themselves, which go out into the dark water and bring back — I do not know what. I have not been allowed onto the dredges. The workers who crew them are a different caste from the corridor-dwellers; they speak less, they smell of the deep water, their eyes are red from the pressure of the dive, and they are given a different ration, which I take to mean a better one. The industry is what makes the place. Without the digesters and the dredges, the settlement would be a submarine village of fishers and kelp-farmers, and it would not have survived the long years, the years when the surface was something you no longer talked about, the years before the lamps were relit after the long dark.

I should describe the people, although describing them feels like a trespass. They are small, mostly. The children are smaller. They move in the careful way of people who have learned not to make unnecessary gestures in a place where every gesture displaces water and disturbs the silt and draws the attention of things in the murk. Their clothes are patched, layered, repaired past the point of recognition. They are kind to me in the particular way of people who have decided I am a temporary phenomenon, and they are also watchful, and the watchfulness is not unkind. They have not yet decided what I am. I have not yet decided what I am.

The dim light falls equally on everything. There is no shadow in the settlement, or rather, the shadows are so soft and so unchanging that they cease to function as shadows and become instead a kind of second color, a darker tone laid over the first. The domes of the buildings are grey-brown. The corridors are grey-brown. The water beyond the windows is grey-brown. The faces of the people, when I see them in close conversation in the lit doorways, are the only things with any color to them, and even that color is muted, as though the murk has gotten into the blood.

The settlement is small. I can walk its full length in twenty minutes. I have done this several times, at different shifts, and I have come to know its rhythms, the way the carts move more often at certain hours, the way the dredge crews return with their holds heavy and their eyes redder, the way the lamps flicker when the pumps cycle up, the way the silence deepens in the small hours when even the digesters seem to be resting. The settlement is small the way a lung is small, or a heart: contained, complete, doing its work, the whole of it pressed close against the whole of everything else.

There is the strange part, which I have been avoiding. The strange part is that the settlement is older than it should be. The concrete is poured in layers that show, when you look closely, different techniques, different tools, different hands. The basalt has been worked and reworked, the embroidery of the crustaceans scraped away in places and allowed to return in others. The domes have been patched with domes. The corridors have been rerouted, blocked, opened again, in patterns I cannot read. The residents do not speak of this, or they speak of it as one speaks of weather, as a thing that has always been and will always be. There is a feeling in the place of great age, of something that has been here long before the current residents, that has gone through other residents, that may go through these. The settlement is a kind of patient. It is weathering, it is settled, it is in its own state, as the parameter might put it. It is in balance with the water, with the silt, with the crustaceans, with the kelp, with whatever the long one is that the residents do not speak of. It is not waiting for anything. It is not going anywhere. It is the kind of place that has decided, with great quietness, to continue.

I will stay here for some time. I will learn the names of the corridors. I will learn the names of the people. I will, perhaps, see the long one, or I will not. The water will continue to press its warm and smothering attention against the domes. The lamps will continue their small yellow work. The dredges will go out and come back. The rice will be grown. The kelp will reach. The silence, which is not silence but a kind of dense and living quiet, will continue to hold everything in its plush and careful hands.

I am, in the meantime, learning to speak in a small voice. I am learning to move without disturbing the silt. I am learning, as the residents have learned, that a place can be warm and dark and full of water and still be a place where a person can sit down and be still, and be fed, and rest, and wait for the long and patient morning that may or may not come.
