# The Basin

The place is cupped into the flank of something taller. A horseshoe of stone rises around it, the rim uneven, not so much a wall as a held breath — and the floor inside is gently sloped, never quite flat, holding water the way cupped hands hold rain. Perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest. Beyond the rim the mountainside continues upward into mist; below, the land falls away in a long, sloping flank. But the basin itself is bounded. You feel the bound as soon as you step inside it. The air thickens. The light settles. The world outside continues, and you can hear it — wind, distant — but here, in the bowl, there is a held quality, as though the place has been thinking about itself for a very long time and has reached a kind of agreement with its own shape.

The stone of the rim is old. Weathered into a soft skin that is almost more moss than rock, with the rock beneath having taken on the slow color of wet iron and lichen. It is not bare stone. Almost nothing here is bare. A fine felt of green — three greens, at least, ranging from a deep bottle-glass in the shadowed cracks to a pale almost-yellow where the sun finds it — covers nearly every exposed surface. Where the rim breaks, where there is a notch or a low place, water comes through. It comes through in threads. It comes through in sheets. It comes through the rock itself, weeping from vertical faces in a slow, persistent sheen, gathering into bead and runnel, finding the lowest point of the basin floor.

The floor is boggy without being a true bog. The water is shallow — an inch here, a hand's depth there — and it moves, but slowly, in a patient circulation that seems to know where it is going. It gathers along certain lines. Where it gathers, the green is darker, the moss is deeper, and certain plants grow that do not grow elsewhere. The soil between the wet lines is also damp, even when the sun has been on it for hours. You can press your thumb into it and water will rise to meet the print within seconds. The whole basin seems to sweat, gently, constantly, even in warm clear weather. When a cloud passes over the rim, the moisture on the stone darkens, and the smell rises — a green smell, mineral, like the inside of a deep well or the breath of the earth after rain.

The plants are sparse but emphatic. They do not crowd. They stand, each one, with space around it, and the space is part of what they are. There is a kind of squat tree — not quite a tree, more a shrub that has committed — with a gnarled dark trunk and a flat-topped crown of small, rounded, glossy leaves. There are perhaps nine of them in the basin, none near the rim but all in the middle distance of the floor, spaced as if they had agreed. Between them, taller, a fern that has no business being where it is — waist-high, fronds rising in pale green fists — and lower, a kind of ground-thing with small white flowers that never quite open, that stay in bud, plump and patient, year after year. There are mosses of several textures: cushion moss, broom moss, a flat sheet of something so close to the stone it could be the stone's own skin. There is one plant you cannot name, a single specimen, growing from a crack in the rim-rock about two-thirds of the way up. It has a thick pale stem and three broad leaves with red veins, and it trembles when no wind is moving. When a wind moves, it does not tremble. This is one of the small strangenesses.

And the stones.

The stones.

At first you take them for the bones of the place — the outcrops, the tumbled blocks from the rim. Then you notice that some of them are shaped. A slab, leaned against the inner wall of the basin, with a flat face that has been ground smooth. Not recently. Long ago. The grinding is worn now, and the moss has crept onto it, and it leans at an angle that has not changed in a long time. A short distance from it, a smaller stone with a depression in its upper surface — a bowl-stone, a mortar-stone, the kind of thing a hand has worked against. Beside it, scattered in the moss, three or four smaller stones that fit the hand. They are not arranged. They are also not random. They lie as if they were set down a long time ago and never picked up.

There is no structure. No wall, no roof, no path, no clear footprint of any civilization. But there is the evidence — unmistakable, patient — of hands. Hands that worked stone simply. Hands that came here and stood here and set things down and went away. The basin has been inhabited, perhaps, in the loosest sense of the word. Visited. Tended. Left.

And the strangeness deepens with the silence.

There are no animals. None. No birds overhead, no insects humming in the ferns, no rustle in the moss, no fox-bark in the distance, no frog-call from the wet places. The basin is profoundly, almost oppressively empty of any moving creature other than yourself. The water does not even seem to host tadpoles or water-bugs. You look, and you look again, and you cannot find anything that breathes. The plants grow without being eaten. The water moves without being drunk. The whole place is a closed, self-sustaining ecology of green and stone and wet, and it operates in a kind of absolute hush.

The sounds that do exist are water-sounds, and they are small. A drip from the lip of a stone. The faint hiss of a thin sheet of water running over moss. A deeper, almost subterranean note when you cup your ear near a wet crack in the rock — the slow movement of water somewhere inside the stone. Nothing louder. Nothing approaching a voice, an animal, a wind in branches. The trees on the rim are too small and too gnarled to catch much wind, and when a gust does reach the basin it moves the ferns and stops, and the ferns return to stillness, and the silence closes again like water over a dropped pebble.

The light is normal — the sun comes up, the sun goes down, the shadows move, the clouds shift. But it falls into the basin strangely because of the rim, and the moss and the wet surfaces respond to it with a kind of patient gleam. In the morning the mist often lies in the basin, a foot deep, white, slowly thinning. In the afternoon the light reaches the floor in patches that travel. In the evening the wet stone holds the last of the light for a long time, glowing a dim rust color, and the dark green of the plants becomes very dark, and the small white buds of the ground-flowers become very bright.

Time here is slow. Not because anything supernatural is happening, but because nothing is rushing. The water moves at the pace of drips. The plants grow by the season, not by the day. The worked stones have been worked and weathered and left and worked-on-by-weather for so long that their shape now is as much a result of moss and frost as of hands. The whole basin is in balance. What grows, decays. What decays, feeds what grows. The water comes, goes nowhere — sinks into the floor, or evaporates, or seeps back into the rock — and then comes again. Nothing accumulates. Nothing depletes. The place has settled into itself the way a river settles into its bed, and it continues.

And yet.

And yet there is the plant with the red veins that trembles when there is no wind. There is the way the white buds never open. There is the fact that no creature has ever crossed the rim, or if it has, it has not stayed, or has not been heard. There is the arrangement of the worked stones — not a circle, not a path, but something more like a thought, half-remembered, left mid-sentence. There is the way the basin feels held. As though it is being cupped not just by rock but by attention, by the long gaze of the place itself, turning slowly inward.

You stand in it, and you feel you have been let in.

And the quiet continues.
