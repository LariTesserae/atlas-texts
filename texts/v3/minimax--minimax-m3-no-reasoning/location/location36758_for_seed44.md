# The Weeping Hall

The first thing is the moisture. It is everywhere. It beads on every surface, drips from every overhang, rises in a thin mist from the warm stone floor, condenses on the rusted iron of the railings, and gathers in shallow copper basins set into the walls at knee height. The air is a wet cloth pressed against the skin. When you exhale, you can see it. The whole interior of this place sweats.

You enter through a low arch and stand inside a vaulted chamber that is, technically, a train station. Or it was. The scale is small — only a hundred meters long, perhaps thirty wide, perhaps that high at the central keystone of the ceiling — but the proportions trick the eye. The arches lift and step inward like the inside of a throat. They are made of old red brick, blackened in patches by a century of damp, and every joint between bricks weeps a slow trickle of groundwater that has migrated down through the layers of soil and root above. There are stalactites, but they are mineral-rust-orange, not limestone-white, and they are short, no longer than a finger, and they bleed.

The plants are the architecture now, or they have become so interwoven with it that you cannot say where the construction ends and the biology begins. Ferns erupt from every horizontal surface, from the tops of the ticket booths, from the sills of the long-derelict windows, from the backs of the wooden benches that are mostly compost by now. There is a fig tree, or a single organism that has organized itself as a fig tree, growing up through the central platform where once passengers stood. It has split the concrete slab in two along a seam, and its pale roots grip the broken edges like fingers. The trunk is nearly a meter across at the base, and it has grown flat against the nearest wall and climbed it, branching into the brickwork, threading its way between the voussoirs of the arches. Its leaves are the size of dinner plates and they are the brightest thing here. Everything else is in the green-brown-black spectrum of a place that is wet and shaded and full of decay in progress.

The ceiling is the second canopy. Spanish moss, or what grows here in the place of Spanish moss — long silver-grey festoons of a Tillandsia relative, or something else entirely, something with the same habit — hangs from every rusted girder and every dripping rebar end. It brushes the tops of your shoulders when you pass beneath it. It is dry to the touch only in the sense that it isn't currently dripping, but it is full of held water, and when a breeze moves through the station it releases a fine rain that smells of tannin and old iron.

There is a sound in this place that does not stop. It is the sound of water falling, water dripping, water trickling along channels it has cut for itself in the brick over decades, water hammering in a steady percussion from somewhere in the upper infrastructure, water somewhere always running. Underneath the water-sound is the deeper sound of the plant respiration — not literally the sound of breathing, but the collective, low, continuous rustle of a million leaves adjusting to air movement, of fiddleheads uncurling, of a vine somewhere tightening its grip on a stone it means to crack. The station breathes. It is loud with the business of being a wet living thing.

It is warm. The heat comes up from the earth and from the slow decomposition of all the organic matter that has accumulated, and it holds in the vaulted chamber. Sweat runs on you. The plants love it.

There are people here, or there have been. The remnants of a settlement scale in this place — the long wooden platforms with their brass fittings mostly intact, the iron stanchions that once supported a roof of glass over the central waiting area, the painted signs that read, in a font of the previous century, the names of destinations that may not exist in their old form anymore. The signs are colonized. A pothos has climbed one and pinned it flat against a column. Another sign has been taken over so completely that the letters are now legible only as breaks in a wall of green, the painted white visible only where the plant has chosen not to grow.

The technology is here too, and it is the part of the place that is hardest to read. There are machines in the walls. You can see where they have been — the mounting plates, the severed conduits, the empty bolt-holes in a pattern that suggests something large was once bolted there and is now gone. There is one machine remaining. It is in the eastern annex, behind a door that is half-eaten by a strangler fig, and it is a steam pump, or a steam pump's descendant — a vertical iron cylinder taller than a person, with a flywheel and a piston and a firebox that is cold and banked with ash, connected by an unintelligible confusion of pipes to a borehole that has been driven down through the floor into the water table. The pump has not run in years, but the pipes still sweat, and a slow drip falls from the flywheel's lowest point into a catch-basin where a small fern has established itself in the rust-flakes. Someone understood the water problem here. Someone sank a bore and built a pump to keep the station from drowning in its own seeping, and then either the pump failed, or its operators left, or the water won anyway, and now the pump is a curiosity in a museum no one curates.

The settlement that remains is on the upper gallery, where a wooden walkway runs the length of the station at second-floor height. There are four or five structures up there — wooden shacks, mostly, with corrugated metal roofs and small windows, and a larger building at the far end that was once a stationmaster's office and is now something else. A few of the shacks have laundry hung outside, drying slowly in the warm damp air. A single electric light is on, somewhere up there, a bare bulb behind a wire cage, casting a yellow circle that does not reach the floor. Smoke rises from one of the metal chimneys and dissolves into the mossy ceiling without ever making it out.

The people are few. You might see one in an hour. An old woman on the gallery, sorting something into a basin. A child crossing the broken platform below with a stick. They are quiet. The water is louder than they are. They have the look of people who have come to terms with the damp, who wear it, whose clothes are the same green-black as the walls. They do not seem to find this place remarkable. It is, for them, the way the world is.

Animals are rare. A small lizard, green-brown, that lives in the brick joints and eats the insects that are themselves eating the wood. A single cat, orange, that is asleep on the warm iron top of the dead pump. Mosquitoes, of course. An owl, perhaps, in the upper reaches — there are pale droppings on the gallery floor that suggest something roosts there at night.

The dynamic of the place is slow. You could sit on the broken platform for an hour and see almost no change. A leaf would unfurl. A drop would fall. A root would thicken imperceptibly against a brick. The fig would continue its patient work of pulling the station apart. The water would continue its patient work of pulling the station apart. In a hundred years, nothing of the original structure will remain, except perhaps a few of the iron fittings, the deeply-buried foundation stones, the brass nameplate of the station if someone has been careful with it. In a thousand years, even the iron will be mostly mineral. The fig will be a forest, fed by the water, holding the memory of the brick in its wood.

For now, though, the two processes have reached a kind of balance. The plants are winning, but they are winning slowly, and the structure still asserts itself in the geometry of the arches, in the regularity of the brick courses, in the flat planes of the platform edges that the roots have not yet been able to break. The water is winning too, but it is winning as a permanent condition, not as a flood. It is a wet place, settled into its own wetness.

The sound is loud. The sound is the sound of water falling on water, water falling on stone, water falling on leaves, leaves falling on water, and under it all, the slow grind of the pump that is no longer pumping, the memory of a mechanical heartbeat in a place that has gone back to a biological one.

It is warm. It is wet. It is dense with green. It is small enough to walk across in two minutes. It is a cathedral that has decided to be a forest that has decided to be a home.

It is not strange. It is exactly what happens when you leave a warm wet place alone for a long time, and people stay.
