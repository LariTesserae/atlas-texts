# The Verdant Furnace

There is no ground here. Not really. What registers as ground is a dense, unbroken crust of root — a single organism's body, or ten thousand organisms' bodies woven so thoroughly together that the distinction has ceased to matter. The crust is dark, almost black in the dimness, and it gives slightly under any weight, the way a living thing gives. Walk across it and you feel the whole surface shift, a continent-scale shudder, because the root mat is hundreds of meters thick and it is breathing.

Above the crust: a forest. But not a forest of trees, exactly. A forest of *growth* — stalks, columns, buttresses, fans, lattices, all pressed shoulder-to-shoulder in the packed dark. They are the color of old iron, of dried blood, of the underside of a mushroom cap. Some are smooth and columnar, ten meters across and rising out of sight into a permanent twilight. Some branch into thickets of softer, paler filaments — these are the photosynthetic surfaces, though the light they receive is meager. The plants are doing something other than photosynthesis, or something additional. They are eating the air. They are eating the heat. They are eating the faint metallic tang that rises out of the root mat below.

The air is hot, and the heat is the point. The whole landscape is hot — has been hot for so long that the plants are not adapted to heat, they are *constituted* by it, every enzyme optimized, every membrane tuned to temperatures that would cook water and boil sap and reduce a Terran leaf to black paste in seconds. There is no water. There has not been water for longer than these plants have existed. Their internal fluid is a briny supercritical liquid, held under pressures that would rupture any Earthly cell wall. They are not alive in the way a fern is alive. They are alive in the way a kiln is alive, or a smelter.

And they are old. The deepest root layers are mineralized — petrified into a kind of dark stone, laced with metal sulfides, compressed under their own cumulative weight into something that could be quarried. New growth burrows into the old growth, eats the old growth, replaces the old growth. The whole system has been churning on itself for so long that the *idea* of an original substrate is archaeological. Every surface you see is at least tenth-generation recycled matter. The plants are their own ancestors. The air is recycled exhaled gas from a billion respiring columns. The dust in the air is recycled cell wall. Nothing new has entered the system in geological ages. It is an old, old, very old place that has eaten itself many times over and somehow kept going.

What breaks the dark is the infrastructure.

It comes in at the edges first — the perception of edges, the way the columns thin or the root crust undulates into a shallow depression. Then the shapes resolve: pipes. Gantries. Conveyors made of what looks like woven living fiber, the same dark red-black as the columns around them, but with a regularity that the plants do not possess. The plant columns accommodate the pipes. The pipes accommodate the plant columns. They have grown into each other over centuries and you cannot tell anymore which is using which.

The pipes carry heat. They carry vaporized minerals, slag, the volatile fractions of old root matter that has been cracked somewhere below. They carry a thin bright liquid that is not water but does some of the same jobs, and where the pipes leak — and they all leak, they are all leaking, this is not a system that was ever watertight — the liquid falls in shining threads through the dimness and is caught by the plant fans below, which close around it in slow peristalsis. The plants drink. The pipes feed them. The plants, in turn, are harvested. You can see the cut stalks, the regrowth, the careful geometry of a column that has been topped and is sending out new shoots in a whorl around the wound. There is a smell of char and caramel and something ammoniac.

The industrial machinery is mostly underground. You infer it from the sounds — a low, irregular throbbing, the groan of something large being pumped or compressed, the hiss of a pressure relief somewhere in the middle distance. The surface machinery is just the intake vents, the access shafts, the loading gantries where dark fluid is being siphoned from a column that has been tapped with a metal spile. There are figures moving in the dimness, but you cannot see them well. They are small against the columns. They are wearing something reflective on their faces. They move quickly and they do not speak. The sound of their boots on the root crust is a soft crush, repeated.

The dark is not the dark of night. It is the dark of a closed space, a high place, an atmosphere thick with particulates. The sky, if there is a sky, is the color of a furnace viewed from inside. There is a dull red glow at the horizon in all directions — not a sun, not a sunrise, just the ambient luminescence of a world that is too hot to be dark and too dark to be seen, and the glow leaks down between the columns and turns the dust in the air into a slow-falling rust. The columns themselves are faintly bioluminescent in places, in long pale streaks, where the tissue has been stressed or wounded. The whole forest glimmers with a heat-light that does not quite illuminate. You can find your way by the gleam of wounded plants.

It is quiet. The hum of the machinery is felt more than heard, a subsonic pressure in the chest. The leaves — the soft filaments — do not rustle, because there is not enough moisture in them to rustle with, and the air does not move enough to push them. What sound there is comes in occasional events: a distant crash as a column is felled, a long slow hiss as a pipe is tapped, the muffled percussion of something detonating far below, the groan of a load-bearing root giving way somewhere across the mat. These sounds travel strangely. The packed growth absorbs them, muffles them, and you cannot tell if a sound is near or far until it has been going for several seconds. The darkness and the sound and the heat together produce a particular disorientation, the sense of being inside something that is breathing and that you are very small.

The place is turbulent. It does not stop. The plant growth is always advancing, always being cut back, always regrowing. The pipes are always being unclogged, rerouted, extended. The columns are always being tapped, harvested, replaced. The whole forest is a slow riot of consumption and regrowth and harvest and regrowth, and the humans — if they are humans — are moving through it at the pace of the harvest, doing the necessary work, keeping the pipes flowing, keeping the columns from choking the gantries, keeping the whole system from collapsing into itself. The dust falls. The glow seeps. The columns breathe. The pipes leak. The dark holds everything, and the heat holds the dark, and the plants hold the heat, and the people hold the plants, and the cycle goes on and has been going on for so long that no one in it can remember a time when the cycle was not.

It is not a hostile place. It is not a friendly place. It is a place that has settled into its final form and is running, running, running, the way a galaxy runs, the way a heart runs, and the running is the place, and the place is the running, and you are standing in the middle of it in the dimness with the heat on your face and the dust in your lungs and the soft crush of root under your boots, and the forest is watching you with a billion pale wounds, and the pipes are hissing, and somewhere far below something detonates and is immediately swallowed by the sound of the next thing beginning.
