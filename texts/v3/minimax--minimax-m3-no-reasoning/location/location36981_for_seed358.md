# The Hollow Floor

You descend a half-mile of switchback stair cut into wet clay, and the world above — the world of wind and weather — ends as a rumor. Down here, the air is something you have to push aside with your face. It weighs. It presses inward from all sides with the patient solidity of honey.

You arrive at a floor.

It is a single vast plane, and it has not yet decided what it is.

Beneath a low ceiling of limestone that weeps constantly — water gathers in the dimples of every stalactite, swells, drops, rings against the stone below — a brown light hovers in the air as if unwilling to settle. There is no sun. The light comes from the machines. They are everywhere.

---

## The Packed Light

Stand still for a moment and the eye begins to separate them. Banks of iron frames, each one the size of a wardrobe, each one fitted with a hundred small bulbs behind thick quartz. The bulbs are not bright, individually. They are not meant to be seen. They are meant to *amount*. Thousands of them — tens of thousands — hung in long tight rows along the ceiling ribs, along the walls, along pillars of rough brick that climb from floor to roof without breathing room. The light is the air. It does not fall on surfaces so much as fill them. The stone underfoot glows faintly back. Your own skin glows faintly back. Nothing casts a shadow here. Nothing has anywhere to throw it.

The machines are older than the town. Or perhaps the town is older than the machines. It is hard, on the floor, to keep the question.

---

## The Wet Floor

Water is the floor's first material, and the floor has not yet learned to refuse it.

Across the plain stretches a skin of standing water, knee-deep in places, ankle-deep in most, and never quite absent anywhere. It moves, but only in the way honey moves — downward, sideways, in slow patient sheets toward the long central drain that runs like a dark spine the length of the cavern. The drain is clogged with the works of the floor: it has been clogged for years. The water knows to lap around the obstruction rather than challenge it. Around every brick pillar, the water has built a small smooth-bouldered eddy. Around every machine-base, a moat. The floor is becoming, foot by foot, an archipelago. The islands are each the footprint of a workingman. The channels between them are each the width of a shoulder.

Step, and the water makes no sound against your boot. There is too much other sound for it to bother.

---

## The Noise

There is no silence on the floor. There has not been silence on the floor for as long as anyone can recall.

From every machine, from every pipe, from every brick column, a low continuous hum rises — not mechanical, exactly, but the sound that mechanical things make when they are pushed past the dignity of their design. The hum is everywhere and goes in every direction; it has no source and no center. It is the room's own voice, heard from inside the throat. It is the frequency at which the limestone weeps most steadily. It is the frequency at which the water chooses to fall from the stalactites above. A man who has lived here forty years cannot hear it any longer, in the same way that a man who has lived beside a fall cannot hear the fall. Newcomers weep the first day, not from grief but from the strange pressure in the inner ear, the way the sound seems to lean against the eardrum and wait.

Bolted to the iron frames, small hammers strike small anvils in staggered patterns. They are stamping something — dates, perhaps, or lot numbers, or the names of towns that no longer exist — onto small square things that fall from the machines into waiting hoppers. The hoppers overflow. The stamped squares spill across the brick islands, into the water, lap against the bases of the pillars. The floor is made of them, in some places. The water is made of them, in some places. You walk on them. They ring faintly under your boot like the thinnest possible bells. Above the hum, the ringing is a kind of punctuation. Above the ringing, the hammers themselves: *bam, bam, bam* — never all together, never quite apart.

A voice could not live here. Conversation is conducted palm-to-ear, mouth-to-ear, in the manner of soldiers under bombardment. The town on the floor has developed its own language of leaning and mouthing.

---

## The Town

The town is not so much *built* as *accumulated.*

Between the machines, against the pillars, tucked into the lee of the great iron frames, the people have made their small settlements. A cot hung from a nail. A stovepipe rising through a hole in a brick wall. A canvas sling for a child. None of these dwellings rise higher than a man's chest when he lies down. They are pressed into whatever floor-space the machines have not yet claimed, and the machines are always claiming more. When a new iron frame is craned down from the upper galleries, the dwellings nearest the chosen spot are simply disassembled overnight, and the families who lived in them wake to find their cots reassembled against the next nearest pillar, six feet further on. Nobody protests. There is nowhere above to protest *to.*

The people themselves are small and brown and quick. They move between the machines with the deft unhesitating certainty of eels in a familiar river. The children are smaller and quicker. They are sent, at six or seven, along the narrow ledges that ring the upper walls, where they adjust valves and unstick hoppers in places no adult can reach. The children sing up there, faintly, in counterpoint to the hammers. The sound comes down softened, like a remembered thing.

---

## The Animals

Rats are everywhere. The rats have grown long and pale and unfrightened of anything. They swim the channels between the islands. They ride the stamped squares down the chutes. They have learned to time the hammers and slip between their falls. There is nothing here for them to fear — the cats are also long and pale, and the cats have grown so lazy on stamped squares and machine-grease that the rats cross their paths without breaking stride. The cats watch. The rats permit themselves to be watched. The whole arrangement has the quality of a treaty observed too long.

Above, in the dimples of the stalactites, white crickets live and sing. Their sound is small and many. It threads through the hum like a finer thread through a coarser cloth. The cave is thick with them, and the cave is loud with them, and if a man stands very still for a long time he can hear, far above the ringing and the hammers and the hum, the slow irregular dripping of water from the highest points of the ceiling, where the machines have not yet climbed.

That dripping, down here, is the sound of the floor still being made.

---

## The Continuing

It is not finished, the floor. It is being extended, year by year, into the deeper limestone. The crews at the far edge — and the floor is a full mile across, and the far edge is half a mile from any man alive — are cutting new galleries, lowering new frames, threading new wires. They do this in slow time, in a single long forward motion. The water follows them in, threading the new channels. The rats follow the water. The crickets follow the rats. The people follow the rats. The machines follow the people.

The town on the floor will eventually be a town under a town under a town. The ceiling will rise. The water will rise with it, as water does. The hammering will go on. The dripping, far above, will go on. The light will continue to fill the room without ever lighting it.

If you stood at the very edge, where the crews are cutting, and cupped your hand to your ear against the noise, you might — if you were very patient, and if the moment were very still — make out, far ahead in the dark stone, the small sound of water finding a new way down.
