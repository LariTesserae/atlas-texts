The plain is hot and flat and stretches further than anyone bothers to walk. The ground is dark — a blackish gray, the color of wet ash, the color of old soot, the color of fill that was poured here decades ago to build on. Where the surface has cracked — by frost heave in some past, by the slow buckling of concrete under its own weight — you can see layers, but the layers are all the same color. Blackish gray. Older concrete over older ash over older fill. A faint slick of something lives on the wet surfaces — a microbial sheen, gray-green where the light catches it, never enough to be called growth — and that is all.

The air is wet and heavy and sits on you like cloth. Rain comes often. It comes sudden — a wall of it, horizontal in the wind — and it stops twenty minutes later, and the whole plain steams. The steam hangs, because the air is too thick to lift it. The clouds are gray-white and low and never seem to clear. The light that comes through them is dim and directionless, the kind of light that makes it hard to tell what time it is. In the morning it is slightly brighter. In the afternoon it is slightly brighter still. You cannot see the sun. At night, slightly darker — but the haze holds a faint amber glow from the plant lights and the flare stacks and the sodium lamps along the access roads.

There are buildings, but they are spaced far apart. Most of the plain is empty. Just the dark surface, and the pipe racks running to the horizon on their stanchions, and the access roads that aren't quite roads but just harder-packed ash. The buildings themselves are boxy, low, practical. They were built to last, and they have lasted — paint flaking, steel rusting in the permanent wet, but the structures are sound. Some are still in use: steam off them in the cooler hours, lights inside, trucks parked out front. Some are not. You can tell the difference. The unused ones are slowly returning to the weather — water creeping in through seams, doors swelling in their frames, the small fauna already inside.

The water is everywhere. It condenses on the pipes that run above ground. It condenses on the sides of the cylindrical tanks, runs down them in slow sheets. It weeps from the joints in the catwalks. It drips from the valves, plinking into the puddles below. The puddles are warm, black, still. After a rain they spread and connect, and the whole low ground becomes a sheet of water, and the buildings rise out of it like the things they replaced.

The fauna is overwhelming, but it is quiet. Rats — millions of them. They are under the buildings, in the pipe trenches, in the empty tanks, in the wall cavities, in the bundles of insulation behind the unused facilities. They move in waves at dusk, brown rivers running between the cover of one structure and the next. The largest are the size of small cats. They eat what they find — the industrial waste, the dead of their own kind, the food the workers bring, the grain in the storage sheds, the small things that live in the drainage ditches. Above them, on the higher steel, on the crossbeams of the pipe racks, on the rims of the flare stacks, are hawks and owls and a few ravens. On the ground, between the structures, are feral cats in loose colonies — mousers gone back to mousing, hunting the rats and hunted in turn by the hawks. There are no songbirds. There are no insects you can hear, except the mosquitoes, which rise after every rain in clouds so dense you can hear them as a single thin note, somewhere between a hiss and a hum.

There are also people, but they are few, and they come and go. They work the operating facilities and drive the trucks and service the flare stacks. They eat in the canteens and sleep in the dormitories and leave for the city at the end of their rotations. The place is not a town. It is not a city. It is an industrial region — a zone, a corridor, a stretch of plain given over to the things that need to be made and the things that need to be stored. Cables run overhead on wooden poles. Conduits run underground. The rail line comes in from somewhere and stops at the loading gantry and does not go anywhere further. The whole layout was planned, once, by people who knew what they were doing, and the planning is still legible in the grid of the roads and the right-angle jogs of the pipe trenches.

The sound is mostly quiet. You can hear the rats if you listen — a faint rustling, a scrabbling inside a wall. You can hear the larger animals moving through the pipe trenches. You can hear the water — dripping, weeping, plinking. You can hear the low hum of the operating facilities — pumps, fans, the slow breath of the place — but it is low, because the air is thick and absorbs the sound, and because the facilities are spaced far apart and there is too much empty plain between them for the hum to carry. When something loud happens — a flare stack firing, a pressure relief valve lifting, a hawk hitting a rat at speed — it sounds louder than it should, because the quiet is so complete.

There is weather, but it has no variety. Hot. Wet. The same. The rain comes, the rain stops, the steam rises, the haze thickens, the light is always the same dim gray. The seasons, if there are seasons, are not visible here. There may be seasons upstream, where the air comes from, but here the weather simply goes on, the way the pipes drip, the way the rust grows, the way the rats breed and the rats die and are eaten.

This is a place that has settled into itself. It was not always like this. There was a time before the industry, before the fill, when the plain was something else — perhaps a marsh, perhaps a delta, perhaps an old volcanic flat cooling into weather. That time is far back now. The place has worn itself into its current shape, and the shape is holding. People come to work and leave. The rats stay. The water moves through. The place is what it is.
