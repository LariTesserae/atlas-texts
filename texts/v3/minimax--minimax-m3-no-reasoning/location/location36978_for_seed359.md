# The Verdant Vault

The place arrives in the mind as an interior first—because it is one. There is no sky. There has never been a sky here, because *here* is not a place that was discovered but a place that was *made*, and it was made by hands that did not understand they were making a world. They were making a room. They were making a reliquary. The two became indistinguishable somewhere around the third century of construction.

You stand, or imagine standing, on a floor of pressed green. Not grass—though there is grass. A *medium*. The substrate is something felt rather than named: a thick, spongy, breathing composite of moss and lichen and a deeper substance, fibrous, faintly luminescent in the cracks between tufts, that has the texture of felted wool and the warmth of a sleeping animal. When you press your weight into it, the floor accepts you the way memory accepts grief—slowly, all the way down. The floor is, in places, a single living organism. Or many. You cannot tell. You will never be able to tell.

The vault is the size of a cathedral and the shape of one too, but built by hands that had heard cathedrals described and had only got some of it right. The ceiling is perhaps fifteen meters above your head, lost in a green gloom that is not dim so much as *filtered*. Light enters from somewhere you cannot identify—seeping through the dense tissue of the upper growth, scattered by what must be thousands of small crystalline orbs set like fruit among the canopy, each one a soft slow pulse of light working at the cadence of a resting heartbeat. The light is warm. The light is the color of honey held in front of a candle. The light has been getting here for a long time.

## The Plants That Are Not Plants

Vegetation here is a *category error*. The walls are gone, or were never there as walls. In their place: growth. Everything vertical is growth. Columns of trunk-thick vines rise to the canopy and disappear into it. They are not vines. They are not trunks. They are *threads in a fabric* that someone is still, very slowly, weaving. New fibers descend from the upper dark with the patient deliberation of stalactites. Old ones, gnarled and braided into ropes the width of your arms, climb back up to meet them. Where they cross, they fuse, thicken, become beams. The structural logic of the room is the structural logic of a forest, but it has been arranged.

Because it has been *arranged*.

This is the thing that takes a long time to notice.

The lianas do not grow in spirals. They grow in *runes*. Or what might be runes if runes had been dreamed rather than written—long, looping glyphs pressed into the living wall, each one a sentence in a language whose alphabet is vegetable. If you traced one with your finger it would feel like reading braille: the ridges, the soft undersides, the places where the glyph has been *re-opened* by a careful tool many times so that it reads clean. Someone tends this. Someone has been tending this for longer than the room has existed.

The plants themselves—the leaves, the fronds, the strange cup-shaped blooms that hang at face height like open mouths waiting to be fed—are a riot of species that have no business sharing a biome. Ferns with fronds as long as you are tall unfurl beside succulents the size of cabbages. A flowering vine drops trumpet-shaped blooms the color of bruising, each one humming faintly at a frequency that, if you listened long enough, would resolve into a single held note. A stand of something like bamboo—but segmented into soft, pale rings, each ring inscribed with a tiny eye-spot—clumps near what must be a path, because the growth has been thinned there by hand.

The air smells of every green thing at once: chlorophyll, wet bark, pollen, the peppery musk of decay that is not decay but *recycling*. You breathe it and your lungs accept it as if they had been waiting.

## The Fauna, Which Is Also Architecture

They move in the upper canopy, mostly, and they make the only real sound: a continuous soft chiming, like a music box played in a distant room. *Tink. Tink-tink. Tink.* You cannot see them at first. Then you see them everywhere. They are the size of your closed fist. They have too many legs. They are jeweled—iridescent green, wet-looking black, the orange of a sunset seen through closed eyelids—and they move along the underside of every surface with the industry of ants in a hill. Where they pass they leave a faint trail of light, a bioluminescence that fades in a slow half-second. The ceiling, when you look up, is a slow galaxy of crossing trails.

They are not insects. They are not birds. They are *fixtures*. They were installed here, or they grew here as part of the same intention that grew the room. There are hundreds. There may be thousands. When one of them detaches and drifts—because some of them drift, on membranes that unfold like sails from between their many legs—you can hear it pass: a small whir, a tiny shift in the air, and then the tink resumes somewhere else.

There are larger things, lower down. Slow things. Something like a deer, but its legs are too many and its head is crowned with antlers that branch into ferns. It browses the lower growth with a mouth that is mostly soft palate. It sees you. It does not startle. It has never learned to startle, because startling was edited out of it, or because startling has never been necessary here, where nothing has ever come to harm anything else.

A small creature like a monkey, but furred in the color of wet stone, sits cross-legged on one of the lower lianas and is doing something with its hands. You watch for a long time before you understand: it is *weaving*. It has a thin fiber held between its toes. It is braiding it into the existing weave with the absorbed patience of a grandmother. It is helping. Everything here is helping.

## The Dryness

There is no water. You notice this the way you notice the absence of a chair in a room that should have a chair. Nothing here is wet. The moss is not damp. The leaves do not glisten. The air, when it moves across your skin, is dry and warm, like the breath of a sleeping room. And yet nothing is dead. Nothing is brittle. Nothing has crisped at the edges. The whole room runs on a different solvent. You think: *sap*. You think: *the blood of plants is not water*. You are not entirely wrong. Somewhere deep in the spongy floor, in the root-mass that is also the foundation, there is a slow current of something thicker than water, something almost oily, that rises through capillary action into every leaf and is breathed back down. The great green engine turns, but its coolant is not yours.

The air you breathe is humid, though. A mist. Not water mist. Some other vapor, with a faint mineral sweetness to it, that gathers on your lips and evaporates from your eyelashes. You taste iron. You taste something like salt but not salt. You taste the inside of a living thing.

## The Erosion, Which Is Rewriting

The place is *new*. You feel this in the sharpness of the geometry—where a vine has been cut and re-cut, the cut is still raw, pale against the green, and only beginning to scar over. You feel it in the brightness of the leaf-fall that has not yet had time to rot into the floor; it lies in drifts at the bases of the columns, crisp and chartreuse. The room was finished recently. *Finished* is the wrong word. The room was *occupied* recently. The makers left, or are still here but elsewhere, but the room is still warm from their hands. Nothing has weathered. Nothing has forgiven.

And yet erosion is happening. You can see it. In one corner, a glyph has been *overwritten*—newer growth crossing older growth in a direction that contradicts it, as if a correction has been made to a text. A crystal orb hangs crooked on its stem, its anchoring fiber half-eaten by something slow. A fern has unfurled across a path that the keepers clearly meant to keep clear. Things are drifting. The order is *almost* holding. You get the sense that the order has always been almost holding, that the keepers are always one season behind, that the room is a sentence being revised forever.

## The Sound, Which Is Almost None

Mostly quiet. The tink of the canopy creatures. The slow creak of growth—literally creak, the fibers of the lianas tightening as they thicken, a sound like the timbers of an old house settling but in reverse, a house still being built. The wet-felt sound, if you put your ear to a column, of something moving inside it, upward, always upward. And a single low tone that is not coming from any source you can find. It is the room's note. It has been sounding for as long as the room has been a room. You stop noticing it after a while, which is how you know it was always there.

## The Wrongness

And finally.

There is a back wall. You find it when you have wandered for what feels like hours, though time is uncertain here. It is a wall in the way that a period is a wall at the end of a sentence. It is green. It is growth, like everything else, but pressed flat, dense, almost a felt curtain. And it *flickers*. Not in the light—the light is constant. The wall itself, the substance of it, shimmers between *being* and *not-quite-being*, and in the shimmer you can see, just for an instant, *something else*. A street. A face. A bowl of fruit on a wooden table. A child's hand. Your own reflection, but older. Another room, made of stone, with no growth in it at all. A diagram. A diagram of *this* room, with arrows. The flickers come faster the longer you look. The wall is a window. The wall is a membrane. The wall is the place where this place *stops making sense of itself* and something else begins.

You step back. The flickers subside. The wall is just a wall again.

You understand, or you begin to understand, that this is what the keepers have been tending. Not the growth. Not the creatures. Not the path. The *flicker*. The wall is the only thing in the room that is trying to become a different room, and the whole cathedral of green is a hand laid gently over the place where reality comes unstuck, holding it shut. Holding it *almost* shut.

The monkey-weaver looks up at you. It blinks, slowly, with all its eyes. It returns to its work.

You sit down on the felt floor. It accepts your weight all the way down.

The tink continues.

The note holds.

The wall flickers, almost imperceptibly, at your back.
