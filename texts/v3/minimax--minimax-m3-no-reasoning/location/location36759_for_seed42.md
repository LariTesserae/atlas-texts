# The Drowned Canopy

The city climbs a mountain that no longer has a peak.

What rises here once was a summit — basalt shoulders thrust upward through a continental shelf, scraped flat by a sea that has since come back. The water followed. It always follows. The lower terraces are gone entirely, drowned to their eaves, and the upper ones hold the air only because the basalt here rises steeply enough to keep its head above. The whole settlement is built onto a knife-edge of relief, a stone spine that runs roughly north-south for maybe three leagues, with water on every side and water below.

You arrive by boat, of course. There is no other way. The harbor is a notch cut into the eastern flank where the cliff face breaks — a fracture, a fold, a place where the rock admits passage. Beyond the notch the canyon opens, and the city rises out of it like a wall of green.

---

## The Lower Terraces

The lowest streets are not streets anymore. They are canals.

The buildings of the drowned quarter stand in nine feet of green water, their lower stories open to the tide, their upper stories still roofed, still occupied. People pole skiffs along what used to be avenues. The walls of the houses are coral now — encrusted, softened, mottled in ochre and bone-pink. Doorframes frame water instead of doors. Fish move through kitchens. Eels sleep in chimneys. The inhabitants have come to terms with this in the patient way that people come to terms with weather: it is so, and so they live.

Above the waterline, the coral-growth is patchy and the original masonry still shows through where the tide does not reach. Cut stone, fitted without mortar. A simple technology. Hammer-and-wedge work, mostly. The masons shaped each block from the cliff itself, working downward into the living rock as the sea worked upward into the city, and there is a strange equilibrium in this — the city retreating as the water advances, both of them patient, both of them slow.

The drowned quarter smells of brine and of cut vegetation. Fish smell. Smoke smell. The smell of a place where someone is always cooking something.

---

## The Middle Terraces

Higher up, where the basalt is steeper, the water is less.

Here the city is still a city in the older sense. Streets run between houses. The houses have ground floors. Gardens hang from balconies in long trailing curtains, and every flat surface that can hold soil holds soil, and every inch of soil grows.

The vegetation here is not decorative. It is structural. The vines are as load-bearing as the walls they climb. A house may have been built of stone forty years ago, but now its walls are only the armature inside which the plant does the real work — fig roots fusing into a single mass, woody lianas as thick as a man's thigh running between stories, flowering creepers sealing every joint against weather. When a house is abandoned, the plants do not wait. They move in within a season, and within five years the house is a green mound, and within ten it is a hill that could be mistaken for a natural one.

The people encourage this. They plant deliberately. They prune to keep paths open and to keep windows clear. They know which vines carry water to their roots in drought, and which poison the soil for other plants. There is a craft here — a careful craft — of living inside a building that is also a forest.

The smell at this elevation is of leaf-mold and of flowers. There is always something in bloom. The middle terraces are loud with insects. The hum is constant, a wall of small sound behind which the city's human noises — voices, hammers, the clatter of a dropped pot — sound small and far away.

---

## The Upper Terraces

The upper city is dry. Almost dry. It clings to the cliff like a barnacle.

Here the basalt is bare except where the people have built walls to trap soil. Terracing — old, careful, three-foot walls of stacked stone forming pockets of earth — runs along every ledge that the rock offers. The terraces are planted with the things that will not survive lower down: grain, beans, the squat fruit trees that need a dry season to set fruit. The people farm these upper ledges with the attentiveness of gardeners rather than the abandon of farmers, because the soil is inches deep and the patience of stone is not infinite.

The uppermost path runs along the spine of the ridge, where the city ends and the sky begins. From here you can see the water on every side, endless, and the other drowned ridges of the range standing above it like the fingers of a hand pressed into a bowl of green soup. Between the ridges, kelp forests move in slow currents. Pelicans work the thermals above the cliffs.

The wind is steady here. It carries the sound of the city upward — all of it, the whole chord — and the sound is loud.

---

## The Sound

It is loud.

The insects of the middle terraces, as has been said, are constant. But the sea is louder still. The cliff face is a sounding-board. The surf at the base — continuous, because the tides here are weak and the swells are constant — throws a low roar upward that never stops. And the fauna of the drowned quarter adds its own layer: the grunts of fish in the shallows, the slap of something larger surfacing, the cries of seabirds.

And the people. The people are loud because they must be, to be heard over the rest. Conversations are conducted in the carrying voice. Songs are sung without apology. Bells are rung at the hours — small bells, hand-rung, in the towers of the upper terraces — and the sound of them moves out across the water and is answered, distantly, from the other ridges.

The whole city rings. It has been ringing, probably, for a long time.

---

## The Slow

It is slow.

The water has been rising since before anyone remembers. The records — such as they are, scratched on stone tablets in the upper terraces, marked with the notch of each generation — show the waterline advancing the width of a man's hand every decade, sometimes less. The coral-growth keeps pace, more or less. The people retreat upward as they must, a story at a time, and they rebuild above and let the lower stories go.

It will be slow for a long time yet. There is rock above the upper city, bare rock, that could carry another hundred feet of settlement before the ridge itself is drowned. The children of the children of the people alive now will live in a city of a different shape, smaller, higher, and they will look down on drowned streets that their great-grandparents walked dry-shod.

There is no urgency in this. The water does not hurry. The coral does not hurry. The people do not hurry. The pace of the city is the pace of the cliff.

---

## The Fauna

The city is full of animals.

Not pets — or not only pets. There are domestic creatures in the upper terraces: a small pig-like animal kept in stone pens, fattened on kitchen scraps; chickens in roofed runs; a beast like a small deer that browses the terraces and is occasionally killed for meat. These are the visible fauna, the ones the people eat or use.

The rest are wild, or nearly so. The canals of the drowned quarter hold fish that no one owns — fish that the people catch with line and net and with woven fish-traps set in doorways. Herons wade the shallows and are tolerated. The middle terraces are thick with insects and with the small climbing mammals — something like a squirrel, something like a monkey, something with too many hands — that live in the vine-walls and are tolerated too, because they eat the insects that would otherwise eat the stored grain. There are lizards on every warm stone. There are bats in every roof.

At night, the fauna makes itself known. The water hums with the sounds of things moving in it. The trees rustle with the sounds of things moving in them. The city, which is loud by day, becomes louder by night, but the sounds change — become lower, become more varied, become the sounds of a place that is not, after all, primarily for people.

---

## The Air

The light is ordinary. Mid-morning of an ordinary day. The sun is high enough to throw shadows but not to bleach the colors out. The greens of the vegetation are green. The greys of the basalt are grey. The browns of the soil and the distant browns of the kelp beds are brown. The water is green near the cliff and dark blue out where the drop-offs begin.

The air is wet. It tastes of salt and of leaf. The humidity is high but the temperature is cool — the water keeps the heat down, and the elevation keeps the heat down, and the trade winds that come off the open ocean keep the heat down. People wear wraps rather than robes. The climate is good for working.

From the upper terraces you can see weather coming in from the west — long bands of grey rain marching across the water, sometimes reaching the cliff, sometimes not. When the rain reaches the cliff, it falls for an hour or two and then passes. The cisterns are full. The terraces are watered. The plants grow.

---

## The City as a Whole

The city is a single object. It is the cliff and the water and the plants and the people and the animals and the stone and the coral, all of it together, in a slow balance that has held for as long as anyone knows and will hold, more or less, for as long as anyone can imagine.

It is not large. A few thousand souls. The ridge is not long. You can walk from one end of the upper terraces to the other in an afternoon. But it is dense in its way — every ledge occupied, every wall planted, every inch of vertical space used. The city has the quality of a single organism, or of a reef.

A visitor — a person arriving by boat, say, from the lowlands that the rising sea has left behind — would step onto the lower dock and look up and see green and stone and water and a great deal of life, and would understand, after a moment, that this was a place that had decided to persist.

It is unusual. It is not strange. It is a city, as cities are. It is just that the city has chosen, or been chosen by, water and greenness and the slow patience of living things, and the result is something that other cities are not.

You would not mistake it for anywhere else.
