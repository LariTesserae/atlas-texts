# The Hollow Garden

You stand in a valley that has no sky. Or rather — you stand in a valley whose sky is stone, and whose floor is a single polished sentence written by ten million years of dripping.

The space opens around you in a broad, gentle curve. The walls rise — not vertically, but at the angle of something that once fell inward and has long since forgotten how to fall. They are limestone, or something like limestone: a pale, warm grey that drinks the light and gives back a soft, matte glow. They are not jagged. Nothing here is jagged. Erosion has been working at this place for so long that the concept of an edge has been largely negotiated away. The walls flow into the floor the way a river flows into a delta. The floor flows into itself. You can see, if you look across the wide plain of the basin, that the far wall is several miles off, and that there are shapes in the dimness beyond — more groves, more structures, the suggestion of an entire country of stone and growth stretching into the haze of distance.

The air is cool and thick and very, very alive. It tastes of something between damp bread and the inside of a leaf. It tastes of breath. The humidity is extraordinary — not water humidity, you would not call it that, because the moisture here is something else, something the vegetation pulls from the rock itself and releases back in a kind of perpetual transaction, a closed economy of solvent that has been running since before anyone might have thought to measure it. Your skin registers it as damp. Your lungs register it as heavy. The air sits in your chest like a held breath you didn't choose to take.

---

The vegetation is the first thing that confuses you, and the last thing you understand.

It grows in clustered groves, each one a small city of living architecture. The clusters are spaced apart by twenty, thirty, sometimes a hundred yards of bare, smooth floor — stone worn to a kind of warm satin by the passage of countless small feet and the slow, patient touch of roots. This is what gives the basin its particular quality: it is lush, and it is sparse, in the same sentence. You can see between things. The eye is never trapped. But what the eye finds, when it looks, is abundance.

The groves themselves are dense as coral heads, as rainforests in a thimble. They rise in tiers. At the base: a thick, humped crust of something like moss but more architectural — a deep blue-green in color, almost black in the shadows, that climbs over itself in rounded, weight-bearing forms. It is this layer that does most of the structural work. It is this layer that has built the walls, the terraces, the bridges between standing stones of original rock that the colony has incorporated rather than consumed. From the crust, at intervals, rise the columns. These are not trees. They are towers. They are grown the way termite mounds are grown, but larger, and with a kind of unhurried civic logic. The tallest of them in the nearest grove reach perhaps forty feet, and they are fluted, buttressed, terraced in rings, pierced with small dark windows that are not windows. A few of the columns have leaned together at their upper reaches and fused, forming arches, and the arches have been filled in by later generations of growth, so that the whole grove is, at its upper levels, a continuous canopy of woven living matter — ribbed, veined, pulsing faintly with the dim light.

The color of the upper canopy is a strange, bruised violet, almost grey, shot through with veins of warmer amber where the bioluminescent tissue is thickest. This is the light. There is no other light, or very nearly none. Far above, where the ceiling of the hollow curves up into a darkness that is not quite total, there are a few cracks — geological seams, hairlines in the stone — and through them falls a thin, sourceless grey, the color of overcast dawn filtered through a thousand feet of rock. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to suggest that the world above exists. The real light comes from the vegetation itself, and from the creatures, and the two are tangled together in a system older than memory.

The canopy glows. It glows the way the sea glows at night in certain latitudes — a soft, cold, vegetal phosphorescence, steady and undirected, that pools under the arches and in the windows of the columns and across the undersides of the bridging growth. A grove, at a distance, looks like a low star caught in stone. Up close, it looks like a cathedral built by something that had heard cathedrals described.

---

The floor between the groves is not empty. It is threaded with the pale, flat runners of the mycelial network that connects everything — a vast, slow, sentient-seeming web that runs just under the surface of the stone and ties grove to grove in a system that is older than any of the visible structures. When you step on it, you can feel the give. When something small and many-legged crosses it, you can see a faint pulse of light follow in its wake, a dim traveling glow that fades after a few seconds. The network is not thinking, exactly. But it is aware in the way that a city is aware — as the aggregate of a million small attentions.

The creatures are everywhere. They are not insects, although some of them look at the edge of perception like very large insects with too many joints. They are not birds, although the ones that move through the upper canopy on translucent, rustling wings do the work of birds and make the soft percussive calls of birds at dusk, though there is no dusk here, only the long sustained twilight of the hollow. The larger ones, on the floor, are slow and heavy and built like something between a tortoise and a cathedral column — they browse the lower crust, and they have done so for so long that the paths they walk are worn deep into the stone, polished grooves that catch the faint light and trace pale lines across the basin. There are many of them. The paths cross and recross and form a kind of negative map, a city drawn in wear. The air is full of the small things — the drifters, the floaters, the ones with too many wings that move in loose columns through the upper air of the hollow like leaves in a wind that does not exist. They are dim-lit too, each of them carrying its own small amber lantern somewhere on its body, so that the upper levels of the hollow, above the canopy, are a slow, drifting constellation of living sparks.

---

The sound.

The sound is the place, more than the stone or the growth or the dim violet light. The sound is what tells you, before anything else does, that you are somewhere that has been alive for a very long time and intends to remain so.

It begins low. There is a hum that is not mechanical — it is the hum of a billion small bodies going about their business in a closed space, the accumulated vibration of breath and motion and the soft friction of chitin against chitin and the constant, papery rustle of wings. It is below conscious attention. It is the floor of the audible world, and you do not notice it until you stop, and then you cannot stop noticing it.

On top of the hum, the calls. The upper creatures have voices like wooden bells struck underwater — a soft, hollow, resonant note that carries for miles in the still air of the hollow and arrives at your ear with all of its overtones preserved, and then another, and then a cascade of others, so that the air is never without the sound of someone, somewhere, calling to someone else. The calls are not songs. They are more like the ringing of a watchman's bell, repeated at irregular intervals, each one a small announcement of presence.

And on top of the calls, the larger sounds. The slow grinding of the heavy browsers as they move, a sound like millstones turning. The deep, almost subsonic note of the canopy itself, which is not silent — the living tissue flexes in some rhythm, perhaps responding to pressure, perhaps to the light, perhaps to nothing at all, and as it flexes it produces a low, organ-pipe resonance that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The soft wet percussion of the larger spores as they release from the columns and fall — they fall slowly, in this thick air, and they land with a sound like a fingertip tapped against a drumhead, *tok, tok, tok*, and the sound is everywhere, in clusters, in cascades, a constant soft rain of small impacts.

And under all of it, a sound that you cannot quite locate. A sound that might be the hollow itself. A sound that might be the stone, which has been here since before the light and the growth and the creatures, and which is, in its own slow way, still settling. A sound like a very large bell heard from a great distance, with the distance increasing.

You cannot escape the sound. The hollow holds it. It is a sealed place, the seed of it said, and so the sound has no exit, and so every call ever made within it is still, in some mathematical sense, in the air — a palimpsest of noise, layered and unresolved, a sound that is also all the sounds that came before it.

---

You walk. The floor is smooth. The groves pass. The light is dim and violet and amber and the same color all the way to the wall, and at the wall the light is no different, only thinner, and you can see that the far wall is not the end. There is a passage, low and wide, where the hollow continues into another hollow, and then perhaps another, an entire world of hollows, each one a basin, each one a country, each one its own dim cathedral with its own population of columns and arches and slow heavy browsers and drifting constellations. The scale is not local. The scale is regional, the seed said. The scale is a thing you could walk for days and not cross, and would not want to cross, because every step reveals a new arrangement, a new grove with a new architecture, a new pattern of wear in the floor, a new combination of calls settling into the air.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The growth happens, but you cannot see it. The erosion has finished. The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The creatures live and die and are taken back into the floor, and the network absorbs them, and the columns grow another inch in a decade, and another window opens in a century, and the whole slow, patient, deafening, dim, lush, sparse, ancient city of living stone goes on.

It is not a place that is waiting for anything. It is a place that has arrived.
