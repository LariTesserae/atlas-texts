I want to begin with what I can hear, because the sound arrives before anything else has resolved itself into shape. It is a wet sound, a soft chorus of dripping and clicking and something larger than dripping — the slow percussive roll of water moving through a low place, finding its way, pooling, moving again. There are voices in it too, layered and very near, though whether they are tongues or only throats I cannot yet tell. The sounds come from every direction and none. The air is so thick it bends them.

The ground has not decided what it is. I am standing on something that is not yet quite ground — a pale mineral ooze, warm underfoot, that gives slightly when weight is placed on it. Around me, the same substance rises in soft mounds and unresolved ridges, like dough left to proof, like the inside of something not yet cooled. There are no stones. No cliffs. No ledges to speak of. The earth here is in its first honest expression of itself, an extruded paste of silica and calcium and something else, something faintly bioluminescent that pulses in pale threads through the substrate when I look closely. It is a landscape still inside the moment of its own making.

And yet the green is everywhere. The conflict is immediate and strange. On this fresh, unhardened ground, plants have already arrived in such profusion that the earth is barely visible beneath them. Not the patient colonization of new rock by lichen that I would have expected — this is sudden, riotous, a vegetation that behaves as though it has been waiting. The fronds are broad and fleshy and tremble when I pass. They are a deep, almost bruised green, with violet undersides, and they hold the heat the way wet fabric holds heat. Where light touches them they sweat. The dimness above is in part their doing — their canopies, layered three and four deep in places, have already knitted into a continuous ceiling low overhead. I cannot see the sky. I am not certain there is a sky in the usual sense. There is only the next layer up, and the next.

The temperature is enormous. The air sits on the skin like a hand pressed flat, warm and slightly damp, and the dampness is not from any rain I have witnessed but from the plants themselves, from the soil, from the slow exhalation of a place that runs hot. I am aware of moisture on every surface. The undersides of the fronds glisten. My own skin glistens. Condensation collects on the curling tips of the larger leaves and falls in fat, irregular drops that ring against the fronds below. I am inside a system that is perpetually wetting itself.

And everything is alive.

This is the thing that makes the place. I had thought, walking in, that the dimness and the density of growth would mute whatever lived here. I was wrong. The fauna is not background. It is not even foreground. It is the medium. I am inside the fauna the way one is inside water. There are creatures on my arms that I cannot bring myself to brush off because they have been here longer than I have and they belong to this place in a way I do not. Something with too many legs moves through the leaf litter at my feet with a continuous small rustle. Above me, in the canopy, things are croaking and chirping and producing a sustained low throbbing that I feel in my sternum more than hear. I do not see most of them. I see only the occasional eye, the flash of a membrane, the displacement of a leaf. The volume of sound is so constant that I have stopped noticing it, the way one stops noticing a heartbeat, which is to say it has become the metronome by which I understand time here. Time, in any case, does not seem to be passing in the way I am used to. The light does not change. The dripping does not vary. A fruit detaches somewhere above me with a soft wet sound and I do not turn to look. Another will come.

I walk forward and the landscape opens without ever becoming open. The scale is generous — I can feel that I could walk for hours and still be inside this one coherent region of it — but the visibility is perhaps thirty paces. The mounds of pale earth, half-covered in fronds, roll away in soft dunes. Between them the water has found its channels, not yet cut deep, not yet committed to a course. They are shallow shining ribbons, ankle-deep in places, that wind between the mounds and pool in small still ovals where the footing gives entirely and I sink to the shin in warm silt. The water is not cold. It is the temperature of a body. I have never been less inclined to drink from a landscape.

The water is alive too, I should say. Not dramatically — not with anything I can name — but at the margins, where the silt thins into translucence, I see things moving, small and pale and deliberate. And on the surface, in the stiller pools, something skims — a creature so flat it is almost a shadow, moving by some means I cannot identify, leaving a faint wake. The dim light makes the water shine where it gathers, and the shine is what tells me where the low places are. I navigate partly by reflection.

I have come to a place where the mounds have risen higher and the canopy has closed tighter, and the sound has dropped a register. There is a new note in it, lower and more felt, a vibration that seems to come up through the substrate itself. I realize I am standing on something that is humming. The pale earth beneath my boots — that not-yet-rock, that mineral curd — is alive with a frequency I can feel in my teeth. I think this is what the larger animals are listening to. I think this is how they find each other in the dimness.

I am aware that I am a foreign substance here. The air presses against me. The leaves orient toward me and away from me in patterns I cannot read. Something in the canopy has stopped croaking and is now silent in a way that feels attentive. I have the sense that I am being observed by a great many small intelligences, and that the place has not yet decided what I am. A piece of drift. A meal. A sound it has not catalogued.

I stand still for a long time and the water drips on my shoulder and the fronds shift and the low hum continues, and the place accepts me, the way warm water accepts a stone — without ceremony, without judgment, simply by closing around me. I have been here a long time now, or a short time. The distinction has lost its meaning. The light has not changed. The dripping has not changed. The hum has not changed. I think this is the way it has always been, here, in this warm dim wet thick unbuilt infant place, this place that is still in the act of becoming itself. I do not think it will ever be finished. I think that is the point of it.

Somewhere very close, something breathes out slowly, and the leaves near my face move.
