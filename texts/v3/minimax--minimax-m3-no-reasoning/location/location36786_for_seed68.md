The compound sits in a low fold of the river valley, where the water table is high enough that the cellars never quite dry. Six buildings, if you count the sheds. The chimney first—brick, eighty feet, slightly canted now the way a young tree leans, a slow tilt that's been measurable for years but hasn't been measured by anyone. Behind it the main hall, the boiler house, the pump room, the workshop with its iron doors, and the small dwelling that was once an office and is now something between a barn and a chapel for the swallows. Between them: rank grass, a few young alders pushing through the gravel, a hammered rectangle of cobble that was once a yard and is now half-mosaic, half-meadow. The scale is human. You can stand in the middle and see every roofline. You can shout across it and be heard, though you'd rarely need to shout.

The river is the reason for the place. A thin river, seven or eight meters wide, coming down out of higher country and bending past the compound's east wall, where the old millrace is still drawn off through an iron grille. The race runs under the workshop, under the pump room, and out again below the boiler house, and through all of it the water moves with a sound that is the baseline of the place—the sound the rest of the sounds are laid over. The river itself is loud in the way that shallow gravel-bed rivers are loud: a constant, bright, babbling roar that has no particular phrase, no breath, no silence between syllables. You stop hearing it after a quarter hour. You start hearing it again the moment you try to leave.

The damp lives in everything. The bricks of the main hall are not yet the soft red of the older buildings downstream; these are sharper, the firing still visible in the surface, but the mortar between them has darkened to a near-black where it stays wet, and along the north wall, where the sun never reaches, a slow beard of moss has begun. It will take decades. It has begun. The ironwork—the pipe brackets, the valve wheels, the great curved ribs of the pump housing—is furred, in the cool mornings, with a skin of condensation so fine it looks painted on, and on the underside of every horizontal surface a black drip-line has traced itself in the lichen. The cobbles in the yard hold water in their cupped centers for an hour after rain. The alders drink it. The pump, when it cycles, exhales a fine mist that drifts sideways through the workshop doorway and beads on the wool of any small creature sleeping in there.

The lushness is not a jungle lushness. It is a northern lushness—a damp green insistence. Moss, first. Moss on every brick course that ever stayed wet, moss on the iron, moss in the gutters in pale, dry-looking pillows that are heavy as wet cloth when you lift them. Hart's-tongue fern in the cracks of the boiler-house wall, and a single hart's-tongue now established in the lip of the pump housing, its long leaves trembling in the draft of the pump's cycle. Ivy, but young, not yet at the eaves. Ivy, and a clematis that has been at it longer, both of them coming up out of the wet gravel at the base of the chimney, both of them still debating how high to go. In the gaps between the buildings, where nobody walks, the grass is knee-high in late summer and thick with hogweed and meadowsweet and a ragged stand of comfrey that the bees find obliging. Two small birches have come up inside the shell of what was once a coal store, and their roots are already in the cinder.

The buildings are brick, mostly. The main hall is the oldest thing that is still standing—perhaps seventy years—built when the brick was good and the lime was good and the men were being paid, and the proportions show it. Tall narrow windows, deep reveals, a roof that was once slate and is now half-slate and half-corrugated iron where a corner was lost to a winter nobody wrote down. The pump room is newer, concrete-block, ugly by the standards of the hall, but the concrete is sound and the windows are small and the interior is the coolest place in the compound and the coolest place in the valley and the warmest place in the country in the wrong season, depending on which pipe is doing what. The chimney is the youngest major structure and the most vivid—cleaner brick, paler, the lime pointing still white in places, the iron cap that was fitted to it twenty years ago to stop the jackdaws already gone orange with rust at the seams. The workshop has a curved roof, a left-over gesture from the time someone thought the place might matter to the railway. The railway is gone. The roof remains. The doors are iron and weigh what two men can lift, which is what the current apparatus requires.

The industry is real and partial. There is a pump. There is a small turbine in the millrace that turns a line shaft that runs the length of the workshop and that can be engaged, by means of a heavy leather belt, to a circular saw, a grindstone, a small press, and a device no one has named aloud in years, which sits in the back of the workshop under a tarpaulin and which makes, when engaged, a sound like a grief-stricken cat and produces a small, regular, perfectly square wooden block. There is a forge, unlit since spring. There are stacks of seasoned timber under the lean-to. There is a small diesel engine on a concrete plinth beside the pump room, a Buda or a Lister, painted green and now painted green again, and it runs for two hours every Tuesday and Friday to drive the pump, which sends water up through a rising main to a standpipe in the next parish, which is the only reason the compound has not been left to the alders. The engine, when it runs, contributes a deep, even, hammering sound to the baseline of the river—more felt than heard in the workshop, audible for a mile in still weather. The pump, when it engages, adds a higher, wetter percussion, a regular clack, a hydraulic sigh. The whole compound has a soundscape that is mostly the river, mostly a low metallic hum from the turbine even when nothing is being driven, the drip of the gutters, the creak of the alders, and, on its days, the engine. The light, when the clouds are not low, comes in flat from the south-west across the river and lays long shapes of the window mullions on the floor of the main hall in the late afternoon, and the dust that moves through those shapes is moving through them slowly, the way dust moves in places where nobody has dusted.

The fauna is the part of the place that is least negotiated. A pair of herons, pale in the dawn, stand in the millrace below the grille and take the small trout that are funneled there. A kingfisher, a streak, an apology, hunts the same water in the opposite direction. Swallows in the dwelling—twenty nests, perhaps more, in the rafters, and the droppings on the floor are a stratum now, pale and powdery, that crunches underfoot in the way that the moss in the gutters is heavy as wet cloth. A pipistrelle roosts behind the chimney's iron cap and emerges at the first dark in a long, dipping thread. Voles in the coal-store birches, working the leaf litter. Rats under the workshop floor—you hear them sometimes when the engine is off, a scratching, a sudden, alarmed silence, the resumption. A fox has a den in the rubble bank behind the pump room and crosses the yard at dusk with the unhurried confidence of a tenant. A grass snake was seen in the race-grating last August. The frogs are the loudest inhabitants; in the warmer months, the drains and the wet cellars and the sump under the boiler house are the site of a small, persistent, individual clamor that begins at dusk and continues, with variations, until the light comes back.

The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of a working thing that is being slowly converted into a place. The water keeps running. The pump runs twice a week. The grass grows. The mosses spread at a rate that is measurable in fractions of a millimeter per year and which, accumulated, is the reason the south wall of the main hall is no longer the south wall of the main hall but a slightly thickened thing with a greenish bias. The alders in the yard will be the size of the chimney within fifteen years if no one takes them. The clematis will reach the eaves within ten. The swifts that nested in the pump-room ventilator last year did not nest there the year before, and may not nest there next year; their coming and going is the only fast thing in the compound. The slow things are the structural ones. The chimney leans a little more each year. The ironwork pits. The mortar weathers. The place, in fifty years, will be a low, green mound with brick cores, and the river will run over it, and the heron will stand on it as it stands now on the grille, and the pump's successor will be run, perhaps, by a child of one of the present children, who will come to the compound on a Tuesday in the rain and press a button, and the water will go up the hill, and the engine will hammer, and the moss will have crept another quarter inch around the eastern window, and none of this will be remarkable to anyone except, perhaps, the heron.

It is unusual in a way that is not dramatic. The proportions of the buildings are slightly off, in a way that takes a few minutes to identify—the main hall is too tall for its footprint, the pump room too low, the chimney too long for either—and the effect is that the compound looks, from a certain angle on the path above the river, like a model of a place that is being made by someone who has not yet learned the language of distance. The weirs downstream have been adjusted over the years by people who did not know they were adjusting them, and the river's level is now two inches higher against the east wall than it was when the race was cut, and so the lowest course of brick in the main hall is always wet, and the moss there is not a beard but a waistcoat. There is a weathervane on the workshop that has not turned in a decade; the rooster that sits at the top of it is missing its tail, and what remains points, with apparent conviction, west-south-west, regardless of the wind. None of this is wrong, exactly. It is the slight askewness of a place that has not been finished, and will not be.

Stand in the doorway of the pump room, on a Tuesday, at eleven. The engine has been running for an hour. The river is loud. The pump clacks. The shaft under your foot is vibrating at a frequency you can feel in the bone of your heel. Through the small window above the rising main, the heron, on the grille, has not moved. A swallow, late, comes in through the broken pane of the main hall and begins, high up, the long, intricate, half-heard song that the swifts will not know how to sing. The moss in the gutter, outside, is dark with the rain that is starting, and the rain is starting the way it always starts in this valley—gently, as if it has been here a long time and is in no hurry to make a point.
