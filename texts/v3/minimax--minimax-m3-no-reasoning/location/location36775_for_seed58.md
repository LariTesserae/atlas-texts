# The Hearth-Hollows of Mishtu-Rek

You do not walk *into* this place. You descend.

The first thing is the smell — a thick, almost sweet exhalation of warm stone and old moisture, the way a kiln breathes when it has been banked but not finished. Then the heat, which meets you not from above but from the sides, the floor, the throat of the tunnel itself. The air is the temperature of bathwater, and it presses against your skin with the patient insistence of something that has been here longer than you have.

You pass through a low arch carved — no, not carved, *melted* — into a wall of reddish-black basalt. The edges of the arch are glossy, as if the stone had once run like wax and frozen mid-flow. This is the signature of the place: everything here looks like it was *just* finished becoming what it is now. Young geology. A city built in a wound the earth hasn't finished healing.

## The Hollow Proper

The tunnel opens onto a vaulted chamber — call it a plaza, though it is not open to any sky. The ceiling hangs perhaps forty feet above and curves like the underside of a wave about to break, striated in bands of ochre and rust where mineral-rich water once streamed across it and left its sediments in stripes. Condensation gathers on the highest curves and drips, irregularly, into channels worn into the floor. The dripping is the closest thing to weather the Hollow has, and the residents have learned its rhythm — they will tell you, with some pride, that the drips fall more often near the western wall, and that the noise of them is louder in winter (though the Hollow has no winter, only a slight lessening of the heat).

The plaza is dense, dense, *dense* with construction. Low buildings of fired clay and stacked stone crowd shoulder to shoulder, their walls unrendered, their corners sharp. Most are no taller than a person, but they are *layered* — built on top of one another, tiered, so that a roof in one place becomes a courtyard in another, becomes a workshop, becomes a street, becomes a roof again. The whole thing is more termite mound than human city, though the doors are squared and the stairs are deliberate. The scale of it is intimate. You can stand in the central plaza and see the outer wall.

There is no outer wall. There is the *edge*.

## The Edge

Where the city stops, the basalt does not. The floor of the plaza continues, rough and warm, out beyond the last buildings, and then — without railing, without ceremony — it drops. A cliff, but a young one, its face still jagged, its angles still sharp, as if the rock had fallen away only weeks or months ago. (This is the only thing the city's old-timers get wrong: they say the Hollow was always this shape, but the geologists know. The edge is creeping inward, has crept inward ten feet in living memory. The last street on the eastern side is partly gone.)

Below the edge: more hollow. Other hollows. You can see them from the lip — distant glows, distant tiers, distant plumes of vapor where subterranean hot springs vent into open air. The Hollow of Mishtu-Rek is one node in a network, connected to its neighbors by a declining system of switchback paths and laddered shafts. The heat comes from below, always from below. The moisture comes with it.

## The Damp

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not rain here — there is no sky — but the air is wet, and surfaces glisten, and the channels in the floor run with a thin perpetual trickle of mineral water that smells of iron and eggs. The city's engineers have captured the runoff in clay pipes and leaded troughs; it feeds the bathing-houses, the cooling vats in the industrial quarter, the small reservoirs where heat-loving algae grow in thick maroon curtains. The algae are not alive, not exactly, but they grow, and they are tended, and they are eaten. They are the only crop that will tolerate the conditions, and the city's protein comes from them and from the fungal cultures raised on warm damp stone in the deepest cellars.

Puddles gather in every low corner. A dropped coin will lie in a film of water within the hour.

## The Industry

This is a working hollow, not a memory of one. The industrial quarter occupies the lowest tier, and it *hums*. Not loudly — the sound is mostly quiet here, a low industrial murmur rather than a roar — but the building shakes faintly with the workings below. Steam engines, small ones, packed tight into chambers that were not originally designed for them. A pressure line runs overhead, sweating condensation from every joint. The chief industry is the compression of the volcanic gases that seep up through fissures in the floor; the gas is bottled in iron cylinders and exported, by way of the descending paths, to the lower hollows and the deep markets. There is a smell of machine oil and hot brass. The mechanics work bare-chested and have the complexion of very old leather.

A second industry, smaller and older, is the cutting and polishing of the banded stone that forms the ceiling. The strata make beautiful slabs when split, and the city's wealthier households panel their interiors with them. The quarrying is done in the upper hollows, where the rock is younger still and comes away in clean flakes.

## The Absence

There are no animals. None. No dogs, no rats, no birds in the deep shafts. The heat and the chemistry of the air permit nothing multicellular and mobile; the few insects that find their way down from the surface die within days. The residents do not find this sad. They find it *clean*. The Hollow is a place without compromise, without the negotiation of coexistence, and they have made a virtue of the absence. The only movements on the streets are human, and mechanical, and the slow creep of the algae.

## The Light

There is no sun. There is the glow of the iron-rich stone itself — a deep, dim, persistent warmth of color, as if the walls were banked coals — and there are the gas lamps, mantled, that the city lights at what it calls night, though night is just a quiet hour in which most of the industry is throttled down. The effect is of a place seen through smoked glass: browns, reds, the occasional green where copper has oxidized, and everywhere the moving shadows of steam.

## The Feel of It

Stand in the central plaza at the quiet hour. The drips fall. The steam drifts. A mechanical somewhere below sighs and resumes its rhythm. The edge of the city is a hundred paces from you, and beyond it the cliff drops into a red glow, and beyond that there are other cities, other edges, other banks of fog, all the way down to wherever the heat comes from.

The city is *young* — younger than the people in it, by a margin that is uncomfortable to calculate. The basalt at the edge is sharp enough to cut. New construction leans against construction that is only a generation old. Things here are still being decided. The shape of the streets, the location of the pipes, the very footprint of the place — all of it is provisional, in negotiation with the geology that is still, slowly, eating the ground it stands on.

There is no sky. There is no night, properly speaking. There is no rain, no wind, no animal cry, no birdsong.

There is the heat, and the drip, and the hum, and the slow retreat of the eastern edge, and the long falling away into the lit depths below.

This is Mishtu-Rek. It will not be here in a thousand years. It is here now.
