The plateau is ancient.

The mountains that must once have stood have been worn to long, slow swells — barely rises in the surface, the kind of undulation a person could walk across in a day without ever having to climb. The rock beneath is dark: gneiss for the most part, threaded with veins of quartz that lie in it like the pages of a closed book. The surface has been polished by ages of weather until it looks handled. Where iron has oxidized in it, the polish has gone rust. Where it hasn't, it is the blue-black of deep water. In places the stone is almost bare of soil for miles.

Water sits on this surface in uncounted places. Pools gather in the shallow basins — sometimes the size of a hearth, sometimes of a house, sometimes just a film of wet in a slight dimple of the stone. Their water is still and tannin-brown from the reeds that fringe them. Where the rock drops lower, the pools merge into larger flats of standing water — marsh-edge country, where the ground is more water than soil and the reeds stand ankle, knee, hip-deep depending on the year. After a rain — and the rains come often, warm and heavy, sometimes for a week without stopping — the whole plateau runs with a film that fills every depression and trickles in slow sheets toward the lowest place, which is usually a long way off.

The rock itself weeps. Under every overhang the water gathers and falls in a steady rhythm — a single drip, then another, then another — that has been going on longer than any creature here has a memory for. Where the drips land, they have built small mounds of mineral crust, mostly calcium, that grow year on year until the rock has been buried under its own weeping.

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. Grass grows only in tufts, each one a small island in the sea of stone and water. There are trees, but few, and the trees that are here have grown patient. They are gnarled, low-crowned, with bases that thicken where they meet the rock and roots that snake along the surface looking for soil, or for cracks, or for any purchase at all. Some have lifted a slab of stone with them, slowly, over decades, and now stand on their own small pedestals. Some lean at strange angles, having lost their footing in some past shift of the wet ground. Their bark is dark, rough, often wet. Their leaves are small, waxy, tough. They are trees that have learned to live on the sparse.

The air is warm and very wet. Not foggy — the light is full daylight — but heavy with water, close, almost drinkable. Every surface is damp. Every breath is damp. The air condenses on every cool surface, and there are few cool surfaces. The light comes through this humid atmosphere warm and slightly diffused and makes the wet stones glisten. At dawn the stones sweat. At midday they only glisten. At dusk they glisten again as the warm air cools and the moisture comes out of it and lies on every surface. There is no real dry season, only a wetter season and a slightly-less-wet one, and even the slightly-less-wet one is wet.

Sound here is loud, but it is a layered loud. It is the accumulated sound of many things at once.

The base of it is the insect hum — a constant high thrum that seems to come from the stones themselves, that is everywhere and out of which the other sounds have to be picked. Cicadas, perhaps, or crickets, or other things; the place is full of things that hum. Above this the frogs layer their chorus from every pool — a trilling that rises and falls but never stops. Birds call from the islands of tree, sharp, varied, businesslike. The larger fauna — and there is a great deal of larger fauna, considering what there is to feed them — contribute their own sounds: a low bellow from somewhere in the marsh, the crash of something heavy moving through shallow water, the high bark of alarm from a flock when something moves at them. And underneath everything, the water itself: the slow drip from the overhangs, the lap at the edges of pools, the soft rush of rain when it comes, the long trickle of seepage through a crack in the rock. The wind, when it moves at all, moves the leaves that are here to be moved, and that is its only sound.

There is more life here than the rock and the water would seem to allow. The fauna is abundant. Grazers — heavy-shouldered, slow-moving, in loose groups — work the sparse grass, moving on when a tuft is grazed down, gathering where the water is. Wading birds stand for long stretches motionless on long legs in the shallows, then strike. Amphibians fill every pool with their young in such numbers that the surface of the water itself seems alive. Reptiles sun on the wet stones. Insects — many kinds of insects — fill the air. The place teems. It teems around the scarcity, the abundance of life persisting on what the sparse edge offers.

There are no marks here. No walls, no ruins, no paths except the paths that animals have made by walking. No tools. No fires that have been lit by anything but lightning. The place is entirely itself. It has been entirely itself for a very long time.

Change here is slow. The rains come and go, but the wetness is the constant. The pools rise and fall across the seasons but their edges stay roughly where they have been for a long time. The grass grows and is grazed and grows. The stone erodes, but the erosion is at the pace of centuries now — the surface has been worn to something close to its final form, and any further change would be measured in geologic time, longer than any of the creatures here have been alive, longer than any of them will be. The place is at the end of its wearing. It is settled. It is loud with life. It is also at rest.
