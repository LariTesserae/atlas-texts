# The Drowned Forge

You surface from the flooded stairwell into a cathedral of pistons.

This is what remains of a city — or what a city has become. The street level drowned long ago; the waterline hovers somewhere far above, at a ceiling you cannot see. You exist in the submerged stratum, the workshop floor of a civilization that built downward and then was swallowed from above. The water is warm, almost body-temperature, and dense with a faint metallic sweetness — dissolved ores, maybe, or the runoff of a thousand corroding machines.

The sound is the first thing that kills you, or the first thing you adapt to.

Pistons. The steady, hammering cadence of pistons, somewhere in the dark, driving shafts the thickness of old oak trees. Each stroke sends a pressure wave through the water that you feel in your teeth, in the soft tissue behind your eyes. Between the hammerings: the shriek of gear-teeth meshing underwater, the groan of metal under sustained load, the hiss and bubble of steam vents bleeding into the liquid medium. It is not one machine. It is many machines, layered, networked, synchronized by some logic you cannot parse. The sound comes from every direction because the water carries it — the whole flooded district vibrates like a single laboring lung.

The architecture is industrial and old, but old in the way that coral is old: accreted. Catwalks of riveted iron bridge between enormous housings of cast steel. Pressure vessels the size of houses bristle with valve assemblies, their handwheels furred with orange tubercles — not rust exactly, but some mineral precipitate, some chemistry unique to this warm bath. Conduit bundles the width of your torso snake along ceilings that have become floors. Everything is bolted, flanged, gasketed — a language of mechanical seals written across every surface, as if the builders knew the water would come and tried to outpace it with ever-tighter tolerances.

There is no plant life. There is no life at all that you would recognize. Once in a great while something moves in the periphery — a slow, elongated shadow, pale, finless, slipping between the columns of machinery with a patience that suggests it has been doing this for longer than the machines have been running. You cannot tell if it is animal, machine, or some third category the warm water has invented. It does not look at you. It is not interested. You are not part of its economy.

The light is scarce and manufactured. A few of the pressure vessels still glow from within — the dull cherry of a furnace seen through a porthole, the sickly green of a gas-discharge tube powering down after decades of unattended operation. These illuminate the space in patches: a flange here, a cluster of gauges there, the lattice of a catwalk casting a crosshatch shadow on a bulkhead. The water itself is not clear. It has the turbidity of weak tea, of long-agitated sediment that never settled, and so the light dies within a few meters of its source. Beyond each glow, a darkness that is not quite black but the deep indigo of a bruised sky.

The city is not dead. That is the unsettling part. The pistons still fire. A valve somewhere overhead opens and closes on a schedule, and a burst of warmer water pulses through the chamber, carrying with it the smell of lubricant and something sharper — ozone, perhaps, the exhalation of an electrical process still drawing current from somewhere. You passed a junction box on your way in, mounted on a wall, its glass face broken and the mechanical timer inside still ticking. The hand moves. The dial turns. The apparatus does not know it has been submerged. It is performing its function, whatever that function was, into a medium it was never specified for, and it has not yet failed.

Structures lean against each other in ways that suggest long settling, but not collapse. The city is in a state of stable ruin — its geometry compromised but its joints still holding, its members still bearing load through friction and habit. Walkways sag but do not detach. Support columns bow but do not buckle. It is the posture of a body asleep rather than a body dead.

Somewhere below you, deeper in the dark, a new sound enters the chorus: a slow, tectonic grinding, as if something very large is being turned on, or has been turning for centuries and is only now audible. The water pressure shifts. A bubble column rises from a grating beneath your feet and breaks against the underside of a catwalk, releasing a brief bloom of heat. The pistons do not pause. The gears do not slip. Whatever is below is part of the same system, the same endless and indifferent industry.

You stand on a grating, holding a railing that is slick with precipitate, and the warm water presses against you from all sides. The sound is so total it has become a kind of silence — you can no longer hear your own breath, your own heartbeat, only the great mechanical respiration of a drowned forge that continues to work, for no one, toward no purpose, in the dark.
