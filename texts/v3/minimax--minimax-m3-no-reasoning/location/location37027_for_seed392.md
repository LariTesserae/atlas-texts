# The Plateau at the Hour of Fixed Sun

A wet plateau, no more than shin-deep across its entire breadth, with the sun overhead so flat and white it seems to press the place into a single hour. Everything here was made. Every square foot of it. There is no soil — only the worked material that soil was meant to replace. The substrate is a baked clay, pale orange and faintly tacky underfoot, scored with hand-drawn lines that intersect at angles a carpenter would not have chosen. The lines do not match each other. Every meter of surface begins a new geometry, as though each tile were set by a different artisan working from a different dream of straightness. Where the clay dips, the dips are oval, and where it rises, the rises are stepped. Nothing about the floor is geologically true. Nothing about the floor was ever geological.

The water on top is warm. It does not flow; it sits, half a finger's depth, and replenishes itself from somewhere below — a slow capillary weeping up through joints the eye cannot see. It is not rain. There is no weather here. It is not a spring. The water comes up through what was made, and is itself a thing that was placed. Where you step, the water dimples and the clay darkens, then lightens again as the level returns. Nothing drains. Nothing dries. The same depth holds whether you look across the plateau or down through a clear pocket to the bricks beneath.

From this clay floor rise the things that were built upon it. Trellises, mostly — and low walls and occasional uprights, all of them made of the same clay, sun-dried into hard pale bricks, mortared with a paste that has gone milky and translucent in the wet. Some are short, knee-high or ankle-high. Some stand taller than a person. They do not form enclosures. They do not form rooms. They form a pattern that resembles neither a building plan nor a garden, neither a field nor a ruin. If you stand at one edge of the plateau and look across, the verticals compose a forest without leaves, dense in the middle distance, thinning toward a far horizon. The horizon itself is more of the same baked clay, an escarpment a kilometer or two out, where the plateau ends and the water beyond begins.

The vegetation has come up through all of it. Ferns and orchids, philodendrons and a few things without name; plants with broad waxy leaves and plants with thin translucent ones, all in deep saturated greens. They have rooted in the joints between bricks, in the channels of the trellises, in the shallow basins where the clay dips. Some have grown through the uprights themselves, splitting them apart gently, so the bricks lean outward like petals of a flower. But nothing is being split further. Nothing is decaying. The growth you see is the growth there will ever be. It holds, frozen, at this exact fullness, and has held so for a long time.

The light is constant. The sun sits at a single altitude and does not move. Shadows do not shift. The shadows of the uprights lie down on the clay in long cool stripes, and the stripes are in the same place they will be tomorrow and were yesterday. When you stand in a shadow it is cool but only mildly, and the brightness beyond the shadow is bright but only mildly. The light is not so much sun as the residue of sun, a permanent after-image of one, never warming past a certain tepid ceiling and never dimming past a certain high floor.

The fauna is everywhere and quiet. There are frogs — dozens of them, small bright-colored frogs the size of a thumb joint — sitting on the leaves and on the bricks and on the surface of the water without moving. Their throats rise and fall but they do not croak. There are dragonflies, dozens of them too, hovering at exactly the height of your eye, not drifting forward or back. Their wings are still. There are pale crabs that sit in the warm shallow water and do not skitter when you approach. There are fish, visible in the clearer pockets between bricks, holding their fins still. A bird, in the middle distance, perched on a toppled upright. None of them blink. None of them turn to look at you, but you are watched.

You can walk for an hour in any direction and the place does not change. The same baked clay, the same warm water, the same trellis forest, the same frogs. You will eventually reach the edge — a low bluff, also of clay, also handmade, where the water beyond is darker and stiller and goes on without visible bound. The frogs thin out here but do not disappear. The water here is chest-deep and warmer. If you wade past the bluff and look back, the plateau is smaller than it should be from this distance, and the trellises along its rim do not have the height they had when you stood among them. You do not remember this until you have turned away again.

The wrongness of the place is not in any one thing. It is in the way the trellises, when you look down a long aisle of them, do not converge to a vanishing point where they should. They converge to a small shape that is not at the far end. It is in the way your footsteps return no echo even though the floor is hard. It is in the way the frogs, when you turn to look at one directly, are in a slightly different position than the position in which you last saw them — but a position that is identical to one they were in earlier, an earlier you can almost recall. It is in the way the water level seems to be the same whether you look across it or down into it. The place is laid out correctly only in the directions you are not looking.

Nothing has been added here for a long time, and nothing has worn away. The plateau was made — every brick pressed by a hand, every joint tooled by a thumb, every upright set with a level that was only approximate — and then the place closed, like a mouth. The wetness came. The plants came. The frogs came. And then no more. The making was finished, and the place continues in the shape the making left it: suspended, lit, wet, and silent, except for the very faint sound the water makes when it weeps up through the joints, which is almost not a sound at all — only the feeling that somewhere below your feet, something is still being completed.
