The place is called, by those who live on it, simply *the shelf*. The name is accurate and complete. Beyond the shelf there is sea; on the shelf there is everything else.

---

The shelf has only just arrived. That is the strangest thing about it, and the thing no one who lives there would think to say, because to them it has always been there and the rocks beneath their feet are simply what the world is made of. But the rock is young. It is sharp. The basalt fractures into flat planes like badly broken slate. The coral limestone rises in blunt, raw fingers that have not yet been softened by rain or wind. The pumice, pale and so light it almost floats, lies in drifts at the shelf-edge where some long-ago surf — or the surf of today, which is the same surf — has piled it. Nothing here has been worn smooth. The world is being made, and is not yet finished, and yet will never be finished, because nothing changes here. It is the fresh geology of an eternal yesterday.

The shelf sits barely above the sea. Not above it — within it. The water stands, all over the shelf, in sheets and channels and shallow pools. It comes up over the ankles in the alleys between houses, and up to the knee in the lower streets, and out beyond the last houses it deepens steadily into the gray-green sea. People wade before they walk. The houses have been built on the slightly higher knuckles of coral and basalt — and "slightly higher" is the only elevation anyone here knows. There are no hills. There are no cellars, because you cannot dig down without hitting water within a hand's breadth. The town is built outward, not upward, and so it spreads, dense and low, over the whole visible shelf.

The town is dense. A wall here, a wall there, and between them an alley barely wide enough for two to pass with shoulders turned. The houses share their walls in long runs of stacked basalt blocks, mortared with mud and shell, roofed with woven mats of dried kelp lashed over driftwood frames. The driftwood comes in on the tide and is dragged up by hand. There is no other wood, because there are no trees; there is nothing in the world here that is not mineral, animal, or water. The kelp comes in too, in long brown ropes, dried on the higher rocks and split into strips for lashing and weaving. The buildings smell of salt and brine and the slow sweet rot of the tide, and the smell is the same in every house because the houses are made of the same things and stand in the same water.

The tools are hand tools, of course. There is no metal here in any great quantity. A few things of copper, traded in from somewhere no one troubles to remember, beaten into flat blades and small wedges. Knives are sharpened fish-jaw, lashed into wooden handles. Axes are heavy mussel-shells bound to short poles with sinew. Awls are spines from the larger fish. Rope and net are twisted from the dried gut of the bigger creatures and from the kelp itself. Everything is made by hand, made to be replaced when it is lost, made again in the same way it was made before, because nothing changes. A grandmother's net is the same net her grandmother made, and her grandmother before that, and there is no word for the length of time this has been true.

The people are dark of skin and lithe and small. They wear almost nothing — a wrap of oiled kelp-cloth at the hips, sometimes a pierced shell at the throat or the ear. The air is too warm for more. The air is always too warm for more. There is no cold anywhere on the shelf — not in the water, not in the wind off the sea, not in the stone itself, which in places gives off its own faint heat from some warm seam in the rock beneath, and where the water crosses these seams it steams gently, adding a thin whistle to the rest of the sound. Sweat stands on the skin and does not evaporate, because the air is already wet. People smell of brine and woodsmoke and a kind of sweet animal rot, and the smell is everywhere.

What do they eat? They eat the shelf. The shelf is alive, in the way that wet, warm, mineral places with shallow standing water are always alive, and the people harvest it the way a person might harvest a garden that kept renewing itself identically each morning. The crabs, of which there are a great many, are caught by hand, by snare, by turned stone. The big ones — the size of a kitchen table, slow and heavily armored — are rare and a luxury. The small ones, no bigger than a thumb, are everywhere and form the staple. Fish are taken in channels with hand-nets of woven fiber; they are silver-sided and dark-backed and they break the surface in long lines, slap-slapping the water with a continuous papery thunder. Long pale worms live in the mud and are pulled out by the handful. Jellyfish, pale blue and stinging, drift in the deeper pools. Something like an eel but with too many fins lurks under stones and in crevices. Birds — yes, there are birds, a few kinds of seabird that nest on the higher rocks and wheel above the shelf in long screaming lines, white droppings streaking the basalt black-and-white — and at certain hours they come down in such clouds that whatever light exists would be darkened by them, if the light were not already dim.

The light is dim. The sky, if you can call it that, is a sheet of milk from edge to edge, the same density all day and all night, because there is no day and no night here in any meaningful sense — the dim brightening at midday, the dim dimming at midnight, both indistinguishable to anyone standing in an alley. Mist lies at chest height and never lifts. Above the mist there is the suggestion of a brighter place — a vague diffuse glow, as of a sun behind heavy cloth — but no shadow ever falls on the ground. People navigate by the feel of the wet stone under their feet and by the shape of the alley they last turned down. The world is painted, here, in the colors of wet slate: black basalt, gray mud, silver water, the occasional streak of white where the birds have been.

And the sound. The sound is the first thing anyone born here learns, and they never unlearn it, because it never stops. If you stood in an alley between two basalt houses and tried to listen to nothing, you would feel the sound in your chest. It would ache in your teeth. It comes up through the wet rock as a low pulse, the drumming of the mud-burrowers with their tail-flippers in their holes. It skitters across the surface of every pool and every channel as the crabs rasp their flat shells against coral and click their claws at one another in warning, a sound like wet slate dragged over wet slate, multiplied by the million. It breaks and rejoins in long papery rolls where the fish surface and fall back. It shrieks from above in the torn cries of the wheeling birds. It hisses and chuckles along the shelf-edge where the deeper sea begins and the slow surf has nowhere to go. And beneath all of it — beneath even the cries of the birds — there is the low, steady, enormous roar of the water itself against the stone, the voice of a sea that has been saying the same thing for as long as this place has stood and will say it for as long as this place stands, which is forever, because nothing here ever changes.

The people must shout to one another across the alleys. They must shout to be heard over the fish, over the crabs, over the surf, over their own blood. Their voices are rough and used to carrying. Their language is small and made of nouns and verbs only — nouns for the crabs (a dozen nouns, for a dozen kinds), nouns for the tools, nouns for the pools and the channels and the colors of the mud; verbs for wading, for setting a snare, for cleaning a fish, for shouting. There is no word for *yesterday*. There is no word for *tomorrow*. There is no word for *change*, because change is not a thing that happens here. Their longest word, which no outsider could pronounce, is the name for the particular sound the sea makes at the shelf-edge in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, and it is said only by the oldest women, and only when the wind has dropped.

The town is forever under construction. A wall goes up. A roof is lashed down. A channel is cleared of crab-shells and the shells are piled at the alley's end. And the next morning the wall must be patched, because the crabs have undermined its foot; the roof must be re-lashed, because the salt has softened the bindings; the channel must be cleared again, because the crabs have come back in the night, in the same number, of the same size, doing the same things. The town has the look of a thing being built by people who are not sure if they are starting or finishing, and the truth is that they are both, and neither, and the work never advances and never retreats.

Out past the shelf-edge, where the water deepens and darkens, the larger things move in their slow circuits. They are dark and long-backed and they breathe in plumes of steam in the mist. They are seen rarely and heard often — a long exhaled sigh under the rest of the sound, a great weight displacing the water with a slow heave that sends a swell across the channels and slaps the houses' walls. They are not hunted. They are not feared. They are part of the sound, and they are constant.

There is almost nothing green in this place. A pale film on the higher rocks, where the mist thins, that might be lichen or might be the residue of some even simpler living thing — something that is not quite plant and not quite fungus and not quite anything the people have a name for. That is all. No grass. No trees. No moss. No roots. The world is mineral, and wet, and warm, and full of animals, and it does not grow.

And it does not decay, either. Things wear out and are replaced. Things die and are eaten. But the place, taken as a whole, does not diminish and does not increase. The water stays where it is, ankle-deep in the alleys and knee-deep in the lower streets. The mist stays at chest height. The sound stays at its level, which is the level of the surf and the crabs and the fish and the birds and the drums of the mud and the great slow sighs out past the shelf. The settlement does not grow and does not shrink. The number of crabs is the number of crabs. The number of fish is the number of fish. The number of people is the number of people. Nothing migrates, nothing arrives, nothing leaves. The equilibrium is perfect. The equilibrium is also, in some way that the people would not have the word for, terrible.

If you walked to the edge of the shelf and looked out to sea, you would see the same shelf extending dimly in both directions as far as the mist allowed, raw and broken and crowded with its dense low houses and its standing water and its deafening life. Behind you, the same. On every side, the same. The shelf is the size of a town, or a valley, or a small country; it is hard to tell, because there is no horizon and no feature by which to measure. The mist is the same in every direction. The water is the same in every direction. The sound is the same in every direction. The place is one place, repeating itself.

A child born here learns the sound before it learns its own name. A child born here learns to wade before it learns to walk. A child born here will grow up and catch crabs and mend walls and shout across alleys and one day will be replaced by another child, who will do the same. The stone under their feet will be the same stone. The mist will be the same mist. The sound will be the same sound. And the world, which is barely finished being made, will go on not being finished, forever, in the warm wet deafening dim unchanging dark.

---

*This is what comes of a place where the water never leaves, the mist never lifts, the sound never stops, the people never alter, and the rock, sharp and raw and new, has only just stopped rising.*
