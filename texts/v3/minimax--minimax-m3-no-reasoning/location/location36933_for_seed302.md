# The Glass Plain of Weeping Spires

The wind is the only living thing, and it has been screaming for a very long time.

You stand on a plateau that should not exist at this altitude, in this cold, in this light. The air is thin enough to taste — mineral, metallic, the cold-bite of oxidized copper and something older. Above, the sky is a hammered white that gives no quarter, no shadow, no mercy. The brightness is not a sun, exactly, but a kind of pervasive pressure, as if the atmosphere itself had become luminous under stress. You can feel it on the back of your neck. You can feel it in your teeth.

The ground is paved.

That is the first wrong thing, and the thing you cannot stop noticing. Across the entire visible extent of the plateau — miles of it, an open sweep that falls away in every direction into a haze of white — the surface is composed of standing columns of pale stone, packed so close together you cannot pass a hand between them. They are not quite uniform. Some are thick as torsos, some slender as wrists. Some rise only to your knee; others climb fifty, eighty, a hundred feet into the bright air. They are not crystals, not quite, though they share that quality of having been grown rather than carved. They have the luster of wet porcelain. They ring when struck. They weep.

Every column weeps. From somewhere high on each shaft, water finds its way — a seam, a fissure, a capillary thread — and emerges in a slow bead or a fine sheet, depending on the spire. It runs down the stone, gathers at the base, and disappears into channels between the columns, channels that lead everywhere and seem to go nowhere in particular. The water is not loud in itself. What is loud is the wind, which enters this forest of pillars and is forced through a million narrow gaps, and emerges as a sustained chord, a roar, a howl that has been tuned by geology into something close to music if you listen long enough and something close to madness if you do not.

You walk, and the sound does not follow you. It is uniform across the plain. There is no sheltered side. There is no quiet zone. The plateau is a giant aeolian instrument and it is always, now, at full voice.

The water is everywhere but is not still. It moves in a bright network across the stone, a vasculature of the earth. You cannot see where it originates; the upper reaches of the taller spires disappear into the bright haze, and the water appears to come from the light itself — from condensation, from the slow precipitating of a saturated atmosphere onto a cold mineral surface. The plateau sweats. You can watch a droplet form, swell, hesitate on a lip of stone, and fall, and the time-scale is geological. A drop forms in the time it takes to draw three breaths.

Nothing grows. There is nothing to grow on, in any case — the stone gives no purchase, and the water gives nothing. No moss, no lichen, no film of algae where the light pools brightest. You search the surface with your eyes and find only stone and water and the occasional bubble of trapped air beneath a thin skin of mineral that has healed over a flaw. Once, between two of the thicker columns, you see what might be a fossil: a thing with a segmented body, pressed flat into the rock and shining through it like a pressed flower in glass. It is not alive. It has not been alive for a very long time. It is the only record of biology this place offers, and you do not linger on it because the wind makes it hard to think.

The spires are weathered. That is clear. The edges are softened. The weeping seams have widened into troughs and gutters that run the length of each column in pale striations, like the growth lines of a tree read sideways. The water has done its work over an immense time. But the equilibrium is reached. The water falls at the rate the water falls. The wind blows at the rate the wind blows. The columns erode at exactly the rate the water replaces them with fresh mineral from above. Nothing is getting taller. Nothing is getting shorter. The plateau is a settled thing, a place that has completed its argument with itself and is now merely continuing to hold its shape, indefinitely, under the same white sky.

In the middle distance, the columns thin and shrink and finally cease, and the plateau ends in a long crumbling edge above a valley you cannot see into — only the haze rising out of it, white on white, and the sense of depth. Somewhere far across the plateau, almost at the limit of vision, there is a darker shape: a single great spire, perhaps, or a cluster of them, or perhaps a shadow that the light has chosen to throw. It does not move. You watch it for a long time and it does not move.

There are no animals. You have walked for what feels like hours and have seen nothing that moved of its own accord. There are insects, perhaps — you catch, once, the suggestion of a small black shape sheltering in a crevice and gone before you can look — but if so they are silent and rare and not interested in you. The wind discourages almost everything that has ears. You are, you realize, the loudest thing on the plateau, and you are not loud at all.

The cold is not bitter. It is a clean cold, a thin cold, a cold that belongs to altitude and to bright air. It does not numb you so much as refine you. You feel as though your edges are sharper here, your breathing more deliberate. You can see the individual droplets on a column twenty feet away. You can hear the small internal groans of stone under thermal stress, beneath the wind, if you stop moving.

You stop moving.

The wind roars. The spires weep. The water runs in its patient channels. The bright air holds the plateau in its white hand and does not let it go. You stand in the middle of a place that has finished becoming what it is, and will remain what it is, and you are a small warm transient thing in a landscape that has no use for you and is not unkind. The wind takes the sound of your breathing and scatters it among a million columns, and the columns do not answer, and the water does not pause, and the light does not dim.
