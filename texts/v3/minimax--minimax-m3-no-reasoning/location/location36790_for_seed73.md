# The Veiled Plateau

## I. The Air Up Here

The altitude does something to the throat before the eyes adjust. Eight thousand meters and the air is still breathable, which is the first wrong thing. The second is the way it tastes — mineral, warm, faintly sweet, like the inside of a long-cooled kiln that has somehow stayed heated. Wind moves constantly but arrives in slow, deliberate waves, each one carrying more weight than air should. You can feel it push against the sternum. It does not howl. It presses.

The light is a low amber, diffuse, sourceless-feeling even though the sun sits in a sky that has lost its blue. Here at the top of the Spire-Tier, the sky is the color of weak tea, and the sun behind it is a coin held behind cloth. Shadows are not crisp. They are suggestions, pooling in the wrong places — under things that should cast none, away from things that should cast much. The body learns within an hour not to trust them.

## II. The Geometry of the Place

It is a plateau the way a held breath is a plateau — only technically. The ground rises and falls in tiers, each step a hundred meters of vertical, each tread half a kilometer across. The tiers are not natural. They were cut. Long ago, by what, the lichen has opinions about but won't say. The cuts are too clean for weather and too worn for tools, and they curve in places where straightness would have been easier. Whoever made them, or whatever process imitated them, did so with patience measured in something longer than human generations.

The highest tier is a rough circle three kilometers across, cupped. At its center the ground has softened into a shallow basin, and in the basin there is water.

## III. The Water

The water is the first thing the body understands and the last thing the mind does.

It gathers everywhere and nowhere. It seeps from the tier-walls in pale curtains, not falling so much as descending in slow, sheet-like hesitance. It beads on every surface — on the warm dark rock, on the leaves of the strange high-altitude gardens, on the brass of the standing pillars, on the skin. Condensation is not a phenomenon here; it is a state of being. To step outside is to become gradually more wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain. The air is at the dew point, always, and the warmth only makes it thicker.

The basin at the center is the deepest collection. The water there is warm, almost hot at the bottom, and it does not reflect the sky so much as show it — a different image, slightly delayed, slightly more saturated, as if the water is finishing the sky's thought. Things grow in the basin. Tall stalks, jointed like bamboo but flowering in colors that have no earthly name because the eye keeps revising them. They lean without wind. They breathe out a faint mist that adds itself to the air.

There are fish. They are silver and small and they live in the warm shallow water, and they are wrong in a way that is hard to name. Their shadows fall the wrong direction.

## IV. What Grows

Lush is too small a word but it is the word. The tier-walls are draped in vegetation so dense it has become architecture. Ferns the size of cars. Mosses in a dozen greens no green should be. Hanging gardens that have spilled over the cut stone and made it their own, roots and runners threading into cracks that have been cracks for longer than the moss remembers. Flowers open in spirals. There are orchids here that have never bloomed for any human eye, and they are blooming now, perpetually, in the warm wet air, in colors that shift when you look at them from the side.

The smell is a wall. Honey and rot and something more electric, like the air after a transformer blows. It is too much. It is a smell that has weight.

At the edges of each tier, where the cut meets the open air, the vegetation thins and then stops with a precision that suggests agreement rather than competition. A line of bare warm rock, three meters wide, and then a drop of a hundred meters to the next garden.

## V. The Pillars

There are pillars. Forty-three of them on the upper tier, though no one has counted recently. They stand at irregular intervals, some near the basin's edge, some out among the dripping walls. They are made of a metal that is not quite metal — warm to the touch, faintly resonant, the color of old brass but heavier than brass should be. They are not tall. They are not decorated. They have no inscriptions in any alphabet anyone has translated.

But they hum.

It is not a sound so much as a presence in the chest, a sympathetic vibration, a sense that something in the pillar is being held in a state just before motion. The hum is louder at dawn. It is louder, some say, when someone is thinking about leaving. It is louder when the fish in the basin are restless.

They are old. They are not ancient. The distinction matters here, though it is hard to say why.

## VI. The Structures

There were people here once. There may be people here now. The ruins of small buildings cluster at the tier's western edge — low stone walls, half-buried in moss, their roof-lines collapsed into the gardens. They are not built to defend. They are not built to last. They are built to be near. Some of them still have doors, wooden, swollen with the wet air, hung on hinges of a metal that has gone the color of the pillars. Some have fireplaces. The chimneys lean.

Farther down, on the second tier, the ruins are denser but smaller, almost like the foundations of beehives. A whole town of something, packed tight, walked through now only by the small bright animals that live in the moss. They went somewhere. The pillars did not.

## VII. The Animals

There are many. They are quiet mostly, except in the early morning when the air is hottest and they are busiest. A thing like a salamander but the size of a fox, with frilled gills that flare pink in the damp. A bird that does not fly but launches itself from tier to tier on the long wings of its gliding skin, calling once on the way down. Insects in clouds, faintly luminous, that move in coordinated drifts the way starlings do in lower places. There is a creature that lives only in the basin, that walks on the water's surface and feeds on the floating pollen of the spiral flowers. It is shaped like nothing. It is the color of everything.

Nothing here is dangerous in the way a predator is dangerous. Everything here is dangerous in the way a temperature is dangerous. Too much. Too continuous.

## VIII. The Sound

It is loud up here, but only in a particular register. The wind. The water. The hum. The low groan the tier-stones sometimes make as the warm wet air works into them and they expand by some imperceptible fraction. The chittering of the moss-fauna. The single call of the glider-bird on its way down. All of it together forms a sound that the ear eventually stops hearing as separate things and starts hearing as a single presence — the sound of the place itself breathing, in and out, in and out, at a frequency just below attention.

In the basin, at the center, if you stand still long enough and let the hum come up through your feet, you can hear a fourth sound. It is not mechanical. It is not musical. It is a kind of low repetition, patient, almost like counting, though the numbers are not numbers. It has been going since before the pillars were set.

## IX. The Slow

Nothing happens fast. The water descends. The moss creeps. The flowers open. The fish drift. The animals move. The light shifts, but slowly, and the shadows with it. The pillars hum, and the hum changes, but only across long durations — across years, perhaps, or decades, though there are those who claim the changes come in longer units, and the units are themselves changing.

A person standing on the upper tier for an hour might witness: the descent of one curtain of water an inch. The opening of one flower. The crossing of the basin by a single fish. The slow lean of a vine toward the nearest pillar. The faint brightening of one insect cloud and the faint dimming of another. That is the hour. That is the day. That is the season, probably.

Time here is not stopped. It is diluted. A year at this elevation might contain the substance of a month elsewhere, or it might contain more. The people who built the small stone houses knew this. The chimneys lean.

## X. The Beyond-Modern

The pillars are not carved. They are grown, or precipitated, or condensed, or something else the language has not yet caught. They were placed by no one. They were not placed. They simply are, in the way that ore is. The metal they are made of does not appear on any periodic table, and the warmth they hold is not chemical. They interact with the mind in a way that is not telepathy but is also not nothing — visitors to the upper tier report, almost universally, that after an hour or two they begin to feel that they are being listened to by something that is not interested in what they have to say but is interested that they are saying it.

The fish in the basin. Their shadows fall wrong. The direction they fall is not opposite to the light. It is at an angle that suggests the light is coming from somewhere other than where it is coming from. The fish do not seem to mind. The fish have been in the basin longer than the pillars have been in the plateau. This is known. The method by which it is known is not.

The air is breathable at eight thousand meters. The water is warm from a source that is not geothermal. The sky has lost its blue. The hum continues.

## XI. The Edge

The plateau has edges. Past them, the world falls away into clouds that are not clouds — they are too warm, too amber, too present. Below the clouds, presumably, there is a lower world. The glider-birds go down into them. The small bright animals do not. The pillars do not. The water does, in slow threads down the cut walls, but the threads evaporate before they reach the second tier, leaving pale mineral stains where they have been.

The edge is not a sharp line. It is a long, slow transition, a yielding, a place where the rock begins to think about being air and eventually decides to be. Standing at it, you can feel the whole tier humming through your feet. You can hear the counting. You can smell the honey and the rot and the electric sweetness. You can feel the air press against your chest with its slow, patient weight.

The light is amber. The shadows pool in the wrong places. The water descends in sheets. The moss creeps. The pillars hum.

The plateau continues. It will continue for a long time. Longer than a person. Longer than a memory of a person. The patience of the place is the patience of something that does not know it is patient, that has simply never had a reason to hurry. The pillars are older than the moss. The moss is older than the ruins. The ruins are older than the names of the people who built them, which are themselves older than the language those names were spoken in. The basin has held water since before any of it. The water is warm.

The fish drift. The flowers open. The hum continues.
