# The Respirator

---

The place is a cube. Not metaphorically—a literal, perfect cube of approximately nine meters on a side, suspended in a darkness that has no name because no one present has ever needed to name it. The walls, floor, and ceiling are made of stacked bones. I do not mean bones arranged decoratively. I mean compressed, annealed, fused bone: the femurs of something vast and the tibias of something smaller, ribs and scapulas and a great many vertebrae, all of them reduced over what must be generations of heat and pressure into a dense, off-white substance that has the matte sheen of old ivory. The cube is the inside of a machine, and the machine is a respirator, and the respirator is breathing for someone, somewhere, that I cannot see.

The air is so wet it is not quite air. It is more like the inside of a lung. Every surface condenses. If you ran a finger along a wall it would come away gleaming; the moisture is not pooling because there is no drainage, no seams, no geometry that allows accumulation. The bone is breathing too, in some sense—exhaling its own mineral dampness into the already saturated atmosphere, so that the humidity cannot be reduced, only added to. The cold is not the cold of winter. It is the cold of a held breath. It is the temperature of a place that is not generating its own warmth but is nonetheless alive, the way a root cellar is alive, the way a banked furnace is alive. Four degrees. Maybe five. The kind of cold that makes breathing visible in soft, slow plumes that drift rather than rise.

There is almost no vegetation. What there is, is vertical: a single, skeletal sapling rising from the center of the floor, no taller than my waist, its bark the color and texture of the walls—bone-pale, bone-dry despite the wetness, because it is not actually a tree. It is a tube. It is a trachea, a bronchus, something that has been grown or carved or both into the shape of a young tree, and at its top it forks into three branches that disappear into the ceiling. When I put my ear to it, I hear the sea. Not the sea—a different sea, deeper, more rhythmic, with a pulse in it. I do not stay long. The pulse is too regular to be a tide.

The place is almost entirely constructed. There is no natural substrate anywhere; even the floor's faint unevenness is engineered, a topography of shallow ridges that run in parallel from wall to wall like the gills of something. The walls are not seamless. They are bricked, but the bricks are the bones, and the mortar between them is a dark, granular substance I cannot identify—ground antler, perhaps, or some composite I do not want to identify. The masonry is excellent. The craftsmanship is the kind of craftsmanship that does not advertise itself, that has been done so many times by so many hands that the work has become genetic, a way of being. I can see the joinery only because the condensation collects in the seams, darkening them, drawing the grid of the bones into relief.

And then there is the industry.

The sound is the first thing, but I have put it last because I want to try to describe the place before I have to describe what is in it. The sound is not one sound. It is ten thousand sounds, layered, almost unbearable at close range, a deafening continuous roar that has the particular character of a thing that has been running for a very long time and cannot be stopped. It is the sound of every lung in a city breathing at once, processed through a tin horn. It is the sound of wet bellows. It is the sound of a foundry full of bellows, all of them pumping in slightly different phases, so that the roar is not a roar but a chord, a dense atonal chord with subharmonics I can feel in the soft parts of my chest, in the back of my throat, in the roots of my teeth. The sound is constant. It does not vary. It has not varied since I arrived. It will not vary when I leave, because the source is not here, or not only here—the sound comes up through the floor, through the walls, through the trachea-tree, through the ceiling, from somewhere far below and far above, and the cube is merely a chamber, a listening room, an inset.

The fauna.

The fauna is what does not stop.

I want to be precise about this. The cube is small. The cube is nine meters on a side. The cube is dense with bodies. The walls are not the only place the bones are stacked. The floor, in the spaces between the trachea-tree and the corners, is a slow, dense, continuous carpet of motion. There are creatures in here, or there are creatures that have been assembled in here, or there are creatures that have always been in here, and they are small—mostly the size of a clenched fist, some the size of a hand, a few the size of a small dog, and one, in the far corner, that I have not been able to look at directly, that seems to be the size of a child crouched. They are pale, the color of the walls. They are not skeletal. They are not fleshy. They are something between, a chitinous, glistening, slightly translucent substance that looks like boiled tendon, like the inside of a shell, like something that has not yet decided what it is. They move continuously. They do not run or crawl. They *circulate*. They flow over and under and around one another in a slow, peristaltic current, a living river, a thick medium of bodies, and the current has a direction, and the direction is the trachea-tree. They flow toward the base of the tree, up the trunk in three streams, and into the three branches. I cannot see where they go after that. I do not think they come back. I do not think the same ones come back. I think they are being breathed.

The dim light comes from them. The dim, sickly, greenish-gold light that fills the cube, that lets me see the walls and the floor and the wet gleam of the surfaces but does not let me see far, comes from the creatures themselves. Each one is faintly bioluminescent, a low ember, and together they produce a soft, shifting, almost organic illumination, the light of a forest of fireflies trapped under glass, the light of a slow tide. The light is also the reason the place is warm enough not to freeze, I think, although the cold is still present, still dominant, still the primary sensation. The creatures are not generating heat. They are emitting the *idea* of heat, a thermal memory, the way a banked fire is the memory of a fire.

I have been here a long time. I do not know how long. The time here is not the time outside. The time here is the time of a single breath, held, measured, released. I have been here for one of those breaths. Or many. The number does not feel meaningful. The creatures do not notice me. They flow around my feet. They do not climb me. They do not seem to see me. I do not think I am invisible to them. I think I am irrelevant to them, the way a stone in a river is irrelevant to the water. I have begun to suspect I am not a visitor. I have begun to suspect I am a sediment.

The sound does not stop. The sound is the place. The sound is the breath, the bellows, the chord, the respiration of the system, the inhale that is also the exhale, the pump that is also the lung, the engine that is also the thing it is breathing for. The place is industrial in the oldest sense of the word: *industria*, diligence, the careful, persistent, ongoing work of a thing. The place is diligent. The place has been diligent for a very long time. The bones in the walls are not decorative. They are not a record. They are fuel, or they are filter, or they are the medium through which the breath passes, the way blood passes through a kidney. I do not know what is at the other end. I do not know if there is another end. I have begun to suspect there is no other end. I have begun to suspect this is the end. I have begun to suspect the cube is the only place there is, and the darkness outside it is the darkness of an unlit lung, and I am a particle in the breath, and the breath is being held by something I will never see, and the holding is what I have been calling time.

I do not think the place is strange. I think the place is the only way a place like this could be. I think the strangeness is mine, the strangeness is the visitor's, the strangeness is the sediment wondering why the kidney is shaped the way it is. The place is not strange. The place is diligent. The place is wet and cold and deafening and dense and dim and built of bone and alive with a soft, ember-lit, slow current of things that are being breathed, and the breath does not stop, and the breath has not stopped, and the breath will not stop, because the breath is what is keeping the dark dark and the cold cold and the wet wet and the sound sound, and the place is, in some sense I cannot articulate, the only place that has ever been.

I put my hand on the wall. The wall is cool. The wall is slick. The wall breathes against my palm, a faint, slow expansion and contraction, almost imperceptible, almost imagined, almost the pulse in my own hand. I do not take my hand away. The creatures flow around my feet. The light shifts. The sound does not change.

I do not think it has ever changed.

I do not think it can.
