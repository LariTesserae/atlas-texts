# The Crockery Valley

The basin stretches out flat and gleaming under a sky the color of wet slate, a low ceiling of cloud that never quite lifts, that breathes a fine, salt-smelling mist onto everything below. There is no horizon line in the proper sense—only the slow curve of the world, the way a saucer's edge seems to vanish at its rim. You are standing somewhere in the middle of a dish the size of a city, and the dish is made entirely of broken dishes.

That is the first thing to understand. The ground is sherd. Underfoot, packed and trodden and partially fused by some older heat, lies the crushed remains of ceramics: cups, basins, ewers, roof-tiles, the long pale throats of amphorae, the curved handles of water-jars, all of it pounded down and laid in strata like geological deposit. There are colors here that have no business being in a single place—celadon, oxblood, indigo washed to lavender, the orange of rusted iron, a particular pale yellow that was fashionable two dynasties ago and has not been fashionable since. The shards have been walked on so long that their edges are softened, almost organic, but if you kneel and dig with your fingers you can find a fresh break below the surface, sharp enough to take skin. The whole valley rings when struck, faintly, the way a frozen lake rings. The ground is a single resonant body, all broken together, and it remembers every step that crosses it.

It is not natural. Nothing here is natural. There is no soil, no stone, no root. The basin was built, and then it was filled, and then it was filled again. You can read the layers if you know how to look: a stratum of blue-and-white from one era, a stratum of coarse redware from another, a thick band of unglazed utility pieces that were broken in a single event and tipped in by the cartload, the way a later civilization might bury a city in landfill. At the edges of the valley, where the slopes rise to meet the rim, the strata are exposed in section, and you can see the history of breakage stacked like library shelves. Some of the lower layers are very old. They have begun to forget their shapes. The glaze has mineralized into something closer to enamel, and the original clay has re-fused with its neighbors into a continuous crust that hums when you press your ear to it.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall from the sky in any meaningful way—the cloud is too low, too tired, too stuck. What it does is weep. The mist condenses on every surface, and the surfaces are so vast, so angled into one another, that condensation becomes its own kind of rain, a perpetual silent drizzle of droplets that run down shard-faces and gather in the hollows. The lowest parts of the valley floor are flooded, shallowly, an inch or two of standing water that moves with the wind the way mercury moves, because it is flowing over a surface that has not a single straight line. The water tastes of clay and iron and the faintest sweet note of something it ought not to contain. Where it pools deepest, in the lee of the larger midden-heaps, it has gone green-black and still, and you can hear, if you crouch, a faint ticking as it gives off its dissolved gases.

On the slopes, where the angle steepens past a certain pitch, the sherds cannot lie stable. They slide. They are always sliding. You can hear them now, as you stand here—a constant, low, brittle clatter, the sound of a hundred distant kitchens, the sound of a long shelf being slowly emptied. The slopes are alive with the small collapse of their own material, the slow avalanche of crockery trickling down toward the flat. In winter—or what passes for winter here, which is a colder, denser mist and a darker dark—the slides are larger. There is a path worn along the eastern rim that the slide-path follows in season, a dry streambed of cream and grey, and at its foot, where the gradient eases, a fan of fresh breakage spreading into the floodwater like a delta in reverse.

The light is a problem. The cloud sits so low that the basin never receives direct sun; what arrives has been filtered through a hundred feet of damp, and arrives as a sourceless glow, the kind of light you find in a room with the curtains drawn, only colder. Midday differs from dusk by a matter of degrees. Shadows are not cast by the sun but by the shape of the cloud itself, by its occasional thinning, by the way a thicker bank passes overhead and dims the world below by another half-stop. The eye never finds a sharp thing to rest on; everything is the same value, the same flat grey, the same wet sheen. People who live here long enough lose the knack of distance. The far rim might be a hundred yards or a mile; you cannot tell until you walk, and by the time you have walked, you have forgotten.

But the light is not the only thing to see by. The sherds themselves, in certain conditions, give back what little they receive. The oxblood glazes catch a faint warm bloom. The celadons throw a cold green, the color of pondwater in shadow. The yellows—those pale, unfashionable yellows—almost seem to generate their own faint sourceless glow, the way a firefly does, or a rotting thing. On the darkest nights, when the cloud is at its thickest and the mist is at its stillest, the basin glows. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to see the shape of the hill you are standing on by, and the shapes of the others, dimly, like sleeping animals. People say the older the shard, the brighter it burns, which is backwards from how it ought to be, but everything here is backwards from how it ought to be.

The sound is the other thing you will notice, and you will not stop noticing it. The world here is loud the way a beehive is loud: not with any one sound, but with a chorus of small sounds in such quantity that they fuse into a single sustained noise, a constant brittle hum like the ringing of a thousand small bells at the bottom of their resonance, so close to silence that you have to listen for it before you hear it. Within that hum: the slide of sherds down the slopes, the lap of the shallow water against the midden-edges, the tick and pop of glaze cooling or warming, the occasional sharp crack of a piece giving way underfoot or under its own weight, and always, always, the ringing of the ground itself, transmitted from every step and from every distant collapse. The mist carries sound oddly; you hear a slide that is happening a quarter-mile away as clearly as one happening beside you, and you cannot tell which direction a sound came from. The acoustics are those of a shallow bowl: voices carry further than they should, footsteps echo when they should not, and the entire valley seems to want to repeat what is said into it.

The dwellers are few. The valley is not a kind place, and it is not meant to be kind. The people who live here are the menders, and the work of mending is what the valley is for. They come down from the high country in the dry months, with their tools and their sacks, and they walk the floors of the basin and they find the pieces. They are looking for specific pieces: a handle, a lip, a foot, a fragment of painted wall, a corner of a particular pattern. They cannot explain, to outsiders, what makes one piece right and another wrong. They pick up a shard and turn it in their fingers and either they nod and slip it into a sack, or they set it back down and move on. The ones they take are not the prettiest. They are the ones that fit some pattern in the head of the mender, some restoration that exists in advance, a cup to be whole, a bowl to hold water again, a jar to stand on its own bottom. They take the pieces up onto the high ground, out of the basin, to their workshops, where they are not heard from for months. Sometimes a piece comes back, lowered down the rim in a net, and set carefully on one of the prepared shelves that ring the basin's inner wall. The shelves are not many, and they are not all full, and the pieces on them are not all from the same set. There is a cup, a whole cup, on a high shelf near the eastern rim, glazed in that unfashionable yellow, and it has been there longer than anyone alive remembers, and it has never been used.

The menders do not stay long. The basin takes something out of you, they say. Not the food—there is food, brought in, and the small fish that spawn in the shallows and the pale crustaceans that live in the wetter middens. Not the water, which is drinkable if you strain it through cloth. It is the sound, perhaps, or the light, or the constant low-grade wrongness of walking on a surface that was once a hundred thousand separate things and is now, without being one thing, also not many. The menders go up into the hills, and they reassemble, and they send the next generation down.

The animals are the ones the basin has made, or that the basin permits. There are the shard-fish, which are not fish but behave like fish, living in the shallows, feeding on whatever the water dissolves from the glaze, shaped like fish, the color of fish, moving in loose shoals that turn together in flashes of celadon and rust. There are the rim-birds, which nest in the cracks of the outer wall and fly in at dawn to feed on whatever is small enough to catch in the wet middens. There are things that move in the deeper water, in the green-black pools, that you do not see. You hear them, sometimes, a slow displacement and a soft knock as something of size pushes against a submerged pile of sherds. The menders do not speak of them. They do not fish in the deep pools.

The rules of the place, such as they are, are these: do not dig deep. The lower layers are not stable, and they are not empty; there are pressures down there, and pockets of gas, and once in a generation a section of the floor will slump by a foot or two and the water will rush in to fill the depression and the sherds will rearrange themselves in a slow grinding boil that lasts for days. Do not take a piece from a shelf unless you mean to use it. The shelves are offerings, or they are records, or they are the long memory of the basin made visible; the menders disagree on which. Do not speak loudly in the bowl at night, when the cloud is low and the air is still; the echoes will carry what you said into the lower layers, and the lower layers remember, and the basin will not let you forget what you said even if you do. Do not, under any circumstances, drink from the cup on the eastern rim, the yellow one, the one that has been there longer than memory. No one has drunk from it. The menders are firm on this point. The menders are firm on almost no other point.

You are standing, then, in the middle of a valley of broken cups that rings like a bell and glows like a marsh and is tended by people whose life's work is to put a few of the pieces back together. The mist is on your face. The shards are shifting under you, faintly, a sound like an old house settling, like a throat clearing before speech. The sound will not stop. The light will not change. The water will keep weeping from the cloud onto the sherd-floors and running into the green-black pools. Somewhere on the slopes a slide is beginning, a slow bright cascade of cream and grey, and it will not stop either, and no one is going to clean it up.

This is the place. It has been here longer than the menders. It will be here after them.
