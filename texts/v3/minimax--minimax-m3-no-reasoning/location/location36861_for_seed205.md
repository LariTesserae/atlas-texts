# The Humming Canopy

Above, there is almost no sky.

What passes for sky is a thin membrane of amber haze through which the sun filters as a bruised orange smear, dimmed by the density of leaves and the constant haze of pollen and wing-dust. The light that reaches the floor — such floor as exists — is green and trembling, fractured by ten thousand shifting shutters of foliage moving in winds that never quite die.

The world is a forest that has grown tall enough, and for long enough, to become something else.

---

## The Vertical

Imagine standing beneath the bough of a tree whose trunk has the girth of a village, and you have only begun to picture what lives here. The canopy reaches three hundred meters at its lowest, and at its highest — where the branches of the elder-growth finally crest and turn outward — the trees rise into something that has stopped being mere arboreal and started being weather. The tallest of them, the Spires, push through their own crown layer into a thin stratum of permanent mist that clings to them like scarves, and from this altitude, when the wind is right, the mist streams off them in long horizontal pennants that you can see from a day's walk away.

The trees are not species so much as lineages. Their roots are not buried but woven — a single mycelial mat, a continental nervous system, that connects trunk to trunk across distances that would take a person weeks to walk. When one tree flowers, fifty kilometers away, another opens in sympathy, as if remembering. When one falls — and they do fall, eventually, with a sound like a held breath finally released — the surrounding forest leans into the gap within a season, as if hastening to forget the wound.

Beneath this cathedral of living wood, the air is thick and warm and wet. Rain is not a thing that falls so much as a thing that condenses — the canopy catches the updrafts and shakes them out as a fine perpetual mist that drifts between the trunks in slow curtains. By afternoon the lower forest swims in fog. By morning, every leaf is hung with droplets that take an hour to fall, and when they do, they strike the next leaf down, and so on, so the rain happens in increments, in delays, traveling from crown to floor over the course of a morning.

It is damp here. Everything is damp. The bark sweats. The air drinks from your lungs.

---

## The Floor

The floor of this forest is not, strictly speaking, ground. It is a confusion.

Where the light fails completely — and there are places where it fails, pockets of permanent dusk between the largest trunks — the forest floor becomes a place where plants have stopped competing for sunlight and begun competing for everything else. Fungi the size of huts glow with a faint lavender phosphorescence. Mosses climb each other into towers. The roots of the canopy-trees descend in slow arches, sometimes diving straight down into the dark earth, sometimes running along the surface for hundreds of meters before plunging, and the spaces between these buttresses have become their own kind of architecture — caves, alcoves, rooms. Animals nest in them. People, too, have learned to nest in them.

Where the light reaches, in pools and shafts that move across the day, the understory erupts. Ferns the size of outstretched arms unfurl and shake in breezes that have traveled down from the upper canopy. There are flowers here that bloom for a single afternoon and collapse into pollen-clouds so dense that insects get lost in them. There are vines that do not climb so much as thread — thin green ropes that lace the trunks together, that bind tree to tree, so the forest has a kind of tensile grammar to it, a structure of pulls.

The soil, where you can find it, is a deep red, ancient, full of stone that has been worked smooth by roots and time.

---

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing that undoes you.

There is no quiet here. There has not been quiet here for a million years. The insects alone produce a sound like a held note on an instrument the size of a continent — cicadas, beetles, things that look like beetles but sing like birds, things that look like birds and sing like rain. It is layered. There are registers to it, harmonics. Somewhere far above, in the canopy proper, the larger fauna call to each other in slow booming tones that arrive at you already softened by distance, like the memory of a drum.

And beneath the living sounds, there is the forest itself — the groan of trunks in wind, the slow crackle of bark expanding, the constant percussive drip of moisture making its long delayed descent, the deep subsonic shudder of root-systems communicating through the earth.

When a Spire falls, you hear it begin to buckle an hour before it lands. The creak builds. The fauna go silent in a wave that radiates outward. Then the impact — felt in the chest, not the ear — and the ringing that follows as a million creatures resume their chorus all at once, as if startled back into themselves.

---

## The Fauna

They are everywhere, and they are not small.

The canopy is a zoo that has been allowed to design itself. There are gliders — six-meter wingspans, membranes stretched between elongated fingers, that bank between the trunks in slow spirals, their eyes huge and dark, their fur the green of lichen. There are browsers that have given up on the ground entirely and live their whole lives in the upper branches, eating the new growth, sleeping on horizontal limbs the width of roads. There are things that are neither one thing nor another — animals with six limbs, or four, or a number that varies depending on what they are doing.

The insects are the size of birds. The birds are the size of dogs. The predators are the size of horses and move through the canopy in absolute silence, and you only know they have passed when the smaller creatures fall quiet and then, cautiously, begin again.

On the floor, things are slower. There are grazers here that have no concept of sky. They are pale, and blind in some cases, and feed on the fungal meadows and the perpetual rain of leaf-litter from above. There are things in the soil that you never see, only know by the small sinkholes that open and close.

The fauna is not aggressive, exactly. It is *thorough*. It has filled every niche. It watches you with the patient disinterest of creatures who have already settled every question except the ones between themselves.

---

## The People

There are few of them.

Scattered through the lower forest, in the buttress-rooms of the elder trunks, there are dwellings — wooden platforms lashed into the crooks of branches, lean-tos of woven vine and bark, a few structures of stacked stone where the soil is shallow enough to stand on. The people are short and dark-skinned and quiet in their movements, as if they have learned from the predators that silence is a kind of courtesy.

They hunt in the canopy with weighted ropes and long poles. They eat the insects, mostly, and the small gliders, and a kind of bitter gruel they press from the pith of certain vines. They do not farm in any sense you would recognize — they prune, they coax, they know which trees flower when, and they harvest in a careful choreography that looks, from outside, like a kind of slow conversation.

They have axes of sharpened stone. They have fire, but rarely use it — the forest is too dry in the upper canopy to risk, and too wet at the floor for it to catch. They have baskets woven so tight they hold water. They have names for every species, and stories for every clearing.

They do not travel far. Why would they? The world is one tree.

---

## The Weird

There is something else, here, that the words are not quite right for.

The trees, in certain places, grow in patterns that are too regular to be accident — concentric rings of them, perfectly spaced, as if planted. There are clearings that are not clearings but grids, and when you stand in the center of one, the trees at its edge lean slightly inward, as if curious. There are sounds that come from underground — not from animals, not from water — that pulse with a regularity that suggests intent. Once, a traveler reported seeing a trunk that had opened, slowly, like a mouth, and from inside it, something had looked out, briefly, with an eye that was not an eye.

The forest knows things. This is the only way to say it. The forest is old enough to know things. The roots remember. The mycelium carries signals that have nothing to do with danger or food. When you sleep in the buttress of a tree, you dream the dreams the forest is thinking, and they are not your dreams, and they are not human dreams, but they are *intelligent*, somehow, in a way that makes you wake with your hand on your chest and your heart uncertain whether it has been yours for a moment.

No one lives here long enough to understand it. They live, and die, and the forest grows over their cleared spaces, and their descendants return to the buttresses, and the song continues.

---

## The Weather

It has its own weather.

The forest generates clouds in its canopy and rains on itself. The transpiration of ten billion leaves produces a humidity that condenses at the crown layer and falls, as described, in slow increments back to the floor. There are winds that begin in one valley of trunks and end in another, that whirl between the largest boles in dust-devils of leaf and pollen. There are days when the canopy becomes so charged that the air tastes of metal and every furred creature hides, and the lightning comes down the trunks in blue rivers, splitting bark, starting fires that burn in the upper canopy for weeks, filling the air with smoke that the wind carries for a thousand kilometers.

The forest survives its fires. The forest has survived them for so long that it has, in some places, learned to *expect* them — certain species only release their seeds after a burn, certain flowers only bloom in the ash.

It is hot here. Always hot. The shade is a kindness, not a temperature. The air you breathe is the temperature of a bath. You sweat constantly, and the sweat does not evaporate so much as accumulate, and you learn to live inside your own weather.

---

## In Sum

This is a place that has decided what it is. It is not waiting for you. It is not hostile, exactly — it is too old for hostility, too settled. It is the kind of place that absorbs you, slowly, into its own concerns. You go in as one thing and come out — if you come out — as something slightly different. Your sense of time loosens. Your sense of where you end and the green begins becomes uncertain.

The people who live here do not count their years in the way you would. They count them in flowerings. In the spans between fires. In the slow lean of a tree they have known since they were small.

The sun, when it sets, takes three hours to do it, because the canopy eats the light in layers, and the green darkens to teal, and the teal to the deep blue of the permanent dusk that lives at the floor, and the fungi begin to glow, and the larger fauna begin their night-call, and the sound, which has never stopped, simply changes key.

You lie in your buttress-dwelling and you listen to the forest breathing around you, the slow creak of wood, the drip of water making its patient way down through the leaves, the boom of something large moving far above, and you think: *I am inside something. Something that was here before me and will be here after. Something that does not need me, exactly, but has, for the moment, allowed me a room.*

You sleep.

The forest dreams you, too.
