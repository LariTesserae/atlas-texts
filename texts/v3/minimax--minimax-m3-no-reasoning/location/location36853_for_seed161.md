The mesa holds itself up against the sky on a pillar of older rock, and the pillar has been holding it up for longer than anything remembers. The mountains that gave rise to it are gone now — worn down to a chain of gray-blue shoulders at the horizon, lower than the Tabletop by a thousand cubits at least, and lower still each century. The mesa is the last high thing. It is the only place that has not yielded to the patient appetite of the air.

The top of it is nearly flat. Eons have scrubbed it smooth, leaving a long oval of pale stone no more than a few hundred paces long, half that across — a small place, easy to walk across before the midday meal. The edges drop vertically into nothing on three sides: a clean, sharp fall, the kind of fall that birds use as a launch. On the fourth side, where the rock runs back down to the rest of the world, the path comes up — a switchback trail cut into the saddle, partly natural, partly worked, narrow enough that laden animals must go single file.

It is warm up here, even when the sun is gone. The stone holds the day's heat in its body and gives it back slowly through the night, so the temperature moves in a small patient arc rather than the wild swing of the lowlands. The air is thin and tastes of mineral dust and something faintly sweet — a vapor that rises from the lichen fields at dawn and condenses against every cool surface. There is no standing water on the Tabletop. There has not been standing water on the Tabletop for as long as anyone can reckon. But there is wet. There is always wet, in the mornings and the evenings and through the long fog-banks that come up from below and pour themselves over the edges like milk poured off a table.

The light is dim, even at midday, even in the dry season. The clouds move through the Tabletop often. They come in from the west and they stay, sometimes for days, sometimes for weeks, and the sun is something that happens behind a curtain. The stone, the buildings, the plants, the animals, the people — all of it lives in a gray diffuse wash that has no sharp shadows. Eyes grow larger here. The eyes of the people, the eyes of the creatures, the eyes painted on the lintels above doors. Everyone is looking, all the time.

The settlement is on the top of the Tabletop, all of it, crowded against the edges like a settlement that has run out of room. The buildings are stone and clay and the bones of older buildings. They are three or four stories in places, and where the ground tips toward the edge they are three or four stories over a drop. They are not decorated. They are old. Some of them are older than the families that live in them, and the families are old.

The construction is primitive in the way that anything is primitive when it has been done the same way for a thousand years — stone split with wedges and patient hammer-blows, mud mixed in pits with bare feet, beams of a slow-grown timber hauled up the saddle-path at great cost. There is no metal here that anyone admits to. The tools are stone and bone and wood. The buckets are hide. The lamps are stone bowls of animal fat with a twist of moss for a wick, and they smell, when they are lit, of something between tallow and warm dust.

The buildings press close. The lanes between them are narrow enough that two people cannot walk abreast without one of them turning sideways, and in many places a person must duck under a beam or step up onto a wall-stone to pass. The air in the lanes is different from the air on the roofs — damper, warmer, full of the breath of families and animals and cooking. Smoke rises from a thousand small fires and gathers in a low ceiling over the settlement, and from a distance the Tabletop looks like a flat stone with a gray fleece laid over it.

The vegetation is dense. This surprises visitors, if there were visitors. There are no visitors. But the stone of the Tabletop, every surface not walked on, is dressed in a deep mat of growth — succulents with thick pale leaves, lichens in gray and rust and one particular shade of green that is not found anywhere else, mosses that swell in the fog-mornings and shrink in the afternoons and have been swelling and shrinking for so long that they have made their own soil in the cracks of the rock. There are small trees, too, of a kind with twisted branches and waxy leaves that lose almost nothing to evaporation; they grow in the courtyards and on the rooftops and in the lanes where no one walks. They are pruned close. They give a little shade. They bear a small fruit that is eaten green, with salt. There are flowers. There are always flowers — the small white ones that open only in fog, and the orange ones that open only when the sun briefly breaks through, and a creeping purple vine that has been trained up the sides of buildings for as long as anyone has been training it.

The fauna is abundant. This is the loud part. From somewhere on the Tabletop, at any moment, there is the sound of many animals doing many things. There are the domesticated ones — the shaggy ruminants kept for milk and wool, the smaller goats kept for meat, the chickens, the pigeons in the rooftop coops, the ferret-like things kept for hunting the rock-rats in the granaries. There are the wild ones — small foxes, large insects, lizards that flatten themselves against the warm stones in the afternoons, a kind of hawk that nests on the cliffs and feeds on the pigeons, a kind of crow that nests in the trees and feeds on everything. At night, the bats come out from under the eaves and the sound of their wings is a soft continuous hiss. There are the bees — small, dark, mountain bees — that work the fog-flowers and make a thick almost-black honey that is the only sweetener anyone knows. There are dogs. There are cats. There are children. The children are part of the fauna in the sense that they are everywhere and they are loud and they do not wait to be invited.

The sounds of the place are layered and constant. Wind, always — the wind is the loudest thing, even when it is not blowing hard, because at this height the air never truly rests. Wind across the stone, wind in the lanes, wind under the eaves. Below the wind, the animal chorus — the bleat and the cluck and the bark and the buzz and the long high piping of the hawks. Below the animals, the human sounds — voices in the lanes, voices from windows, the rhythmic tap of stone being worked, the scrape of a hide being stretched, the splash of nothing being poured. Somewhere, always, someone is singing. The singing is not melodic in the way of lowland songs; it is more a kind of measured exhalation, a long phrase with a small ornament at the end, repeated until it becomes part of the weather.

Things move slowly on the Tabletop. The erosion that wore the mountains down is still at work, but it has very little left to do on the top — the stone is hard here, harder than the rock below, and it gives up its surface only in dust that is blown away in the dry season and laid down again in the wet. The buildings are repaired rather than replaced. The walls are patched, the beams are spliced, the roofs are re-laid. The lanes are swept. The lanes are always being swept. The lichen grows a little and is eaten back. The moss swells and shrinks. The water comes and goes, comes and goes, and is gathered from the surfaces of stones into small stone basins and used for the animals and the moss-beds and the few cooking things that require it. Nothing happens fast. A child born on the Tabletop will, if it lives, grow up in the same lanes, marry in the same lanes, age in the same lanes, and be carried out across the saddle-path to be buried on the slope below, where the air is thicker and the ground is softer. The slow is not sad here. It is the way the place breathes.

What is most lasting about the Tabletop is that it has not changed. In the lowlands, things rise and fall — cities, languages, whole ways of living. Up here, the wind has worn the mountain down to a tabletop and the people on top of the tabletop have worn themselves into a groove that fits the tabletop exactly. They are the right size. Their tools are the right size. Their animals are the right size. The settlement has been here for as long as the fog has been coming up, which is the same length of time as the wind has been wearing, which is forever.

If you could see the place from very far away — if you could stand in the haze of the lower mountains and look up at the Tabletop through a rent in the clouds — you would see a flat stone in the sky, with a gray fleece on it, and out of the gray fleece the small green of the rooftop trees, and the thin smoke of the lamps, and the slow drift of the hawks. You would see a place that has been finished for a long time.
