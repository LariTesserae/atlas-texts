# The City at the Roof of the World

They say it took ten thousand years to build, though no one here would understand the question. There is no "they" anymore, and there has never been a "build." The city grew the way lichen grows, the way frost grows on a windowpane — by being the inevitable shape that surfaces and water settle into when given enough time and no reason to stop.

You arrive — if you arrive is the right word, and here it isn't — at an altitude where the sky has lost all conviction. It is the color of cold pewter, the color of unripe plum, the color of something that has been gray so long it has forgotten it is not supposed to be. The air is thin and damp both, which feels like a contradiction until you have lived here long enough to understand that this place *is* the contradiction made geography. Condensation beads on every surface not because the air is wet but because the air is uncertain. It is rain that never decided to fall. It is mist that never decided to lift. It is vapor caught in the act of becoming and held there by some stubborn local god of indecision.

The city sprawls. The city sprawls in the way that oceans sprawl, in the way that ice sheets sprawl. You do not walk *to* the city. You walk *into* the city the way you would walk into weather. It has no edge. It has no center either, not anymore, though there was once — perhaps — a center, and that center is now a memory held only by the stones at the center, which have arranged themselves in the silhouette of a memory no one remembers.

The buildings are stone. Cut stones. Stones that have been chiseled and fitted and fitted again by hands that knew their work the way a river knows its bed. The architecture is old in the way that granite is old — not the old of wear and tear but the old of simply never having been young. You can see tool marks on the lintels. They are not primitive. They are not advanced. They are the marks of hands that had time, and used it. The arches do not spring; they lean. The walls do not rise; they *deepen*. The streets do not run; they *descend*, as though the city is sinking into itself, slowly, the way a stone sinks into moss, the way a bell sinks into the sound it made.

There are people here. There are people here in the way that lichen is "there" on a rock — present, distributed, answering to no logic but their own. They live in the upper stories of buildings whose lower stories are in the cities below, which are also in the cities above, which are also the cities themselves. They speak softly. They speak rarely. They speak in a language that is mostly gesture and the rest is breath. They have copper tools and they have stone tools and they have tools of bone, and they do not distinguish between these categories because none of the categories have ever occurred to them as needing distinction. A knife is a knife. A wall is a wall. A person is a person, though which of these is the most durable is not a question they have asked.

The streets are wet. The streets are always wet, though it is not raining. The water comes from somewhere — from the condensation on the stones, from the breath of the people, from the slow weeping of glaciers that once covered this plateau and now exist as a memory held in the rocks that used to be the underside of the glacier. The water finds its way along the cracks in the paving stones, and the cracks in the paving stones were cut to guide it, though the stones do not remember being cut. They think they cracked this way for many reasons of their own.

Vegetation here, and the vegetation is wrong, and the wrongness is what makes it real. There are mosses that grow downward from the ceilings of vaulted chambers, hanging in beards that have never been measured. There are lichens that bloom only when no one watches, and they are watched now, by me, by the damp air, by the city itself, and so they will not bloom. There are ferns that have furled themselves against the cold and will not unfurl, and have not unfurled for a thousand years, and may never unfurl, and the unfurling is a memory held only in the spores, which sometimes drift on the damp air and settle in cracks in the paving stones and grow into something that is not quite a fern and not quite a memory.

There are no animals. There have never been animals, or there were once animals and they left, or the animals are still here but in a form that does not register as animal to anything except the things that have learned to look. Sometimes in the upper chambers you find feathers. Sometimes you find bones. Sometimes you find footprints in the wet paving stones, going somewhere, coming from somewhere, ending in the middle of a footprint as though the animal decided to step out of being an animal mid-stride. The people do not speak of these. The people do not speak.

The cold here is not the cold of winter. It is the cold of elevation, of altitude, of being close to the roof of the world and the roof not being warm. The cold is a thin blue line that runs through everything, a thread of chill that you can feel when you put your hand to a stone wall, when you breathe, when you close your eyes. The cold is the medium in which this place is preserved. Without the cold, the stones would forget being stones and become something else. The cold is what holds them.

The light is the light of overcast days that have been overcast for so long that the sun has become a theoretical object. It is bright enough. It is the light of a single late afternoon extended across centuries, smeared thin across the wet stones, pooling in the hollows of the worn steps. It does not cast shadows long. It casts shadows only briefly, as though it is embarrassed by them.

And the sound — the sound is mostly quiet, and the mostly is the point. There is the sound of water finding its way along cracks. There is the sound of breathing — your breathing, the breathing of the people, the breathing of the stones, the breathing of the place. There is sometimes a sound that might be wind, or might be the city settling, or might be the sound that glaciers make when they dream of being glaciers. There is rarely a sound louder than speech, and rarely speech.

And the place is vast, and the weather is the place's own weather, and the weather is the place. Rain falls here that is not quite rain. Mist rises here that is not quite mist. Snow sometimes falls here, though no one remembers what season it would belong to. The clouds come down into the streets and do not leave, and the town rises into the clouds and does not mind, and the two have become so closely bound that it is no longer clear which is holding up which. The clouds shift when the city shifts. The city shifts when the clouds shift.

This is a place that has been settled into its final form, and the final form is not finished. It is still settling. It will always be settling. The stones still wear. The mosses still grow. The water still finds its way. The cold still holds. The light still pools in the hollows of the worn steps. The people still do not speak.

This is the city at the roof of the world. There is no other city like it. There has never been another city like it. There will not be another one. The city is the only one of itself, the way a person is only that person, the way a stone is only that stone. You have been here, or you will be here, or you are here now, and the city is patient, and the cold is patient, and the damp is patient, and the light does not hurry, and the water finds its way, and the people do not speak of you, and the stones remember the feet of whatever walked here, and the wear here is the only history this place has ever needed.
