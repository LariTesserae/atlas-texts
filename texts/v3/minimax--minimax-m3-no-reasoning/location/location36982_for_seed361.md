The place fits inside a clenched fist of stone.

I want to start with the sound because the sound is what you meet first. It comes up through the soles of your feet before your ears catch it — a deep, rolling thrum, continuous, like a giant's breath being drawn somewhere beneath the floor of the world. Out of it, higher up, the keening starts: layered cries, not one voice but dozens braided together, each one bending around the next, none of them quite pitched for a human ear. They make shapes in the air you can almost see, shimmering arcs that hang for a moment before dissolving into the next phrase. The whole gorge rings. If you cupped your hands around your mouth and shouted, your shout would come back wrong — slowed, deepened, as if it had been swallowed and reconsidered.

The gorge itself. This is the pocket-scale of the place: a cleft maybe three hundred meters long, twenty across at its widest, walled by stone so old it has forgotten how to be anything other than itself. The rock is a dark red-brown, almost the color of dried blood, and it has the particular smoothness of stone that has been here too long — every sharp edge sanded down by more time than weather can account for, as if the mountain quietly pressed its own thumb against itself until it gave. Pock-marks stipple the walls, little hollows where the surface has flaked away in coin-sized discs. Lichens, the color of weak tea, climb into every crevice. The floor is the same stone, but there the erosion has gone all the way: it is a shallow basin, slightly cupped, the lowest point running like a spine down the middle of the cleft, and that spine is where the water lives.

A rivulet, no wider than my arm-span, traces the long axis of the basin. It comes out of a crack in the up-canyon wall and vanishes into a similar crack at the down-canyon end. It is not fast. It moves like something that has been moving for a very long time and has decided on its pace. Where it passes over a lip of harder stone it makes a small sound, almost a chuckle, almost a bell. The rest of the time it is glassy, dark, and if you look down into it you see the sky and the cliff faces drawn in miniature, trembling.

The water is everything here. The walls weep — every surface glistens with a film of moisture that never quite becomes a drop. Condensation runs down in threads. Where a fern has managed to root into a fissure it drinks from the air as much as from the soil. The air itself is a damp cloth pressed against your face; the moment you stop moving, the moisture settles on your skin and beads in your hair. The smell is the smell of a greenhouse at dusk: warm green, leaf-rot, the metallic breath of wet rock.

Vegetation has colonized the gorge the way a thought colonizes a quiet mind — gradually, then completely. Tree ferns stand up from the basin floor with their trunks furred in dead fronds, their crowns spreading into fractal fans three meters across. Between them, moss climbs the trunks and the stones in emerald and bottle-green pillows. Creepers loop across the walls in ropes as thick as your thigh, anchoring into the rock with what look like pale hands. There are flowers — strange ones, mostly: flesh-pink trumpets on black stems, clusters of pale blue bells that chime faintly in the still air though no one touches them, a low groundcover of something like clover but with leaves the size of saucers and a faint sour smell. The lushness is intense but contained. Nothing here competes. Every plant seems to have found exactly the place it wanted to be and stopped trying.

Above the cleft the sky is a strip of pale blue. The light falls in from directly overhead for an hour at midday, then the walls take it back and the gorge passes into a green half-twilight that lingers. The shadows are not dark — they are tinted, a permanent dusk-glow that gives everything the softness of an old photograph.

Now the fauna, because the fauna is what you notice next after the sound, and what the sound belongs to.

They hang in the air.

There are hundreds of them. The gorge is full of creatures in flight — though flight is too small a word. They are roughly the shape of skates, the shape of scarves, the shape of torn flags, anywhere from a hand-span across to three meters wing-to-wing. Their bodies are thin, almost diagrammatic: a central spine of something pale and cartilaginous, a lattice of darker ribs radiating outward, and between the ribs a membrane that is not quite skin — it has the iridescence of oil on water, blues and greens and copper shifting as they turn. They have no heads that you can find. No eyes. No beaks. Where the head should be, the spine simply ends in a small bulb that pulses with a dim internal light, somewhere between lantern-yellow and the color of a fever.

This is what makes the sound.

Each one sings. Or emits. Or resonates — the word keeps slipping because the mechanism is not quite any of those. From that pulsing bulb a tone comes out, and each creature's tone is slightly different from the next, and as they wheel and bank around each other in slow, weaving circuits they pass through each other's tones the way two chords pass through each other. The result is the keening I described, and the underlying thrum: the thrum is all of them at once, the sum of the chord, and the keening is the upper harmonics as they cross and uncross.

They do not seem to feed. They do not seem to land. They move as if gravity were a suggestion they had chosen to honor only loosely. A few of the smaller ones will occasionally dip and brush the surface of the rivulet — I think they are drinking, but the gesture is so slow and casual it could be anything. When one of the larger ones passes close to a tree fern, the fern trembles, not from wind but from the displaced air and the sound itself, the fronds shivering in sympathy with the passing chord.

There are other things. On the walls, pale slugs the length of your forearm move with terrible patience, leaving behind them a trail that glitters faintly. Something like a spider but with too many legs moves in the moss — I catch only the legs, never the body. In the rivulet, shapes flicker: pale eels, or fish, or something else with too many fins, slipping between the rocks. Small things chirp in the undergrowth. The whole place is inhabited to the brim, but the creatures do not fight. I have watched for a long time and have not seen one act against another. They pass through each other's territories the way the tones pass through each other — by overlapping, by agreement, by an old arrangement that no one is renegotiating.

The structures.

At the up-canyon end, where the water emerges, there is a thing built into the rock face that I have been circling for an hour without deciding what it is. It is a disc, perhaps five meters across, set into the cliff as if grown there. Its surface is the same red-brown stone as the wall around it, but it is segmented — divided into something like petals, or the cells of a citrus, or the chambers of a heart. There is a seam running around its circumference, fine as a hair, and from that seam a thin glow leaks, the same lantern-yellow as the bulbs on the flying creatures. It hums. When I lay my hand against the stone near it, I felt the hum in my wristbones.

I think it is a door.

Further down the gorge there are other marks: a fallen pillar of the same dark stone, half-buried in moss, its top carved into a shallow bowl that always holds an inch of rainwater even on dry days. A row of niches in the wall, each one containing a sphere of pale glass that does not reflect what is in front of it but something slightly off — a half-second delay, a smearing, as if it is showing you the world a moment after the world has happened. A bridge of the same red-brown stone, arched, too narrow to walk on, spanning nothing — there is no chasm beneath it, just more floor. These structures are not ruins. They are not decayed. They are simply *finished* — done in the way a fruit is done, the way a wave is done. They belong to the same process that made the smooth walls and the cupped floor. They are what the place has decided to be.

The strangeness here is not the strangeness of the alien. It is the strangeness of something that has been here longer than strangeness has had a name. The technology, whatever it produced, does not feel like technology. It feels like geology that learned to do tricks. The flying creatures do not feel like fauna. They feel like the local weather, made of flesh. The whole gorge is one continuous thing, one long event that has been happening in this cleft for so long that the event and the place have become indistinguishable.

I keep thinking of the scale of the place: the gorge is small enough that I can throw a stone from one wall to the other. The creatures wheel between the ferns. The water moves at the pace of breath. And yet none of it feels small. It feels like the most concentrated place I have ever stood in, the way a single word can contain a whole sentence if the word is old enough.

When the wind comes — and it does come, in soft gusts that drop down through the cleft from the unseen plateau above — the ferns bow and the creatures adjust their chords and the pillar-top bowl briefly rings a thin clear note and the disc in the cliff face pulses a little brighter, as if pleased, as if acknowledging something I cannot see. The water keeps moving. The sounds keep braiding. The light keeps falling down the strip of sky and being swallowed by the green.

I have been here a long time now.

I do not think I am going to leave.
