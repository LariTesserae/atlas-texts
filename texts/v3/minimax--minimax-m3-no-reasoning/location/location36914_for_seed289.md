# The Lithophone Garden

The world here is a single room — though "room" implies walls, and what surrounds this place is closer to the architecture of a geode turned inside out. You have somehow arrived in a hollow beneath the surface of something. The ceiling hangs low and fractured, perhaps four meters above at its highest point, perhaps lower where the stone drips down in mineral beards. Light pours in from everywhere and nowhere: a sourceless bright luminescence that seems to live in the air itself, rendering everything visible without casting a single shadow. It is the kind of light that makes you suspect your eyes have begun to glow.

The space is perhaps the size of a small courtyard, but the eye cannot confirm its dimensions because the walls are so thoroughly, so aggressively occupied. Every square centimeter of vertical surface is claimed.

**The Structures**

What rises from the floor are not buildings in any conventional sense but standing stones — thin blades of mineral, some no taller than a hand, some rising nearly to the ceiling, packed shoulder to shoulder like the pipes of a vast organ. They are made of something that is not quite rock and not quite crystal but something between: a translucent, faintly amber material shot through with darker striations, as if bands of sediment had been stretched and frozen mid-flow. They ring. When air moves through them — and air is always moving here, a slow exhalation from somewhere below — they produce sound. A chord has been sounding, without pause, for longer than memory. Low tones. High overtones. The chord is not music exactly; it is geology singing.

Between the blades, on the uneven floor, there are scattered small constructions: cairns of the same amber stone, each no bigger than a boot, arranged in loose clusters. They look human. They look abandoned. They look as if whoever placed them left in a hurry and did not come back, though whether "human" is the right word here is unclear, because nothing about this place suggests a human hand. The cairns may be natural. The cairns may be something else.

**The Moisture**

The air is damp in the way a sealed jar is damp — not raining, but saturated, every surface wearing a fine coat of condensation that catches the sourceless light and makes the whole chamber gleam. Pools of water lie in the depressions of the floor, shallow and still, and in them the stone blades are mirrored perfectly, so that looking down one sees a second garden descending into a second sky. The water is not cold. It is cool. The entire space holds a cool temperature, steady and unwavering, as if the laws of thermodynamics had reached an agreement here and signed a contract. Touch the stone, touch the water: the same temperature, always. Nothing warms, nothing cools. The place has found its equilibrium and refuses to leave it.

Occasionally a drop falls from a stalactite somewhere at the edge of perception — *tok* — and the ring of the stone blades shifts momentarily before settling back into its chord. That is the only punctuation.

**The Life**

The floor between the cairns and the pools is not bare stone. A pale vegetation spreads across it in patches: something between moss and coral, branching in fractal geometries, the color of cream or old bone. It is sparse but precise, each colony knowing its exact bounds. It does not move. Nothing here moves. The world is a photograph of itself, held still.

And yet it is full of animals.

They are everywhere, though "everywhere" means within this single small chamber. Small pale creatures — they might be insects, they might be something else, they have too many legs or not enough, their bodies are segmented in ways that suggest either crustacean or mechanical — cling to the stone blades, swim in the shallow pools, rest in the cups of the cairns. They are white or translucent. They are abundant. They do not seem to feed on anything, nor does anything feed on them. They exist in a state of total population saturation, a balance so complete that no individual needs to do anything at all. Some cling motionless to the blades. Others drift in the water. A few crawl, slowly, with apparent purposelessness, across the moss-coral. They make sounds — small clicks, small scrapes — that layer into the chord of the stones until the whole space rings with life and mineral together, a single sustained chord with a thousand small ornaments.

**The Atmosphere**

There is no wind, but the air moves in currents so slow they can only be felt on the skin, never seen. The breath comes from below, from cracks in the floor where the amber stone is darker, wetter. The light does not flicker. The sound does not fade. The creatures do not die or are not seen dying. Nothing decays. Nothing grows. The cairns do not weather. The moss-coral does not spread. The water level in the pools does not change.

This place is not a ruin. It is not a construction. It is not a cave. It is something that has finished becoming itself, and now simply holds.

**The Sound**

Loud, but not oppressively so. The chord of the stones is constant, a drone in a key that does not exist on any instrument. The small creatures add their clicks and their scrapes, and the water in the pools, though still, vibrates faintly in sympathy with the stone, producing a hiss at the threshold of hearing. And beneath everything, felt more than heard, a low hum, as if the entire chamber were the resonating body of something enormous singing just below the range of perception.

If you speak here, your voice is absorbed by the stone and returned to you as a faint echo half a second later, slightly altered, as if the place is trying to learn your voice and has not quite managed.

**The Experience**

To stand here is to feel the particular strangeness of a world that is complete. There is no past tense visible in the stone, no future tense implied in its stillness. The cairns might be a thousand years old or a thousand years from being built. The creatures might have been here forever. The chord has been ringing since before the concept of ears.

It is bright and damp and cool and loud and silent. It is a pocket universe the size of a courtyard, packed to bursting with life and stone and sound, and it has been waiting, perfectly balanced, for someone to notice it.

It does not mind if you stay. It does not mind if you leave.

It will be exactly like this when you return, and exactly like this if you never do.
