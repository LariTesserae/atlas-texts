# The Filament Hollow

You find yourself inside something that is not quite a building and not quite a forest and not quite alive, though it breathes.

The space is the size of a cathedral — a long vaulted cavity roughly two hundred meters end to end and perhaps forty meters at its widest point, narrowing toward each terminus like the throat of an instrument. The ceiling arches overhead at the apex of a pointed rib that might once have been masonry but is now entirely overgrown. Root and stem and tendril have threaded through the stonework so thoroughly that it's impossible to tell where construction ended and biology began. The walls are the same: dressed blocks, voussoirs, capitals carved in a style you almost recognize — Corinthian? Something older? — but every surface has been taken back, colonized, fused with a riot of plant tissue that pulses faintly in the dim light.

The light itself is the first wrong thing.

There is no clear source. A deep dusk-amber glow seeps out of the vegetation itself — from the leaves, from the bark-like epidermis of the larger growths, from the air just above the canopy where something phosphorescent hangs. It is never quite dark enough to lose your footing, but it is never light enough to read by. Shadows pool thick and soft at the bases of the trunks. The illumination shifts as you move, not because the plants track you but because their internal rhythm is slightly out of step — one section dimming as another brightens, a slow rolling pulse like the chapel windows of a living church changing color.

The plants are the architecture. Great columnar trunks rise from the stone floor — or from where the floor used to be; in places the flagstones have split and soil has gathered in the cracks and become root-plate — and these trunks are not separate from the building. They *are* the pillars. Where the original Romanesque columns stood, the same load is now borne by a living grey-brown bole, three meters in diameter, bark deeply fluted in vertical channels that echo the geometric recessed panels of carved stone. Up they rise, branching at the springing point of the vault, throwing out a tracery of limbs that meets its mirror from the other side in midair and tangles into the ceiling's ribbing. Where you can see through the canopy — and you can, in places, because the growth is dense but not closed — there is only darkness above, the suggestion of further vault, lost in gloom and the slow phosphorescence of high leaves.

Between the great trunks, the space is *empty*. Vastly, hauntingly empty. A nave you could fit a small town in, mostly air, mostly silence, mostly the color of dark honey held up against a bruise. The cathedral is not full. It is hollowed. The density of life is total where it occurs, but it occurs only in structural lines — along the columns, in the ribs of the vault, in tangled skeins that hang from the upper darkness like the ropy roots of a mangrove turned upside-down and strung between arches. Between these living members is a void so complete that sound behaves strangely there. Your footsteps have a muffled quality, as if the air itself is slightly damp. Your voice, if you called out, would travel clean to the far wall and come back to you changed, lower in pitch, as though the space has opinions about what it will echo back.

It is damp. Not raining, but raining *nearby*. You can hear it — a soft susurration off to your left, somewhere behind a wall that is half-living, half-stone. Condensation beads on every leaf surface and occasionally releases, a slow irregular drip that taps against stone or pools in a depression of the floor. The air smells of wet bark, of ozone, of something green and slightly medicinal. There are puddles in the lowest parts of the floor, dark mirrors in which the amber glow of the foliage above is reflected back, doubled, slightly greener than the source.

At the far end of the nave, where an apse might once have curved, the vegetation has done something stranger. It has woven itself into a tight knot — a sphere, perhaps four meters across, suspended in midair between two columns by a net of cable-thick vines. The sphere is darker than the rest of the growth, almost black, and it turns slowly on an axis that is not quite horizontal. Inside it, something flickers. Light, or the suggestion of light, in a color your eyes cannot quite fix. You look at it for a long moment before you realize: it is not rotating. The vines are rotating. The sphere inside is stationary, and around it the sheath of woven root turns like a slow wheel, and in the gaps between the strands you catch, briefly, a glimpse of what might be a room — a smaller room, a different room, lit by a colder light — before the weave closes over it again.

You do not look at it for too long. The sphere is not for you.

Movement. High in the vault, something is moving through the canopy. Your first thought is a bird, but birds do not move like this — a smooth glissade from one branch to another, then a pause, then a long looping swing by a limb, then again. The shape is too fluid, too unjointed. It could be a meter of rope pulled along by an unseen hand. It could be a leg. In the dimness, the color is the same grey-brown as the bark of the great trunks, and the thing has made itself the same shape. When it stops moving, you cannot find it again. You are not sure it was ever there.

Below, on the floor, other presences. A scatter of pale shapes in a pool of darker shadow — mushroom or coral, you cannot tell at this distance, growing in a cluster the size of a dinner table. They pulse, very faintly, in time with the ceiling's shifting light. Near the base of the nearest trunk, a slow rivulet of something that is not quite water: too viscous, too faintly luminous, too purposeful in its movement, tracing a deliberate path down a groove in the stone floor toward a drain that has not drained in a long time. Where it passes, the stone smokes a little, or seems to.

Somewhere, far off, a sound that might be an engine. Or might be a species of insect. Or might be the building itself, somewhere in its deep unreachable plumbing, grinding through the long work of keeping itself together. It is a low sound, felt as much as heard, and it is the only constant. Everything else in the hollow is intermittent: the drip, the rustle of the canopy-creature, the brief clicks and scrapes of whatever moves in the root-mat at floor level, the strange almost-breathing of the air in and out of the apse-sphere. But the hum is forever, or close to it.

You stand in the nave, in the small space of your own presence, and the cathedral holds you. It has been holding something for a very long time. You are not the first. The carved capitals — where the growth has not yet swallowed them — show faces. Not human faces, exactly. The stone carvers who worked here, whoever they were, were not entirely human, or were not carving humans, or were carving something they had seen that they did not have a name for. The faces look down at you from under wreaths of living leaf with expressions that might be recognition. The eyes are worn smooth. You do not meet them.

It is cool here, and damp, and dim, and the air tastes of green, and you are very small, and the building is breathing around you in its slow slow rhythm, and the sphere at the far end turns, and the hum goes on, and there is no clear way out, and no clear way in, and you have the feeling that you are somewhere that is still being built, very slowly, by something that has not yet decided what it is.

You stand in the Filament Hollow, and the hollow stands in you, and neither of you is finished.
