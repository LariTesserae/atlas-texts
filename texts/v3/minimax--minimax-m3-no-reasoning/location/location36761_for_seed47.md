# The Hush-Plain

## I. The Flatness That Has Forgotten Itself

It is difficult to say where the ground ends and the sky begins.

This is not because of fog or mirage. There is no haze. There is barely any air. What atmosphere clings to the surface of the Hush-Plain does so the way memory clings to an old wound — reluctantly, in thin patches. From horizon to horizon, the land runs like a smoothed coin, polished by more time than geology has a name for. The rocks here are not rocks anymore. They are the residue of rocks. They have weathered past their own mineral names into something grey and quiet and almost soft-looking, though when you touch them they are hard as a held breath.

The elevation is extreme — everyone knows this — but the elevation has been *used up*. There are no mountains. There are no valleys. There was a time, long ago, when there were. The ranges that once stood here were taller than anything standing now. They were ground down. They were ground *through*. They were ground past the category of mountain into the category of memory, and then ground past memory too, and what was left was this: a near-perfect flat that runs farther than a body can imagine standing still on.

Someone, once, must have been very high to make a flat this flat.

## II. The Salt Without the Sea

There is no water.

But this is not a desert, not in the way deserts are usually described. Deserts imply the *possibility* of water, somewhere, even if absent. The Hush-Plain does not imply water. It implies, instead, that water was a rumor that never caught. There are salt patterns across the grey stone, but they are not the salt of evaporated seas. They are the salt of *evaporation as a principle* — the slow exhalation of something that was never quite liquid, that crystallized directly out of the rock as the rock itself decided what it wanted to be when it grew up. The salt forms lacy whorls. The whorls form larger whorls. Looking down from any height, the plain reads like the cross-section of a bone, all concentric architecture and quiet.

If you kneel and put your tongue to the stone, you will not taste ocean. You will taste something mineral and strange, and your tongue will feel faintly numb afterward, as if the salt has opinions about contact with animal tissue.

## III. The Long, Cool Light

The light is normal. This is the strange part.

After everything else — the silence, the emptiness, the age of the stone — one would expect the sun to be wrong too, or absent, or some alien configuration. It is not. A pale yellow-white sun, neither larger nor smaller than expected, neither redder nor bluer than a remembered noon, hangs in a sky that holds its color honestly. There is a day here. There is a night. The shadows move at a sane rate, and they fall in directions a person can use to navigate.

This is what unsettles travelers, when travelers come. The light is so *recognizable*. It is the light of a home they have perhaps never had, falling on a place that has no business receiving it. You can see your own shadow clearly. The shadow has edges. This is not a place that lies about illumination.

The temperature is cool — never warm, never cold. Cool as a held metal cup. Cool as the inside of a stone wall in late autumn. The plain does not warm under the sun, and does not radiate after the sun goes. It absorbs a certain amount of heat and stops. This is its only conversation with the sun. The sun continues talking; the plain has said what it needs to say.

## IV. The Sparse, Patient Green

Vegetation, against all expectation, exists.

It is sparse. It is the sparsest thing here, except for sound and fauna, both of which are nearly absent. But what grows, grows *earnestly*. A few species — and they are few, perhaps six, perhaps fewer, no census has ever been completed because no census has ever been necessary — have committed themselves to the plain with the seriousness of people who have decided they are staying. They are low. They are grey-green or silvered or the deep color of a bruise half-healed. They do not reach up; they spread. Their root systems, when the rare specimen is excavated, are preposterously extensive — root to shoot ratios that no botanist would believe without seeing. The plants are mostly root. The visible parts are *announcements* of the invisible parts.

They do not flower often. When they do, the flowers are small and pale and last a day, sometimes less. They are pollinated, as far as anyone can tell, by wind and by accident. They are not in a hurry. The plain is not in a hurry. The plants are roughly the same age as the stone, give or take a few thousand years, and they have apparently come to an understanding.

## V. The Markers

Scattered.

Built structures on the Hush-Plain are not built in any conventional sense. They are more *posited*. A tower here, thin as a held breath, rising perhaps forty meters from a base that is itself only a few meters wide. A wall there, only a few stones long, that does not seem to enclose anything. A line of posts — and the word "posts" is generous; they are *verticalities*, slender things that might once have held wires, or might once have been wires themselves, frozen into standing shapes by a process no longer in operation. They cast thin shadows at noon. They make no sound in any wind.

The scale of these structures is human. That is the unsettling fact. Whoever made them was about our size, or chose to build at our size. They used stone from the plain. They used the salt whorls in some of their foundations, which suggests they understood the salt, which suggests they understood the plain, which suggests they were *from* here, or had been here long enough to be considered from here.

There are pictographs, sometimes. Marks on stone that might be writing or might be decoration or might be the slow, patient doodling of someone with a great deal of time and no one to talk to. They are not consistent in style. They appear to have been made across a span so long that styles changed in the making, as if a single tradition lasted longer than the climate that produced it.

## VI. The Quiet

This is the place's most famous quality, and its least understood.

The plain is not silent in the way a forest is silent, full of small rustlings and hidden machinery. The plain is silent in the way an empty room is silent when you have just remembered something terrible. There is a *hush*, which is different from a lack of sound. A lack of sound is the absence of noise. A hush is the *active suppression* of noise, as if the air itself has been told to be still.

When wind comes — and wind does come, sometimes, in long slow breaths across the flat — it does not whistle. It slides. It arrives as a change in pressure rather than as a sound. Your ears will pop softly. You will feel your skin adjust. You will not hear it.

Some travelers report hearing a faint high tone at the very edge of perception, especially at night. Others report nothing. The disagreement has never been resolved. It is unclear whether the tone is real or whether the brain invents it because a place this old and this flat *should* have some sound, and refuses to tolerate complete emptiness.

## VII. The Turbulence

This is the part the maps get wrong.

The Hush-Plain is, to all appearances, the most stable landscape imaginable. Flat. Cool. Old. Unchanged. A place settled into final form.

It is not.

Beneath the surface — and the surface is thin, only a few meters in most places, before one hits something that is not quite rock and not quite anything else — there is *activity*. The plain is a place of weather, but the weather is internal. Magma does not flow here; nothing so crude. Instead, there are slow rearrangements. The stone shifts over decades in patterns that, mapped and accelerated, would look like the surface of a boiling liquid. Salt patterns migrate. The sparse plants lean, slowly, in directions that change over centuries. The slender vertical markers of the built structures occasionally *fall over*, all in the same week, and then stand up again, or are replaced, or were never really standing.

The plain is turbulent in the way that a thought is turbulent. Something is happening. The something is huge. The something is so slow that nothing witnessing it can prove it is happening at all. But the wind, when it comes, comes in gusts that do not match the weather of any above. The air pressure changes follow no schedule. The shadows at dawn sometimes lean the wrong way for a moment — a flicker, a stutter — and then correct themselves.

This is a place that is busy. It is busy the way deep time is busy. It is busy at scales your body is not invited to participate in.

## VIII. What Lives Here

Rare.

Almost nothing. There is no observable macrofauna. There is, on close inspection, a thin layer of something moving in the salt whorls — too small to call animals in any meaningful sense, more like *animated minerals*, things that eat stone and excrete slightly different stone, that breathe the trace atmosphere and exhale a slightly different trace atmosphere, that have been doing so for longer than vertebrae have existed.

There are rumors of something larger. Travelers sometimes report, in the morning, that the placement of small stones near their camp has changed. They did not move them. The wind did not move them — the wind here does not move small stones, only slides over them. They do not mention it again. They are not sure they saw it. The plain, perhaps kindly, does not press the point.

## IX. The Beyond-Modern

The technology here is not *absent*. It is *finished*.

There are no active devices. There are no lights that light. There are no machines that machine. What there is, scattered in its sparse way across the plain, are *consequences* of engineering. The vertical markers are too straight to be natural. The walls have mortar that has bonded with the native stone so thoroughly that, at the interface, it is no longer possible to say which is which. The pictographs, under magnification, resolve into patterns that are not random and not pictographic and that repeat at scales too small to have been made by any tool that could also have made the larger marks. The whole plain is, in some sense, a *ruin* — but a ruin in which the ruin and the original have ceased to be distinguishable. The technology and the landscape have become the same thing.

This is what engineering at the scale of physics itself looks like, long after the engineers have gone. It looks like geology. It looks like weather. It looks, at this distance in time, exactly like a place that has always been this way.

## X. The Unusual

A few notes, for the record:

- The sky here is slightly the wrong color at zenith — a degree or two bluer than it should be, as if the thin atmosphere is filtering light slightly differently than atmosphere is meant to. No one knows why. No one has been able to sample high enough to find out.
- Stars at night are extremely clear. They are also *fewer* than they should be. Specific constellations are missing. No one is certain which ones.
- The salt whorls sometimes appear to *move* when observed from the corner of the eye, though they are stationary when looked at directly. This is true of nothing else here.
- The cool temperature never varies. Not by season. Not by day or night. Not by altitude, because there is no altitude. There is only the one cool.
- A sense, occasionally reported, of being watched by something that has no eyes and is not necessarily alive and has been watching for a very long time and is patient enough to watch for a very long time more.

## XI. The Whole

It is vast. It is empty. It is turbulent. It is finished. It is ancient. It is cool. It is quiet. It is, despite all this, *recognizable* — the light is right, the shadows are right, your shadow is the right length, your body is the right size for the structures, your tongue knows the salt. It is a place that was made for something like you, or that has been waiting for something like you, or that has forgotten what it was waiting for and is now simply *open* in the way a hand is open after it has dropped something.

It is a place to stand on.

It is a place that lets you.

You will not hear it let you. You will not see it. You will stand, and the plain will hold you, and that will be the whole conversation, and it will be enough.
