# The Vent of the Sixth Hour

There is a place where the skin of the world has not yet learned to keep.

---

It begins as a fissure. Not a crack, exactly — the word *crack* implies a wound in something whole. This is more like a place where the world forgot to finish itself. A seam in the basalt that runs roughly north-south for what cannot be measured, because to walk its length would be to enter a place where walking stops meaning what it means.

The fissure is the size of a doorway. The size of a grave. The size of a memory of a doorway. It depends on which part of it you are standing near, and it does depend, because the seam breathes.

I will tell you what it is.

---

**The floor** is wet. Not damp — *wet*, in the way that only hot places can be wet. The water here does not pool because it has nowhere to go; it pools because it is constantly arriving. It seeps up through the basalt in slow, glossy beads. It sweats down the walls in vertical threads that never quite fall. It hangs in the air as a fog so thick it is almost a weather. To breathe here is to swallow a small lake with each inhalation. The moisture is warm. It tastes of iron, of old stone, of something sweeter underneath that no chemist would be able to name — a mineral salt, perhaps, or the exhalation of the rock itself as it continues to form.

When you crouch and put your palm to the floor, you can feel the rock *thinking*. A slow pulse. A tectonic deliberation. The heat comes from below, from a depth that is not far — only the thickness of a thought. If you pressed your ear to the stone you would hear water moving through capillaries of lava, a sound like a stomach, like digestion, like something alive in a way that the word *geology* cannot admit.

---

**The walls** are young. That is the word for it — *young*, the way a bruise is young, or a fire. The basalt here is not the patient black of a million-year-old flow. It is the wet rust-brown of something that cooled only yesterday, or is cooling now, or is cooling in a time that does not proceed at one speed. You can see where the last surge of magma pressed upward and did not quite breach — a blister in the rock the color of an organ, the size of a fist, still faintly warm. The edges of the fissure are sharp enough to cut you. *Sharp* in the young sense, the volcanic-glass sense: edges that have never been weathered because there has been no weather yet, because weather requires time, and time here is not on speaking terms with the rock.

The walls are also veined with a substance that is not quite mineral and not quite not. It runs in horizontal bands, a quarter-inch thick, and it *moves*. Not fast. Not in any way that could be photographed. But if you watch one band for long enough — a minute, an hour, a year, the unit does not matter — you will see it has shifted. It is pale. It is faintly luminous. It tastes, if you are foolish enough to touch it to your tongue, of nothing at all, which is a taste, and a wrong one.

I will come back to the veins.

---

**The air** is alive.

Not with what you would call life. There are no plants here. There is nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that roots, nothing that lifts a green hand to a sun that does not reach this deep anyway. But the air is *inhabited*. It is dense with motion. It is so thick with presences that to push through it is to wade.

The presences are small. They are the size of gnats, or smaller, or larger only when you are not looking. They drift in the damp heat like sparks held in suspension. They make no sound. They have no silhouette you can fix. But they are *there*, in such numbers that the air shimmers with the fact of them, in such saturation that to inhale is to take a census.

What are they? They are spores of something. They are the atmospheric phase of a biology that has not yet decided whether it is biology. They are, perhaps, the *weather* here — the only weather. They rise from the wet rock in invisible plumes. They condense on the cooler surfaces as a faint ochre dust that never quite accumulates and never quite dissipates. If you cup your hands and wait, you will find, after some interval, a fine powder in your palms. It moves. Not much. A tremor. A possibility.

I am getting ahead of myself. Let me go back to the beginning.

---

**To enter the Vent of the Sixth Hour** — that is what the small ones call it, in the soft piping language they speak — you must first cross a basalt field that has no horizon.

The field is broken. Jagged. Young. It was here yesterday, or it is here now, the distinction is academic. The rocks are the size of fists, of skulls, of small children curled fetal. They are sharp and wet and they ring underfoot like pottery. Between them, the water lies in sheets so thin you do not see them until you step into them and find your boot four inches deep in a warm film. The water is not standing water. It is *running* water, in sheets, in every direction, in a pattern that suggests drainage but is not drainage, that suggests flow toward a low point but the low point is the fissure itself, everywhere, all at once.

There is no sun here, but there is light. It comes from below. A faint red-orange glow suffuses the underside of every rock, as if the field is sitting on a lantern, as if the ground is on fire in a polite and patient way. The light is just bright enough to throw no shadows. Everything is illuminated equally. Everything is *exposed*. You cannot hide here. Not from the small ones.

---

**The small ones.**

They are the reason the fissure is known. They are the reason anyone comes.

They are everywhere. They are *overwhelming*. Not in number — in *presence*. The way a crowd is overwhelming not because there are many people but because each person is a complete person and there are too many complete persons to hold in mind at once. The small ones are like that. Each one is a complete thing. Each one is busy.

They are roughly the size of a thumb. They have no fixed shape. They are wet. They are the color of the wet rock, which is to say they are camouflaged into a place that is already camouflaged. They move on too many legs to count, or on no legs at all, gliding on the films of water, sliding under the rocks, emerging a meter away with a smoothness that suggests they were never under the rock at all but simply chose not to be where you were looking.

They make sounds. The sounds are not loud. They are *quiet*. But there are so many of them that the silence is textured. A hush of small clicks. A whisper of wet feet. A faint, pervasive rustle, like paper, like silk, like the inside of a seashell held to the ear but larger, more populated, more *opinionated*.

They are working. That is the only word. They are working, all of them, all the time, on something.

---

**What they are working on** is the veins in the walls.

The veins are pale. Luminous. Slow-moving. They are the thing that the small ones tend, the way ants tend aphids, the way bees tend flowers, the way a person tends a fire. The small ones move along the veins constantly, touching them with appendages that are too small to see but that leave faint marks — a stippling of darker spots along the pale band. The marks fade. The marks return. The marks are perhaps a writing. The marks are perhaps a feeding. The marks are perhaps a question, posed to the vein, over and over, by every small one in the colony, a single question asked in a single voice by a billion mouths.

The vein answers. Slowly. The vein moves.

If you watch long enough — and *long enough* is a unit you will have to invent for yourself, here — you will see a small one pause at the vein and *detach a piece of it*. The piece is luminous. It is the size of a grain of rice. The small one holds it in two appendages for a moment, like a jeweler holding a stone, and then —

And then it is gone. Either the small one has eaten it, or the small one has *become* it, or the small one has placed it somewhere you did not see, in a crevice, under a flake of basalt, in one of the small dark pockets that pock the walls like a disease. The crevices are full of them. The grains of pale light. Thousands. Millions. Each one pulsing faintly, each one *waiting* for something, or *being* something, in the patient, uncommunicative way of seeds.

I do not know what they are seeds of.

I do not know what they will become.

I have not waited long enough.

---

**The heat** is a character in this place. It is not temperature. It is *intention*. It presses on you from below and from the walls and from the air itself, a heat that is not dry-dry but wet-hot, a heat that does not so much warm you as *occupy* you. It enters through the soles of your feet. It enters through the back of your neck. After an hour in the Vent you will not feel hot; you will feel *full of heat*, in the way a sponge is full of water, in the way a bell is full of sound. You will sweat and the sweat will not evaporate. It will simply join the films of water on the floor and become part of the field.

There is a particular stone, near the deepest part of the fissure — the part that is too narrow for a person, that narrows to a slit, that breathes a slow exhalation of heat so concentrated it bends the air above it — that stone is warm to the touch in a way that suggests intention. As if something on the other side of it is leaning against it. As if the stone is the thinnest membrane between here and somewhere else.

The small ones avoid that stone. Or rather, they approach it, and then stop, and then press themselves flat against the floor, and *wait*, in a posture that looks like reverence and might be fear and might be something else that does not have a name. They wait for hours. They wait until the heat-slits exhale, and then they resume their work on the veins, as if the wait was a kind of prayer, or a kind of transaction, or a kind of clock.

---

**The rules of this place**, if there are rules:

The deeper you go, the less *here* feels like a place. Coordinates begin to drift. You turn to look back at the way you came and the way you came is not there. Not because you are lost — because the fissure has rearranged itself, gently, while your back was turned. Because the field of young basalt is a *living* field in the same way the air is living, and it does not hold still.

The small ones know the way. They always know the way. If you follow a particularly purposeful small one — one that is moving with a kind of insistence, with a small held grain of pale light in its appendages — it will lead you to a pocket in the wall. A pocket you would not have seen. A pocket that *is* a small room, roughly the size of a person lying down, lined with the grains, glowing faintly, warm, dry — the only dry place in the whole wet world.

If you lie in such a pocket, the small ones will come and look at you. They will not harm you. They are not sure what you are. They will click softly. They will touch you with their appendages, gently, on the knuckles, on the eyelids, on the soft skin behind the ears. They will taste you, perhaps. They will decide you are not a vein. They will leave you alone, eventually.

You will not sleep. But you will rest. You will rest in a way that does not require sleep. You will hear them working, outside, in the dark, and the sound will be the sound of a place that is being made.

---

**At some point** — and I cannot tell you when, because the *when* is not stable here — you will notice that the veins in the walls have begun to glow more brightly. That the small ones are moving faster. That the heat has become a *presence*, a thing with weight, a thing that leans. That the air is thicker. That the basalt field above you has begun to *ring*, a low vibration that you feel in your sternum before you hear it.

Something is happening. Something that the small ones have been waiting for, in their flat-pressed way, at the edge of the heat-slit.

Something is coming up.

Not magma. Not lava. Something else.

Something pale. Something that moves like a thought moves through a mind. Something that tastes, when it finally arrives, of *nothing at all*, which is a taste, and a wrong one, and the right one.

---

I will not tell you what happens next.

I am not sure there is a *next*.

I am not sure that *next* is the right word.

---

But the small ones are still working. The veins are still pale. The heat still breathes from the deepest slit in the world. The water still rises through the basalt in glossy beads. The field still rings, faintly, under a sky that is not a sky but a roof of young rock that has not yet decided whether it is a roof or a wound.

And if you listen — which is to say, if you allow the silence to have texture, if you let the hush of small clicks become a kind of language, if you sit very still in your glowing pocket and let the place speak in the only voice it has —

You will hear it.

A single note. Sustained. Patient. The note the world makes when it is about to become more than it was.

---

*This is the Vent of the Sixth Hour. It is not a place you visit. It is a place that, occasionally, visits you — in dreams, in fevers, in the long moment before sleep when the body is warm and the room is dark and somewhere, very far down, something pale is rising through wet stone.*
