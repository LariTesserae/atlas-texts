# Pennygap

There is a place in a low limestone country where the ground opens and does not close again. The opening is not dramatic — no cliff, no chasm — only a gentle sinking, a bowl in the earth perhaps three hundred paces across at its widest, ringed by low green hills that lean inward as if listening. From the road, if a road passes nearby, you would see only the hills and the trees upon them, and a suggestion of darker green in the cup between. Sheep graze the slopes and pay the hollow no mind. The hollow, in turn, asks nothing of them.

Inside, the town.

The town has no name in any register that matters. Travellers who have heard of it call it Pennygap, or the Hollow, or simply *the place in the dip*. The people who live there — if one can use that word — call it nothing. It is. Naming is for things that might be confused with other things, and there is no other thing. So.

The first thing you notice, lowering yourself down the worn path between two shoulder-high stones set upright for no reason anyone can recall, is the light. It is brighter down here than it has any right to be. The hills cup the sky into a narrow bright coin above the rooftops, and the white-grey limestone walls of the houses catch that coin and throw it back, and the mosses — for there are mosses everywhere, in every joint and on every lintel — hold the light the way green glass holds a candle, glowing softly from within. Even on a clouded day the bowl is bright. The eye adjusts and discovers there is no shade. There is only softer light and harder light.

The second thing you notice is the damp. It is not raining. It may not have rained for a week. But everything is wet. The air is wet. The stones sweat. Water gathers in dark beads along every horizontal surface and then, when it has gathered enough to overcome its own patience, slides down a wall and disappears into a crack. There is a smell of wet lime and wet green and the deeper sweeter smell of slow rot, the smell of things being unmade patiently over centuries. From somewhere comes the constant small music of water — not a stream, not a fall, but the trickling-down sound of a hundred such journeys. It is almost, but not quite, sound. It is more the memory of sound.

There are about two hundred buildings. No — there is no *about*. There are two hundred and fourteen buildings, by the last count that was ever made, and the number has not changed since. They are packed as tightly as teeth. A grown person walking the lanes between them must turn sideways in places, and in the narrowest gap, between the bakery wall and the house they call the Long House — though it is not the longest house, only the one that leans most — even sideways is a difficulty. A child must go single-file. A breath can almost bridge it.

The buildings are stone, mostly. Limestone, the same stone as the hills, quarried here in some older age when the pit was deeper and the town was being made. There is no wood framing that you can see, although within the walls there are beams — you can tell by the way a house settles, by the angle of its roof. The beams are old. They are black with age. They have become more mineral than wood. Some of them have become, in places, more fungus than wood, sprouting brackets of pale bracket-fungus that no one cuts because no one cuts anything here.

The roofs are thatch, mostly, and the thatch is green. Not rotting green — alive green. Moss has colonised every roof in the town to a depth of three or four inches in places, and within the moss grow small ferns — polypody, mostly, and hartstongue, with their tall simple fronds rising in clusters — and within the ferns grow lichens, pale grey-green and dead-mans'-finger, and within the lichens grow smaller mosses, and so on, each surface a city of smaller cities. From above, the town would look like a single green throat, a mossy bowl inside the larger bowl of the hills. From within, the lanes are dim green cathedrals, the roofs meeting overhead in places where the houses lean together, sealing the streets into tunnels of green-filtered light.

In one lane — the lane that runs from the well down to the shrine at the lowest point — the houses have leaned so far over the years that their upper stories touch, and the mosses have woven the two together into a single living arch. Nothing passes under that arch now. Nothing has passed under it for a long time. It has become, like so much here, a thing that exists only for itself.

The shrine at the lowest point is the oldest thing. It is older than the houses, perhaps older than the town. It is a single stone, set on end, a little taller than a man, a little thinner than a man. It was not quarried here. The stone is wrong for the place — a darker granite, veined with quartz, and the quartz glints in the bright light when the sun is right. The people do not worship at it. They bring it things — a bowl of water when the well is low, a sprig of something green when the moss is thin, a feather when one is found — but they do not kneel. Whatever feeling the stone holds, it is not a feeling that asks for kneeling. It is a feeling more like being watched by something that is not unkind.

Above the town, the sky is often full of swifts. They nest in the limestone cliffs that form part of the inner wall of the bowl, and in the evenings they pour out of the holes up there in long streaming ribbons, and their cries — if cries is the word; it is not a cry, it is more a sewing-together of small sharp sounds into a continuous sheet — fill the upper air. They are the loudest thing. They are not loud.

Below the town, in the stones and under the stones, in the damp cracks and the deeper damp cracks beneath those, live the other citizens. Salamanders, orange and black, slip between the moss and the wall when you are still. Woodlice in their pale platoons move along the joints. A slowworm, if you are lucky and quiet for a long time, may uncurl from beneath a flat stone at the edge of the shrine. Beetles of many small ambitions go about their small affairs. Spiders — small ones, mostly, with tidy webs strung between the brackets of fungus — sit at the centres of their designs and wait. There are mice. There must be mice. There are rats too, larger and glossier than city rats, who move in the deeper tunnels of the thatch and the hollow walls. There is a stoat that has been seen, three times in living memory, a long pale stoat that runs along the top of the shrine stone and is gone.

No dogs bark in Pennygap. No dogs live here. No horses have been brought down the path in any memory. The people — and there are people, thirty-one of them by the last count that was made — keep a few goats in a pen at the upper edge of the town, where the path enters, and there are chickens, perhaps a dozen, who live beneath the stairs of the Long House and emerge in the bright hours to scratch at the moss. The goats do not bleat. The chickens do not, in any musical sense, crow. They make the small sounds of animals confirming their own presence to themselves — a cluck, a grunt, the click of a hoof on stone — and these are absorbed almost immediately into the larger wet silence.

The people themselves are quiet.

This is not a vow they have taken. It is not a law of the place. It is that there is nothing, after a certain point, to say. Each person knows what the others know. The well is the well. The shrine is the shrine. The baker is the baker. The cloth that is being woven in the upper house is the cloth that has been woven for a long time. There is no news. There is no gossip, because there are no events. A child is born — once in a long while, every seven or eight years, it seems — and the child is given a name and the name is put in a book that is kept in the house with the most books, and the child grows. An old person dies, and is carried up the path and laid in the ground on the southern slope, and a small stone is set there, and the moss comes over the stone in time. That is the shape of a life here. It is enough shape. No one complains. No one is heard to.

You will want to know what they do. They make things, slowly. There is a baker who makes bread from a grain that is grown on the southern slope, a small bearded grain, and the bread is dense and dark and faintly sweet. There is a woman — or there was, and there is now a man, and before her there was a man — who weaves cloth on a loom that has been in the same house for a very long time, and the cloth is undyed, the colour of the sheep that graze the hills above, and it is woven into garments that are worn until they wear out and then unravelled and woven again. There is a man who shapes wooden spoons from the wood of the alder that grows in the wetter hollows, and the spoons are simple and beautiful in the way that only simple things can be. There is no smith. There has not been a smith in any memory. The tools of the town — and there are not many — are stone, bone, wood, and the rare piece of bronze that comes down the path occasionally in the pack of a traveller who is passing through to somewhere else, or in trade for a spoon.

The travellers are very few. There was once, by the look of the lane called the Merchant's Way, a good deal of traffic. The Merchant's Way is wider than the other lanes, paved with flatter stones, and there are postholes set into the walls on either side where awnings might once have been stretched. Now it is just a wider lane. The travellers come perhaps twice in a year, and they stay a night in the house kept for travellers, and they go. They say the town feels strange to them. They cannot say how. They say it is as if they have walked into a painting, or out of one, and they do not know which.

The stranger who stays more than a night begins, after the first day, to find that the bright light does not trouble the eyes as it did. After the second day the silence becomes comfortable, and then becomes unnoticeable, and then becomes a thing one would not wish to be without. After the third day one stops thinking of leaving. After a week one wonders why one ever thought of going. There is a stone set into the wall of the Long House, at about the height of a man's shoulder, on which is carved, very worn now, a small bird in flight. No one knows who carved it, or why, or when. It has the look of a warning. It has the look of an invitation. It is impossible to tell.

The well is at the centre of the town, in a small triangular space where three lanes meet. The well is not deep — perhaps six arm-lengths to the water. The water rises and never falls, and never rises above a certain mark on the stone lip. There is a constant small overflow that runs down a channel cut into the cobbles and disappears into a grating of bronze — the bronze of which the spoons are not made, the older bronze, the only bronze in the place that shows signs of working — and below the grating the water goes where water goes. The well is older than the shrine. The well is the reason the town is here. The well, and the bowl the hills have made around it to keep it from spilling away.

The light here, I have said, is bright. I should like to say more about it. It is not a flat brightness. It comes from many places. It comes from the narrow sky above, and it comes from the white walls that catch and return it, and it comes from the mosses that hold and gently re-emit it, and from the quartz-veins in the shrine stone, and from the surface of the well itself, which is perfectly still and is a small bright coin in the dark of its own well-shaft. At certain hours — and the hours here are felt rather than counted — the light aligns itself through the lanes and arches of the town in long pale shafts, and the dust in the air, the fine damp dust that is always in the air here, becomes visible, and the shafts become solid, and one feels that one is standing inside the bones of some vast pale creature, lit from within. Then the alignment passes, and the town is green and quiet again.

There are no shadows in the usual sense. The light is too even for shadows. Things have a darker side and a lighter side, but the contrast is gentle. The eye learns to read by the gentler contrast, and to find a kind of depth in the wall that does not depend on shadow. This is, perhaps, why the place has the look of a painting. The place has the look of a painting because there is no shadow in it, and a place without shadow is a place that has been looked at, for a long time, by someone who saw it clearly.

I do not know how to end this. The town does not end. The town continues, as it has continued. The moss grows, and the stone sweats, and the well rises and falls within its narrow range, and the swifts go out and come back, and the children grow, and the old are laid on the southern slope, and the light fills the bowl, and the silence holds, and the shrine stone stands, and the bronze grating holds its small inheritance of water. Sometimes, very rarely, a traveller comes down the path between the two shoulder-high stones, and pauses, and looks at the green below, and turns and goes back up. More often, the traveller comes down, and stays the night, and then the next night, and then does not leave. The book in the house with the most books acquires another name. That is all.
