# The Long Plateau Above the Cloud

You arrive at the town by a single rail. The rail is the only way in that doesn't require a person to walk for three days across ground that has not yet decided what it is. The cog wheels catch at the steepest section and the cars tilt, and the windows show nothing for long minutes — just the inside of a fog that has the consistency of wet wool, that beads on the glass and runs down in slow, deliberate threads. When the fog thins, it thins in patches, and through the patches you can see down, once, a great distance, to a plain that is presumably still attached to the rest of the world. Then the fog closes again and the windows are opaque.

The air is damp. Not the damp of lowland places, where dampness means a heavy sky about to break. Here the dampness is already broken. It is in everything. The brass rail you hold onto is beaded. The leather of the seat is dark with moisture. Your own sleeves, by the time you have ridden the cog for an hour, have absorbed enough water that the cloth has changed color. You can feel the water in your lungs. It is cool, this water, and the air that carries it is cooler, and your breath does not quite make a cloud but it tries, it almost does, on the colder days.

The town is called, in the language of the people who came up the rail to build it, a word that translates roughly as *The Long Plateau Above the Cloud*. In an older language, in the language of the people who were here before the town, it has no name, because it did not exist. The rock on which the town sits is younger than the oldest person in the town. The town is younger than the lichen on the cliffs. The lichen is younger than the cracks in the cliffs, and the cracks are younger than the cliffs themselves, and the cliffs are younger than the season. This is a mountain that has not been a mountain for very long. It was something else. It was, according to the geologists who arrived with the second wave of surveyors, a section of seabed that had been folded upward by a process the geologists were still in the process of inventing a name for. The seabed had not finished becoming a mountain. It was working on it. The mountain was working on it the way a sculpture is being worked on, except the chisel is time and the sculptor is not paying attention.

The slopes are raw. They are not the weathered, rounded, ancient slopes of the older peaks visible on clear days from the observation deck. They are sharp. The stone is in the act of being stone. Strata that have never seen weather before are now seeing it, and the weather is winning, but the weather is patient, and the stone is patient, and the contest will outlast everyone. Fresh talus piles lie below fresh cliffs, and the talus is itself being weathered, the edges of the broken rocks dulled, the surfaces acquiring the first thin crust of lichen in the cracks where water gathers. The first soil — if it can be called soil, if a smear of organic decay on pulverized shale deserves the dignity of the word — is forming in the gullies. It is thin. It is brown against the grey. It is, in places, already hosting small brave plants, and the plants are in turn hosting small brave insects, and so the slow construction of a living world proceeds, degree by degree, on top of a world that has only just stopped being another world.

The town itself is built on the only flat place the new mountain offers. There was no flat place, originally. There was a long, tilted shelf of uplifted limestone that the surveyors decided was flat enough, and on that shelf the engineers poured a foundation. The foundation is the largest single piece of masonry in the region. It is a thousand feet long and two hundred wide and in places forty deep, and it is set into the rock with iron pins the length of a man, and it is the only reason the town has not slid off the mountain in the spring thaws. The town above the foundation is a real town, of stone and brick and riveted iron, of paved streets and gutter channels, of buildings three and four stories tall, of a market square and a bathhouse and a rail terminus and a foundry and a mill house and a schoolhouse and a temple or three or four, depending on which census you trust. The streets run in straight lines that ignore the shape of the ground. The buildings are built to a grid that was drawn in an office somewhere down below the cloud line, and the grid does not know about the mountain. The grid is a polite, confident assertion that the mountain will behave.

The town is loud. The loudness is not a polite loudness. It is the steady, patient, industrial loudness of a place that is extracting something from the mountain at scale, continuously, and has been for as long as the town has been standing. There is the sound of the foundry, which is the sound of a large angry animal breathing. There is the sound of the cog rail, which grinds and screams in its housing at the steep section. There is the sound of the ventilation fans, which never stop, because the air up here is thin and the seams where the workers go are thinner still, and the fans push air down into the mine and pull it back up and the air comes back up tasting of dust and of the deep, mineral breath of the mountain. There is the sound of the steam plant, hissing and clanking. There is the sound of the hammers in the mill. There is, underneath all of this, a sound that is not made by any machine and is not made by any animal and is not made by the wind. It is a low, intermittent groan that comes from inside the mountain itself, from the seams and the deeper shafts, and the miners have a name for it but they do not say the name where the foremen can hear. The mountain, they will tell you in the beer hall after a shift, is not finished being made. The mountain is still moving. You can feel it move, sometimes, when you are down in the deep seams. The walls of the shaft shift by fractions of an inch. The floor of the shaft lifts by fractions of an inch. The sound is the sound of a continent deciding.

The lamps in the streets are electric. The wires run on poles along the main avenues, and the bulbs are a pale, sodium color that flattens the world. The light they give is the light of an overcast afternoon, every afternoon, every evening, every night. It is not a strong light. It does not reach into the alleys. It does not reach into the upper windows of the buildings on the upslope side. It does not reach the cliffs above the town, which remain, even at midday, in a kind of dusk, because the cloud is rarely gone. The town is inside weather. Above the cloud, on the rare days when the cloud breaks, the sky is the deep, bruised, almost-purple blue of high altitudes, and the sun is bright enough to hurt, and the shadows are sharp. On those days the town looks like a different place. The stones are orange. The lichens are chartreuse. The smoke from the foundry is a long white plume against the blue. People stand in the streets and look up. They do this rarely. It makes them quiet. Then the cloud closes again and the town returns to its usual color, which is grey, and its usual light, which is dim, and its usual sound, which is loud.

The moisture in the air is not rain. It is not mist, exactly. It is closer to the inside of a lung. You can see it as a faint silvering on every surface. The cobblestones are slick. The iron railings are slick. The stone walls of the houses, where they meet the slate roofs, run with thin water. In the mornings there is a fog so thick that you cannot see the building across the street, and the bells in the temple tower sound muffled and far away, as if rung under water. The plants of the town thrive in this. The moss on the north sides of the buildings is the thickness of a blanket. Ferns grow in the joints of the bridge over the foundry tailrace. A small, pale, determined orchid has colonized the iron fire escape of the schoolhouse, and the schoolchildren are not allowed to pick it because it has been there longer than any of them. The plants of the upper slopes, above the town, are more ambitious. There are, on the cliffs and in the gullies, whole hanging gardens of moss and saxifrage and a kind of dwarf willow that creeps along the ground and roots where it can. There are, in the more sheltered folds of the rock, thickets of a stunted, wind-bent shrub that bears, in the late summer, a small red berry that the townspeople have learned to make into a jam. The jam is sour. The jam is prized.

The animals of the region are a problem. The animals are a problem because there are more of them than there should be, given that the region did not exist a hundred and fifty years ago, and given that the region is mostly bare rock, and given that the region is at an altitude at which most animals, in most parts of the world, decline to live. There are, to begin with, the expected things. There are mountain goats, or something very like mountain goats, that pick their way along the cliffs and look down at the town with the casual contempt of creatures who understand the terrain better than the people do. There are foxes, smaller and thinner than lowland foxes, with fur the color of wet stone. There are large, pale, almost luminous birds of prey that ride the updrafts along the cliff faces and whose shadows, when they pass over the town, cause people to look up. There are, in the gullies, small things that rustle and are not often seen.

There are also the others.

The others are what the townspeople mean when they say *the fauna* in a careful, neutral voice, the way a person might say *the neighbors* in a house they have not yet decided whether to leave. The others do not appear in the official surveys, except as marginal annotations and crossed-out entries. The others are not seen by everyone, and they are not seen consistently, and the people who claim to have seen them disagree about what was seen. There is a kind of large cat, perhaps, that has been glimpsed on the cliffs above the foundry, except that the cat has too many legs, or its legs are in the wrong places, or its shadow does not match its body. There are things in the upper gullies that move like goats and sound like goats and may be goats, except that the herders will not take their animals above the second ridge anymore, and they will not say why, except that the herders have a look in their eyes. There are, on certain nights, lights in the cloud above the town that move in patterns no aircraft of the town's acquaintance could produce, and the patterns are slow, and the patterns are patient, and the patterns, when watched long enough, begin to look like a kind of writing, or a kind of weather, or a kind of attention.

The slow dynamic of the place is not the slow dynamic of an ordinary mountain town that is gradually losing its young people to the cities below. The slow dynamic is older. The slow dynamic is the slow dynamic of a place that is becoming more itself, in a direction that the people who live there are not entirely sure of. The mountain is still rising. This is the geologists' best guess, based on measurements that are not always reproducible, based on instruments that occasionally give readings the engineers prefer to file privately. The mountain is rising, and as it rises, the rules of the place are, very gradually, becoming more local. Gravity, for instance, is slightly less certain on the upper slopes than on the lower ones. A dropped stone, from the same height, falls a slightly different distance depending on the day, and the day of the week matters, and the phase of the moon, and whether there is a particular cloud in a particular place above the foundry. The miners have learned to compensate. They have learned to step lightly on the upper platforms. They have learned not to drop things from a great height, because the things do not always land where they should. The water in the gutters runs uphill, sometimes, in the upper streets, and the townspeople have learned to build the gutters accordingly, and they do not discuss this in front of visitors.

Time, also, is slightly negotiable in the upper town. Clocks in the same room disagree by small, persistent amounts. A person who walks from the lower town to the upper town and back again will find, on a reliable clock, that slightly more time has passed than the walk could account for, or slightly less, depending on the day. A person who sleeps in the upper town sometimes wakes having dreamed a longer night than the night was. The foundry foremen have made a small adjustment for this in the shift schedules. The adjustment is not spoken of.

The town is patient. The town endures. The town pours its smoke into the cloud. The cog rail grinds up and down the steep section. The bells of the temple ring at the canonical hours, and the sound of them is taken up by the cloud and carried away in long, slow, damp spirals. The schoolchildren grow up and go into the mine, or go down the cog rail to the cities below and do not return. The lichen spreads, degree by degree, across the raw stone. The mountain rises, degree by degree, into its own weather. The animals watch, from the cliffs, with their too-many eyes or their correctly-numbered eyes, and they wait, because they have always waited, because the mountain is young and the rules are new and there is time.
