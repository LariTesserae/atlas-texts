# The Spire of Vesh-Khora

You find it the way you find most things at this altitude — by accident, by wrong turn, by stepping past the cairn that wasn't a cairn and through the gap that wasn't a gap.

The spire rises from a plateau of shattered obsidian and pale pumice, and at first it doesn't look built. It looks *forced* — a column of dark glass some three hundred meters tall, fluted and ridged along its length, still sharp-edged enough to catch light on seams that no wind has had time to dull. Geologically it is young. The plateau around it remembers its birth in radiating fracture lines and a skirt of fused rubble. There is no lichen here, no moss, no insect-track or birdsong — only the mineral afterbirth of something that pushed upward through the crust in a slow, violent single act and then stopped. The spire is the color of dried blood mixed with ink, and where the sun touches it, the surface answers in oily violet sheens. The sun, when it comes, is a thing you receive through a haze: dim, coppery, thickened by particulates that never quite settle at this height. Most of the day is a kind of low warm twilight.

The air smells of hot stone and sulfur. The wind is constant but thin. Your ears register pressure more than sound — a faint insistent hum in the sinuses that you eventually stop noticing.

The plateau is empty. Almost theatrically so. For kilometers in every direction there is nothing but broken ground and the long slow slopes falling away toward a horizon lost in brown haze. The spire is the only vertical thing. If you circled it, you would find that its base is not quite circular — it is a heptagon, with seven faces meeting at seven seams, and each seam is a fissure you could not fit your hand into but a shadow could live in. This matters later.

The entrance is on the seventh face, near the top of a sharp angled buttress. There is no door. There is a vertical seam that is slightly darker than the others, and beside it, a plate of the same obsidian set flush with the wall. No handle, no keyhole, no inscription. If you put your palm against it, the plate warms — not from your body heat, from the other side. A faint vibration passes through your wrist bones. Then the seam exhales, a breath of warmer air that smells of copper and something sweet and old, and widens by exactly the width of a shoulder.

You step through sideways, and the world contracts.

---

What you enter is not a building in any sense a builder would recognize. It is a *hollowing*. The spire is not solid. From the inside, it is a stack of chambers — irregular, overlapping, sharing walls of varying thickness — that someone or something has hollowed out of the volcanic column over what must have been centuries. There is no staircase. There is no elevator. There are *transits*: smooth oblong openings in the floors and ceilings of the chambers, slightly wider than a person lying down, through which you descend by stepping off and waiting. You fall, slowly, the way a leaf falls in a dream. The transit takes you down through a meter of rock, or ten, or a hundred — you cannot tell — and sets you in the next chamber with a soft exhalation of air against the soles of your feet. The first time it is terrifying. The seventh time it is merely disorienting. After that you forget you are not walking.

The chambers are small. That is the first thing that strikes you. After the vastness of the plateau, the emptiness of the sky, the indifferent sweep of the horizon, you expected something commensurate. Instead the rooms are intimate — the size of a generous bedroom, or a small chapel, or a long narrow hall barely wide enough for two people to pass. Some are squat. Some are tall as cathedral naves. None are large. The hidden city, if city it is, has been built *for pocket dimensions*: it has the granularity of a body, an interior, a place where the inhabitants' elbows might brush the walls.

The walls are not walls. They are the inside of the spire itself, worked. You can see the strata — dark and lighter bands where different eruptions layered and cooled — but the surface has been finished, or grown, or both. In some chambers the walls are smooth as blown glass and warm to the touch, holding the heat of the stone in a way that suggests insulation, or intervention. In others they are textured, faceted, carved into shallow repeating geometries that are not quite fractal and not quite script and not quite the cross-section of something organic. The patterns do not repeat exactly between chambers. Each room has its own.

The light is low. This is not a place that admits the sun. What little enters filters down through the transits from somewhere above and arrives as a thin orange dust, hardly enough to read by. The chambers make their own illumination, and they make it *quietly*. Embedded in the walls — or growing from them, or condensing out of them — there are slow pale nodes. They glow. That is all one can say about them. They do not flicker, they do not pulse, they do not warm the air noticeably beyond what the stone itself contributes. They put out a dim steady luminescence the color of weak tea, sufficient to see the shape of the next doorway, the curve of the next transit, the texture of the floor. In some chambers there are many of them, set in clusters like fruit on a branch; in others there is only one, a single source in the center of the ceiling, casting long soft shadows. After a while you stop being able to look directly at them. Your eye slides off them the way it slides off a thing in a dream.

---

There is no furniture in any human sense. There are *forms* — ledges at the right height for sitting, hollows at the right depth for sleeping, vertical grooves in some walls that might be for storing long objects or might be decorative. There are basins, shallow, in many rooms. Some are dry. Some are not. This is where you find the water.

It comes from the rock itself. The plateau is dry. The air is dry. But deep in the spire, where the temperature differential between the hot interior and the cooler outer faces draws moisture through the stone by some mechanism — capillary, condensation, or something more particular — water appears. It beads on certain walls in the early hours, runs down into the basins, sits there cool and perfectly clear. There is no source to find, no spring to follow. You simply notice, after some hours, that the basin in the corner is half full. The water tastes of nothing. It tastes of stone, which is to say of nothing. You drink it because you must, and afterward you feel no different, which is its own kind of unsettling.

Some chambers are damper than others. The lower you go — and the transits will take you as deep as you are willing to step off into — the wetter it becomes. There are rooms near the bottom where the walls weep constantly, and the floor is a shallow film of water over smooth glass, and the air is close and heavy and warm. There are rooms where the water has pooled into actual pools, knee-deep in places, dark, still, reflecting the dim nodes back at themselves. There are rooms where, if you sit long enough, you can hear water moving somewhere behind the walls, a faint liquid murmur, the digestive sound of the spire processing what little moisture the air gives it. None of the water moves quickly. None of it falls. It migrates. It accumulates. It waits.

There are no plants. There are no animals. There are no bodies, no bones, no signs that anything with a face has lived here for a long time. But the basins have been kept. The ledges have been worn, microscopically, at their edges. The transits are smoothed where thousands of feet must have passed through. *Someone* has been here. *Someone* shaped this place. *Someone* is no longer here, or is here in a way that does not register as presence.

---

The technology is not on display. This is one of the things that makes it hard to believe this is a city at all. There is no machinery visible. There are no wires, no pipes, no conduits, no screens, no buttons. The transits work without apparatus you can identify. The lights work without wiring you can trace. The water moves without plumbing you can find. The temperature is bearable — warm but not punishing, even in the lower chambers where the stone should be cooking — and you cannot say why.

It is not that the engineering is hidden. It is that the engineering has been *integrated*. The walls are the wires. The stone is the insulator. The spire itself is the machine. Whatever was built here was built into the substrate, the way a nervous system is built into a body, and what remains is the body without the creature — all the quiet persistence of a system still running long after its operator has stepped out. The pale nodes do not know they are lit. The transits do not know they are carrying you. The water does not know it is being delivered.

And yet, in a few chambers, you find things that resist this reading. In one — a tall narrow room near the middle of the stack — there is a shelf of the same glass as the walls, and on it, arranged with no dust between them (the air here is too dry for dust), a row of objects. They are small. They are not made of the local stone. One is a sphere of something metallic and heavy, warm to the touch, that rings when you tap it with a fingernail — a pure sustained note that lasts too long for the size of the object. One is a flat disc, also metallic, marked with concentric circles that you realize, after staring for some minutes, are not circles at all but spirals that almost close. One is a piece of something fibrous and pale, the only non-mineral substance you have found in the spire, and it is dry and light and inexplicable. There are seven objects. The chamber has seven sides. You pick up the sphere and put it down. You pick it up again. It is heavier than it should be, and warmer than the room, and the note it rings is in a key that makes your back teeth ache.

You do not know what they are. You do not know who put them there. You do not know if you are meant to take one, or meant to leave them, or meant to have arrived at all.

---

The place is active. You learn this not by seeing anything move — almost nothing moves here — but by *listening*. Once you have been in the spire long enough, the silence stops being silence. It becomes a low ongoing texture, the sound of a system breathing. The transits, when they catch you, make a soft pop of pressure equalization. The walls tick faintly as they cool and warm with the small thermal cycles of the day above — a sound like a fingernail on a porcelain rim, very far away. Somewhere deep below, there is a rumble that comes at irregular intervals: the slow grinding of magma in a chamber beneath you that has not yet finished cooling. It is not a sound that frightens once you understand its scale. It is the sound of something the size of a small mountain thinking about something the size of a small mountain.

There is, in one chamber near the bottom, a slow bright thread of something — heat, light, plasma, you cannot tell — that climbs a wall in a slow vertical spiral. It takes about eleven minutes to complete one circuit of the room. When it reaches the ceiling it disappears and reappears at the floor and begins again. You watch it for a long time. It does not vary. It does not notice you.

There is, in another chamber, a faint smell of ozone, and the hairs on your forearms rise when you enter, and fall when you leave. There is no visible source. The chamber is empty except for its single node and a long smooth bench of black glass.

There is, in the lowest chamber you can reach, a sound that is almost certainly water. It is the sound of a slow current, moving somewhere behind a wall you cannot open, carrying something somewhere you cannot follow. You sit on the floor with your hand against the warm glass and listen to it for what feels like a long time. The node above you dims, very slowly, and you realize that somewhere above the plateau the sun has set, and the temperature differential has shifted, and the spire is adjusting. The walls tick. The current murmurs. The node brightens again, very slowly, in a different color — cooler, bluer — and you understand that this place does not sleep because it has never been awake. It merely *is*. It responds. It persists.

---

When you leave, eventually, through the same seam on the seventh face, the wind hits you like a door. The light is enormous after the dim chambers. The plateau stretches in every direction, broken and gleaming. The spire stands behind you, sharp and dark, and you realize you cannot tell which seams you came through. They all look the same. You walk for half a day before you stop being able to see it at all, and then it is only a needle on the horizon, and then it is gone, and then you are alone with the broken ground and the copper haze and the thin insistent hum in your sinuses.

You find, later, in the pocket of your coat, a small smooth stone you do not remember picking up. It is warm. It is the color of dried blood. When you tap it against your thumbnail it does not ring.

You keep it anyway.
