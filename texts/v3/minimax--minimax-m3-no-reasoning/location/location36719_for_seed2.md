# The Cisterns of Veritable Deep

The place begins as a rumor of pressure and becomes a continent of it.

You arrive through a vertical shaft — fourteen meters across, lined with hexagonal basalt-colored ceramic tiles, each one stamped with a serial number and the date of its firing. The shaft goes down further than the light from the surface carries, but you know it opens, because you can hear the breathing of the open volume below: a low, tidal sound that is partly mechanical and partly the resonance of an enormous contained space finding its equilibrium with itself.

## The Geometry

The Cisterns are built. Everything here is built. There is no ground to fall through, no bedrock to call home — only structure, and the water held inside it.

The space is a single honeycomb of colossal chambers, each one a polyhedron roughly the size of a small country. The walls between them are perforated by thousands of arches at varying heights, so that when you fly (and you fly here, because the platforms are too far apart to walk between, and the water is too cold and too deep to safely swim across the long ways) you pass through cathedral mouths of stressed concrete, hung with conduits and inspection gantries, and come out into the next vast cell, and the next.

Each chamber has its own character because each chamber has its own purpose, though they all share the water. The water is everywhere. It is the air of this place. It is the medium of weather, the carrier of sound, the substrate in which everything happens. You breathe it through your regulator and your ribs feel the pressure differential — two atmospheres at the upper platforms, climbing to six or seven in the deep cells where the pressure-bearing walls thicken and the gantry ladders are shorter because almost no one goes down there.

The light is normal, which is to say it has been engineered to feel normal to the animals evolved here. Phosphor strips are inset along every major arch and platform edge, running in long unbroken lines into the apparent distance. Their color is calibrated — a careful 4500 kelvin, just slightly cool, slightly blue, the way deep-water daylight would be if daylight could reach this deep. In the upper chambers the strips are bright and the water carries their glow in long pale shafts; in the lower chambers the strips are dimmer and the water turns the color of old glass, and things with eyes live there that have eyes for that color.

## The Water

It moves. That is the first thing you must understand.

The Cisterns are not still. Pumps the size of buildings push columns of water through vertical risers at the chamber centers; the risers emerge from each chamber floor and ascend through the perforated wall-arches into the chamber above, where they disgorge and the water falls in slow, massive curtains back across the platforms and the open volume, and gathers again, and is taken up by the next riser, and lifted again. The whole structure is a slow pump, lifting water against the gradient of itself, perpetually.

This is the weather.

There are currents, then, as real as any ocean gyre — predictable, named, charted on the laminated plastic sheets the maintenance crews carry. The **Upward** runs through every riser column and is cold and steady. The **Sheeted** falls across the open chambers and is loud, very loud, a continuous roar that you stop hearing after the first hour and then notice, with a small shock, when you shut your helmet. The **Counter** runs along the chamber floors in the direction opposite to the planet's rotation (which matters, because the rotation gives the water just enough Coriolis nudge to make the floor-currents shear against the riser columns and produce eddies that the night-shift crews learn to read like a farmer reads wind). The **Lee** is the slow dead drift in the lee of every large structure — behind every platform, behind every riser collar, behind every wall — and in the Lee the small animals gather.

You do not swim across the open chambers. The Sheeted current would pin you against a wall. The Upward would lift you helplessly toward the perforated arch above, where you might be pushed through into the next chamber at the wrong depth and have to climb back down a hundred meters of wet ladder in the dark. You travel on the gantries, or in the pressurized trams that run on rails bolted to the walls of the major arches.

## The Platforms

They are islands.

Each chamber has platforms. Some are operational — pumping stations, regulator outposts, sensor clusters bristling with antennae that trail into the water like catwhiskers. Some are residential — clusters of pressurized habitat modules bolted together in arrangements that look, from a distance, like clumps of barnacles on a sunken piling. Some are industrial — smelters, refineries, workshops where the water is filtered and re-introduced and where the output of the place is processed for shipment up the central shaft, where the great hoists take it to the surface and the surface ships take it away.

The platforms are made of things that do not corrode. Polymer concrete. Ceramic steel. Glass. They have edges, sharp in the young way of recent construction, and corners that have not yet been worn round by the patient chemistry of the water. Everything here is young. The Cisterns were built in the last century and a half, and they have not yet had time to forget their blueprints. A geologist of surfaces, set down on a platform deck, would read it as recently-emerged land: sharp edges, fresh joints, the marks of forms still visible in the cured concrete like the seams of an unfingered fruit.

And yet there is weathering. The phosphor strips accumulate a film of biology — a felt of microorganisms that the maintenance crews scrape off on rotation, leaving paler rectangles where the light has not yet dimmed. The platform undersides gather silt in drifts that resemble, if you squint, the lee-side snow of a young mountain ridge. Conduits hum with the vibration of pumps and grow warm, and warmth in this water is a kind of mineral, something that deposits.

## The Fauna

There are fish. There are fish the way a city has pigeons.

The Cisterns are stocked, and also wild. The stocked ones are the working fauna — silvery, dense-fleshed, herded through the chambers in long patient ribbons by the acoustic fences the operators tune like instruments. They eat what the platforms excrete and the filters cannot catch; they are, in a real sense, the final stage of the water treatment. They are harvested on rotation, drawn up through hoists in the residential platforms, processed, and sent up the shaft. The wild ones are smaller, weirder, escaped or feral, and they live in the Lee drifts and along the gantry undersides and in the warm-water plumes above the active smelters. They are not harvested. They are studied.

There are other things.

A pale, slow animal, the size of a large dog, with too many fins and a face that suggests a thoughtful herbivore, browses the platform undersides in the deeper chambers. It was not engineered. It arrived as food stock, once, and something in its lineage escaped and adapted and found a niche in the dark. The crews call it a **mower**. It is unafraid of the lights. It is unafraid of the gantries. It will look at you, if you stop on a ladder above it, with an expression that is hard to describe as anything other than patient.

Smaller things glimmer in the phosphor-lit water — copepods, larval forms, the endless small drift that makes the beams visible when you look along them. Above the active risers, where the Upward current carries warm effluent toward the next chamber, these small things bloom in pale clouds that the fish follow, and the mowers follow the fish, and the small predators follow the mowers, and the whole lit column is a slow, vertical, perfectly legible food chain.

## The Sound

It is loud and it is constant and you learn to subtract it.

The pumps. The risers. The Sheeted falls, each one a continuous thunder that does not crest or break. The tram rails, which sing when the trams pass, a note that dopples as they come and go. The hydraulic hiss of the habitat modules as they equalize. The far-off, deep, structural groan of the pressure-bearing walls as the water weight shifts slightly with the planet's tidal bulge — a note so low it is felt in the platform deck more than heard, but the crews know its schedule by heart and complain when it is late.

In the residential platforms, inside the modules, the sound is dampened. There is a hush. People speak to each other in low voices, partly because they have lived here a long time and partly because they are, like all the fauna, products of their medium.

## The People

There are not many. The density of the place is its most striking feature after its construction. For all the volume — for all the chambers, for all the cathedral arches, for all the water — there are only a few thousand people in the Cisterns at any given time. They live in clusters around the working platforms. They ride the trams. They tend the pumps. They watch the gauges. They eat the fish. They send reports up the shaft. They receive, in return, what the surface sends down: equipment, food they cannot grow, news, replacements.

They are quiet, mostly. The place does not require loudness from them. They have small ceremonies — a name for the day a new platform is commissioned, a quiet drink when a riser pump is replaced, the careful observation of the slow procession of the planet's seasons, which manifest here as a faint change in the water temperature and a corresponding migration of the fish stocks between chambers.

## The Vastness

You can stand on a gantry in the central chamber — the largest, the oldest, the one called, simply, **the Main** — and look out into the water and see almost nothing.

The phosphor strips recede. The platforms diminish. The arches above and the arches below become suggestions, then absences. The water, lit from a thousand directions and yet fundamentally dim, takes on the quality of an atmosphere, and the space becomes, for a person standing on a small catwalk in a helmet, indistinguishable from standing on a high ridge on a clouded night and looking out into the weather.

You feel, then, the scale of the place. You feel that you are inside something that has its own physics. The water has weight here that it would not have on the surface. The pumps move volume that would be a river, and there are hundreds of them. The walls hold back pressure that would, if the structure failed, become a wave moving at the speed of sound through a habitat module.

It is vast. It is built. It is full of water and fish and the patient hum of industry. It is young and has not yet been worn smooth. It is, in its way, a country — the only country many of its inhabitants have known, a country whose climate is pumped and whose weather is engineered and whose seasons are the slow turning of a pump cycle and whose fauna are partly designed and partly wild and whose geology is the inspection log.

You stand on the gantry. The Sheeted current falls past you, half a kilometer away, a pale moving wall of water that has been falling, in this chamber, since the chamber was built. A mower browses, far below, on the underside of a platform you cannot see the top of. The fish drift in their long silvery herds, tracing the currents like cursive writing.

You are, you realize, inside a sentence the surface has written.

It is a long sentence. It has not finished.
