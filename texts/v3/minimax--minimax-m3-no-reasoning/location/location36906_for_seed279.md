# The Dripping Quarter

The valley is a low thing, more a fold than a valley, where the ground slopes inward from three sides and then simply stops being interested in altitude. What rises around it is not so much mountain as memory of mountain — pale stone teeth and shoulders that look like they were quarried out of existence rather than worn down. The rock is young. It has the unfinished look of things that have not yet decided what kind of stone they want to be: knapped, conchoidal, breaking in sharp arcs that catch what little light there is and throw it back in quick, flinty winks. Basalt in places, mostly, and other things that do not have names because nobody has been here long enough to name them.

Water has been here longer than anyone. That is the first fact.

It arrives in every conceivable form. It condenses on the inside of your mouth if you stand still long enough. It beads on the rocks and crawls down them in threads so thin they appear motionless until you watch for a minute and see they have moved. It stands in shallow pans between the folds, dark and still, and it falls, sometimes, in a fine, near-horizontal rain that doesn't seem to come from anywhere. The air is so wet that breathing feels like drinking. Every surface weeps. The sound of it — and the sound is never absent — is the second fact.

There is a roaring, low and constant, that seems to come from the stone itself. It is the sound of water entering rock. Every crack in every surface is a pipe, and through each pipe something is being pushed or pulled. The valley is a hydraulic organ. There are places where the water emerges from the rock face in fans, in sheets, in single trembling threads; places where it wells up from below through gravel that shifts underfoot like something breathing; places where it falls into holes and you can hear it falling for a long time after it has disappeared. The loudness is not a violent loudness. It is the loudness of many small insistences summing into something you cannot tune out.

It is cool. Not cold — there is no bite to it — but the kind of cool that makes you aware that the air has weight, that the water in it has been waiting a long time to become weather. Fog moves through the valley in slow lobes, rarely more than waist-high, and where it touches the warmer rock it curls and leaves a residue. Things look stained. Everything is stained. The stones have dark shoulders. The hollows are darker still. The light, what there is, comes from a sky that is overcast in a permanent, uncommitted way — neither storm nor clearing, just a low, pearly ceiling that admits a flat silver luminance without ever quite committing to a direction. Shadows are short and soft. You cannot tell what time it is.

In this wet, cool, dim, loud, slow place, nothing grows. Or rather — almost nothing. What grows does not look like growth as most worlds would understand it. There are crusts. There are patches of something that might be mineral precipitate or might be a kind of life so slow it has not declared itself: a thin, ochre, leathery skin that follows the contours of the rocks near the water lines, darker where the water is, paler higher up. It does not move. It is, however, dense. The valley is full of presences. The presences are mostly stone.

The stone is dense. The valley is dense with stone.

It is a place of accumulations. Boulders lie in piles and drifts the way snow lies in drifts in other places, except these boulders are sharp and wet and they clink faintly when the temperature shifts and they expand or contract by some amount too small to feel but audible if you put your ear to them. There are stacked stones, tall thin stacks, some of them balanced with an implausible delicacy — cairns, almost, but not made by anyone, made by the slow, patient subtraction of softer stone around harder cores. There are pavements of fitted slabs that are not pavements. There are walls, low and partial, that do not connect to anything: straight runs of fitted stone a few meters long, ending in raw rock. There are pillars standing alone in the pans of water, waist-high, that look carved but are not carved. There are rectangular holes cut into the rock faces that go down to dark water and you cannot see the bottom.

These are the scattered marks. They suggest intention. They do not confirm it. There is no tool-markshine, no wheel-ruts, no glass, no metal. Nothing has been smelted. Nothing has been fired, or if it has, the firing was not a human firing. The shapes are too regular for wind and water and too irregular for hands. They sit in the landscape like questions no one is answering.

The fauna, what there is of it, is sparse and furtive. Something moves in the water — a shape, a flex, gone. Something small and pale clings to the underside of an overhang and you do not see it until it has stopped moving. There are calls, occasionally, that sound like a single struck note held too long, coming from directions you cannot triangulate because the wet air bends the sound and the falling water smears it. The valley is loud with its own processes and the fauna has learned to be brief about its existence. You might go an hour without seeing a living thing. You would not go an hour without hearing something move.

The slowness is everywhere. The weathering is patient. The crusts creep outward at rates you could not measure in a human lifetime. The water rearranges the gravel in the pans, but slowly, in winter-to-summer shifts, and the difference between this visit and the last visit is something you would only know if you had been coming for decades. The stacks of stone are not going to fall today. The fog is not going to lift. The roar is not going to stop.

And yet it is not dead. The wetness forbids death. Things are too busy becoming other things. The basalt weeps and what weeps from it is not basalt anymore. The ochre skins spread. The water carries dissolved stone from the high places and lays it down in the low places in thin, pale rims that mark the old high-water lines like the rings inside a tree. The valley is young and it is busy. The strange thing about it is not that it is empty of life as you know it. The strange thing is that it is so full of itself.

You stand in it. Your boots darken. The sound fills the hollow of your skull. You cannot see far. The rocks stare back with their wet, mineral patience. Somewhere, very far up, there is a sky, but the valley does not seem to be in a hurry to admit it.

You stay a while.

The valley does not notice.
