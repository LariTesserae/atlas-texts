# The Stone Flats of Hevelkar

## The Shape of the Place

The plateau has not been here long. In the way that mountains think — across ten thousand winters — this land is almost newborn, pushed up in hard lurches by something beneath it that will not be quiet. The rock is the color of unripe wheat, with bands of iron-red where mineral water has bled through and dried. It is not worn soft. The edges are sharp. A boot finds no friendly purchase here; the stones split under weight rather than yield, and the air carries that fine pale grit that gets into the lungs and makes the spitters of Hevelkar common on the lower streets.

To the north the land drops. Not gently — the plateau ends in a wall, a true wall, two hundred spans of near-vertical basalt that the first inhabitants were too sensible to build their city directly on top of. They built it one league back, where the basalt shelf shivers into something like foothills. You can stand at the city's edge and look north into the Cold Reach, the valley that opens like a held breath. Mist lives down there. You cannot see the bottom of it on most mornings.

To the east the land goes on. That is the trouble of the place: it goes on, and on, and on. There is no sea within walking distance. There is no forest worth the name. There is stone, and wind, and the occasional green stain of lichen that proves water remembers this country sometimes. A traveler bound east will not find a tree taller than a man for nine days' hard march. They say the eastern wastes end eventually. The citizens of Hevelkar do not speak of this with any enthusiasm.

## The Sky and Its Weathers

The sky is enormous and very near. At twenty-eight hundred spans the air is thin enough that a foreigner gets a headache on the second day and a nosebleed on the third, but the sky pays for it. Clouds do not skim — they wheel. You can watch a storm take an hour to assemble itself over the eastern flats, the way you might watch a flower open in a sunbeam, except the flower is the color of soot and smells of ozone and means you ought to be indoors.

Rain comes in two flavors. There is the *cool rain* of spring and autumn, which falls for a day at a time and leaves the city washed and glistening, and the *hard rain* of late summer, which falls in sheets so heavy the streets run with pale mud and the gutters sing. Between these, weeks can pass where the sky is the color of old bone and nothing falls at all. Condensation gathers in the mornings on the east-facing stones, so that the whole city weeps before the sun finds it, and the dripping down the basalt walls makes a sound like distant applause.

In winter the rains stop and the *dust* comes. This is the country at its most patient and most cruel. The dust is iron-fine, lifted from the bare flats east of the city by a steady, thin wind that blows from late December until the first spring storm. It powders the snow, gets into the stew, makes children cough, makes the old die. People speak of winter in the tone that sailors speak of the sea.

## The City

Hevelkar sits on the basalt shelf like a man sitting on a fence — not committed, not at home. The city is built of the only material the plateau provides in quantity, which is stone, and of the only material the city bothers to import, which is timber from the river valleys six days west. The stone is pale, almost cream in the morning light, and the timber is dark, almost black where it has been tarred against the wet. From a distance, looking down from the inner plateau, Hevelkar appears as a confusion of pale cubes stitched together with dark lines — a child's drawing of a city, done by a child who has heard cities described but never seen one.

There are perhaps nine thousand souls inside the walls. The walls themselves are a joke at ground level — twelve spans of unmortared basalt, dry-stacked by the founders and never properly maintained, leaning outward in places where the frost has heaved them. They are a serious wall above, where a man on the parapet can be seen against the sky from a quarter-league off, and the wind at the top is strong enough to lean into. They were built by people who had recently been raided and meant to be built again, and they have been, in patches, four times.

The streets are narrow because the wind is a consideration, and because stone does not lend itself to the carving of avenues. They switch back on themselves without logic. A stranger who has been given directions will, on the third turn, give up and simply follow the smell of cooking bread toward whatever district holds the bakers. The city is dense the way a fist is dense — packed, layered, every gap filled. Houses stack on houses. A family will occupy the ground floor while another family occupies the floor built onto their roof twenty years later, and the only way up is a ladder that lives inside the lower house. Privacy is a small terrace where the laundry dries in moments of weak sun.

There is no plumbing. There are channels cut into certain streets where the occasional rain runs, and there are cisterns in every courtyard that the women of the house tend with religious attention, and there are the public wells — six of them, deep and stone-lined, drawn by hand-winch. In a bad year the wells drop low enough that you can hear them breathe. In a good year they brim, and the children are sent down to clean them, and the city smells of wet limestone for a week.

The market is in the lower town, where the basalt shelf begins its drop. It is a flat space, paved where it can be, and crowded on the six days of every nine when the caravans come in from the west. The caravans bring timber, dried fish, salt, the small tough apples of the river valleys, the wool of the high goats. They take away, in exchange, the only thing the plateau produces in marketable quantity, which is the *white stone* — that pale, easily-cut basalt that masons in the lowlands will pay well for, because it holds a carved edge and weathers clean.

## The Stone Beneath

The basalt is young stone. You can see it in the way it splits — conchoidal fractures, sharp edges, the snap of fresh glass. It rings when you strike it, which is why the masons love it and the quarrymen go deaf young. The basalt is shot through with veins of something paler — calcite, the masons call it, though the old men call it *bone-vein* — and these veins the carvers follow when they make the small religious figures that are sold to pilgrims. The figures are crudely done by the standards of any city with a hundred years of artistic tradition behind it. They have the wide eyes and short limbs of folk art. They sell because the pilgrims believe the stone itself is holy, which it may be.

Beneath the basalt, lower than any well has reached, there is water. You know this because it sometimes comes up. There are places on the eastern flats where the ground is permanently dark, where the moss grows in a color almost black, and where a man who digs for an afternoon will hit a seep and fill two skins before sunset. These are the *wet places*, and they are sacred in the local religion, which holds that the plateau is the back of a sleeping god whose dreams are the only water the country has. The herders who take the goats out to the flats in summer know the wet places by heart. They do not speak of them to strangers.

## The Animals

The animals of Hevelkar are not many in kind, but very many in number. The high goats are everywhere — small, mean, sure-footed creatures the color of the stone they stand on, who crop the sparse grass of the flats and produce a milk that is thick and a meat that is gamy. They are the wealth of the place. A man who owns forty goats is a man of substance; a man who owns four hundred is the kind of man who sits on the Council.

There are also the *carreiros* — the burden lizards, long-bodied, low-slung, with feet that grip the stone. They are not quite reptiles; they have a fine fur, and they bear the cold better than any beast of burden ought to. They pull the carts of white stone down to the river for loading onto the barges. They can be eaten in a famine, and they have been.

In the sky there are the white hawks, which are sacred to the city and untouchable by law. They hunt the small rodents that live in the cracks of the basalt — the *flat mice*, ugly things, useful, infinitely numerous. The hawks wheel over the flats in such numbers that their shadows cross the streets at certain hours, and the old folk say this is the city being blessed.

In the lower town there are pigeons, and rats, and dogs. The dogs are mongrel, hard to kill, friendly in the way that dogs are friendly when they have decided you are not going to beat them. They sleep in the sun when there is sun. They sleep in the cold when there is not.

## The Sounds

Hevelkar is mostly quiet. The wind is the constant sound — a low, thoughtful sound at the city's edge, a keening through the narrow streets that makes conversation in the open air difficult on a bad day. Inside the houses, behind three walls of stone, the city is nearly silent. You can hear your own fire. You can hear the woman upstairs hanging her washing on the line.

In the morning there are the well-wheels, six of them turning at six different speeds, and the calls of the water-sellers, and the small bells of the goats being taken out. In the afternoon there is the ring of the masons' tools, and the shouts of the cartwrights, and the laughter of children in the one square that catches the sun. In the evening there is the smell of cooking and the sound of it — a stew of goat and root and dried apple, eaten late, eaten by all, eaten in the company of family and lodger and whoever has climbed up the ladder tonight. The clatter of bowls. The clink of the one cup that has traveled far and is shown on feast days.

At night, when the wind drops and the dust has not yet come, you can hear the white hawks on their roosts in the cliff walls. They make a sound like a creaking door. The old folk say this is the god turning in his sleep. The young folk say this is the hawks. Neither answer is wrong.

## The Long View

What is strange about Hevelkar is that it is still here. A city on a young plateau, in a country with too little water and too much wind, built of stone that was not made for building, ruled by a council of goat-keepers and stone-masons, served by a priesthood that worships the wet places — it ought not to persist. And yet it does. The caravans come. The wells refill in the wet years. The white stone is carried away, and the timber and the salt and the apples are carried in, and the children are born on the stone floors and grow up and learn to read by the light of a tallow dip and go out onto the flats with the goats, and the city turns.

It turns slowly. That is the nature of the place — patience. The plateau rose in a day, the masons say, but the city is being raised over generations, and will be raised over generations more. There is no rush. The god sleeps. The wells breathe. The wind blows, and the dust comes, and the rain comes, and the goats crop the thin grass, and the masons carve their wide-eyed figures, and the white hawks wheel over the basalt flats and cast their moving shadows on a city that has decided, against all argument, to continue.
