The valley sits low and cool, a shallow depression in older hills where the land has had time to settle into its own shape. The settlement — hamlet is the better word — has been here long enough that the boundaries between what was built and what grew have become suggestions rather than lines.

The damp comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is not raining. It has not rained in days, perhaps weeks, but the air holds its moisture close, and condensation gathers on every cool surface: the iron straps of the old machinery, the brickwork of the drying shed, the broad leaves of the burdock that crowds the lane. In the early morning the whole valley steams faintly, and by afternoon a slow drip has established itself from every overhang, every rusted gutter, every frond. There is a stream, or the memory of one — a bed of dark stones running down the center of the valley, holding shallow pools in its deeper bends, occasionally a continuous trickle, mostly a sequence of separated wet places. The mill wheel turns when there is water for it. The rest of the time it waits, patient as the rest of the valley, and the moss climbs its paddles in slow green increments.

The buildings are brick and timber and the dark iron of an earlier century, but the plants have made themselves architectural. Wisteria has colonized the north face of the press hall, its woody stems now as thick as a man's arm, woven into the brick until pulling them free would pull the brick with them. The roof of the boiler house is gone — taken down for salvage forty years ago, or so the records say — but the ivy has built a new one, layered thick enough to shed the heaviest rain, dense enough that from above the building would look like a low mound in the forest, indistinguishable from the surrounding trees except for the smokestack still rising from its center, bricked halfway up and capped with a rusted iron collar. Inside, the floor is a carpet of feathery woodfern, and the light comes down green and broken.

There are perhaps thirty structures in the valley, though only a dozen are recognizable as buildings from any distance. The rest have softened into the landscape — collapsed roofs, crumbled walls, and over all of it the patient work of vegetation, the patient work of time. The forest stands close on every side. It is not a dense forest; the trees are spaced with a kind of courtesy, birches and alders and the occasional oak, their canopies not quite touching, the understory sparse — woodruff, ferns, the dark glossy leaves of wild laurel. It feels like a forest that was once a wood, and before that a pasture, and before that something else again, each layer only partially erased.

The industry is small and quiet. The mill at the center of the valley is a paper mill, or was — the equipment inside is a confusion of rollers and vats and long drying cylinders, but the cylinders are rusted and the vats hold rainwater and oak leaves now. What runs is something else, something that has grown up around the bones of the old operation: a single steam engine, fired once a week from cordwood piled in the shed, drives a press that bales the dried burdock leaves into cubes for export to apothecaries further down the valley. There are two men who operate it. There is a woman who tends the drying racks. There is a boy who brings the cordwood. The rest of the labor is the forest's, which does what it has always done, slowly, without complaint.

The sounds are small and infrequent. The drip from the gutters. The low complaint of the steam engine on its weekly day. The clatter of the press. The creak of the drying racks as they swell with moisture or shrink with dryness. Birdsong is rare — a few thrushes in the upper branches, a wren that has nested in a gap in the boiler house wall — but the valley feels mostly unpeopled by anything with a voice. The wind moves through the leaves but the leaves are heavy with moisture and do not rattle as they might in drier air. The stream, when it is running, makes a sound like someone turning the pages of a large book far away.

The light is the light of a temperate valley in late summer — long and golden and slightly filtered, coming down through the canopy at an angle that picks out the wet brick and the wet leaves and leaves the deeper places in shadow. The mosses on the north faces of things glow almost greenly bright. The whole valley has a feeling of having been paused, of being held in a long moment between one process and another: the forest advancing, the industry receding, the buildings slowly being translated from one vocabulary into another.

There are rules here, though no one has written them down. The plants come back. The iron rusts. The stream runs when it rains. The steam engine fires on Thursdays. The wren nests where she nested last year. The two men who work the press are growing older. The boy who brings the cordwood is growing taller. The valley does not hurry. The valley has not hurried for a long time.
