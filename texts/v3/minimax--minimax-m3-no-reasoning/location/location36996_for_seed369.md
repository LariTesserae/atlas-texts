The place fits inside the curve of one hill, and the hill curves around it like a hand made of stone and moss and root. It is a bowl, a hidden concavity in the flank of something much larger — a sunken room, an inverted cathedral. The opening is to the sky, but the sky is rarely sky here; it is cloud, or the suggestion of cloud, a grey-white ceiling of moisture that filters the light into something softer than daylight and more definite than dusk.

The bowl is small. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other if the walls were not wet with a constant thin sheet of water, and if the stone did not want to land in the pool below. The walls are not sheer. They curve inward in tiers, the stone banded with the green of mosses, the rust of iron-bearing runoff, the soft black of liverworts and the things that grow where light never quite lands. The tiers have been made by water and by time, and they have been making themselves for longer than there is any way to count.

Water is the principal inhabitant, and it arrives by every possible route. From above: a fine rain, a mist, a constant almost-fall that doesn't quite reach the floor before being caught by the mosses and given back to the air. From the walls: sheets, threads, beads — sliding down the stone in slow longitudes, pooling briefly in cupped leaves, then continuing. From below: in the lowest part of the bowl, the ground is not ground at all but a shallow, warm pool of dark water that breathes. The water is warmer than the air should allow at this height. It smells of stone, of green things slowly becoming more green, of something faintly mineral and alive. If you put your hand in, you cannot tell whether the water is moving at all, or whether it is the surface of something patient and enormous pretending to be still.

A few trees grow here. They are small, but not in the way of young trees — small in the way of old trees that have decided not to get any taller. Their trunks are thick for their height, knotted, leaning at odd angles toward the opening above, as if they remember a sun that visits less and less. Their bark is furred with moss; their branches feathered with ferns and bromeliads in crowns that hold small private pools of water, each one its own little bowl. Between the trees, the floor is a weave: mosses, liverworts, a few flowering things with white or yellow blooms that never quite open, and a carpet of fallen leaves so old they have become a part of the floor themselves. The trees are not crowded. There is space between them, and the space is not empty but it is not full either — it is the space of a long pause.

There are no birds. The place is too high, too wet, too enclosed. Sometimes, very rarely, something moves in the upper branches — a small rustle, a quick weight, gone. Once, in a long while, a small grey creature with red eyes watches from a moss-shelf and is gone before you decide whether you saw it. The absence of animals is itself a kind of presence. The wet air holds the shapes of things that might be there and aren't.

The sound is the strangest part. Water, of course — falling, sliding, dripping, the slow cataract of it down the walls and into the pool, the soft percussion of drops in the standing water. But under that, or beside it, a low constant tone, almost too low to be a tone at all. The bowl hums. The stone itself seems to vibrate, or the water does, or the air. The trees lean in it. The mosses grow toward it. If you stand long enough, the hum gets into your chest and you stop noticing that you are hearing it and begin noticing that you are part of it.

Nothing is built. There is no path. There is no wall made by hands. But along one edge, in a place where the stone is flatter and the moss is shorter, there is a circle of small white pebbles, evenly spaced, all the same size, none of them native to the bowl. Beside them, half-eaten by moss, a single tool of pale green stone, its edge still sharp after however many wet years have passed over it. The bowl has not seen hands in a very long time, and the place is content with this. The pebbles and the tool remain. The moss advances on them one slow year at a time, and will, in its own unhurried way, finish them.

The light changes, but slowly, and the changes are mostly changes of cloud. The opening above is sometimes bright, sometimes dim, but inside the bowl the light is always the same — a flat, diffused green-grey that does not cast clear shadows. Things grow toward it but never reach it. The shadows that do exist are the shadows of water and mist, soft and doubled, as if the place existed in two versions at once and you were seeing the one that was slightly less real. Or slightly more. It is hard to tell, here, where the water has been running for so long that the stone itself has begun to remember the shape of water instead of the shape of stone.
