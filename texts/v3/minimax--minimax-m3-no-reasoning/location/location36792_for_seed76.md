# The Calcified Forest of Verreth

I want to begin with the light, because the light is the first thing that becomes you here.

It arrives from everywhere and nowhere in particular. The sky is not so much a sky as a condition — a high, washed-out gold that doesn't read as a color so much as a pressure on the back of your eyes. The sun, if there is a sun, has either set hours ago or never risen, but the brightness persists. You learn, after standing in it long enough, that the illumination comes from the air itself, from some thin scatter of mineral suspended at altitude — calcium, perhaps, or powdered silica — catching and re-radiating whatever weak source exists beyond the visible. The light is honest but not generous. It shows you everything and warms you not at all. The air sits around sixty degrees year-round, no matter what year-round means on a world that may not have years.

What it shows you is forest.

The forest is the puzzle, and it is the fact.

The trees of Verreth do not drink. There is no water. There has not been water — not in the way you mean the word — for as long as the oldest of them has been growing. The chemistry here is dry-chemistry: calcium carbonate pulled from the substrate by capillary action and root-pressure, deposited through living tissue the way a stalagmite is deposited by a drip. Each tree is, in some real sense, a stalagmite pretending to be a tree, or a tree that has learned the slow patience of stone and adopted the architecture of forest. They are pale. They are the color of bone left in sun, of parchment, of old teeth. Their bark — if it can be called bark — is layered and slightly translucent. Where a branch has fallen and shattered, the cross-section shows growth rings the way any tree would, but the rings are made of mineral, and the heartwood rings are denser than the outer ones, and if you tap a trunk with a knuckle it rings faintly, like a tuning fork or a porcelain cup.

They grow close enough to one another that you can sometimes see three or four in a frame, and far enough apart that you could lay a long building between any two. The spacing is not random. It is the spacing of competition, of root-systems that have already fought and resolved their negotiations underground. The soil between them is pale grey, almost white in the full light, and it is composed of a fine powder that gives slightly underfoot the way fresh snow gives, then hardens again as soon as pressure releases. Walking here leaves prints that vanish within an hour. Wind erases them faster than that. The wind is the only weather, and it is the main weather, and it is, in its way, a kind of slow water — a desiccating river that moves across the calcified groves in long, deliberate currents, carrying the powdered substrate in sheets like driven snow, except that what moves is not cold, and the grains are not flakes, and when the wind stops, the piles it has left behind are ridges that will, over decades, become the next stratum of forest floor.

The vegetation is, against all this, lush. This is the part that takes adjustment.

The canopies are full. They are full of growth that has no business being full in a place with no rain. The leaves — and they are leaves, they have veins and a suggestion of lamina and they rustle when the wind comes through them — are narrow and pale and thick, almost succulent, and they are held at angles that maximize the dim ambient light. They do not transpire in any way you would recognize; they exchange gas through pores on their underside in a process that takes a day for what a normal leaf does in an hour. Some of them are white. Some are the faintest yellow. Some are the color of weak tea, as if they have absorbed the long pigments of the soil. A few, in the deeper groves, are almost pink, and you do not know why. The understory is a low and crowded tangle of similar things — ferns that are not quite ferns, mosses that are not quite mosses, a groundcover of small succulents with leaves like fat teardrops, the color of milk glass. Everything is pale. Everything is thick-skinned. Everything is, in its own slow way, working.

There are no animals. That is almost true. There are, occasionally, things that move.

In the distance, you will see, perhaps once a day, a pale shape shifting between the trunks. It will be the size of a deer or the size of a small dog, and it will be the same color as the trees, and if you watch it for a long time you will see that it does not walk so much as resolve from one position to another, as if it has decided to be elsewhere and is now there, the way a thought is suddenly in your head. The locals — there are no locals, but if there were, they would tell you — say the forest is not empty, only patient. The shapes in the distance are not the forest's animals. They are the forest's moods. The forest, having no fauna of its own, has, in the long erosion of its own mineral dreaming, begun to produce approximations. They graze on nothing. They drink nothing. They are, possibly, the calcium doing something other than growing. They are, possibly, your own attention made visible, the way figures emerge in woodgrain when you stare at it long enough. They will not approach you. If you walk toward one, it will not flee; it will simply not be there when you arrive at the place you saw it. The forest does not permit closer acquaintance. It has been in its current state for a very long time, and it has, in that time, learned a kind of dignity.

The sounds are few and far between. There is the wind, which is the closest thing to a voice this place has, and it is not loud. There is the faint, almost subliminal hum of the calcified trunks under thermal stress — a tick and a low tone as the day shifts and the material expands or contracts. There is the occasional percussion of a branch giving way under its own accumulated weight and shattering when it meets the ground, the way a piece of dropped ceramic shatters, with that particular crisp and final sound. There is the soft crunch of your own feet in the powder, and the even softer sound of the powder resettling after you have passed. If you sit very still for a long time, you will hear what might be a kind of distant, slow percolation — water somewhere, far below, moving through stone. There is no water here, but the stone remembers water. The stone is, in some way you cannot verify, singing about it.

The elevation is genuine. You are not in mountains, exactly, but you are on a high plateau, and the forest is the surface of that plateau, and the plateau rises a long way above whatever the next thing down is. The air is thin enough that on a hard walk you can feel your pulse in your temples. The light is thin enough that the shadows are long even at noon, and the trunks cast stripes across the ground that move in a slow parallax as the hours pass. The horizon, when you find a clearing, is the upper edge of the forest on a ridge far away, and beyond that ridge there is only more sky, and beyond that sky, more light. The scale of the place is planetary. The forest, you realize after a day of walking and not finding an edge, is not a forest. It is a forest. The grammatical accident is the right one. There is no other word. You are standing in a single forest, a continental organism of mineral growth, and the weather it makes — the wind, the slow precipitation of dust, the rare and unaccountable fogs that rise in the small hours and vanish by midmorning — is a weather of its own devising, in a system of its own chemistry, in a silence that has not been broken in a very long time.

It is quiet. It is bright. It is the color of old teeth and new paper. It has been here longer than you have a unit for, and it will be here longer than you. It is, in a way you did not expect to find, lush. It grows, after its fashion, without cease. The wind moves. The shapes in the distance resolve and unresolve. The branches fall. The forest floor drinks the powder of the old ones and gives it back, slowly, to the new. There is no water. There is no fauna, exactly. There is no sound, exactly. There is a kind of fullness, though, that you did not bring with you and that you will, if you stay long enough, find difficult to leave.

The light continues. The forest continues. The patience continues.
