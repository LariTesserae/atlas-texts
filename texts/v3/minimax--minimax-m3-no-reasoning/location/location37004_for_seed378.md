# The Plateau of Dripping Iron

The settlement sits in the saddle of a long ridge that has been here longer than the air has been wet, which is to say it has always been wet, and the ridge has weathered it into its current form over an unfathomable span of patient geological time. The ridge is basalt, mostly — the dark grey kind that fractures into hexagonal columns when it cools, though here the cooling happened so long ago and the rain has come down so steadily for so long that the columns have softened at their tops into rounded knuckles of stone, like the spine of some buried animal. Iron has bled up through it from below. The exposed faces are streaked with rust — not the bright fresh rust of new metal, but a deep brick-orange that has been worked into the stone by centuries of water moving across it, painting the cliffs in long vertical stains that darken as they reach the ground where the runoff pools.

The heat is real. Even at this altitude the air sits close, heavy with vapor that never quite becomes rain. The sun is up there, pressing down through the thin mountain atmosphere, and the wet rocks throw the light back so that everything seems to glow from within its own surface. There is no green anywhere. The elevation is too high and the soil too thin and the heat too constant; nothing has ever put a root down in this place and won the argument. What grows here grows in other ways. Crusts of pale lichen — or something that does the work of lichen, spreading in starbursts across the wet stone, throwing out fruiting bodies that look like tiny white trumpets after a long hot day. Mats of something blue-black and gelatinous that colonize the seep-lines where water emerges from the rock face. Thread-fungi, hair-fine, that follow the moisture down into cracks and come up somewhere else. The world is alive, but it is alive in the manner of minerals and films, not in the manner of leaves.

The town itself is built across the saddle and down both shoulders of the ridge. It was not planned so much as it accumulated, layer on layer, each generation of builders using whatever the previous had left behind. The earliest structures are carved directly from the basalt — chambers and tunnels cut into the ridge itself, their entrances now partly buried under later construction. Around these, scaffolding of metal and stacked stone grew outward. Then came the industrial additions: the long low sheds with their riveted walls of iron plate, the smokestacks, the pipework that runs along the ground in taut parallel lines connecting one building to the next. The pipes weep constantly. Not because they are broken but because the humidity condenses on their surfaces faster than it can drip off, and so every horizontal run of pipe wears a fringe of clear droplets along its underside, and every vertical drop feeds a slow steady stream into the gutters below. The gutters feed cisterns. The cisterns feed the boilers. Nothing here is wasted; water is too valuable in a place where it falls from the air but never falls from the sky.

The factories are running. You can tell because the thin plumes that come from the stacks shift direction with the breeze and because a faint orange light leaks from the furnace doors at the ends of the long sheds. But the sound of them is almost nothing. The heat has thickened the air and the air eats noise. A hammer strike lands dull and short, swallowed within a few meters. The diesel engines of the ore-carts chug along the rails but their sound arrives flattened, the consonants of their running smoothed away into a low continuous hum that you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. From a hundred meters away the whole industrial district is silent. From a kilometer, in the right conditions of still air, you might catch the faintest suggestion of a pulse, the way you can sometimes feel the ocean from a great distance even when you cannot see it. It is not silence exactly, but it is the next thing to it.

The fauna has made its peace with this. The creatures that live here are mostly small and quiet and built for the heat. Long pale worms, blind, in the perpetually wet rock seams. Crabs without shells, smooth and grey, that pick their way across the hot stone in the early morning when the temperature has not yet climbed to its full brutality, and then retreat into cracks until evening. A kind of lizard, slate-colored, that basks on the iron-stained outcrops and is so still it is easy to mistake for a shaped stone until you notice the slow rise and fall of its breathing. There are flying things too — pale insects with veined wings that fold like fans when they land, that drift through the wet air in loose columns above the heated stone, and that make no sound at all. They eat the gelatinous films. The crabs eat the worms. The lizards eat the crabs. The insects die in their thousands against the lamp-panes at night and are swept up in the morning and fed to the boilers. The whole ecology of the place is short and quick and almost entirely invisible unless you learn to look for the right kind of motion.

Above the town, the ridge continues. It climbs toward a summit that is partly natural and partly cut, where the iron-stained stone has been carved into a working face and a cable-tram descends in long lazy arcs toward the settlement below. The ore that comes down is dark and dense and slightly damp. Below the town, the slope falls away into a basin where the runoff collects, and the basin is mostly flat and white where the water has evaporated and left its mineral cargo behind. Salt flats, mostly. They shine in the brightness. From the high points around the saddle you can see them stretching for a long way, and you can see the dark shapes of other settlements on other ridges, and other plumes of vapor rising from other stacks. The region is not empty but it is sparse. There is a great deal of space between presences. A man could walk a full day in any direction and not be forced to greet another human being, though he would hear, if he stopped and listened very carefully, the distant operating pulse of somewhere else that was doing what his town did.

The sky above is washed out, almost white near the horizon, deepening to a hard pale blue overhead. The sun is a bright disc that throws shadows so sharp and short at midday that they look like cuts in the ground. The light is everywhere. It catches the wet surfaces and returns them in tiny points of brilliance. It enters the eye from above and from below, from the rock faces and from the standing water and from the gleaming backs of the basking lizards, so that to be in this place is to be inside a kind of brilliance that has no single source. The shadows are very dark by contrast. When a cloud — they are rare, and thin — passes over the sun, the temperature drops a fraction and the light changes and the whole landscape seems to exhale.

At night the heat does not leave. The stone radiates what it has absorbed. The lizards come out in greater numbers. The flying insects thicken around the lamps. The wet rocks glisten. The light is the warm orange of furnace doors and lamp-pane and the rare pale glow of the moon through the high thin clouds. The settlement does not sleep exactly but it slows. The ore-carts stop running. The furnaces bank down to a low ember-glow that you can see from a great distance as a line of faint warmth against the dark. Somewhere a single hammer rings, briefly, on a distant anvil. Then it stops. Then there is nothing but the drip of water from the pipes, and the hiss of steam from a cracked joint, and the small wet sounds of the insects working the wet air.

The place is strange, but not in a way that announces itself. The strangeness is in the combination: an industrial town on a high bare ridge in the wet heat, with no plants but with many small creatures, with machinery that runs in near-silence, with water that comes from the air and not the sky, with light that arrives from every direction at once. A place that has settled into its own form over a long time and does not particularly wish to be elsewhere. A place where, if you stand still long enough, you begin to feel that you are being slowly absorbed into the mineral pattern of the ridge yourself, that the iron stain is creeping up the soles of your boots, that the wet air is settling into your lungs and beginning to be part of you.

It is not unfriendly. It is just complete.
