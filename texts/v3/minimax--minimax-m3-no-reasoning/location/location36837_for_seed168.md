# The Copper Terraces of Arrin Vael

The silence here is not the silence of death. It is the silence of industry that has not yet begun.

---

I. THE GROUND

You stand on a plateau three thousand meters above the world that does not exist below you. The rock beneath your boots is new. Geologically new — you can tell because the grain has not yet decided what it wants to be. It has the quality of something cooling: faintly warm, not dangerously so, but warm the way a cast-iron skillet retains its morning heat long after the stove has been turned off. When you kneel and press your palm to it, the warmth enters your wrist with a steadiness that feels almost intentional.

The stone is copper-colored. Not metallic-copper — oxidized, weathered, old-coin copper, the greenish-pink of a roof eave that has seen a century of rain. But there is no rain here. The color is intrinsic to the mineral itself: born of iron and sulfur and something else, something that does not have a name in the languages of the place, because the languages here have not yet been invented. The surface is a matte that catches light without returning it. At midday it glows like a spent ember.

The ground is terraced. Not by hand. By the slow, patient tectonics of a world still deciding how to fold itself. Each terrace rises half a meter above the one before, like the unfinished seating of an amphitheater built for an audience that will never arrive. The risers are sharp, almost knife-edged in places, but only where the rock is youngest. Older terraces have begun to round. The lowest, oldest, farthest from the rising center, have softened into undulations that — if you squint in the copper light — look almost like dunes frozen mid-motion.

There is no sand. There is no soil. There is only rock, cooling, in air that tastes of iron and distant ozone.

---

II. THE SKY AND THE LIGHT

The sky is the color of cream left too long on a counter. A luminous off-white, faintly yellowed, faintly bruised at the horizon with the same copper-pink as the ground. The sun — if there is a sun — sits high and unmoving. Not because the planet has stopped rotating. Because your eyes have stopped accepting the evidence of motion. The light does not change. The shadows do not move. The warmth is constant, patient, industrious in its constancy.

There is no blue. There has never been blue here. The atmosphere is thin — thinner than it should be for a world warm enough to support the growths that cover the terraces — and what atmosphere there is carries a faint haze that softens every edge. Distant terraces blur into the haze after about two kilometers. Beyond that, you can see the suggestion of a far ridge, and beyond that, nothing. Not darkness. Not infinity. Just the haze, continuing, level and featureless, all the way to where the world presumably curves beneath you.

The light arrives from everywhere and nowhere. Shadows are short and sharp, pooling at the base of each terrace riser like dark water that cannot decide whether to spill.

---

III. THE GROWTHS

This is the thing that does not belong. And yet it is everywhere.

From the cracks in the terraces — from the seams between cooling plates of stone — emerge plants. Not plants as you know them. They are upright, yes. They have structure, yes. But they are not green. They are the same copper-pink as the rock, shading in their densest portions to a deep, almost arterial red. They grow in clusters of three to seven stalks, each stalk rising from a single basal node that clings to the fissure with a grip that looks mechanical, like a flange bolted to stone.

The stalks are segmented. Each segment is a hollow cylinder of what appears to be a ceramic material — hard, smooth, faintly warm to the touch. They are not wood. They are not metal. They ring with a clear, bell-like tone when struck, and the sound carries across the empty terraces for an extraordinary distance before fading into the silence. When the wind moves them — and the wind moves them constantly, though you can barely hear it — the stalks clink against one another in soft, irregular patterns. Not quite music. Not quite language. Something in between that no one has yet learned to read.

The growths do not move. They do not sway. They stand rigid, their hollow segments tuned to slightly different pitches, and the wind plays them the way a child plays a row of bottles filled with water. Each cluster has its own chord. Each terrace has its own silence between chords.

There is no canopy. The stalks top out at about your waist, rarely your chest, and then stop. They do not branch. They do not flower. They do not seed. They simply stand, in their thousands, across the terraces, covering perhaps one percent of the visible surface — which is to say, dense enough to be unmistakable, sparse enough to leave the copper rock dominant. The arrangement looks deliberate. The arrangement is not deliberate. It is what happens when a wind-dispersed spore settles in a crack and finds the conditions favorable.

No one has determined what the conditions are. No one has determined what the spores are. They have not been found in the air. They are not in the soil, because there is no soil. They emerge from the rock itself, as though the rock is excreting them, as though they are a byproduct of the cooling, a sediment of biology precipitating out of geology.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. Nothing moves among the stalks except the wind, and the wind barely moves.

---

IV. THE SILENCE

This is the most disorienting feature of the place.

There is almost no sound. The atmosphere is too thin to carry much, and the surface is too regular to produce the kind of turbulence that would generate a roar or even a whisper. What sound exists is generated almost entirely by the growths: the faint, high, almost-not-there ringing of ceramic stalks touching in the wind. It is the kind of sound you might hear if you held a seashell to your ear, except that it is real, and it is coming from ten thousand tiny sources at once, and it never stops, and it never gets louder.

If you stand still for long enough — and there is nothing here to prevent you from standing still for as long as you like — the ringing begins to feel like a property of the place itself. Not a sound being made. A sound being emitted. The terraces ringing. The planet ringing. The way a bell rings after it has been struck, long after the clapper has come to rest.

The silence between the rings is not empty. It is full of the expectation of the next ring.

---

V. THE INDUSTRY

Here is the part that does not make sense.

The terraces are warm. Not from the sun — the sun's contribution is constant but diffuse, the same today as yesterday as a thousand years from now. The warmth comes from below. The rock is still cooling. Somewhere beneath you, perhaps ten kilometers down, perhaps fifty, there is a core of molten material that is conducting its heat upward through the crust at a steady, measurable, predictable rate. The math is clean. The heat flux is uniform. The cooling curve is a textbook curve.

This is industrial.

Not in the sense of factories and smokestacks. In the sense of a system designed for output. The planet is a heat engine. The growths are — perhaps — radiators. The ceramic stalks, hollow and thin-walled, present an enormous surface area to the thin atmosphere, maximizing convective and radiative heat loss. The terracing maximizes surface area at the macro scale. The cracks in the rock maximize it at the micro scale. The growths are placed where the heat flux is highest. The entire visible landscape is an optimized heat-dissipation system.

For what purpose, no one knows.

There are no pipes. There are no buildings. There is no infrastructure. There is no evidence of intention — only the overwhelming impression of design. The terraces are too regular. The cracks are too well-placed. The growths are too efficient. The silence is too perfectly tuned.

Someone built this. Or something. Or something is building this, still, slowly, one terrace at a time, one cooling cycle at a time, and has been for longer than memory and will continue for longer than anticipation.

There is no one here to ask.

---

VI. THE FEELING

You are alone.

You have been alone since you arrived, and you will be alone when you leave, and the place will not notice either event. The terraces will continue to cool. The stalks will continue to ring. The sun will continue to not move. The silence will continue to be full.

You can walk for a very long time in any direction and the place will not change. The terraces will continue. The growths will thin slightly as you move away from the heat source — away from the center, toward the periphery — but they will not disappear. They go all the way to the haze line. They go beyond it. They go as far as anyone has walked, and beyond.

You can sit on a terrace riser, your back against the warm copper stone, and listen to the ringing. After a while, you will stop hearing it. After a while longer, you will start hearing it again, but differently — not as a sound but as a presence, a thing that is happening to you, a vibration in your sternum that has no source you can point to.

You will feel, sitting there, that you are inside something very large and very patient. That the silence is not the absence of noise but the presence of attention. That the planet is listening to itself cool.

You will not feel watched. You will feel *included*.

The stalks will ring.

The warmth will enter your back.

The light will not change.
