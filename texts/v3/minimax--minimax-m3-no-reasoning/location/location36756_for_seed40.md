A high plateau of bright metal, stretched until it has weather.

The thing is, it isn't a plateau in the proper sense — there is no proper sense here. It is a surface roughly the size of a small district, give or take, that behaves like terrain because the eye has no alternative. From a distance, ascending one of the long access ramps that feed its upper edge, you can see the convincement fail in places: a corner where the floor insists it is also a wall; a trench that opens in the direction of up. These are not jokes the geometry is making at your expense. The geometry is doing something else, something older than joking, and the shape of the wind through it is therefore also wrong, in the same slow way that water moving under ice is wrong — that is, calibrated correctly but to a different physics.

The elevation is real enough. At this altitude the air is thin and cool, and the sun arrives with the kind of force that only altitude allows: not hot, but *decided*, a white-blue light that finds every seam in the metal and lays it open. Visibility is excellent in the mornings and dissolves by afternoon, when the condensation — the only weather here, really, beyond the rare and amnesiac rain — thickens into a moving gray that drapes itself over the structures from above, as if the sky has begun to leak. Things reappear out of this gray in the same places they went in, but slightly altered: a railing has changed its profile; a vent you could swear was not there yesterday is now bolted and breathing.

The structures are sharp. Everything is recently emplaced, recently welded, recently tensioned with a kind of grim conviction. The surface beneath your boots — or whatever you are walking on, since the substrate is sometimes a grate, sometimes a stripped-back panel, sometimes a poured pad of something that has the appearance of concrete but breathes like a lung — is engineered to take weight, channel water, and survive the next ten thousand cycles of mist. Erosion here is not erosion as such but intention, the slow wearing-down of intent: an edge becomes a fillet becomes a chamfer; a weld bead is eaten by rust into a ribbon; a stamped serial number loses its first digit, then its second, and the part becomes anonymous, which is the closest a manufactured object can come to becoming soil.

The damp is everything. It is in the way a railing is cold through a glove. It is the smell — a metallurgical smell, sweet at one remove, dry at another, with a basement tang of mineral oil and ozone when you pass near a substation. It is the sound, which is barely anything. Despite the density, despite the layers of conduit and switchgear and heat exchanger stacked like books in a forgotten archive, despite the high-tension lines that walk across the upper terrain on their stilted pylons and hum a frequency that opens the back of the skull — despite all of this, the place is mostly quiet. The machines are running. You can feel them in the soles of your feet and in the bones of your jaw. But the sound has been damped out by the mist, by the altitude, by the mass of material, by the careful engineering of this place as a place that does not broadcast itself. It runs the way a heart runs: present, expensive, and largely inaudible.

The fauna is the part you remember later, when you have stopped trying to make sense of anything else.

There is no vegetation. There is *no* vegetation, in the sense that there is no green thing, no root, no fiber pushing itself between stones. And yet, in the absence of anything else alive, the place is teeming. Lizards the size of a finger, slate-gray and four-valved, lie along the seams of expansion joints and watch you with the patience of things that have nowhere else to be. They are not exactly lizards. Their skins do not breathe; their ribs are visible through their flanks as fine radiused wires. Small things the size of a thumb, articulated like insects but furred, scramble through the cable trays, and they eat — what do they eat? — they eat the condensate, perhaps, and the trace metals, and the faint outgassing of the rubber boots they nest inside. A pale fox-shape, jointed in too many places, trots across a transformer pad three hundred meters ahead of you, its gait perfect and slightly wrong. It sees you. It does not stop. There are birds — not real birds, but wing-shaped devices, gulls in form, that ride the thermals thrown up by the hot panels and the cold mist and they are screaming somewhere between a gull-scream and a klaxon.

The creatures are dense here. They are layered into the place. They occupy the ledges, the gutters, the warm side of every housing, the underside of every manhole cover you would not consider lifting. You are outnumbered. You are also, despite the size of some of them, not really threatened by them; the place is indifferent to you in the manner of a thing that has not yet decided what you are.

The light is the thing that does not stop.

It pours. Even when the mist comes down, the light pours through it and the mist becomes *lit* — not darker, but denser, more architectural, holding its shape the way smoke holds its shape under a spotlight. There are lamps here too, industrial floods mounted on the upper structures, but they are backups; they flick on only when the natural light dims, and even then they brighten rather than replace it. The place does not go dim. You begin to suspect that the light is being *manufactured* — that the geometry of the upper surfaces is arranged to catch a particular angle of sun and amplify it, the way a parabolic dish amplifies a whisper — and the suspicion becomes a certainty when you notice that shadows fall in directions the sun cannot reach. The shadows are wrong, and bright, and they move slowly through the day, and the place rotates around them rather than around the sun.

The slowness is the last thing you understand.

Nothing here moves in a hurry. The mist thickens and thins over hours. The oil in a distant reservoir climbs its sight glass at the rate of a slow heartbeat. The birds wheel on their thermals and you can track one bird across the sky for a long time before it has clearly gone anywhere. The lizards do not move unless you move first. The pale fox-shape did not stop, but it did not speed up; you would have had to run to make it interesting. Even the strange shifts of geometry — the trench that opens upward, the corner that turns — these are happening, but slowly; if you sit with your back against an exhaust housing and watch the same feature across the basin for an hour, you will see it migrate, the way a river migrates, the way a coastline migrates. The place is *becoming*. It is not finished. It may never be finished. It is doing what young land does: deciding what it is, over the patient course of a season that is not a season.

When the rain comes — and it does come, occasionally, in brief hard vertical curtains that seem to fall straight through the mist without touching it — it does not pool. It is gone into the grates and the channels in seconds. The wet shines on the metal for a while, then dries, then shines again from the condensation of the cooling air. You stand in the bright midday haze, surrounded by a small wilderness of machine, and nothing is alive and everything is alive, and the place is quiet the way a thinking thing is quiet, and you are a small warm shape inside its attention, and the day is long, and the day is not yet half over.
