# The Last Engine at Karst's Edge

The wind has had the better part of a million years to learn this place, and it has not found much to say.

What remains of Karst sits along a high shoulder of pale rock — call it a plateau, though the word implies more variation than the land can offer. The elevation has been negotiated by water that no longer comes, the rock etched and re-etched across deep time until every line is rounded, every crest softened, every drop converted into a long, slow ramp of mineral dust and fractured stone. From a distance, the town is indistinguishable from the rock it rests upon: a collection of low, dark geometries pressed against the pale apron of the plateau like something that grew tired of standing.

You notice the smokestacks before you notice the houses. There are seven of them, arranged in a shallow crescent, and from their mouths the thinnest possible threads of vapor issue into the dark. They have done this for a hundred and sixty years, the locals say, and they will do it for a hundred and sixty more, because the engines inside them have no mechanism for stopping. The coal is synthetic. The water is not water but a slow-circulating mercury-and-salt amalgam kept in leaded cisterns beneath the foundry floor. The heat it carries away is not for anything. The factory simply must be cooled, and the coolants must be processed, and the products of that processing must be vented, and so a thin, chemical haze sits over Karst on every day that the wind is too tired to push it elsewhere.

The light here is poor. The sky is the color of something that has been turned over and examined and put back down. A dim amber seam persists along the western horizon, and above it the firmament climbs through umber into something not quite black. The sun, when it travels, does so as a rumor — a brief and diffuse brightening that fails to cast real shadows and then is gone. The buildings of Karst are tall enough, in places, to occlude what little comes from above. Inside, the streets are lit by the same small bulb-lamps that have lit them for generations: coil-filaments in sealed glass, burning at a temperature chosen to be visible rather than efficient. The town's founder was a woman named Arest who believed, according to the archive, that "a place where the eye must work is a place where the mind must also." Her engineers complied.

The streets are mostly empty. Karst holds perhaps three thousand souls, distributed across a built footprint that could comfortably hold thirty. The buildings are wide, low, and thick-walled — constructed of a poured mineral compound that sets to the color of the surrounding stone and weathers identically with it. After several decades, a Karst house becomes a Karst boulder becomes a Karst hillside; the town's outer blocks have already begun this transformation. The interiors are warm in the literal sense (warm enough that the residents shed layers when crossing certain rooms) and warm in the older sense (that of places where speech is unhurried and doors are not closed). The heating comes from the engines. Karst does not have a furnace in the usual way; it has *proximity to industry*, and its people have arranged their domestic geography accordingly, the way an alpine village arranges itself around a hearth.

There is, properly speaking, no biology here that anyone would recognize as such. The pale dust on the rocks is not lichen. The faint tracery on the cistern walls is not mold. There are creatures, though — small, slow, and few. The Karstian rock-lice, half an inch long, that feed on the mineral outgassing of the smelters and retreat into cracks during the cooler hours. The tall pale fliers seen occasionally at dusk, which may be animals or may be loose sheets of metal warmed and lifted by thermals; no one has settled the question. A dog, in certain quarters — the last dogs of Karst, kept and not bred, aging in courtyards. The children are sometimes permitted to look at them. The cats are doing better than the dogs and worse than the fliers.

The sound is almost nothing. The wind is constant and low, a barely-pitched moan that has worn itself smooth across the stone. The engines hum — a felt more than a heard thing, transmitted through the soles of the feet and the small bones of the inner ear. When a smelter cycles, there is a deeper note, half an octave below the hum, that arrives and then recedes like a tide. Conversations carry for surprising distances on the plateau's flat acoustic. Someone laughing, three streets away, will arrive at your ear intact and unhurried.

What is strange about Karst is not any single thing. It is that Karst should not exist. The plateau holds no strategic position, no resource of obvious value, no access to anything. The alloys produced here are serviceable and unremarkable. The foundry's output is consumed by Karst itself, mostly — reinforced beams for new buildings, replacements for the slow-degrading facades, the lamp-coils that burn out at a known rate and must be re-spun. The town is, functionally, a machine that maintains itself, and it is not clear, even to its residents, why the machine was built or why it continues. There is a civic religion of sorts, centered on the doctrine of *the useful remainder* — the belief that whatever is left after every reason has been subtracted is the thing of true worth. The founder's memorial, a low obelisk near the central plaza, is inscribed only with a single line: *"We are what remained."*

You can stand at the town's western margin and look out across the plateau. The stone runs on for a great distance, pale and softly contoured, into a darkness that does not lift. Other low structures appear occasionally in the middle distance — outposts, cistern stations, abandoned wings of the foundry that were cut off by a long-ago reorganization and now stand as small dark geometries against the pale apron, inhabited by no one and slowly being absorbed. Beyond them, the land continues. There are rumors of other settlements. The southerners claim a working harbor two hundred kilometers downslope; they describe water. No one from Karst has been to see it in living memory, and the journey would take weeks across stone that has never known a wheel-track.

The wind moves a little dust. The thinnest threads of vapor rise from the seven stacks and dissolve, almost at once, into the amber and umber dimness above. The engines hum. The rock-lice emerge, briefly, into the warmer air between the buildings, and then withdraw. A door opens somewhere, and the warmer interior air meets the cooler outside, and there is a brief exhalation, almost a sigh, and then equilibrium again.

Nothing here is going to change. Nothing here has changed in a long time. The place has settled, the way stones settle, the way the dust settles, the way a sound settles into the bone of a person who has lived here long enough. Karst will be here tomorrow. It will be here in a hundred years. It will be here as long as the engines require cooling and the engines require nothing, and so they will run forever, and the town will persist around them like a question that has forgotten to ask itself.

You walk back to your lodging. The stone under your boots is warm from the day's slow sun. A lamp ahead of you begins to glow, on a timer, before you arrive.
