# The Substrate

The city lives under the hill.

A young landform, freshly heaved — the strata here are still sharp-edged, knife-cut where the upthrust happened only a few centuries ago. The fault scarps haven't had time to soften. Slate cleaves into rectangles that ring like struck glass when the humidity changes, and the air is always changing humidity, because the ceiling weeps. Water moves through the rock along every bedding plane, every joint, every flaw the uplift opened, and it gathers on the underside of things and falls in long threads, deliberate as piano wire, into the basins below.

The basins are lit, but barely. The light is not the light of any sun. It is the light of the city itself — a phosphorescence carried in the mist, a sub-biological glow that clings to every wet surface and makes the slate walls appear to breathe in pale green and rusted amber. You can see by it. You cannot read by it. Shadows pool in the deeper rooms and don't leave.

The city is built into the weeping.

That was the first thing the engineers understood, or thought they understood, when they came down here. The water was a fact before them. So they built with it, against it, around it. Their avenues are channels. Their plazas are cisterns. Their walls are dammed stone, tiered so the constant drip falls from one terrace to the next, gathering in controlled layers, harvested at the bottom by intakes that feed — nobody now remembers what. The infrastructure is layered because the engineers layered themselves: six or seven strata of construction, each speaking a different architectural dialect, each more confident, or more desperate, than the last. Lower levels are older and more intact; upper levels are younger and more ruinous, because the upper levels are where the seep is worst, and where the strange work happens.

There are no people here now. There are also no animals, none at all — no insects in the standing water, no fish in the cisterns, no mites in the moss that grows wherever a beam of the phosphorescent light pools long enough on a flat stone. The moss is the only living thing, and even the moss is questionable, because it does not behave like moss. It grows inward toward the light sources. It retracts from the dark. It has a texture more like felted hair than like anything vegetal, and it hums, very faintly, at a frequency just below the threshold of hearing. The engineers, or their successors, left it alone. You can see where they built around existing patches — small circular clearings, the moss colonies respected like shrines or like hazards.

The sound is the city.

You cannot separate the place from its noise. Every terrace has its own dripping — fast, slow, syncopated, glissando — and the dripping does not stop. It accumulates. In the upper corridors it is a hiss, a kind of rain-in-reverse. Lower down, where the channels converge, it becomes a roar that has shape to it, that has eddies and standing waves, and the slate walls transmit it back as a low vibration you feel in the soft plates of your skull. At the lowest level, the Intake, the noise is a single sustained chord, the sound of water entering something that wants more water than it gets, and the chord is tuned, whether by accident or by craft, to a frequency that makes teeth itch.

Above this, woven through it, there is another sound. The younger infrastructure hums. Capacitors buried in the stonework, or what look like capacitors — braided cylinders of an alloy that doesn't corrode in the wet, despite being old enough to corrode in — emit a tone that rises and falls with the water level. When the cisterns are full, the hum is high and tight, almost a whine. When the cisterns are low, the hum drops into a register that flattens the air and makes the moss colonies shiver. No one currently adjusts the cisterns. No one currently reads the hum. It simply goes on doing what it was set to do, with the mechanical patience of a thing that does not require an operator to continue being itself.

The light moves. This is the part that makes the place feel inhabited even though it isn't.

Somewhere in the middle levels there is an apparatus — or a system of them — that takes the phosphorescent mist and pushes it through the channels along prescribed routes. You can watch it happen. A slow tide of pale green wash sweeps across the ceiling of one of the larger chambers, pauses at the intersections, and then drains, over the course of an hour or a day, into the next. The city is breathing. The breath is visible. The breath does not require lungs.

There are doors that are not doors. There are corridors that open onto drops, and drops that open onto platforms, and platforms from which you can see, far below and across a gulf of wet air, other platforms arranged in a pattern that suggests intention but refuses to resolve into a plan. The wet gleams on every surface. The slate is dark. The green light moves.

And the water falls, and falls, and falls.
