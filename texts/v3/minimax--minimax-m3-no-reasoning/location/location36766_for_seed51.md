# The Sounding Mire

The land here has forgotten how to be still.

You stand at the edge of something that should not exist — a wetland the size of a small country, stretched across a lowland basin between two ridges that have eroded themselves into long, patient humps. The basin floor sits barely above the water table. In places it does not sit above it at all. Rainwater, groundwater, and something else — some other kind of water, if water is still the right word — all share the same dark plane.

The light is failing. Not twilight; the sun is well up, but the sky has thickened into a low pewter lid, and the clouds move in a direction that has nothing to do with the wind at ground level. They move *inward*. They press down. The shadows under every reed and hummock are absolute. Color has drained from the world except for the plants, which are defiantly, almost offensively green — a green that seems to be the only pigment left, as if chlorophyll has consumed the spectrum.

The vegetation is a problem. It is too much. Every surface that is not water is biomass. Sedges tower over your head, their blades as wide as your hand, their edges serrated with a precision that looks deliberate, engineered. Moss climbs in ankle-deep drifts. Ferns uncoil in slow-motion pirouettes, each new frond bigger than the last. The air smells of rot and ozone, simultaneously, in alternating waves — rot when you inhale, ozone when you exhale, as if the atmosphere is being reprocessed by your lungs.

And the sound.

The sound is the first thing that breaks you.

It begins low, at the threshold of hearing, a pressure more than a tone. Then it braids upward into something orchestral. Every reed is a stopped pipe. Every pocket of trapped gas beneath the water's skin is a kettle drum struck at irregular intervals. Something beneath the surface — no, something *within* the surface, as if the water itself has become a vibrating membrane — produces a tone that travels through your sternum rather than your ears. You feel the mire sing. It has been singing for a very long time. The singing is not for you.

The water is the wrong temperature. Cool, but not uniformly. There are seams of cold so sharp they feel like cuts, and seams of warmth that have no source. Where the two meet, the surface dimples and forms rings, as if fish are rising, but nothing breaks the meniscus. Whatever is producing the rings is operating at a depth that is not a depth — a layer that cannot be plumbed because it is not located in the third dimension the way you expect. Sometimes when you look, the rings are concentric. Sometimes they are spirals. Once, briefly, they arrange themselves into a glyph, and you decide not to look again.

The technology here is not visible. There are no structures. There are no artifacts. There are no machines. But the engineering is here. You know this the way you know when you are being watched: the sensation predates evidence. The humidity organizes itself into standing waves that drift across the basin like slow luminous nematodes, three meters tall, faintly lit from within, curving around obstacles that are not obstacles — passing through you, for instance, with a chill that is not a chill, that is information, that is *exposure*. They are scanning. They have always been scanning. The scanning predates you.

The fauna is sparse. You have not seen an animal in hours. You have seen tracks, though: prints in the mud that change species every few meters, as if some single creature is trying on bodies. Once, in a clearing of dark water, something the size of a deer stood watching you from a distance that you cannot estimate, because every time you tried to estimate it, the distance revised itself. It had too many legs, or its legs were in the wrong places, or it was standing on something that wasn't there. You did not look at its face. You understood, without being told, that looking at its face would constitute an agreement.

Reality is breaking down slowly, like a document being soaked. The erosion here is not geological; it is ontological. The basin has existed for so long in this state — this singing, this scanning, this semi-permeable membrane between the world and whatever the world rests on — that the laws governing it have become informal. Gravity works, but locally. Causality works, but you cannot always identify which direction it is running. You saw a tree fall upward earlier, slowly, as if rising into a wind that exists only inside its trunk. You saw a fish swim through the air between two reeds, pausing mid-curve to regard you with a small black eye that held no recognition and no lack of recognition, only a sort of bureaucratic attention.

There are no buildings. There are no roads. There are no signs that anything with hands has ever decided to stay here. But the soil at your feet is patterned. Hexagonal. The hexagons are exactly six centimeters across. You did not notice when you arrived. You notice now. You suspect the hexagons were always there, and that the mud has only now become honest enough to admit them.

You are standing in an instrument. The whole valley is an instrument. It has been tuned. It is being played. The player is not visible, and may not be local, and may not be singular. The music does not require your consent. But now that you have heard it, you will not be able to step out of it. The reeds will keep singing. The rings will keep spreading. The standing-wave nematodes will drift through you, gathering whatever it is they gather, adding it to a chorus that has been building since before this basin had a name.

Somewhere, very far away in the direction that is not a direction, something answers.

The mire is patient.

The mire is dense.

The mire is dim, and wet, and cool, and old, and loud.
