# The Relay Vault

## I. The Container

You find the entrance, if it can be called that, at the bottom of a cirque. A natural amphitheater of granite, except nothing here is natural. The cirque was *built* to suggest a cirque, its curves mathematically elegant, the rock-face poured and tooled to mimic the patient work of ten thousand winters. The lichen — sparse, pallid, threaded in grays and the faintest acid yellows — was seeded. The frost-shattered look of the stone was applied by hand.

The vault is small. Cathedral proportions belong to other places. This is the size of a generous kitchen, a master bedroom, a holding cell for something precious. The granite walls rise maybe forty feet before they curve inward and meet at a central point. The floor is uneven — you have to step carefully — because the engineers who built this place believed, or claimed to believe, that the simulated erosion added *character*.

The air is wet. That is the first thing the lungs register. Not a clean wet like rain, but a thick, condensing wet, the kind that forms on the inside of a lung. It condenses on the metal. It condenses on you. At this elevation, nine thousand feet or near enough, the air already moves close to its dew point, and whatever the machinery below is doing pushes it past.

## II. The Turbine

The deafening thing.

It comes up through the floor. It comes through the stone. There is no part of your body that the sound does not find. It is not a roar — roars have edges — but a low, granular, omnipresent thunder, the sound of something being *turned*. The granite beneath your boots transmits a vibration you can feel in the bones of your ankles, your knees, the small bones of your inner ear. When you press your palm flat against the wall, the stone hums against your skin like a sleeping chest.

The sound has direction. It comes from below, from the throat of the chamber, from a circular iron grille set into the floor at the lowest point of the slight basin in which you stand. Through the grille you can see — if you kneel, if you press your face to the cold iron — a moving disc of pale water and the slow, deliberate rotation of something enormous and pale, like the underside of a planet, turning in the dark. A turbine, or something that does the work of a turbine. Its purpose is hydroelectric, or pneumatic, or some compound word that does not exist yet. The water that powers it is glacial. It is also pumped. Both are true.

The grille is warm. The water below it is colder than the air above. This produces the constant fog that pools in the vault, knee-high, sometimes waist-high, sometimes low enough that you forget it is there until you taste it on your lips.

## III. The Walls

The lichen is functional. It is not decorative. It is a living indicator layer, and someone — some operator in a low room below the turbine hall — watches its color. Acid yellow means the air is balanced. Gray-green means sulfur. The deep, almost blackish green that has begun to colonize the upper corners of the vault means something else. The operators send signals. The signals arrive as small, quiet adjustments in the ventilation — a sigh of cold air from a hidden register, a shift in the mineral smell of the fog.

The walls themselves are pierced with pipes. Copper, mostly, and some alloys that have no common name. They run along the granite in neat runs and then disappear into it, and where they emerge, they weep. Condensation runs down their lengths and finds the stone and travels along the tooled seams of the false erosion until it reaches a low channel carved into the floor. The channels run, in spirals, toward the central grille. The water is gathered. The water is sent down.

You do not see the operators. You may never see the operators. The system runs itself through what appears to be the persistence of cold water and the inertia of great iron, but somewhere a hand is on a lever.

## IV. The Cold

Cool is too soft a word. Up here, at this elevation, in this wet and this shade, the temperature holds at perhaps four degrees Celsius. Your breath does not quite plume. The metal pipes are colder than the air. When you brush one with the back of your hand, the skin there goes white and you do not feel it for ten minutes afterward.

The cold is the medium in which everything is preserved. The turbine turns. The water falls. The cold is the contract — the agreement between parts.

## V. The Quiet Inside the Loud

There is no animal here. No bird could stand the sound, or the air, or the cold. No moss of the quick, breathing kind. No insect. What lives here is the lichen, and the lichen is slow, and the lichen does not object.

You can sit on a granite outcrop near the wall and let the sound press against you until your body stops trying to fight it. Then there is something almost peaceful. The fog moves at knee height in slow drifts. The lichen watches you with its acid eyes. The turbine, far below, does its patient work.

The vault was built in 1923, or 1987, or 1841. The dates on the plate riveted near the entrance do not agree. The plate is in three languages and one of them is a conlang, an engineered tongue, devised perhaps for the operators, perhaps for the lichen.

There is a small iron door on the far side of the vault, set into the wall, painted the same gray as the stone. Behind it is the spiral stair down. Behind the spiral stair down is the rest of the place — the relay room, the valve gallery, the long descent to where the water begins.

You do not have to go down. You can stay up here. The fog will keep you company. The lichen will watch. The turbine will turn.

The sound will not stop.
