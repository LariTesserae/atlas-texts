# The Cistern

The floor is marble, and it is wet, and it has been wet for so long that no one remembers when it was not. Every surface holds a skin of water so thin the stone beneath it looks darker than it is, and when you walk, your feet find a sound like a tongue pressed to a glass — a soft, intimate *clack* followed by a sigh of parting. The chamber is small. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other, though no one has ever thrown a stone here, because the chamber does not have a ceiling high enough for a stone to matter, and stones here are too precious to waste on distance.

It is a room. Or it is several rooms. The walls are dressed stone, fitted without mortar in the old way, the way the old ones built before they remembered how to lie about what they were doing. The blocks are vast — taller than a man, broader than a man can reach — and they are not quite square, and the joints between them weep. Each joint is a tiny black mouth, perpetually open, and from each mouth a slow thread of water gathers itself and runs down the wall and finds the floor and joins the skin and becomes indistinguishable from it. The whole room is in this slow act of becoming wetter, or perhaps of becoming itself. The weeping never stops. If you sit very still, the weeping is the loudest thing.

It is dim. There are no lamps, because there has not been a lamp lit in this place in a long time, and the lamps that were once here have been taken or have rotted or have been eaten. The light comes from above, where a long fissure in the roof admits a thread of grey weather — a sky that is more weather than sky, more cloud than anything, a permanent overcast that presses down through the crack and lies on the water-skin like a second skin. The light is water-colored. The light is the color of looking through a bottle. Everything in the chamber is the color of itself seen through a film.

The chamber is built. There is no other word. The stone underfoot is the only ground, and the stone up the walls is the only wall, and the stone across the ceiling is the only ceiling. Even the water that lies on every surface is itself partly built — it does not rain in here, except the one thin rain that falls through the crack; the rest is condensation, drawn from the air by the cold of the stone, and the cold of the stone is something the builders chose. The air is damp the way a mouth is damp. When you breathe in, you taste nothing. When you breathe out, you contribute.

It is cool. The chill comes up through the marble through the soles of your feet, and it is the kind of chill that does not deepen no matter how long you stay, because the room has already decided what temperature it will be. The chill is the room's opinion of itself. In the corners, where the air does not move, the chill settles in thin pale fogs that lie along the base of the walls and never quite rise. Sometimes a fog will detach itself and drift across the floor like a slow animal, and you will not know whether to step over it or wait for it to pass.

---

The inhabitants are loud. This is the part that surprises visitors, if visitors come, which they do not, because the place is difficult to find and the finding does not reward you with anything except more of the same.

They are insects. Or they were once insects. They are the size of a child's hand, and they have too many legs, and their legs are not jointed correctly, and they make a sound that is somewhere between a fingernail drawn along a comb and a small, insistent argument. There are many of them. They live in the joints of the walls, in the weeping mouths, and they feed — as far as anyone could tell by watching for a long time — on the weeping itself. They gather at the wet seams and they drink, and they make their argument-sound, and they do not seem to be afraid of anything because there is nothing here large enough to threaten them except the cold, and the cold is a constant, and constants are not threats. They are the color of wet stone. If you did not move, you would not see them.

They breed in the fogs. A fog that detaches from a corner and drifts across the floor will sometimes arrive at another corner and be absorbed, and the insects will be more numerous afterward, and the fog will be slightly less. No one has counted. The math of it is the room's own concern.

There are other things.

There is a sound that comes from beneath the floor, a deep, slow, percussive thing, like a heart that has decided to take its time. It comes up through the marble. It is not regular. It is the sound of a place remembering to be a place. It is the sound of whatever the builders built the room on top of, still doing what it was doing before the room was built. The insects time themselves to it. The fogs drift in sympathy with it. When it is louder, the weeping from the joints is faster, and the insects are more active, and the fogs are thicker. When it is quieter, the room is quieter, and the insects slow, and the fogs lie still along the walls. The room is breathing through the floor, in a way, and the breath has a rhythm no one taught it.

There is a single object in the chamber, against the far wall. It is a basin. It is made of the same stone as the floor, but it is the only thing in the room that is not wet, because it is full. It is full to the brim with water that does not spill, because the water in the basin is the same temperature as the basin is cold, and the surface tension has decided, for now, to hold. If you were to lean close, you would see your own face, and the face would be the color of the room. The basin is older than the room, or the same age, or both. The insects do not go near it. The fogs do not enter it. It is a separate country, ruled by its own quiet laws, and the room politely does not intrude.

---

If you stand in the middle of the floor and do not move, the room will eventually notice you. It will not do anything. It will simply adjust, the way a body adjusts to a new weight on a mattress — a slight redistribution of the cold, a slight re-routing of the weeping, a slight shift in the rhythm of the thing beneath the floor, as if the thing beneath is making space. The insects will begin to approach you, not because they are curious but because you are warm, and warmth here is a resource. They will walk across the tops of your feet with the soft, wrong-jointed click of their too-many legs. The fog will find your ankles and curl there. The sound beneath the floor will slow, just slightly, as if the heart beneath has decided to be careful of you.

And you will stand there, in the dim, in the wet, in the small total room, and you will hear the weeping, and the comb-argument of the insects, and the slow percussion from below, and you will not know if the room is waiting for you to do something or if it has already accepted that you are part of what happens here now. The basin across the room will hold its still water. The fissure above will admit its grey weather. The cold will continue to be the room's opinion of itself.

You will not be cold, exactly. You will only be aware of the cold, the way you are aware of your own breathing when someone mentions it. The room is like that. The room is the kind of place that knows you are in it and has decided, for now, what to do about you, and the decision is mostly to continue.
