# The Condensing Quarter

## I. The Roofline

A district that has sunk, over decades, until its second stories are ground stories. The first floors drowned long ago and no one drained them; instead, the city built *over* them, sealing the old streets with new flagstone and forgetting what lay beneath except as a sound. A kind of thrum. A wet, patient percussion, like something breathing in a sealed room.

The new quarter sits low and close. Tile roofs of fired red clay, slick with the condensation that never quite stops. The eaves weep continuously — not rain, not quite; the air itself is so wet that moisture condenses on every underside and drips, drips, drips into the gutters, which are perpetually full, perpetually overflowing in a thin sheet that runs down the outside walls. The walls are brick, mostly, but the brick is going green and going black, and the mortar between is more moss than sand. Doorways are arched, low, dark. The streets are not streets so much as canals by another name, and the few wheeled things move on iron rails set above the waterline.

It is warm. It is always warm. You sweat into your collar and the sweat does not evaporate; it just becomes part of the air, joins the general wet, finds a tile to drip from.

## II. The Engines

The quarter is loud because it is industrial, and it is industrial because of the engines. They are not the gleaming kind. They are the iron-fat kind: broad, dark, sweating themselves, bolted to brick piers, breathing steam in slow deliberate pulses that match no rhythm a person would call natural. They pump. They compress. They drive shafts that turn shafts that turn wheels that turn belts that turn other wheels. They send water up from somewhere below — from the drowned first city, or from the aquifer beneath that, or from something deeper still, depending on which old engineer you believe.

The engines are warm. Everything is warm. The steam that vents from the relief valves drifts down the alleys in white sheets, and where it meets a cooler wall it condenses and runs. The lamps in the quarter are not flame-lamps; they are phosphor-lamps, bulbous glass things screwed into iron brackets, dim and greenish, that look as though they are exhaling. They give off barely enough light to see a face by. Beyond ten paces the world is suggestion: a shape, a sheen, a moving darkness.

The quarter is loud because the engines never stop, and because the workers speak loud over them, and because the *condensation* is loud if you listen: a billion tiny drips, a billion tiny ticks, a sound like sand falling in sheets, like rain that has forgotten how to commit.

## III. The People, Such As They Are

There are people. Or there are presences. The quarter is dense with motion — dark shapes moving in lanes between the brick, hands visible at doorways, voices in the steam — but the dimness and the wet and the slow movement of everything makes them hard to fix. You see a woman leaning from a second-floor gallery, hair plastered to her neck, calling something down to a figure below who is half-lost in mist. You see a boy with an iron rod, poking at the gutter. You see a man with oil-blackened arms crossing a footbridge over a wider channel. They move the way water moves: finding the path of least resistance, sliding around each other, never quite colliding.

They do not seem to see you. Or they see you and go on. The quarter is full enough already; you are an additional density in an overfull place, and the room made for you was not made by anyone.

## IV. The Water Itself

Every surface is wet. The iron rails gleam. The bricks darken in patches where water has found a route. There is a smell of it — not clean, not foul, but mineral, loamy, the smell of water that has been somewhere and brought the somewhere back with it. It tastes of iron if you breathe through your mouth. Your clothes, within an hour, will be heavier than when you put them on.

In the wider channels — the old streets, now canals — something moves below the surface. Slow shapes. Not animal. Not quite mechanical either. The engineers say they are the remnants of the old machinery, drowned and operating still, on some pathetic principle, in the dark. The children throw stones at the ripples the shapes make. The stones sink slowly, as though the water is thicker than it should be.

## V. The Lush

And yet it grows. The wet and the warmth and the dimness — these are conditions that *favor* certain things. Ferns erupt from every crack in the masonry. Moss fills the gutters before the water can carry it away. There are trees here that should not exist in a place so choked with brick and steam: figs, their roots splitting the parapets; a single magnolia behind a wall, blooming without sun, its flowers the color of old candlewax. Vines have taken the eastern face of the engine hall so thoroughly that the windows look out from a curtain of green. When the wind moves — and the wind does move, sometimes, in slow gusts that come up from the drowned lower city — the whole facade shivers.

The quarter is overgrown and overbuilt at once. There is no square foot that belongs entirely to one or the other. The brick and the leaf have been negotiating for a hundred years and have reached a stalemate neither can break.

## VI. Time Here

Nothing fast happens. The engines exhale. The water drips. The moss spreads at a pace you cannot measure in an afternoon. The people move at the pace of people carrying weight through wet air. A bell rings somewhere — a single note, sustained, fogged at the edges — and you cannot tell whether it has just rung or has been ringing for an hour.

You stand in a doorway. The water runs past your boots. Above you, on a roof you cannot quite see, something — a tile, a leaf, a small bird that should not be here — releases and falls. You hear it strike the water a long time later.

You are in the Condensing Quarter. You will not leave it quickly. No one does.
