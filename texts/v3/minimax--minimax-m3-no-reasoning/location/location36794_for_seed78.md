# The Lung-Spine of the Upper Air

You climb, and climb, and the cold sharpens. Not the cold of winter — something purer, an altitude-cold, thin and glinting, the kind of cold that lives in the tongue and stays there.

The ridge you crest is not rock. It is **root**.

A spine of pale, fibrous wood rises out of the slope and runs the entire visible horizon — fifty miles, a hundred, lost in the haze of a light that doesn't know how to commit. The wood is the color of old bone in low light, and where it weathers it sloughs in papery curls that drift sideways on a wind you cannot feel at your back. You are standing on the spine of something that has been growing upward, slowly, since before the weather learned its name.

The root is hollow. You did not know this until you walked into one of the open nodes where it forks and found that the ground beneath you is a rind, and beneath the rind is air, and the air goes down, and the air does not stop.

This is a **pneumatophore forest**, and you are in its **upper canopy**.

---

## The plants

Everything here is plant, in the way that everything in a cathedral is stone. The trunks — you must call them trunks, there is no other word — are **aerial columns** that have been racing each other toward a sky they will never reach, and in the racing they have built a continent. Each column is a hundred meters around at the base and tapers over a half-mile of vertical distance to a flaring, fingered crown that braids into the next column's crown, and the next, and the next. Between them, **bridges of cambium** have grown and re-grown and knotted themselves into a hanging deck — a platform of living wood suspended a quarter-mile above the unseen floor. The deck sways. It always sways.

The **fronds** are the size of city blocks. They are not green. They are the deep blue-black of a bruise held up to a candle, and their surfaces are matte and faintly damp, and they are the loudest thing in the world. Each frond is a lung. It inhales when the wind exhales, and exhales when the wind inhales, and the slat of moisture across its millions of tiny mouth-slits produces a **sound**. Not a rustle. A *roar*. A constant, low, oceanic roar that is the wind being chewed by ten million leaves at once, with the wet slap of condensation flipping over in each throat of the frond. You cannot shout over it. You do not need to.

The fronds feed the columns. The columns feed the roots. The roots feed the floor of the world, somewhere far below, where the real business of water and soil is conducted in a hush you will never visit.

There is no soil up here. There is only **plant**, and the patient work of plants finding each other across impossible distances.

---

## The water

It is damp the way a mouth is damp. Always. The cold condenses on every surface the moment the surface stops moving — on your sleeve, on the inside of a cupped palm of frond, on the underside of every cambium bridge. At dusk, the columns weep. A fine mist climbs from the unseen floor, gets caught in the braid of crowns, and falls back as a slow, freezing drizzle that does not so much land as *adopt* the wood it lands on. The wood drinks it before it can pool. There is no mud. There is the constant suggestion of mud, everywhere, and the constant denial of it.

A person could die of thirst up here, surrounded by water they could not catch.

Once a year — once, possibly — the sky remembers that it is a sky, and a true rain comes sideways. For a day and a night the forest becomes a single wet drum, and the roar of the fronds becomes a roar of a different kind, deeper, hungrier, and the deck underfoot turns treacherous and the bridges sag and somewhere a bolt of cambium gives and a whole section of the upper canopy peels away and falls, slowly, slowly, into the dim below, shedding fronds the whole way down like a torch shedding sparks.

After such a rain, the air smells of **green cinnamon** and something else, something almost animal, and the wood of the deck is so soft you can dent it with a thumb.

---

## The light

There is not much. The crowns are dense. What light there is arrives sideways and exhausted, filtered through half a mile of layered frond, and the color it lands as is **slate and ash** with a single weak thread of gold running along the horizon at dawn and dusk like a coin placed under a door. At noon, on a clear day, you might — *might* — see a pale disc the color of a fish belly move across the highest gaps in the braid. You will not feel its warmth. You will feel the absence of a deeper cold when it passes, and that is the sun, and that is all the sun does here.

Eyes adapt. After a week, you can read by the faintest of the fronds' mouths, which carry their own dim bioluminescence — a blue so weak it is almost a rumor, but it is enough to walk by, and after a month it is enough to live by. The forest glows. The forest has always been glowing. You just could not see it.

---

## The animals

You do not see them at first because the forest is the color of its own shadow and so are they.

**Hangflies** are the smallest, the size of a child's fist, with wings of stained-glass membrane and a habit of roosting in rows along the underside of the cambium bridges, mouths-down, sleeping. They eat the mist. You watch one open its many small mouths and the mist simply walks into them, and the fly grows heavier on its perch by a fraction, and that is its whole life. There are **billions** of them. The roar of the fronds is, in part, the sound of billions of tiny papyrus wings being buffeted.

**Weavers** are larger — goat-sized, antlered, the antlers flat and wide and hung with the damp frond-fuzz they use to line the hanging nests they build in the crooks of the columns. The weavers do not come down. They are born in the nest and they die in the nest and the nest is the entire world. They eat the cambium bridges. They digest the wood with the help of a bacterium they culture in a second stomach, and the bacterium makes them slightly drunk, and the drunk weavers sing in the dark in voices that sound exactly like someone trying to remember a hymn. Their song, and the fronds, and the wind, are the three voices of the upper air. You will hear a weaver cry out sometimes in the night and you will think it is a person, and you will be wrong, and you will not be comforted.

**Burrowers** are the ones you fear. They are the size of a small horse and they are made of the same plant the forest is made of, because they are, in some strict sense, plant. A spore finds a weaver's nest, takes root in the accumulated frond-fuzz, and grows inward through the sleeping animal over days, and the animal does not wake, and what wakes up in the morning is something with weaver-antlers and a burrower's hungers and a body that is half-wood and half-meat. It drops from the nest and begins to fall, and the fall is the first part of the hunt, because the **falling thing** is what the hangflies scatter from, and the scattering is what the smaller weavers panic at, and the panic is what the burrowers drop *toward*. They hunt by falling. They are very good at it.

You do not see them often. You see the results.

---

## The people

There are people.

There are perhaps two hundred of them in the world, here, in the upper canopy. They live in **rafts** — flat platforms of lashed cambium, hung from the columns by ropes of woven frond-fiber, that drift with the slow sway of the deck and are moved, when they need to be moved, by twelve people pulling on long lines of braided root. They are dark-skinned and sharp-faced and they have **no word for ground**. They have, instead, a word that means *the place where the things that fall are*, and another word that means *the place where the things that fall are coming from*, and they do not speak of either in polite company.

They have fire. They have, by the most ancient of accidents, learned to strike a spark from a particular black nodule that grows in the heartwood of the columns, and they keep these sparks in a kind of communal clay pot that travels the raft-cluster and is never allowed to go out. They use the fire to **scorch the deck ahead of them** when they walk, because the burrowers do not like the smell of burned wood, and the small mercy is that a person walking in this forest walks in a faint corridor of char, like a monk in a censer.

They have a word for this. The word is the same as the word for *prayer*.

Their **carvings** are the only art. They carve, with hand-tools of antler and obsidian chipped from a single vent-stone in the southern columns, small figures of the burrowers — always with the mouth open, always mid-fall — and they hang these figures from the raft-ropes so that they twist in the wind and make a small clicking sound that is, they say, the burrower's footfall. They want to remember the footfall. The forgetting is worse.

---

## The rules of the place

You learn them.

You do not walk at night without a brand. You do not walk in the day without a brand. You do not touch a weaver's nest, ever, for any reason. You do not eat the cambium raw — only scorched, only after the inner wood has been held to the fire long enough to blacken, and the blackening is not for taste, it is to kill the spore. You do not step off the deck. The deck is the world. The deck is **all** the world. Below the deck is the place the things that fall are, and the place the things that fall are coming from.

You do not trust a silence. The roar is the normal. When the roar stops — when the wind dies, when the fronds go still, when the weavers stop singing and the hangflies stop their papyrus rattle — something is above you, and it is falling, and the time you have is the time it takes a horse to turn over in the air.

You listen. You always listen. The forest is loud and the loudness is the only mercy, because in the loudness the small sounds — the click of a falling antler, the whisper of a frond parting from a stem — are the only warnings, and you must learn to hear them the way you have learned to see the blue of the bioluminescence: by giving yourself over to the place, by letting the place become the instrument, by understanding that you are a small, soft, fire-carrying thing in a cathedral that has been growing for longer than your species has had a word for *cathedral*, and that the cathedral is not for you, and the cathedral does not know you are here, and the cathedral is, in its slow vegetable way, *indifferent*, and that the indifference is not a cruelty but simply the only posture available to a thing that has spent a million years learning how to stand up out of the mud and never once asked to be understood.

You learn the rules.

You learn them well.

You do not learn all of them. No one has.
