# The Last Plain

You stand, if you can call it standing, on a surface that has been worn for so long it has become theoretical. The continent here is older than memory, older than the kind of old that gets called old. What remains is a near-plane — slightly dished, slightly rippled, mostly just *flat* — and the flatness is not the flatness of a prairie or a salt-pan or a tidally-exposed shelf, but the flatness of something that has exhausted every other possibility. Erosion here has finished its argument. There is nothing left to take away. The land is the residue of residue.

The plain is vast. Vast the way an ocean is vast — not in the sense of an expanse you can see across, but in the sense that you cannot see across it. The horizon is wrong. It bends, or the light bends, or you are not actually looking at a horizon at all but at the place where the wet air becomes too thick to see through. From certain vantage points (there are none) the curve of the planet would be visible, and the curve of the planet here is so gentle as to be a kind of insult.

It is warm. It is wet. These are not separate facts about this place — they are a single fact, expressed twice. The ground is wet in the way a wrung-out cloth is wet: not dripping, not flooded, but saturated at every pore. The air is wet in the way a held breath is wet: heavy, condensing, ready to give up its moisture onto any cooler surface, which means onto every surface. Everything here weeps. The stones have a permanent sheen. The few upright things — and there are few — carry a glistening. The surface of the plain is half-water already, a thin film that moves and trembles underfoot, that gathers in shallow pans without ever becoming a pond, that retreats and returns without ever becoming dry. You cannot tell where the ground ends and the standing water begins. You cannot, after a while, tell where the water ends and the air begins.

The atmosphere is the same medium as the sea. It hangs low and close and it is full. There is no emptiness in it. Every cubic span of this place is *occupied* — by vapor, by mist, by fog at varying densities, by the smell of warm water and warm stone and something underneath both of those that is neither mineral nor vegetable nor animal but is the smell of *process*, the smell of things happening continuously. There is no room here. The place is full up.

Vegetation is sparse but it is not absent. The plain carries its life in clusters — tight, low, dark bunches of something that is not grass and not moss and not lichen but shares the qualities of all three. These clusters live on the harder patches, on the rare outcrops that erosion has not yet been able to grind down. They are the color of bruised green. They do not flower. They hold on. Between them, and around them, and underneath them, there is mostly just the wet and the warm and the moving air.

The built is faint. Almost apologetic. Across the plain there are scattered marks — a line of stones, half-drowned, that might be a wall or might be a geological accident and there is no longer any way to know. A cairn somewhere, the stones of which have begun to grow into one another, fusing at their contact points as if the place is slowly ingesting them. A single vertical thing far off in the haze, which might be a tower or might be a chimney or might be a standing stone or might be a tree that got petrified a million years ago and has been upright ever since. There is no geometry to these things. No right angles. They are the work of either very patient hands or very patient time, and the difference no longer matters. Whatever was built here was built when the building was young, and the building is no longer young, and the place has had time to absorb almost all of it. There is almost nothing left that says *human* or *intention* or *tool*. There are just these few irregularities that the wind hasn't quite been able to smooth away.

There is no technology here in any modern sense. There may once have been. There may have been, in a time when the plain was younger and higher and drier, all sorts of devices, all sorts of arrangements. None of it remains. Even the scattered ruins feel less like the ruins of a culture and more like the ruins of *the idea of culture* — old enough, finally, that the category has dissolved.

The light is dim. The sky is a thick lid of cloud that does not move so much as *churn* — slow boiling, the whole visible ceiling in slow circulation. There is no sun. There is no sun-shape behind the clouds, no brighter region, no compass-point you can read. The light comes from everywhere equally, and so it comes from nowhere. The plain is illuminated without being lit. There are no shadows, or rather there are shadows everywhere in equal measure, which is the same as saying there are none. Colors are dampened. The brown is a wet brown. The gray is a steam gray. The green of the sparse clusters is the green of pond-water, of things seen through too many inches of atmosphere.

Sound is the fact of this place. The place is loud in a way that has nothing to do with any single source. There is no thunder, exactly. There is no wind-noise, exactly. There is no surf, exactly. What there is, constantly, in every direction, is the sound of a great deal of *something* moving against a great deal of *something else* — the air moving across the wet ground, the wet ground resisting, the air then moving across the film of water, the water rippling and lapping and kissing the stones, the stones grinding faintly against one another as the temperature shifts them, the temperature shifting constantly because the air is constantly moving against the water which is constantly moving against the air. It is the sound of a place that is working very hard to be what it is. It is the sound of erosion not as an event but as a *condition*. It is a roar that never peaks and never stops. After a while it becomes the only sound there is, and then it becomes no sound at all, because you can no longer imagine silence.

Fauna is rare. There are things, here and there, that move through the wet. A pale shape skitters at the edge of a pool. A long-necked something lifts itself from a stand of reeds and considers you from too far away, then lowers itself again. A bird calls, sometimes, but the call is immediately swallowed by the constant roar, and you cannot tell if you heard it or imagined it. The fauna that is here is old-fauna — not primitive but *elderly*, the surviving representatives of lineages that have been here as long as the place has been here, and have been bred by it into something specific and unrepeatable. They do not fear you. They do not notice you. You are not a category they have a response to.

The weirdness of this place is not the weirdness of a hallucination or a vision. It is the weirdness of a place that has been itself for so long that the idea of *itself* has worn thin. The horizon does not always behave as horizons behave. Sometimes it curves up at the edges, as if the plain is the inside of a shallow bowl and you are at the bottom of it, even though you are clearly not at the bottom of anything. Sometimes a far-off cairn or stone-line appears to *lift* off the plain and float for a moment before settling back. The water, when it pools, sometimes shows you a reflection of something that is not above you. The fog, when it thins for a moment, reveals a structure in the distance that resolves to nothing as the fog thickens again. The place has been so thoroughly worked over by its own conditions that it has begun to misbehave at the seams. Reality here is not *broken* in the sense of shattered — reality here is *thin*, like a stone worn through to a membrane, and the membrane occasionally shows what is on the other side, and the other side is not a place, it is just *more weather*.

The dynamic of the place is constant. There is no moment here that is not a storm moment, but the storm never intensifies into a climax because it is already climax, it has been climax for so long that climax has become the baseline. The water shifts. The fog rolls. The rain does not fall so much as *condense out* — the air simply becoming water around you, a slow accumulation on every surface, a steady soaking that does not pause. The wind is not a wind; it is a *circulation*, the entire atmosphere of the plain engaged in a slow churn, and you are inside the churn. The temperature fluctuates by small degrees and the fluctuations produce visible effects: steam lifting off a warmer patch, condensation darkening a cooler one, the brief appearance of a sheen that is not quite a rainbow and not quite a mirage. Things change in this place. Things are always changing. Nothing is settling. Nothing is settled.

And yet the place is old. That is the strangeness of it. This is not a young chaos. This is not a place in the throes of becoming. This is a place in the very long moment of *being*. It has been like this for longer than it has been like anything else. The wetness, the warmth, the roar, the churn, the thinness of reality — these are the conditions of *end*, not beginning. The continent has nearly finished becoming plain. The plain has nearly finished becoming wet. The wet is nearly finished becoming air. The air is nearly finished becoming weather. The weather is nearly finished becoming sound. The sound has been going for so long that it has become the medium itself, the way water is the medium of the deep sea, the way light is the medium of the upper air. You are in a place that is *still happening*, very loudly, in the moment of its own long finish.

There is no path here. There is no way to leave that is faster than walking. The cairns do not mark a route; they are just where they are, and the route is whatever you make between them, and whatever you make will be reabsorbed within a year, a season, a single shift of weather. If you go very far in any direction you will arrive at the same kind of place. If you go very far in every direction you will die, not from anything dramatic, but from the slow accumulation of being wet and warm and loud and dim and unceasing, the way a stone dies, which is to say the way a stone does not die, but is simply no longer the stone it was when you started looking at it.
