The swampland stretches past where any single eye could watch it, a wide wet plain under a permanent bruised twilight that doesn't quite qualify as night. Something in the upper air has scattered the light so thoroughly that no shadow falls anywhere — every surface glows with the same dull pewter luminescence, as though the clouds themselves have become the source rather than something to be obscured. You cannot tell where the sun is, or whether it is up at all. The horizon is simply where the reeds end and the gray begins.

The terrain is young, geologically speaking — barely older than memory. The low ridge of slag that runs east-west through the middle distance was poured here, you understand, though you don't know how you know that. It still steams in places where groundwater seeps across its surface, the runoff collecting in long silvery ribbons that pool and slip toward the central channel. Younger mounds rise from the marsh: clinker heaps, their dark sides cooling, their crests still too hot to have grown anything. Between them the water is the color of weak tea and smells of iron and of the particular sweetness that bacterial action leaves in standing pools.

Vegetation has not had long to make up its mind. Tufts of gray-green sedge cling to the drier crowns. A species of willow has colonized the slag ridge in single-file lines, their trunks leaned hard against the prevailing wind, their leaves a curious blue-gray that catches the ambient light and gives it back. Higher ground hosts tall stalks of something not quite grass, not quite reed, with seedheads that hang heavy and bearded, bowing down in the wet air. There is no tree canopy anywhere. The land is too new, too wet, too low.

The built presence is faint and repeated. Concrete piers emerge from the marsh at odd intervals, sometimes solitary, sometimes in long parallel rows that march out into the water and stop. Each pier supports a small shed — some intact, some opened like empty mouths, some half-melted to a shape that suggests they once were sheds but is no longer quite that. Brick foundations sit in the shallow water with nothing above them, the courses of brick still crisp, the mortar still lighter than the brick it joins. A single smokestack, very tall, very thin, stands far across the wetland, its upper third lost in the murk. Occasionally there is a glint of metal track or a curve of cast iron that once held something. Nothing is being used. Nothing has been used for a long time. But nothing has rotted either, and that is the part that presses on you.

Industrial evidence is everywhere in a subtle, secondhand way. The slag itself is one big industrial product, the residue of a furnace or a smelter or something more ambiguous whose name you can't quite place. The channel down the middle of the valley has the straightness of a thing made rather than grown. Pipes, sometimes broken, sometimes only disconnected, run along the higher ground, and from one of them — this you notice as you walk — a slow thick liquid emerges and feeds a tributary of the central waterway. The liquid is not water and not oil and not any substance you can later describe coherently. You watch a drop of it fall and it falls correctly, but when it hits the pool, the surface does not ripple outward so much as *inward*, gathering itself, and then resumes its stillness. You decide not to look at it again.

The fauna is dense and noisy and one of the only things that is unambiguously alive. The reed beds are full of a heavy rustling that is not wind. Something amphibious — round-bodied, pale, the size of a large dog — moves through the shallows, breaking the surface in long undulations that leave wakes which take too long to settle. Insects are present in clouds so thick they appear as a local darkening of the air itself; their hum is the loudest sustained sound in the valley, a wall of it that sits behind your ears, and you have to consciously listen past it to hear anything else. Birds — if they are birds — roost in the willows on the slag ridge, and from their throats comes a noise that resembles speech at a great distance: voices heard through several thicknesses of water, with the consonants missing. When you turn to face the sound, it stops, and when you turn away, it resumes.

The reality of this place is a coat of paint that is peeling at the edges. Up close, the young slag is slag, the brick is brick, the water is water. But the further you try to see, the less certain it becomes. Distant features duplicate themselves faintly — you can see one smokestack but you are never sure whether you are seeing one or two or five, their images stepping sideways in the murk like badly-tuned ghosts. The sunless light does not behave: it does not dim toward the horizon, it only ends, and beyond the gray boundary the marsh goes on in ways that the geometry of the foreground does not strictly permit. Walking north for an hour puts you where walking south for an hour did not. The hills in the deepest distance are not the right color for hills. They have the color of distant hills as remembered, or as painted, or as described in a report by someone who had only heard of hills.

You keep walking anyway. The ground is mostly firm under the sedge; only the lowest places give, and even then you can feel a strange resistance below the mud, as though something flat and horizontal is buried there, just under the surface, all through the valley. Some structure, maybe. Some floor. Whatever it is, it holds the land up, and the land holds the water, and the water holds the light, and the light doesn't fall.

Sounds, when they come, are mostly quiet ones. The insect wall. A slow metallic tick from somewhere near a pier, as if a pipe is cooling and contracting in the unchanging temperature. The wet slop of the amphibious things in the channel. A thin, recurrent hiss from the fresher clinker mounds, steam escaping from cracks that are not quite large enough to see. And, on the wind that is not quite wind, the distant birds repeating their unintelligible phrases — always just out of earshot of being understood, like a language you are standing too far from to parse.

Everything happens slowly here. The slag cools a little each century. The willows grow an inch a season. The water creeps a yard in a lifetime, redistributing itself according to gradients set down before you arrived, gradients that will not be completed for longer than you will be watching. The broken pipes weep their slow contents. The brick foundations wait. The birds keep saying their almost-words into the unreplying gray, and the smokestack in the distance — that one, or that other one, or that one — goes on standing in the murk it has been standing in, with the patience of something that is not waiting because there is anywhere else to be.

It is warm. It is wet. It is full of creatures who do not seem to mind any of it. You take another step, and the ground holds.
