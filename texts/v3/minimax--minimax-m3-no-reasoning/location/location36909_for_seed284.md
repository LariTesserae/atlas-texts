# The Hollow Summit

The place fits inside the broken crown of a spire, and the spire is one of many. You reach it by climbing through the throats of three younger volcanoes, all extinct so recently that their lava still sits black and glassy on the slopes, unsanded by any wind you can feel. There is no wind up here. There is barely air. The sky at this elevation has the indifferent violet of a bruise, and the sun, when it arrives, does not warm — it interrogates, throwing long blue-edged shadows across ridges that are still sharp enough to cut rope.

You do not see the pocket from outside. The spires are dense, almost touching at their tips, and the pocket is the *between* — a vertical seam of chambers carved, fused, bolted into the youngest of the basalt columns. It is not a large place. You could walk end to end in a quarter of an hour if the walkways weren't interrupted by shafts, by drops, by the running traffic of the colony itself. It is a pocket the way a fist is a pocket. The rock holds you whether you like it or not.

The rock itself is the first thing you learn about the place. It is sharp and dark, with the streaked red of unoxidized iron threading the black like dried blood in glass. Nothing has softened it. There has been no water to round an edge, no lichen to digest a face, no frost to lever apart a joint. The spires are young. The pocket inside them is younger — cut, fused, bolted, lit by industry in geological real time. A working seam in a young mountain.

---

The colony is not a city in the sense of streets and squares. It is a city in the sense of *layer*. Every horizontal surface that can bear weight has been claimed and built upon, and then built upon again. The original basalt floor is buried under a meter of alloy grating in some corridors; under that grating run pipes of dull metal carrying something that is not water — too viscous, too dark, and it hums faintly when the pressure is right. Above the grating, the colony climbs.

Lower levels: heavy industry. Smelters that never quite go cold, fed from chutes that descend the outside of the spire and disappear down into the still-hot vent below. The smelters are squat, thick-walled, sealed against the thin air. They do not glow so much as smolder — a deep amber, the color of old beer, the color of a coal bed that has not been told it is supposed to be ash. Around them, the air is the warmest thing in the pocket, and still it does not warm. The ambient temperature of the place is the temperature of an autumn evening at sea level, held steady by altitude and the sheer indifference of the stone. Workers here wear layers. Workers here *are* layers — gloves, hoods, faceplates, filter masks. The ones that breathe.

Mid levels: fabrication, refining, the smaller engines. This is where the colony earns its name as a *place* rather than a hole. The corridors widen here into chambers stacked like the cells of a beehive, each one humming at its own pitch. Stamping presses. Wire-draw towers. Assemblers whose purpose is not immediately legible unless you stand and watch for several minutes and see the slow emergence of a thing. The things they make are small, mostly. Components. The colony is a parts depot for the spires below.

Upper levels: habitation, control, the dim lights. Up here the architecture is more considered. Windowless rooms with thick glass ports that look out through drilled shafts at the bruised sky. Cables run in fat bundles along the ceilings, tied at intervals with ceramic clamps, occasionally shedding a fine dark dust that settles on every surface like a kind of soot. The lighting is amber and blue — the amber from the smelters rising, the blue from strip-lights that have been dimmed past the point of usefulness, more suggestion than illumination. You can see, but you cannot see well. Everything has soft edges. Everything is half a rumor of itself.

The light is the second thing you learn about the place.

---

The creatures are the third.

There is no plant life. There is nothing green. There is no chlorophyll, no mycelium, no spore-puff in any still corner. What there is, is *fauna*, and the fauna is everywhere.

At first you take them for machines. They move with the patient mechanical rhythm of things that do not tire. Small bodies — the size of a thumb, the size of a fist, occasionally the size of a forearm — segmented, many-legged, made of something between ceramic and chitin. They climb the walls. They cross the gratings. They pour through gaps you would not have seen if you hadn't watched for a full minute. They pour the way water does not pour here, in continuous streams and eddies, each one independent but the whole of them behaving like a single weather.

They eat stone.

You learn this by watching. A creature — a worker, you start to call it — will settle onto a bare patch of basalt and remain motionless for hours. When it leaves, the surface where it sat is pitted, faintly hollowed, the iron-threaded glass turned to a fine black powder. They eat the spire from the inside out, in microscopic bites, and what they cannot digest they excrete as the dust that drifts down from every cable and ledge. The colony lives in a kind of slow digestion of itself. The colony's economy, you eventually understand, is the creatures.

They are not machines. They are animals. They have — you realize, watching a clutch of them clustered around a thermal vent — a behavior that is not algorithmic. They jostle. They yield. They do not yield. They retreat from a worker who is larger than they are, and they press against a worker who is smaller, and they make a sound with their legs against the grating that is not mechanical. It is a sound like dry rice in a bowl, like paper crinkling, like the inside of an old clock. It is the constant sound of the place, and once you hear it, you cannot stop hearing it.

They are farmed. Cultivated. Engineered, perhaps — the colony's biotechnologists work in windowless rooms on the mid levels, and what they are doing to the creatures is not conservation but *improvement*. The creatures are bred for appetite, for tolerance of the cool dry air, for the ability to digest the particular mineralogy of this one spire. There are rumors of creatures older than the colony, deeper in the rock, that eat what the bred ones cannot. The colonists leave those alone. You do not improve a thing that is already perfect at its work.

The creatures are also, you realize on your second or third day, *loud* if you count them right. Each one is quiet. A billion of them is a soft, pervasive, papery susurrus that fills the pocket from floor to ceiling. It is not a sound that demands attention. It is the sound of a held breath. It is the sound you do not hear, until you step into a chamber where, for some reason, they have not gone, and the silence there is enormous.

---

The dynamics of the place are not the dynamics of weather. There is no weather. There is no rain, no fog, no frost, no dew. What there is, is *the colony*, and the colony is its own weather.

Shaft 7, on the eastern face, begins to cough on a Tuesday. You do not know it is Tuesday. You learn later that it was Tuesday. A cough of fine black dust, then a longer exhalation, then a sustained plume. The colony reacts in seconds, not minutes. Workers descend on the shaft from three directions. Smelters in the lower levels throttle back. Fauna in the upper corridors thicken, drawn by the disturbance, jostling toward the smell of the plume. A creature-tracker on the mid levels marks the event in a log that will never be read. By the time the plume has settled into a slow trickle, the shaft has been re-capped, the workers have retreated to their layers, the fauna has dispersed back into the walls. Total elapsed time: four minutes.

You did not see it happen. You heard it. The whole colony shifted, briefly, and the shift registered as a change in the hum. The amber of the smelters dropped a register. The papery susurrus rose. Then both settled.

This happens twenty, thirty times a day. Shafts cough. Smelters surge. Creature populations bloom and contract in corridors that were empty yesterday and are full today. The colony is not stable. The colony is not trying to be stable. The colony is *turbulent*, in the way a fire is turbulent — a thing that maintains itself only by consuming, that would collapse into ash if it ever paused. The peak is being digested. The creatures eat. The smelters melt. The pipes carry the melted rock up to the assemblers. The assemblers make parts. The parts go down into the spires, into the vents, into the mountain's still-warm throat. The mountain is being *replaced*. Slowly. At industrial scale. The pocket is a wound, and the wound is being dressed, and the dressing is also a wound, and the wound will never close.

You live inside the dressing for a while. You learn the rhythms. You learn which corridors are quiet in the morning shift and which are loud. You learn that the creatures do not bite, but they will, given provocation, swarm — and a swarm is not dangerous so much as it is *humiliating*, a small dry tide of segmented bodies pouring over your hood and gloves and into every seam of your clothing, and you stand very still until they decide you are not stone and move on. You learn that the dim blue strip-lights in the upper chambers are dim on purpose, because brighter light agitates the creatures, and agitated creatures eat faster. You learn that the papery susurrus is louder after a shaft-cough, and softer after a smelter has run hot. You learn to read the colony the way a sailor reads a sea.

You do not learn where the colonists came from. They have been here a long time, or a short time. The pocket feels both ways. The bolts in the rock are corroded in some shafts and bright in others, and no one you ask can tell you which shafts were cut first. The creatures have been here longer than the colonists, perhaps. Or the creatures were brought here. Or the creatures *made* the colonists, in some long-ago act of unrecorded engineering, and the colonists are now the creatures' way of making more creatures. You do not ask too directly. The colonists find the question impolite.

---

At the very top of the pocket, in a chamber no larger than a closet, there is a glass port that looks out at the spires. The other peaks, sharp and black and red-threaded, crowd the view. They are young. They are sharp. Between them, far below, the lower world is a haze of pink and pale ochre — the dry mineral plains that the spires rose out of, the salt-fields, the sealed basin where no water has lived for a million years. The light from that lower world is the same dim light that fills the pocket, rising up the spires like a slow tide.

You stand at the port for a long time. The colony hums below you. The creatures move in the walls. The papery susurrus is everywhere, and you are inside it, and you have been inside it long enough that the silence of the lower plains feels like a kind of death.

You turn away from the port. You descend. The colony takes you back.
