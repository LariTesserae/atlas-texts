A high plateau, raised and barely worn. The stone here is fresh — not weathered into the rounded shapes that older geology has, but still close to the angular fracture it had when it came up from below. Rain falls often, and where it falls it sits in shallow basins that don't yet have names, slowly carving the first tributaries of rivers that will, in some future geological span, become something. The wetness is in the air, which condenses against anything that holds a temperature different from itself. The wetness is in the ground, where water never sinks deep because the bedrock is too newly broken to have porosity. The wetness is in the lichen that is just now beginning to consider the rocks.

Cold. A wind comes over the edge of the plateau and stays for days. The temperature rarely lifts above what would make a person uncomfortable without effort. Snow falls in the shoulder seasons and lingers in the shadow of outcrops even at the height of summer.

The settlement sits across this. It is not a city in the older sense — no concentrated core, no radiating density. It is spread. Tracts of construction separated by raw ground, by wet meadow, by open rock. You can stand on a roof and see for tens of kilometers in any direction, and most of what you see is not built. The buildings are made of materials that match the local stone so closely that the place looks like a kind of architecture the geology is doing to itself — straight lines where the rock wants to be angular, surfaces that hold their shape without visible seams, edges that don't chip. There is almost no ornament. The construction looks extruded rather than assembled. The same color as the outcrops beside it. The same temperature to the touch, mostly.

The technology is in the materials, and not visible anywhere. No towers. No antennas. No obvious machines. The buildings are warm without chimneys, lit without visible lamps, connected by surfaces that move without engines. People travel between them on paths that don't appear to have been laid. Whatever the technology is, it has folded itself so thoroughly into the place that nobody points at it. It is not invisible; it is just not separable from the rock. It behaves like the climate. You don't remark on the climate. The buildings behave like weather. You don't remark on the buildings.

The fauna is everywhere. Birds — many of them — wheel over the plateau in seasonal passages that the city's presence doesn't interrupt. Small grazing things move through the wet meadows in tight herds, quick and rust-colored, with thick coats and small metabolisms. Predators follow at a distance, patient, unhurried. They don't seem to mind the construction. The buildings don't repel them, don't even seem to interest them. They cross over and around the structures the way they cross over and around stones. The city has been careful, or the tech has been careful, or both, and the result is the same.

Weather is the loudest thing. Wind on the open rock. Rain on the few flat roofs. Condensation ticking somewhere that doesn't look like a gutter. Sometimes a crack of distant thunder from a system coming over the edge of the plateau. Inside the buildings, almost nothing. The quiet of a place where the ambient machinery has been smoothed away. You can hear your own breathing. You can hear, if you attend, the small sounds of animals at the edge of hearing — a call, a footfall, the soft tearing of a tuft of something at the margin of where vegetation has begun.

The plateau is in motion, in its slow way. Water is finding its routes. The first thin soils are accumulating in the lee of outcrops. The lichen is beginning. The animals are coming and going in patterns that don't reference the buildings but pass through them anyway. The city is in motion too. Tracts of construction shift over years; new ones rise on ground that has only just become interesting. The technology makes this easy, and the easiness is part of why the place is quiet. There is no construction noise, because there is no construction in any sense that would produce noise. There is just the slow re-arrangement of a settlement across a plateau that is itself still arriving.

At the edge of the plateau, where the rock falls away, you can see clouds below. The city is above them, mostly. Not always. Sometimes the cloud-base lifts and the settlement is briefly inside a white world, and the buildings lose their edges and become shapes in the mist, and the animals are sounds more than presences, and then the cloud-base drops again and the plateau is itself again — vast, cold, raw, sparsely inhabited by people and densely by everything else, going on with its slow work.
