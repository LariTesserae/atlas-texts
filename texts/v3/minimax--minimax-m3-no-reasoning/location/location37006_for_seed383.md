# The Lithic Reaches

Beneath a sky of hard, untempered white, the city sprawls across the basin like a wound that has been waiting to close for ten thousand years. The light here is merciless — a sodium-pale glare that pours down without warmth, illuminating everything and warming nothing. Shadows exist but have no soft edges; they fall like cuts.

The basin sits below the surrounding plateau by perhaps two hundred meters, a shallow declivity carved by waters that no longer run. Long ago — so long that the erosion has flattened even the memory of the original topography — rivers descended from highlands now indistinguishable from the rest of the sky-line. They pooled here. They carved. They dried. What remains is a pavement of pale sediment stone, cracked into irregular polygons, each fissure filled with a fine pale dust that puffs up when disturbed and settles again with the patience of something that has been settling for geological time.

The city is built into this floor rather than on top of it. The builders — or the building, it is unclear which verb applies — worked downward, excavating chambers and passages and low spiraling ramps that descend from the basin's cracked skin into a denser layer of older rock. The constructions are urban in scale but not urban in logic. There are avenues wide enough for a dozen abreast, but they do not lead anywhere coherent. They terminate in plazas that open onto other plazas, which open onto other plazas, a fractal recursion of public space that suggests either a civilization that valued gathering above destination or one that had forgotten how to finish what it started. The walls are fitted stone, fitted with a precision that the current inhabitants could not replicate — no mortar, no chinking, each block cut to the curvature of its neighbors with an intimacy that suggests hand-work performed across centuries by a patient population that has since departed.

No plants grow here. None have grown here for a long time. The stone is too smooth in places, scoured by the lost waters, and in other places too fractured to hold root. What organic matter falls — and things fall, carried in on the wind or deposited by the few birds that wheel overhead at impossible altitudes — simply desiccates, bleaches, and becomes indistinguishable from the dust. The few patches of color in the basin are mineral: a green-black stain of old moss that has been dead for so long it has become geology, a rust-colored seam of iron running through an exposed wall, the pale blue of copper salts weeping from a drainage channel.

But the water — the water returns. Not as rain, not reliably, but as condensation. Every evening, as the bright light begins its long slow withdrawal, the temperature differential pulls moisture from somewhere — from deep aquifers, from atmospheric rivers passing far overhead, from the lungs of the few breathing things that remain — and it settles onto the stone. A film first, then droplets, then in the deeper excavations actual standing pools that reflect the last of the light like eyes opening. By morning the stone is damp to the touch, and the air carries a smell of mineral wetness, of calcium and silica dissolved and re-emerging. Then the light returns, and the water retreats again into whatever hidden reservoirs feed it, and the stone dries, and the cycle repeats. The city's builders designed for this. Every plaza has a slight concavity at its center. Every avenue has a shallow channel running along its edge. They built a city meant to catch its own water, drop by drop, from an atmosphere that provides almost nothing.

The inhabitants are not builders. They are not human, though they occupy the human-scale spaces without complaint. They are small and numerous and brown, with many limbs and a carapace that clicks softly when they move. They live in the uppermost layer of the city, in the chambers closest to the surface, where the morning condensation pools deepest and lasts longest. They do not maintain the structures. They do not understand them. They use them the way water uses stone: by being in them, by finding the paths of least resistance, by slowly wearing the surfaces they touch into shapes that suit them. In some of the deeper plazas, their generations of passage have polished the floor to a mirror gloss. In others, where their tunneling has undermined a wall, a collapse has occurred, and the rubble has been repurposed as a nursery, a midden, a place to die.

They are turbulent creatures, despite the silence. Their colonies pulse. Brood-chambers flare with activity for a season, then empty as the young disperse into the lower warrens. Entire districts are abandoned and recolonized in cycles that follow no visible logic — or follow a logic written in moisture gradients and mineral content and the slow migration of something edible in the stone. Their sound is the sound of the place: clicking, scraping, the faint grinding of many bodies moving through passages, the occasional sharp percussion of a warning or a dispute. When the wind blows — and it blows, often, funnelling down from the plateau rim and howling through the avenues in a way the builders cannot have intended — the sounds of the inhabitants are drowned, and the city itself seems to breathe, the wind singing through narrow slots in the walls that were perhaps once windows.

Far below, in the deepest excavations, there are chambers no living thing enters. The stone there is older than the city, older than the basin, older than the lost rivers. It is a different color — a deep red, almost organic, veined with something that catches light and throws it back in wavelengths the eye cannot quite process. These chambers are flooded, permanently, with water that is not quite water — too dense, too still, too cold. Things move in it, occasionally. Shadows that are too large for the spaces they occupy. The light from above does not reach here. Whatever illumination exists in these depths is its own, and it is not the sodium white of the sky but something older, something that pulses in rhythms that make the small brown inhabitants of the upper city press themselves flat against the stone and wait for the pulse to pass.

The city endures. The light endures. The water comes and goes. The small things click and scurry and breed and die, and in their dying they polish the floors of a civilization they will never understand, built by hands that are now dust indistinguishable from the dust in the cracks. The wind sings. The deep water pulses. The basin holds its breath, as it has held it for ten thousand years, as it will hold it for ten thousand more.
