The place has no name that anyone living there would think to give it, because no one living there can speak, and no one passing through stays long enough to ask what it is called. It is, by most measures, a kind of room — but the word *room* implies walls one can put one's back against, and the place has nothing so accommodating as a wall. What it has, instead, is the suggestion of a wall: a curved inner surface that arcs overhead and away into a dimness that is not quite dark, not quite lit, but the color of a closed eye remembering light. The place is below something. This much is certain. There is rock above, and rock below, and the air between them is warm and very still, and it has been still for a long time.

The floor is a pale mineral, somewhere between bone and salt, and it is not quite solid. When first a foot falls upon it, the surface gives a little — a soft, dry compression, like the top of a drum — and then holds. It has the texture of something that was once powder and has been lightly fused: a crust, a sinter, the residue of a long evaporation that left its ghost behind. Underneath this crust there is more of the same, deeper and softer, and underneath that, a long way down, something that is not quite liquid and not quite solid, a kind of dense paste that shifts when the whole place shifts, and the place shifts often, though slowly, the way a sleeping animal shifts in its dreaming.

From this floor there grow things.

They are the first thing one notices, after the dimness, because the dimness is the kind that one only notices once the eye has given up on noticing anything else. The growing things are tall. Some of them are as tall as three men, and slender, and they are not green. They are a deep wine color, almost black where the light is thinnest, and a kind of bruised violet where the light gathers at their bases. Their surfaces are smooth and faintly glossy, like the inside of a shell, or like skin. They do not move in any wind, because there is no wind, but they have a slow, internal movement, a kind of peristalsis, as if they are drinking something out of the floor and passing it upward through themselves. If one puts a hand against one — and one is careful, because the air here is full of sound and one does not want to draw attention to oneself — the stem is warm, warmer than the air, and faintly resilient, and the warmth has a pulse.

The air is loud. This is the second thing one notices. The sound is not a sound one can locate. It is everywhere, or it comes from the floor, or it comes from the growing things, or it comes from the curved ceiling far above, which is hung with other growing things, hanging down, root-thick, and pale, and wet-looking though the air is dry. The sound is a low, continuous tone, somewhere between a hum and a moan, and layered into it are smaller sounds: clicks, as of beads shaken in a leather pouch; a faint, papery rustle, as if a great many small wings are being folded and unfolded in slow rotation; and a deeper, occasional thud, felt in the crust of the floor before it is heard, as if something very large, somewhere far down, has turned over. The sound never stops. It has the quality of being the place's own voice, the sound the place makes by existing, the way a kettle makes a sound by being about to boil. One stops hearing it after a while. Then one hears it again, and is startled, and then stops hearing it again.

The growing things are not all the same kind. The tall wine-colored stems grow in clusters, three or four together, and at the top of each stem there is a kind of cupped opening, like a bell with the clapper removed, and inside the cup there is a small, dense growth that is a different color — a pale, almost luminous yellow, the color of the inside of a honeycomb. These yellow growths pulse. They pulse in a rhythm that is not quite the rhythm of the stems' internal movement, but that is close to it, the way two clocks in the same room will drift in and out of phase. When a yellow growth pulses, it brightens slightly, and at the same moment there is a small, high sound, almost a chirp, that joins the general hum for an instant and then dissolves into it. From time to time, one of the yellow growths detaches. It does not fall. It lifts. It rises a little way into the air, turns slowly, and then drifts, with a kind of lazy authority, toward the hanging pale growths on the ceiling. When it touches one of the ceiling growths, there is a soft, wet sound, and the yellow growth is absorbed, and the ceiling growth pulses once, in its own slower rhythm, and the pulse travels down the hanging stem and into the ceiling and is gone.

There is no water. There is moisture, of a kind, but it is not water. The air has a faint, mineral dampness to it, the way the air in a cave has a taste, and the ceiling growths are slick with a thin, clear liquid that is thicker than water and does not run. If one touches it, it is cool, and slightly sticky, and it evaporates slowly, leaving a faint residue, a powder, a dust, that smells of nothing one can name. The stems, when one cuts one — and they can be cut, though the tool required is heavy and the place provides none — bleed a dark, slow sap that is almost black and that hardens, on contact with the air, into a brittle red lacquer. The yellow growths, if one manages to catch one before it rises, are full of a thick, sweet paste that is the only edible thing in the place, and that tastes of overripe fruit and iron.

The creatures are the third thing one notices, and they are the most difficult to describe, because they are not quite creatures. They are shapes, and they move, and they have a kind of attention. There are several kinds, or several sizes, or several speeds; it is not clear which. The largest are the size of a large dog, and they move along the floor with a slow, boneless gait, as if they are made of the same paste that lies under the crust. They are the color of the floor. They have no visible eyes, but they have a front, and the front is oriented toward whatever they are attending to. They eat the fallen yellow growths that miss the ceiling. They do not catch them; they wait beneath the clusters, and when a growth lifts, they follow it, and when it lands on the floor, they fold around it, and the growth is gone. They are the source of the deeper thuds: from time to time, one of them will stop, and settle, and press itself into the crust, and the crust will yield, and the creature will sink, slowly, into the floor, and the floor will close over it, and there will be a long, low thud, and then silence, and then the creature will be somewhere else, a little way off, and it will be moving again, and the cycle will repeat. They are not aggressive. They are not afraid. They seem to regard any presence other than themselves as a kind of weather.

The smaller creatures are more numerous, and more varied. There are ones that live on the stems, clinging to the glossy surface with many small, dry feet, and that are the same wine color as the stems and almost invisible against them. They move quickly. They make the clicking sound. They feed on the yellow growths, too, but in a different way: they chew at the rims of the cups, and the chewed edges weep a little of the dark sap, which hardens, and they eat the lacquer. There are ones that drift in the air, though the air is still, and that have the same lazy, drifting motion as the yellow growths on their way to the ceiling. They are paler, and translucent, and they glow, faintly, with a light that has no source, and they make the papery rustling. They do not seem to eat. They do not seem to do anything. They are part of the sound. They are the place's weather, as the larger creatures are its fauna.

The floor, in the open spaces between the clusters of stems, is marked. The marks are faint, and old, and they are not marks one would recognize as writing, though they have the regularity of writing. They are shallow grooves in the crust, and they form patterns — concentric rings, intersecting lines, a kind of long, looping script that spirals inward toward a point that is always just out of sight, because each pattern ends before it reaches its center, and the next pattern begins, and ends, and so on. The marks are not made by any tool one can see. The crust is not scratched; it is shaped, the way a tile is shaped before it is fired. Whoever — whatever — made these marks was working with the floor, not against it. There are also, in a few places, small arrangements of objects: a circle of pale, flat stones, each the size of a hand; a low pile of the dark, brittle lacquer, shaped into a rough cube; a single, very long stem, laid straight, that has been carefully split and hollowed and that has, at one end, a small, sharp point of a different, harder mineral, set into the hollow and bound with more lacquer. This is a tool. It is the only tool in the place. It is very old, or very well made, or both. It lies where it lies, and it has been there for a long time.

There is a light, though the light is dim. The dimness is not the dimness of a place the sun does not reach. It is the dimness of a place that is its own light, and that is not very bright. The light comes from the ceiling growths, faintly, and from the drifting creatures, faintly, and from the yellow growths in the cups, faintly, and from the floor itself, which has a kind of phosphorescence, a slow, cold glow that rises out of the crust when the crust is disturbed. The light is enough to see by, barely. The light is the color of the inside of a shell. It does not flicker. It does not change. It is, like the sound, the place's own light, and it has been the same for as long as the place has been the place.

The place is below the surface of something. The crust of the floor is not the bottom of anything. Above the curved ceiling, above the hanging growths, there is more rock, and more, and the rock is warm, and the warmth comes from the rock itself, from a deep, slow heat that has been there a long time and will be there longer. The place is a kind of organ. It is a kind of lung. It is a kind of mouth, open, breathing, humming, tasting the dark air with its pale, hanging growths, and the dark air tastes of iron and of the powder of the place's own making. The place does not change. The clusters of stems grow, and the yellow growths lift, and the ceiling growths absorb them, and the creatures on the floor eat what falls, and the marks in the crust remain, and the light remains, and the sound remains. The place has been doing this for a long time. It will do it for longer.

The place is warm, and dim, and loud with its own voice, and strange, and still, and it is below something, and it is alive in the way that a river is alive, or a weather is alive, and it does not know it is a place, and it does not mind, and it is very beautiful, in the way that things are beautiful when they are not trying to be.
