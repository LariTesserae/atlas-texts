# The Pith-City of Ulshuur

## I. The Container

One does not arrive at Ulshuur. One descends into it.

There is a crack in the basalt of the high cold steppe — a fissure perhaps two hundred meters long and no wider at the lip than a grown body can squeeze through — and at the bottom of that fissure, after a rappelling of forty meters down rough mineral walls, the traveler finds a lip of stone, and beyond the lip, an opening.

The opening is not a cave. It is the mouth of a bore.

The bore is ancient. Older than the basalt. Older than the steppe. It was cut — by what, no remaining tool records — through something even older still, a substrate of dense compressed chert that rings when struck and holds, even after ages, the faintest ghost of polish where hands once guided tools. The bore is perfectly circular in cross-section, twelve meters across, and runs true and level into the earth for what the local dwellers — there are dwellers — say is "a day and a night of walking, if you walk slow and do not turn back."

I am told it is closer to nineteen kilometers. But then, I am told many things in Ulshuur.

The air in the bore is cool and motionless and smells of green sweetness so concentrated it coats the tongue.

## II. The Substrate

The walls of the bore are not stone anymore. They have not been stone for a very long time.

What they are is *pith*.

A living pith — white, fibrous, spongy when pressed, cool to the touch, and everywhere exuding a thin clear sap that, in the absolute darkness of the tunnel, accumulates in slow icicles along the lower curve of the walls. The pith is the living tissue of something so old and so committed to its niche that it has forgotten, if it ever knew, that it is a living thing. It is *the* living thing. The entire nineteen kilometers of bore is lined, wall to wall, floor to ceiling, with the compacted vascular cambium of a single organism — or so the consensus runs among the scholars who live here, and there are scholars, though they will tell you consensus is a thing they mistrust.

The pith is not smooth. It is *worked*. Into its surface, over what must be many centuries of patient labor, the dwellers of Ulshuur have cut — with tools of bone, of chert, of fired clay, of hard wood imported at great cost from the surface — an entire city.

## III. The Architecture

I will describe a district, and you will understand the whole.

The Fourth Ward is a series of chambers carved into the pith along the upper walls of the bore, reached by a staircase that spirals around the bore's interior in a helix of more than a full turn. The steps are pith too — cut down, left as tread — and they compress slightly underfoot, returning slowly to shape, so that walking the staircase feels like climbing the throat of something breathing.

The chambers themselves are round, three to five meters across, opening onto ledges that overlook the central void of the bore. Between the chambers, narrow bridges of pith arch across, their undersides carved into shallow vaults. From the bridges hang — and here is what one never gets used to — the *roots*.

The roots descend from somewhere far above, from the ceiling of the bore and from its walls, white and branching, each as thick as a man's arm in the upper reaches and tapering toward the floor. They are the organism's water-seeking structures, though what water they seek in this dry mineral world is a question the scholars have argued about for generations. They do not touch the floor. They hang in the air of the bore, swaying — not in any wind, for there is no wind, but in some slower oscillation, some internal pulse of the organism, so that over the course of an hour one can watch a root tip drift a finger's width to the east and back.

Between the roots, on the ledges, in the chambers, on the bridges: the dwellings of the people. Tents of woven fiber — *not* plant fiber, there are no plants here, but the long tough hairs that grow along the pith-veins, combed and spun and woven into something like felt, pale grey, slightly luminous in total darkness because the dwellers have bred, or selected, or merely invited a photophore into the fiber that gives off a faint greenish glow. So the city, when one looks up or down the bore from any vantage, is a constellation of dim green stars strung along the spiral of its staircase, each star a household, each household a family, each family a knot in the long rope of generations who have lived here in total darkness for longer than their histories remember the surface.

## IV. The Light

There is no light. I have to say this again, because the green glow of the felt tents and the white radiance of the pith itself — that cool, that steady, that sourceless emanation from the living walls — conspire to make the eye forget. The brain invents shadows. The brain invents a sky. The brain is a fool.

The pith glows. It glows because it is alive and alive things, even in the absence of any sun, must move energy around, and moving energy around makes light. The scholars have a name for the process. I will not inflict it on you. The glow is constant, dim, slightly bluish-white, brighter near the veins where the sap runs slowest, dimmer near the chambers where the dwellers have cut deepest. In the central void of the bore, where no pith touches, there is true darkness — a black column nineteen kilometers long, vertical (the bore is bored at a slight angle but near enough to vertical), and the dwellers call it the Spine. Nothing hangs in the Spine. Nothing crosses the Spine. Falling into it would be death, though no one has ever fallen, because the dwellers have raised, over generations, a net of woven pith-hair across the bore at intervals of every hundred meters, a latticed floor that catches the slow drip of sap and the slower fall of anyone foolish enough to misstep.

## V. The Sound

The city is loud.

I do not say this lightly. I say it because I want to convey the *wrongness* of it — the way the sound does not match the place. This is a city in total darkness, in a dead mineral world, in the gut of something alive. It should be silent. It is not.

The pith *creaks*. Constantly. The organism is nineteen kilometers long and twelve meters in diameter and it is, after a fashion, growing — has been growing, very slowly, for ages beyond reckoning — and as it grows its walls shift, its roots thicken, its veins pulse with sap, and all of this, in the silence of the underground, makes a sound like a wooden ship in a slow sea. Groaning. Popping. The long low moan of wood under strain, rising and falling in no rhythm the ear can catch, but the body can. After three days in Ulshuur one's heartbeat has synchronized to it, and one sleeps soundly, and on the surface afterward the silence of the steppe feels like a kind of death.

The dwellers add to this. They are people — yes, people, I will come to that — and people make sound. Voices. Hammers. The scrape of chert on pith. Children's feet on the woven bridges. A species of large flightless insect — *no*, I was told no fauna, but the dwellers keep a thing that might be an insect, or might be a symbiont, or might be a part of the pith itself that has learned to detach — that clicks its mandibles in the deep night, a rhythmic ticking like a clock, and the dwellers use it to tell the hour.

And above all of this, rising through it, the dwellers themselves. They sing. They sing constantly, habitually, the way surface people talk — a stream of vocalization, often tuneless, often interrupted, sometimes breaking into formal songs that echo up and down the bore and are taken up by other voices in other wards until the whole city rings with a single syllable sustained for what seems like minutes. The songs are not beautiful. They are not meant to be. They are *navigation*. In the dark, in the dense acoustic of the bore, sound is how one finds one's way, how one knows where one is, how one knows who is near. The dwellers hear the shape of a voice and know the family. They hear the timing of a footfall and know the ward.

## VI. The People

They are pale. Of course they are pale — they have never seen a sun, nor any star. Their skin is almost translucent, and beneath it the blood runs visible, a deep maroon almost black, the color of blood that has given up its oxygen long ago and found other business. Their eyes are large and, in the green glow of the tents, reflect the light back as a startling silver, so that to walk the spiral staircase in the deep hours is to be passed by silver-eyed figures who murmur greetings in a language that has no word for *light* and twelve words for *pith* in its various states.

They are short. Generations of soft living in a soft environment have done what generations of soft living always do. They are broad in the hand and clever in the finger. Their tools are beautiful — chert blades hafted into handles of fired clay, needles of bone for sewing the felt, scrapers of polished stone for working the pith without killing it (the dwellers do not kill the pith; they are very firm on this; the city is a tenancy, not a conquest). Their pottery is, against all reason, the most accomplished I have seen — thin-walled, precisely formed, fired in small kilns that burn the shed pith-hair and nothing else. They have no metal. They have no need of metal. The pith provides.

They eat the sap. They drink the sap. They weave the hair. They carve the walls. They pay rent to the organism — I do not use the word lightly — in the form of careful tending: pruning the roots that grow too thick, opening new chambers where the pith is healthiest, clearing the veins when they clog, seeding the walls with their own waste so the organism can reclaim the nutrients. In return the organism keeps them cool, keeps them fed, keeps them hidden, keeps them *alive* in a world that has, above and below, very little interest in keeping anything alive.

## VII. The Age

I should say something about the age of this place, because it is the age that makes it what it is.

The bore was not made by the organism. The bore was made first, by something else, something that had the technology to cut a perfectly circular tunnel nineteen kilometers long through solid chert. What that something was, no one in Ulshuur knows or pretends to know. The chert is old. The steppe above is young — geologically young, a few million years of basalt flow. Below the basalt, the chert is *ancient*. The scholars, when pressed, will say: older than anything else we have measured. Older than the current arrangement of the land. Possibly older than the land itself, in the sense that the chert may be a fragment of a substrate that predates the formation of this world and was here, waiting, when the world formed around it.

The organism is younger than the bore but older than the basalt. It is, in the scholars' estimation, not native to this world. It came — how, they do not say — through the chert, or with the chert, or in the chert, and found the bore, which was already empty, and grew into it. It has been growing for a very long time. It has, the scholars believe, nearly finished. Its growth has slowed to a crawl. Its roots hang lower each century. Its pith is denser, drier, more like wood and less like flesh. In another few thousand years — perhaps many thousands; the timescales are uncertain — it will stop. It will become truly inert. The glow will fade. The walls will harden. The sap will cease. And the dwellers, who have never known any other home, will have to decide what to do next.

This is the central preoccupation of Ulshuur. Not philosophy, not art, not even politics — though there is politics, there is always politics. The central preoccupation is *succession planning for the end of their world*. They discuss it in low voices in the felt tents. They sing about it in the long hours. The scholars write treatises on it, in a script scratched into clay tablets and stored in a vault in the Fifteenth Ward, and the treatises are long and careful and full of disagreement, and none of them conclude, because there is no conclusion to be had.

## VIII. The Air

One more thing. The air.

It is breathable. I should mention that, because it seems unlikely, and it is. The organism, in its endless slow chemistry, splits the chert to get at trace minerals and releases oxygen as a byproduct. The oxygen has been accumulating in the bore for — well, for as long as the organism has been there. The atmosphere of the bore is approximately Earth-normal, slightly richer in oxygen, slightly poorer in nitrogen, with a trace of something sweet that is the sap vapor. The dwellers have adapted to it. Surface visitors — there have been a handful, mostly scholars who came down and never left — find themselves slightly giddy for the first day, then adjust.

It is this air, more than anything, that makes Ulshuur feel like a city rather than a cave. The air is *used*. It has been breathed and rebreathed and breathed again by generations of people and by the slow lungs of the organism itself. It is warm in a way that pith is warm — not very, but enough. It moves, slightly, in currents set up by the temperature differential between the glowing walls and the dark Spine. A breath of air, in Ulshuur, is a thing that has touched everything and everyone.

## IX. Arrival and Departure

I came down the fissure. I will go up it. I am already dreading it.

The surface, when I reach it, will be a high cold steppe of black basalt under a sky I have not seen in months, and the wind will be like a blade, and the sun will be like a wound, and I will stand there blinking and shivering and feeling, for the first time in my life, that the world is *too large* and *too empty* and *too bright*.

I will miss the creak of the pith.

I will miss the song rising through the Spine.

I will miss the green glow of the felt tents and the silver eyes of the dwellers and the cool slow breath of an organism that is, in its own vast unhurried way, keeping watch over its tenants as they keep watch over it.

I will go up. They will sing me out. The sound will follow me up the fissure and out onto the steppe and I will carry it with me for the rest of my days, a faint singing in the back of the skull, and I will not be able to explain to anyone where it comes from or what it means, and I will not try.
