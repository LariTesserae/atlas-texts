# The High Basin of Saeren

Stand at the lip of the pass and look down into Saeren, and the first thing you feel is the quiet. Not the quiet of a sleeping place, but the quiet of a place that has never been loud. Nothing chirps. Nothing moves in the underbrush. The air sits on your skin like damp cloth.

The basin is roughly oval, perhaps three leagues across and two deep, cupped in the arms of a young mountain range. The mountains are not old. You can see it in every line of them — the ridges knife-sharp, the scree slopes fresh as though something had only just finished breaking the stone. The rocks are dark, almost black in places, a dense igneous kind that rings when you kick it. They have not yet learned to be smooth. Rain has barely begun its long work. A thousand years from now these slopes will be rounded and gentler; for now they cut upward in ragged seams, and water has only just started to find its way down them in pale threads.

And water is everywhere.

It falls often, though you would not call it rain in the usual sense. The clouds sit inside the basin most days, low and pale grey, brushing the shoulders of the higher ridges. Everything below them is damp. The rock sweats. The path — such as it is — is not a path but a sequence of flat stones laid across permanent seepage. Every gully carries its stream. Every hollow holds its puddle. Where the ground is flat enough, shallow pools gather and stay, ringed by green. You walk through air that is wet on your lips and wet in your lungs.

The warmth helps. Saeren sits higher than the clouds should let it be warm, but the basin traps heat the way a bowl traps broth, and the moisture holds it close. The result is a kind of high greenhouse — not unpleasant, not jungle-suffocating, but the steady warm damp of a place where a shirt never quite dries. Ferns grow in the crevices of walls. Moss climbs anything left still long enough.

The settlement is not really a settlement. It is a scattering. A stone house here, with a roof of split slate weighted against the wind. A low enclosure there, the size of a sheepfold, though there are no sheep and have never been. A road of fitted flagstones runs from one cluster to the next, but the road is longer than it needs to be, because the houses have not been built close together — they have been built *apart*, each on its own patch of dry-ish ground, each with a few yards of cleared ground around it where nothing taller than moss is allowed. The clearing is the only sign of human presence, because the people of Saeren do not cut the forest; they only keep it at arm's length.

There are perhaps forty buildings in the whole basin, and perhaps a hundred people. You would not know this without counting, because the buildings are made of the same dark stone as the mountain, and the roofs are the same slate as the scree. The stonecutters work with hand tools — chisel, point, maul — and they do not carve decoration. They fit. The buildings look, from a distance, like a heap of larger rocks among the smaller ones. From close up they are careful work. The walls are double-thick and dry-laid in the lower courses, mortared only where the masons have learned it is needed. The doorways are low. The windows are narrow slits, deeper than they are wide, because the wind comes down the mountain in sudden squalls and the people of Saeren have always minded the wind.

There is a central place, if you keep walking — a flagged square where six paths meet, and a covered structure of heavier timbers than the houses use, raised on stone pillars so the damp air moves beneath it. This is the meeting house. The timbers are green, or were green; they have not been replaced in a long time. The whole place has a slightly silvered look, as if everything has been out in the weather for one season more than it ought to have been, and then no further season ever came. The thatch on the meeting house is intact and old.

The people are not part of this description. They walk through it. A woman carries a wooden bucket of water uphill along the flagged path. A boy sits on a wall and works at a piece of stone with a smaller piece of stone, slowly, patiently, producing chips. Smoke rises from one chimney — one only, because the day is warm — and disperses into the cloud before it climbs ten feet. But these are small notes. The basin itself is the thing.

The vegetation is not jungle and not tundra. It is the lushness of a place that has had time and water to grow but no large things to eat it. The ferns are waist-high in the lowest hollows. There are alders in the wetter ground, and a stunted kind of mountain holly, and trailing ground-cover that throws up pale flowers in the slimmer light. Nothing is dense. The density of the place is low, everywhere — the trees stand apart from each other, the houses stand apart from each other, the people move slowly and alone. The space between is part of the design, though no one designed it. It is the way Saeren is.

In the upper part of the basin, where the slopes steepen toward the ridge, the rock begins to take over again. Here you can see how young the country is. The outcrops are splintered. A boulder that fell this winter has not yet collected moss on its underside. The streams that come down have cut channels no deeper than a hand's width. A path that was made twenty years ago is still a path; nothing has erased it, nothing has rerouted it. Time here has been, for some reason, slower.

The water is the only sound. The streams have voices — small, various, ceaseless, but small. A stone dropping somewhere is a clear event. The drip from the eaves of the meeting house is countable. When someone speaks in the square, the voice carries to the far houses without effort, because there is nothing to absorb it. When no one speaks, there is the water.

The light is the colour of wet slate. The sun, when it shows itself, is diffused through the cloud layer into something pale and sourceless, and when it does not show itself the day is simply a brighter grey than the night. There are no shadows with hard edges. The basin does not darken decisively at any hour. It just dims.

Nothing moves at speed. The water moves at the speed of water. The clouds move at the speed of clouds. The people move at the speed of people who have somewhere to be and are not hurried. The boy with the stone chips has been on the wall for a long time and will be on the wall for a long time still. This is not the slowness of decay. It is the slowness of a place that has arrived. The mountains will not rise further. The streams will not cut deeper. The houses will not be added to. The path between them will not be rerouted. The ferns will grow to the same height they grew to last year. The lichen will creep at the same rate. The water will fall in the same places.

Saeren is wet and warm and high and still. It does not change. It is not waiting to change. It is the kind of place that a person enters and, after the first hour, stops thinking about leaving — not because they cannot, but because the leaving seems to belong to a different kind of country, the kind that is going somewhere. This country has already gone.

The dark stone. The pale water. The scattered grey buildings. The alders standing apart. The one chimney. The smell of wet fern and cold ash. The quiet that is not the absence of noise but the absence of life that would make noise.

That is Saeren.
