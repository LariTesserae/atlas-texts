# The Cistern of Linden and Brass

You find the entrance in a place no cartographer has bothered to name — a notch between two folds of mountain, half-hidden by a waterfall that, when you step through, is not a waterfall at all but a curtain of condensation pressed flat by the altitude. Behind it, a tunnel. The tunnel goes down for a long time. The air gets warmer. The light, which had been grey and mineral, begins to pick up a greenish copper undertone, and the stone under your boots gives way, somewhere around the third hour, to a floor that rings when you strike it.

You are standing in the Cistern.

It is not large. The kind of small that has weight. A cathedral nave would swallow it three times over, and yet when you stand at the rim and look down — *down*, which is the only way to look, because the Cistern is a well, a shaft, a stone throat dropping away beneath you — the dimensions seem to refuse measurement. Your eyes keep wanting to give it more room than it has. This is a property of the place, you will come to understand, not a failing of your sight.

The walls go down. Two hundred feet, maybe three. Hard to say. They are built, every inch, of something that was once alive.

**Brass and green wood and the bones of trees.** This is what the world is made of here, in the Cistern of Linden and Brass. The retaining rings that circle the shaft at every interval are trunks of a tree you have never seen on any mountain — a pale wood, almost butter-yellow when freshly cut, but centuries old now, blackened to a deep umber in places and elsewhere still holding its original cream color, like bone half-buried in a bog. The trunks are enormous: each one a hundred feet long, fitted end-to-end, pegged with hand-forged iron pins the size of your forearm. Between the trunks, the wall is riveted sheet brass, green with verdigris, stamped in repeating patterns of fish and owls and the small, long-limbed lizards that live nowhere else. The patterns are not decoration exactly. They are drainage, or aeration, or prayers — no one living remembers, and the few who still come here to maintain the place do so by rote, copying what their grandparents copied, and the original intent has soaked into the wood.

The wood is not dead. This is the first strange thing you will notice, and it will take you a while to accept it. The linden trunks, even after all this time, even here in the warm dark, are not rotting. They are *rooted*. From the seams between the rings, from the iron peg-holes, from the joints where the brass sheeting laps and curls, pale rootlets push out into the air and hang downward, and where they touch the wet stone they take hold again, and thicken, and become trunks themselves, and bear leaves. The leaves are small, heart-shaped, a translucent yellow-green, and there are millions of them. They soften every surface. The whole interior of the Cistern is furred with them, moss and leaf and the fine felt of new growth, and the sound that comes down to you when you stand at the rim is the sound of all those leaves breathing in the updraft — a slow, sustained whisper, like the inside of a shell, but wetter, and with something else folded into it, a deeper note, the resonance of the place itself.

**It is warm.** The stone at the rim is warm to the touch. The air is warm. The water that falls in a fine, almost imperceptible mist from somewhere above — from the false waterfall, from the condensation of the mountain's breath — is warm, and it is everywhere, not a rain so much as a saturation. You are standing in a cloud that has decided, out of courtesy, to be visible. It moves in slow sheets and coils, and when it clears for a moment you can see the other side of the shaft, and the lamplight there, and the figures moving along the galleries.

**The water is the second strange thing.** The Cistern is not dry. Far from it. The bottom of the shaft — visible from the rim only as a shifting gleam, a long way down — is a lake, or a sea, or a single body of standing water that, when you listen, you can hear has tides. There is a slow, deliberate sloshing. The water is not freshwater and it is not saltwater. It is faintly green, faintly sweet, and if you cup your hand in the mist and taste it, it is neither cold nor hot, but has the mineral tang of the brass and the green-wood resin of the linden. Fish move in it. You can see them, sometimes, when the light catches — pale, heavy-bodied things with whiskers like catfish and the patient, torpid air of deep-water creatures, though they are not deep-water creatures. They are Cistern fish, and they have been down there a long time, and they are old.

The water rises, in places, to meet the galleries. Galleries: yes. There are rings of them, wooden walkways cantilevered out from the walls on brass stanchions, at irregular intervals all the way down. From the rim you can see three or four of them; the rest descend into mist and lamplight and the green dark. The galleries are inhabited. This is the third strange thing, and the most ordinary, which is how you know you have come a long way from any place you have been before.

**There are people here, and they are working.**

Not many. The Cistern is not a city; it is something more like a workshop, or a refinery, or a single extended machine tended by a guild. You count, from the rim, perhaps forty figures visible at any one time — some on the upper galleries, some on the lower, some descending in the open cages of the hoist, which creak and groan and send up a smell of old iron and warm oil. They wear long coats of waxed canvas over leather aprons. They carry lamps. They carry tools you don't recognize, or recognize only in part — a sort of hooked rod, a shallow pan, a long needle. They move with the unhurried competence of people doing a job that will take more than one lifetime and that no one alive will finish.

What they are doing, you will eventually learn, is *listening.*

**The Cistern is a foundry, but it is a foundry for sound.**

The brass sheets that line the walls are not all the same thickness. Some are thin as paper; some are thick as your thumb. Some are stamped with the fish-and-owl patterns; some are pierced with holes; some have had small weights riveted to them so that they hang free and can vibrate. The linden trunks, similarly, are not all the same density. Some are heartwood, heavy and still; some are sapwood, lighter, and these have been hollowed out and fitted with reeds and stopped with wax. The whole interior of the shaft is, in this way, a vast distributed instrument — not a single organ but a thousand small ones, each tuned to a different note, and the mist that fills the air is what sets them singing. As the water rises and falls, as the temperature shifts by a fraction, as the wind in the false waterfall above changes its pitch, the Cistern plays itself. There is always a sound. The sound is always changing. It is never loud in the way a factory is loud, but it is *full* — a chord that never resolves, that hums in your sternum and in the roots of your teeth and in the brass pegs of the walkways.

The workers tune it. This is their work. They climb down into the shaft with their hooked rods and their long needles, and they adjust the wax stops in the hollow trunks, or they add a small weight here, or they file a notch there, and the chord shifts, becomes slightly more consonant, or slightly more dissonant, depending on what is needed. You do not know, at first, what is needed. You will come to understand, after a few days, that the Cistern is being tuned *to itself* — that the workers are trying, with the patience of monks, to make the place sing its own name.

**The fauna.**

The Cistern is not empty of other life. The pale fish in the lake below. The lizards on the walls — small, quick, with long toes and a grip like a climber, greenish-brown, basking on the warm brass. They eat the insects. The insects are extraordinary: they have, all of them, the soft, papery wings of moths, but they fly by day, in the lamplight and the mist, and some of them are as large as your spread hand. They are attracted to the lamps the workers carry, and the workers, in turn, catch them in shallow pans of oil — not to kill them, you will see, but to harvest something. A waxy secretion from their abdomens, used to coat the threads that bind the brass sheets. The moths breed in the upper galleries, where the linden is thickest, and their caterpillars feed on the leaves, and their droppings fall into the lake and feed the fish, and the fish feed — you do not know what feeds the fish. Something in the deep water. You have not seen it.

You have not been to the bottom.

**The light.**

Normal. This surprises you, after everything else. The lamps are oil lamps, mostly, of the old kind — a reservoir, a wick, a glass chimney — but there are also, in the lower galleries, electric lamps of an early and heavy kind, caged in brass, their filaments glowing a deep amber. The light they cast is warm and yellow, and it catches the mist and the leaves and the verdigris and makes the whole shaft glow from within, like a paper lantern. The lamps are not always lit. The workers, you will learn, light them according to the sound — when a particular note is wanted, when a particular gallery is being tuned, when a descent is being made. The lighting is part of the work. There are hours when the Cistern is almost dark, and the sound is different then, and the moths come out, and the air smells of waxy secretion and warm metal.

**The sound.**

It is the thing you will remember, if you remember anything. The sound of the Cistern, at the rim, on a still day, is like standing inside a bell that has been struck an hour ago and has not stopped ringing. It is not loud, exactly. It is *insistent.* It is composed of a low fundamental — the brass, the water, the stone — and over that, a middle register of wood and reed and the small percussive notes of dripping, and over *that*, high and fine, the almost-inaudible singing of the moths' wings and the leaves' breathing. The workers move in it the way a fish moves in water: without effort, without apparent notice. When one of them speaks to another, they do not shout. They do not need to. The Cistern carries sound the way a canyon carries sound, and better — more selectively, more attentively. It seems, sometimes, to be listening to them as carefully as they are listening to it.

**The age of the place.**

The Cistern is old. You cannot tell how old. The linden trunks, where they are not still living, are blackened and polished to a deep shine by centuries of damp and handling, and the brass has the soft, matte patina of metal that has been touched and re-touched by many hands. The patterns stamped into the sheets are worn, in places, almost smooth. The walkways have been rebuilt, and rebuilt again — you can see the ghosts of older galleries behind the current ones, like the foundations of houses in a field. The hoist mechanism is a patchwork of eras: a hand-cranked winch of clearly ancient make, fitted with a later iron brake, fitted with a still-later electric motor in a brass housing, the wiring of which has been repaired so many times that the cable is thicker in some places than the original rope it replaced. The workers speak a language you do not understand, or understand only in fragments, and they use, in their work, units of measurement that do not correspond to any you know.

You are told, by one of them — an older woman with oil-blackened hands who offers you, on your second day, a small cup of the Cistern water — that the work has been going on for nine hundred years. You are not sure you believe her. You are not sure, by then, that the number means anything. The Cistern has the feel of a place that has been here longer than the mountain above it, and that will be here after the mountain is gone. The erosion is so complete that the original purpose — if there was an original purpose, if the Cistern was not always already itself — has been absorbed into the maintenance. The thing and the tending of the thing are the same thing.

**You ask, eventually, what they are tuning for.**

The woman with the blackened hands looks at you. She is not surprised by the question. She has heard it before, or some version of it, from other visitors, over the years, over the centuries.

*We are tuning it,* she says, *so that when the water rises, the sound will be correct.*

She does not explain what will happen when the sound is correct. She does not know. Or she does, and the explanation is one of those things that lives in the body and not in the language. She goes back to her work. The hoist groans. A moth, as large as your hand, lands on the brass railing beside you, and folds its papery wings, and is still.

**Below you, the lake moves in its slow tide.**

The leaves breathe.

The Cistern hums its long, unfinished chord, and the workers climb, and descend, and listen, and adjust, and the mist falls, and the fish swim, and the wood roots, and the brass sings, and the centuries pass like the passing of a hand across a sounding string.
