# The Petal Mires of Verís

The basin stretches for what you can only estimate in the time it takes a single call to die—maybe four miles, maybe six, the distance folding in on itself as sound moves through the warm air. It is a low place, barely an indentation in the long flank of a continent that slopes gently here, as if the land itself were tired and had settled slightly into its own weight. The grass comes up to your thigh if you are standing in it, which you are not, because there is no walking here. There is only the tram, and the tram moves.

The mires are not marshes, not exactly, though the language lacks a better word. They are dampnesses with intent. The water arrives in pulses—a slow capillary rise from beneath, a thin film that appears between the stems of the red grass in the hour after dawn, then seems to climb the plants themselves, glazing each blade until the whole valley becomes a single wet membrane, faintly iridescent, breathing. When the wind moves through, the grasses don't bend so much as shimmer against one another, a million tiny surfaces all releasing a thread of condensation at once. That is the softest sound, almost a sub-sound: a collective whisper of water leaving a surface.

Above the mires, the rest of the place asserts itself.

The kites hang. You must call them kites because they are not kites; nothing living can be, at the size they are. Imagine a geometry: a broad black chevron the length of a small house, four lateral planes extended like a paper crane's wings, each plane vibrating at a different rate so that as the creature drifts overhead it produces a chord, a major seventh, a suspended something. There are thousands of them. They are tethered to the upper canopy of the *orí* trees, the great pale columns that rise out of the mires on stilt-roots, and each tether is a filament so fine it is only visible when the light is bad, which is often, because the light here is bad in the particular way that only lush places can manage.

The sky is the color of old milk. Something at the top of the atmosphere—an engineered dust, a swarm, no one you could ask knows for certain—diffuses the sun into a presence rather than a point, and what reaches the basin floor is the gentlest possible light, grey-gold, with no shadows to speak of. The orí trees do not need shadows. They have already been growing for the span of your great-grandparents' lives, and they have the lumpy, fused, over-built look of things that committed to a design centuries ago and have been elaborating on it without consultation ever since. Their trunks are pale, almost cream, the color of the inside of a coconut, and they are not barked so much as *layered*—rings of a material that looks both mineral and biological, stacked on top of one another, each older ring slightly extruded beyond the younger, so the whole trunk is a column of rims. Some are hollowed. You can see, in the higher reaches, the round dark mouths of the structures built into them: dwellings, walkways, the loops and curves of suspended architecture that the people here call *the high row* and that you, watching from the tram window, might call improbable, except that nothing about the place invites you to call it anything but what it is.

The tram itself is a remarkable concession to the fact that no human or human-derivative can stand in the mires for more than a few hours without the water getting into their shoes and then into their socks and then into the next week of their life. It runs on a causeway of fused silica, a low raised road of something that is not quite glass and not quite stone, a substance that was grown here rather than poured. The engineers speak of it as a *coagulate*. It hums. Everything here hums. The frequency of the causeway is the frequency of the bone behind your ear, which is a piece of information that the tram operators have clearly decided you do not need.

You sit at the window. Outside, between the trunks, the city reveals itself in layers.

It is a vertical city, mostly, because the ground is given over to the mires and the *kites* and, increasingly, to the slow upward growth of the orí themselves, which no longer need to be planted, having graduated to a stage of self-propagation that the city has simply made peace with. The oldest levels are deep in the mires themselves—stone causeways long abandoned, the foundations of the very first *high row*, half-sunk and softened, colonized now by a certain kind of pale moss that pulses faintly in the late afternoon. The middle levels are the inhabited ones: a hundred thousand rooms, balconies, covered bridges, the entire vocabulary of a dense vertical settlement, all of it threaded into and around the orí, never cut, never amputated, the city and the trees having become the same structural problem with the same answer. The upper levels are air, mostly—open platforms, wind-catchers, the dense cables that hold the kites, and the kites themselves, which are not pets.

The kites sing. This is the part you have to get used to.

Each kite is a chord. Each chord is slightly out of tune with the one beside it. The combined effect of ten thousand slightly-out-of-tune major sevenths drifting in a wind that is itself slightly out of phase with itself is a sound that is not a sound but a *texture*, a thing you feel in the back of your teeth, a sound that has decided to become weather. It does not stop. The locals call it *the warmth in the ears*. They mean it literally. At certain hours—when the wind drops, when the mires give up their last exhalation of vapor—the drone thickens until you can feel it in the bones of your wrist, and then there is a click, almost inaudible, a kind of phase-shift, and the whole chordal field *adjusts*, retunes itself, and you realize that what you are hearing is a single very large system regulating itself. The kites are computing. The kites have been computing for, by some estimates, longer than the city has existed.

You cannot tell, from the tram, whether the city is built for the kites or the kites are kept by the city. The relationship is the kind of question that the people who live here do not find productive. They live in a forest that sings. The forest lives in a city that listens. Both are warm, and damp, and dim, and neither one has been in any hurry about the other for a very long time.

The animals of the place are mostly airborne, mostly small, mostly a kind of iridescent green-blue that the eye keeps wanting to call black. They pour out of holes in the orí trunks at certain hours—dawn, dusk, and once in the deep night, for reasons no one has plotted successfully—and the tram window fills with them, a streaming that blurs the view, a moving pane of tiny wings. They feed on the condensation. They feed on the smaller kites. They feed on each other. There is a great deal of sound from them, but it is lost in the larger sound, in the warmth in the ears, in the hum of the coagulate, in the slow exhalation of the mires.

The smell of the place is a single smell: warm water, sweet with something organic, slightly metallic, the way a river smells after a long rain through a forest. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is the smell of a place that has been doing its own thing for a long time and has run out of reasons to vary.

What else is there to say.

There is a wind, and it comes from the south, and it carries, in the late afternoon, a faint pollen or spore, something yellow and fine that the locals will tell you is a courtesy of the orí, though the orí have not, as far as anyone can establish, ever been consulted. The wind is what moves the kites. The kites, moving, sing differently. The city, listening, adjusts. The mires, breathing, lift another film of water onto another billion blades of red grass. The tram moves. You sit at the window and watch a vertical city and a computing forest conduct their long, damp, deafening negotiation, and you understand, for a moment, why the light here is dim: it is because the place is not interested in being seen. It is interested in being heard, and being warm, and being, in the most unhurried and absolute sense, itself.

The tram is the only way in.

The tram is not, yet, the only way out.
