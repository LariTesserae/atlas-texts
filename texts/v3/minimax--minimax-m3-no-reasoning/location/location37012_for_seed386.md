# The Steamwall Expanse

You arrive — if you can call it arriving, when there is no road, no path, no trail worn by feet or wheels — somewhere along a basalt ridge that runs east and west like the spine of some long-buried creature. The ridge crests at what the locals, if there were locals, would call fourteen thousand feet, but here there are no locals. There is only the place.

## The Air

The first thing is the wetness. Not rain, not mist, not anything so definite. The air itself feels like it has been wrung out — saturated, warm, almost liquid. You breathe it and it fills your lungs like warm broth. Condensation beads on your skin within seconds of stopping. The sun, somewhere overhead, is diffused through a high ceiling of cloud that looks less like weather and more like the underside of a lid. Light reaches you as glow, not as beam.

It is hot. Improperly hot for this elevation. You are higher than treeline ought to be higher than, and yet the air sits at a temperature more suited to a coastal swamp. Your breath does not steam. Your sweat does not evaporate. It just accumulates, and runs, and the next breath replaces it.

## The Rock

The ridge itself is dark stone — andesite, perhaps, or something older. It is split, fissured, weeping. Every crack secretes water. Every horizontal surface holds shallow puddles that mirror nothing because nothing above them is worth mirroring. The stone has been here a long time. You can tell because it has stopped trying to be anything. It does not fracture further. It does not crumble. It weeps, and is patient about it.

Below the ridge the land drops away — not in cliffs but in a long, broken slope that descends for miles into a haze of vapor. The slope is composed of the same dark stone in pieces: boulders the size of cabins, slabs tilted at angles that suggest they once stood upright, scree fields that have gone still and begun to grow things. The whole descent is a ruin that has outlived the thing that ruined it.

## The Green

And things do grow here.

The lower slopes are buried under vegetation so dense it is hard to see where one plant ends and another begins. This is not jungle — too high, too rocky. It is something else: a low, matted riot of mosses, ferns, lichens, clubmosses, and a thousand small succulent-looking plants with waxy leaves that hold the wet air like sponges. The color is almost fluorescent. Greens so saturated they seem to generate their own light. Reds, purples, the occasional shock of yellow where a flowering ground-cover has opened in the constant damp.

There are trees, but not many, and they are strange. They grow only in pockets of accumulated soil — pockets that have taken centuries to fill. They are old, gnarled, leaning, festooned with epiphytes. Their trunks are dark with permanent moisture. Their bark peels in long strips that hang like wet cloth. Where they stand, the undergrowth is even thicker, fed by their drip-line.

The ridge itself, at this elevation, has no trees. Only the stone, and the mosses that crust it, and the water that runs between.

## The Water

Water is everywhere.

It emerges from the rock face in thin sheets. It gathers in basins carved by no one. It slides down the slope in rills that braid and rebraid until, far below, they form something that might be a stream, or might be a river, or might be the beginning of a watershed. From the ridge, in the right light, you can see the lower valley shining — a vast silver-brown smear where water has pooled and run and pooled again across a flat that stretches for miles.

There are sounds of water everywhere — not the crash of falls, but the constant susurration of a thousand small flows. Trickle. Trickle. Drip. The sound of a place that is perpetually, gently, draining.

## The Sounds of the Place

Mostly quiet, though quiet here has texture.

The water. The wind, which is steady, which has been steady for so long that the plants have grown around it. The occasional deep groan of stone settling somewhere below — not an earthquake, just the slow adjustment of a place under its own weight.

Bird calls. Strange ones. A low hooting that comes from nowhere visible. A sharp trill, then silence. The whir of something insectile. The crack of a branch, somewhere in the dense growth below.

And — farther off, muffled by distance and vapor — a rhythmic huffing, a mechanical breath. Distant, but present. Something that does not belong, that has been placed here, that is operating.

## The Industry

You cannot see it yet. The haze is too thick, the slope too broken. But the sound gives it away.

There is machinery somewhere down there, beyond the next ridge, around the shoulder of the valley. It runs continuously. It has been running for as long as the stone has been weeping. It is not loud, but it is constant — a low vibration that you feel more in your chest than hear with your ears. Something has tapped into the water, the slope, the constant runoff, and is converting it.

You do not see smokestacks. You do not see buildings. But you see, far down the slope, where the vegetation thins at a long straight line, a structure — a long dark form, low against the hillside, trailing pipes that descend into the wet green like roots. It is not a building so much as a built thing — a mechanism buried in the landscape, fed by it, indistinguishable from it except for the glint of metal where metal should not be.

The locals, again, if there were any, would call it the Lung. Or the Kidney. Some organ-machine. It does what organs do: it takes in, it processes, it puts out. Whatever it is putting out, the valley below is rich in a way that has nothing to do with the natural fertility of wet stone and sour soil. The green down there is too green. The water down there runs in channels too regular.

## The Life

Things move.

On the ridge: lizards, dark and quick, basking on the wet stone in the brief moments when the sun finds a gap in the clouds. Insects with too many legs, slow in the cold that comes with altitude despite the wet heat. A bird — something hawk-shaped — circling without sound.

Below: a denser fauna, hidden. You hear it more than see it. Hooves on stone. A snort. The rustle of something heavy moving through undergrowth. Somewhere down in the wet green, a population of things is going about its business, and you are the only thing on the ridge that does not belong to that population.

## The Feeling

There is no fear here. The place is too old for fear, too settled into itself. The water has been running too long. The stone has been eroding too slowly. The machinery has been humming too steadily. You are a temporary thing standing on a permanent one.

What you feel, instead, is the particular vertigo of being at altitude in the wet — the sense that the air is thicker than it ought to be, that the horizon is closer than it should be, that the clouds above you are not a ceiling but a slow-moving floor for something higher up.

The sun shifts. The water continues. The machine continues. Somewhere below, a branch cracks and something moves away through the green.

You stand on the ridge, sweating in the humid air, and the place does not notice you. It has not noticed anything in a very long time.

It is, in its way, beautiful.
