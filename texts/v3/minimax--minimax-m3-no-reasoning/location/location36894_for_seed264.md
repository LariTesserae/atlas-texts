# The Basin of the Held Note

The first thing is the silence that isn't silence. The place hums. It has always hummed, you understand, and will always hum. Not the hum of machines — there are no machines left that you would call machines — but the hum of a shape, the way a bottle hums when you blow across it, except the bottle is three miles wide and the breath is the wind.

You arrive at the rim and look down into it. Not a crater exactly. A terrace. A succession of terraces, like an amphitheater carved into a low basin, except the carving was never meant as carving — the terraces are the residue. They are the cooled overflow of something that happened a long time ago, when the basin was a different kind of place, when there were vats and channels and metal that moved when it was hot. The metal moved and was poured and cooled in the air and now it sits in the shape of a staircase going down, except the staircase is three hundred meters deep and three kilometers across and made of black glass.

Mostly black. There are zones of it. The outermost terraces are the oldest and the driest, and they have weathered to a dull gunmetal that powders underfoot if you walk on them. The middle terraces are darker — closer to obsidian, with the conchoidal fracture, the razor edge if you stumble. The inner terraces, the lowest, hold water in thin sheets across their surfaces, and these are the most reflective, near-mirrors, and you can see the sky in them upside down and very still.

The sound comes from the wind moving through the channels. There were channels once, cut to move something — slag, slurry, hot iron, you cannot tell anymore — and they were cut into the rock in straight lines and right angles, an industrial geometry. Now they are just grooves in the glass, and the wind moves through them and they resonate, each at its own pitch, and together they make a chord. The chord does not change. The wind does not vary. The geometry does not vary. The chord is permanent.

The water is where you notice the damp. You do not see rain here often. The basin sits in something like a rainshadow, the long ridge to the west holding the clouds. But the stone is warm, even at night, because it holds heat, and when the air cools — and it cools, because the elevation is low and the air is thick — moisture condenses on every surface. The vertical walls of the terraces sweat. The channel grooves collect the sweat into thin rivulets. The rivulets run until they reach a flat surface and pool, and the pools are warm and taste of iron and the inside of a battery. You can drink from them. They never dry up because the condensation never stops, but they never rise either, because the runoff equals the evaporation exactly, and this has been true for a hundred years and will be true tomorrow.

The warmth is the warmth of held heat. The black stone absorbs during the day and releases at night, and the air above the stone is always a few degrees warmer than the air above the dirt around it. There is a thermal belt. Insects — the only fauna that stays — live in this belt, and you can see them in the late afternoon, hanging in the air above the terraces, not moving much, holding altitude. Flies, small wasps, once a dragonfly but that was an accident, blown in from somewhere else. There is a lizard that lives in the cracks between terraces, a rust-colored thing, and it eats the wasps and it is the largest animal you have seen here and it is the length of your thumb. Birds do not land. The light is wrong on the glass, perhaps, or there is nothing to eat. They cross overhead in the mornings and do not come down.

The vegetation is the part that strikes you if you are new to the place. You expect, in any landscape, something green. Even in a desert there is a scrub, a lichen, an opportunistic weed. Here there is almost nothing. The slag is too smooth, too recently cooled in the geological sense — recently meaning within the last century, which is nothing to stone but is everything to lichen — and too lacking in the right minerals. The iron-rich glass does not break down. What does break down is the powdery outer terrace, where the weathering is most advanced, and there you might find, if you look very hard, a single species of something — a black crustose lichen, perhaps, or a colony of cyanobacteria, or some nameless biofilm that has learned to live on iron. It is the only color in the place, and it is not green, it is black-green, and it covers less than a tenth of one percent of the available surface. You could walk the basin for a day and not see it.

You walk down the terraces if you walk. The footing is uncertain. The glass is sharp. The powder is loose. On the middle terraces you pick your way along the channels because the flat surfaces are too slick. In the inner terraces, near the water, you walk on the dry ground — narrow ridges between the pools — and these ridges are warm under your hand.

There is no shelter. There are no structures. There is no roof, no wall, no ruin standing upright. The shapes that look structural — the right-angled channels, the level platforms, the regular spacing of the terraces — these are not structures. They are the cooled memory of structures, the negative space where structures used to be. The vats are gone. The pipes are gone. The trucks, the conveyors, the small buildings where men wore hard hats — gone. What remains is the shape they made in the slag while they were there, the geometry they imposed on the pour, the mold they left behind in negative. The place looks industrial because it was made industrially, but it is not industrial now. It is geology. It is mineral. It is the way stone happens to be shaped here, and the shaping was done by intention a long time ago and the intention is gone and the shape remains.

A truck, or what was a truck: in the third terrace down, you find a shape that might have been a vehicle. It is melted. It is a blob the size of a truck, with hints of wheels and a cabin, but the metal has flowed and resolidified and it is hard to be sure. It is the same color as the slag around it. If you did not know it was a truck, you might think it was a boulder. Maybe it is a boulder now.

A pump: on the fifth terrace, half-submerged in a pool, a shape that was once mechanical. Pipes radiate from it. The pipes are intact for about three meters in any direction and then they merge with the floor. You can hear, if you put your ear to it, a faint sound from inside the pump — not mechanical, not motion, but the trapped air moving with the temperature, a sigh, a thermal tick. It is not alive. It is the metal adjusting to the day.

The sun: the light is ordinary. Midday light, the light of any warm place. It comes down at the angle that light comes down at and makes shadows where shadows should be. The black glass does not reflect it so much as absorb it and re-radiate it. The pools reflect it perfectly. The light here is not strange. The strangeness is in the geometry that the light reveals.

The weather is the same weather. There is wind from the west, the prevailing wind, and it blows every afternoon at the same speed and in the same direction because the basin is shaped so the wind is funneled and accelerated, and this has not changed in a long time. The wind makes the chord. The wind also brings, once a month or so, a cloud, and the cloud drops its rain on the basin in a single afternoon storm, and the rain runs through the channels and fills the pools above their normal line and then evaporates or sinks within a day and everything returns to equilibrium. You can tell when a rain has happened because the glass is wet, and you can tell when a rain has not happened for a while because the glass is dry and the pools have receded to their permanent lines, but these fluctuations are small and the place is the same.

Time is the thing you cannot quite hold in your head. You stand on a terrace and the wind blows and the chord sounds and the water condenses and the lizard moves in its crack and the sun moves across the sky and you think: things are happening. But they are not. They are repeating. The chord is the same chord. The condensation is the same volume. The lizard is the same lizard, or its daughter, indistinguishable. The sun moves but the shadows it casts are the same shadows, in the same places, at the same times, every day. There is no season. There is no weather that builds or destroys. There is no erosion in the sense of change — the erosion is complete, the shape is settled, the balance is achieved. What is left is the surface tension of a system at rest, and you are standing in it, and the wind blows through the channels, and the chord sounds, and it has sounded, and it will sound.

You leave, or you don't. The basin does not notice. The lizard does not notice. The chord does not pause. The place is a held note.
