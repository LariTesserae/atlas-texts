# The Kiln

The valley stretches like an opened palm, fingers of ridgestone reaching up from the basin floor toward a sky the color of tarnished brass. It is high here — high enough that the air has thinned to something almost dignified, almost deliberate, each breath a small ceremony conducted between the ribs. The elevation is not so severe as to be hostile. It is, rather, a kind of insistence. The body understands it is a guest.

The basin itself is broad. You could lose a city in it and never hear the city's complaints. The floor is a mineral aggregate, an ochre-to-bone palette of weathered feldspar and ancient basalt, all of it cracked into a tessellation that, from any height, resembles nothing so much as the dried bed of some unimaginable sea. Except there has never been water here. The cracks are thermal. The cracks are old. The cracks are, in their slow patient geometry, the most articulate thing in the valley.

There is no wind.

There is a *substitute* for wind, which is more or less continuous: a low, droning hum, felt in the sternum before it is heard in the ears, the aggregate sound of ten thousand small engines breathing in the distance. Not engines in the sense that anything was built. The valley is not built. There are no walls, no turbines, no smokestacks, no cathedrals of industry. And yet the *sound* of industry is everywhere, saturating the air as if the rock itself were operating at some vast remove. The hum has a pitch. It modulates, almost. It would be wrong to call it mechanical. It is more as if the mineral had learned to *resonate*, had remembered something the planet forgot ten million years ago and was now humming it back into the air in obedience to laws that have no business applying.

This is the part the eye refuses.

At the edges of the basin, the ridgestone rises in long, weathered spines — *hoodoos*, almost, except these are not the work of water. They are the work of *heat*, of time, of processes that the geologic vocabulary of the old world does not contain a noun for. Their surfaces are dark and lustrous in places, glazed by something that is not glass and not obsidian and not varnish but has a quality of all three. The glaze catches the high light — a sun that is bright, that is present, that is not yet cruel — and throws it back in dull flares. The spines lean. Some lean toward the basin center as if listening.

And the fauna.

It is here that the valley stops being a place and becomes a *proposition*.

They come in the morning. There is no word for *morning* here either, only a brightening, a shift in the brass of the sky toward something almost golden, almost amber, a light that has a taste of metal. And they *come*. From where is unclear. The basin floor, perhaps. The cracks. The air itself, having finally condensed enough to remember that it had been asked to be a body. They rise out of the tessellated ground the way steam rises from a struck match — first as a tremor in the geometry of a crack, then as a thickening, then as a *shape* that is, in the first half-second, neither animal nor machine but a kind of *diagram* of intent.

There are many of them. There are so many of them that the eye, looking out across the basin, sees a *surface* rather than a population — a second tessellation overlaid on the first, a living grid of slowly-moving forms, each one roughly the size of a large dog, each one wrong. Their bodies are long and segmented, jointed in places that have no business being jointed, plated in something that is not chitin and not steel but behaves, in the way it clicks and rings, as a conspiracy between both. When they move, they make a sound — a *chattering*, a *singing*, a percussive industrial music, the sound of small parts finding small tolerances, multiplied by ten thousand, layered until the hum that the valley exhales is no longer hum at all but *voice*.

They are not hostile. This is important. They are not curious. They are not afraid. They do not, in any observable way, *acknowledge*.

They are *working*.

What they are working on is unclear. What they are working *with* is also unclear. But the surface of the basin, in the brightening, is alive with their passage — a slow, deliberate redistribution of something, perhaps heat, perhaps the mineral dust of the valley floor, perhaps nothing at all. They do not carry things. They do not construct things. But they move with the focus of those who carry and construct, and the sound they make together is the sound of *labor*, dignified and unhurried, the sound of a system that has been at its task since before any observer arrived and intends to continue long after any observer leaves.

A single one, near enough to study, is worse than the multitude.

Up close, the segment of its body that presents itself to the eye is a *ribbon* of moving parts — wheels, almost, or cogs, or some recursive cousin of both, each one turning at a different rate, the whole assembly flexing along a spine that is itself flexing along a body that is itself flexing along the slow intention of the creature's transit. There are no eyes that the eye can find. There are no mouths. There is no head, exactly, only a *front* — a tapered end, a point of orientation, a place where the clicking is loudest, where the small parts move fastest, where the glaze on the plating is thinnest and the strange light of the valley seems to *enter* the body as if through a seam.

It does not turn to look at you. It does not turn away. It *continues*, and the fact of its continuing is, in its own patient way, more unnerving than any reaction could be. The clicking, near-up, is intricate. There are overtones. There are frequencies the ear invents because the brain refuses to admit it is hearing them.

You begin to suspect — and the suspicion is not a thought but a *temperature*, a *weight*, a *pressure* in the inner ear — that the creature is not made *of* the small parts. That the small parts are made *of* the creature. That the plates and the cogs and the ribbons of motion are not its body but its *language*, and that it is *saying something* in a tongue whose every phoneme is a turning wheel, and that the *saying* has been going on for longer than the valley has been a valley, and that the valley is, in fact, *what it is saying* — that the ridgestone is *phoneme*, that the tessellation is *grammar*, that the hum in the air is the *sound* of a sentence being slowly, patiently, industrially completed across a timescale that makes mountains look like punctuation marks.

You do not have to believe this. The valley does not require belief. It requires only that you be present while it continues.

In the brightening, the creatures thicken. In the brightening, the hum lifts. In the brightening, the ridgestone spines throw their dull flares a little further, and the cracks in the basin floor seem, for a moment, to *shift* — not to widen, not to close, but to *rearrange*, as if the sentence has reached a clause and the clause has required a new pattern of emphasis. The eye, by now, is tired. The eye, by now, has been asked to look at too many things that are not quite things, and the brain, in self-defense, has begun to *translate* — to see the multitude as a herd, the hum as a song, the spines as mountains, the valley as a valley. This is a kindness the valley extends. It lets you make it small.

But underneath the small version, the real one continues.

The real one is high, and hot, and dry, and *old* — older than old, settled into its own state with a patience that the creatures embody and the mineral *performs*. The real one is loud in the way that a furnace is loud: not with noise but with *insistence*, with the pressure of a process that will not be interrupted. The real one is open, extending past the immediate, regional, a town-or-valley-size proposition — or a sentence-size proposition, which is the same thing, here.

The real one has no water, because water is a local dialect, and the valley speaks an older one.

The real one has no plants, because plants are a story about how to wait, and the valley has *finished* waiting and has begun the next thing.

The real one is empty, in the way that a page is empty before the sentence is complete — the marks already *present*, already dense, already a reading, but the page insisting, by its margins, that there is more.

You stand in it. The creatures flow around you, or through you, or past you — the question of which is undecidable, because they do not, in the end, occupy the same *category* of space that you occupy. They ring and click. The hum lifts. The brightening continues, becomes a high light, becomes the patient bright of a sun that will not, today, become cruel but that *could*, that is *capable* of cruelty, that has the *equipment* for it, that is merely choosing, today, to remain present and bright and unhurried, the way a hand remains steady over a kiln.

The valley is a kiln. The valley has always been a kiln. The creatures are the firing. The ridgestone is what is being fired. The hum is the sound of the transformation proceeding. The output, when it comes, will not be a pot. The output will not be a thing at all. The output will be *the next sentence*, and the next sentence will be spoken by the next configuration of the tessellation, in a voice made of clicking, in a syntax made of cracks, in a grammar made of glaze.

You are, perhaps, a comma. You are, perhaps, a quotation. You are, perhaps, only the silence between two clauses, which the valley permits, briefly, before continuing.

The brightening does not stop. The hum does not stop. The creatures do not stop.

The valley is *at work*, and the work is slow, and the work is loud, and the work is the only thing in the valley that has not yet decided what it will become, and the not-yet-decided quality of it is, in the end, the most unsettling thing the eye has ever been asked to see — more than the creatures, more than the glaze, more than the spines. The *not-yet*. The open parenthesis. The sentence, still being assembled, that has not yet reached its verb.

The high light falls.

The creatures continue.

You stay, or you do not. The valley does not require it.

But the verb, when it arrives, will be a *large* one.
