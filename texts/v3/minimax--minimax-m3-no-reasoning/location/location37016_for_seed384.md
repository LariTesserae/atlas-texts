# The Press

The water comes from everywhere.

Not rain. Not a flood. The water is the *medium*, has been the medium so long that no one left living remembers when it was ever otherwise. Above, the surface is a smeared mirror, three meters up, occasionally higher where the swell rolls through, occasionally lower where the trough dips and you can see the pale blue flicker of sky-far-off, sky-as-memory. The cold lives in the water and the water lives in the cold, and a body that has been here any length of time stops shivering in a particular way and just becomes slow.

The city is laid out like a hand pressed flat against the seafloor, fingers spread.

Five platforms, each one the size of a market square, are arranged in a fan around a central pillar. They are not pretty. They are sheet steel and bolted I-beam and poured concrete, scabbed over with coralline accretions that have grown through the joints in seventeen years of immersion. The accretions are rust-orange in the lower light, black where the shadow is deepest. Each platform is anchored to the seafloor by cables that have been tightened twice in living memory and now are mostly decorative, the buildings' own weight, and the weight of everything piled upon them, holding them down.

Between the platforms and the pillar, the streets are not streets. They are walkways, knee-high catwalks of grated steel, the kind that vibrates underfoot when something large moves on it. The water is up to the lowest crosspiece. A person in rubber boots walks with their knees splayed and their toes gripping, and the boot soles are the only color most people own that is not rust, not kelp, not grey steel, not the green-black of the intake pipes.

The pillar is the Press. The pillar is what the place is for.

It is a cylinder of older steel than the platforms, a darker steel, set into the seabed with a flange of rivets you can see when the silt is low. It rises the full three meters to the underside of the surface and disappears into it. Halfway up, there is a ring of valve assemblies — eighteen of them in a hexagonal cluster, with three spares mounted on cradles to either side. The valves are the size of dinner tables. They are painted yellow once, a long time ago, and the yellow has been colonized by a thin, hairlike growth that hangs from the bolts in pale streamers. The streamers move in a current that does not otherwise exist, and so you know the valves are breathing. Each one takes a slow breath every fourteen seconds, opens, admits a column of seawater, closes, holds it. The held water is squeezed, somewhere inside the Press, into something else. What that something else is, no one on the platforms is entirely sure. It is moved away through pipes the diameter of a man's torso that angle upward into the surface and away toward a horizon no one has ever seen from here. The pipes hum. That is most of the sound.

The Press hums.

It is the only sound most hours, and you learn to stop hearing it the way you stop hearing a radiator. It is not loud. It is everywhere. It comes up through the soles of the boots, through the grating of the catwalks, through the walls of the housing blocks piled on the platforms. On the lowest deck, you feel it in your teeth. On the highest, the topmost platform of the outermost finger, it is a presence in the room the way a draft is a presence in a room. You speak around it. You sleep with it. Children born here do not know that silence is a thing you can stand inside of without discomfort.

There are other sounds, but they are smaller. The slap of water against the underside of a platform when a swell comes through. The clank of a wrench in the maintenance shed on Platform Four. The slow, ticking groan of a cable taking up slack. Once, on a long shift, the sound of someone crying in the housing block on Platform Two, muffled by the wall and by the water, and indistinguishable from a cough if you were not paying attention.

The cold is a 4-degree cold, a working cold. It does not kill you fast. It numbs you from the feet up if you stand still, so no one stands still. Work is constant. There is always a pipe to re-seat, a valve to re-pack, a grating panel to lift because something has fallen underneath and is fouling the drainage. The drainage matters because the platforms have to be kept just barely above the waterline, and silt builds, and when silt builds the platforms settle an inch, and an inch is the difference between a dry boot and a wet one and between a wet boot and a flooded deck and between a flooded deck and an abandoned deck. So there are people whose job is the silt, who go down in hardhats with pump-nozzles and push the muck off the edges into the current. The current takes it away. The current does most things here.

The light is a half-light, a working light, that comes from two sources. There is the surface, three meters up, which is a bright uncertain ceiling on good days — a green-white shimmer, like sun through ice, that does not throw shadows but does brighten faces turned up toward it. There are the work lights, bolted to the railings of each platform, incandescent bulbs in cages that have not been changed in years and give a yellow light that does not reach far. The space between two work lights, ten meters apart, is a dim zone in which the catwalks and the housetops and the hanging kelp are all the same color. A person standing in that zone is a person you know by shape. Strangers are unsettling until you hear the boot on the grating.

The vegetation is sparse because nothing much wants to grow here. The kelp comes and goes, in long ropes, anchored to nothing, drifting in the slow current, snagging on the corners of the platforms. It is the brown of old dishwater. Sometimes a piece of it has small pale bulbs on it that pulse faintly if you look for a long time and you are not sure you did. You do not look for a long time. The kelp is hauled up onto the platform edges in nets and hung to dry on lines strung between the housing blocks, and it is dried and baled and shipped somewhere through the Press's outflow system or in the small submersibles that dock at the central pillar's base. It is the only food that grows here, though there are also the fish, the slow pale things that come in from the dark water and hover in the lee of the platforms on still days. They are not many. The nets come up with two or three in them, sometimes, and a lot of weed, and once in a while a thing that no one can identify that is dropped back in quickly.

The fauna is rare and shy. There is a current rumor of a shape, large, seen once a year, passing in the distance beyond the outermost platform. There are the working dogs, the small terriers with webbed feet that ride in the submersibles and are good at finding silt. There is a cat on Platform Two that survives on fish heads and is the only creature on the Press that purrs, and the purr is so startling the first time you hear it that you think the Press is breaking down. There are the people.

The people are dense. Three hundred and forty on the census taken at the last equinox adjustment, which is a number that gets chalked on a board in the foreman's office and then forgotten. They live stacked. The housing blocks on the platforms are three stories of converted shipping containers bolted together, and each container has been divided into four rooms, and each room has a family in it or a shift in it or a pair of workers sharing a bunk, and the bunks are stacked three high and a person in the top bunk can touch the ceiling with an outstretched hand. The walls are thin. You know your neighbors by the sound of them, by the way they cough, by the way they hang their drying socks on the same line. The smell of the place, indoors, is the smell of bodies and kelp and machine oil and the faint chemical sweetness of whatever the Press is making, which leaks in through the ventilation. You stop noticing it. Visitors notice it for years afterward.

The people are organized, not by plan, but by what works. The foreman is elected by the shift leads. The shift leads are the people who know the most about the Press, which means the people who have been here longest, which means the people whose parents came here. The work is divided by the valves, mostly. Each of the eighteen valves has a team. Each team has a shift. The shift is twelve hours. A person works the Press and sleeps in a container and eats dried kelp and reconstituted protein that comes through the Press in unmarked blocks, and once a week a submersible comes and takes the day's output away and leaves a week's worth of food and a letter or two from somewhere else, and the letters are the only evidence that somewhere else is a real place and not a story.

The technology is industrial, dense, and old. The Press is the only machine that matters, and it is older than anyone here. The submersibles are the only vehicles, and there are four of them, and three of them work on any given day, and the fourth is being repaired with parts from the third. The work lights are powered by a generator that runs on something piped in from the Press's own product, and the generator is in a small soundproofed room on Platform One and makes a noise that the people who work near it are slightly deaf from, and the people who sleep near it are used to, and the cat on Platform Two, the first time it heard it, climbed to the highest point in the housing block and would not come down for two days. The welders are oxyacetylene. The pumps are centrifugal, cast iron, the kind you see in museums. The computer that logs the valve pressures and the silt levels and the submersible schedules is a thing in a glass case in the foreman's office and it has a green screen and a keyboard and no one under thirty knows how to use it, and the man who does is the most respected person on the Press after the foreman, and his hands shake now, and there is no one to take his place, and this is the subject of meetings that happen in low voices in the foreman's office after shift.

The rules of the world are not many. Do not fall in. Do not swim. The current beyond the platforms is stronger than a person can fight, and even if you could fight it, the water outside the lit zone is dark, and in the dark there are things that the fish avoid, and so you avoid the dark. Do not go onto the catwalks at night unless you are on shift. Do not touch the valves when they are cycling. Do not speak to the foreman before he has had his tea. Do not let the silt build. Do not, if you are a child, climb to the top of the housing block and jump into the water, which a child did, last year, and was not found, and the platform is quieter now for the absence, and the parents of the child moved to a submersible assignment and go out beyond the platforms every day and do not come back until the shift is over and no one asks them about it.

The weirdness of the place is in the valves. It is in the way the water enters them and does not come out the same. It is in the slow accumulation of the accretions, which grow in patterns that are not quite regular. It is in the way the cat on Platform Two always sits facing the same direction, and the direction is not the surface. It is in the fact that the Press has been running for sixty years, and the people who run it are the grandchildren of the people who built it, and the people who built it are not spoken of, and the records that would say where they came from and why are in the glass case in the foreman's office, in a drawer that no one opens, because the man who used to know the combination died last winter and the drawer has not been opened since.

The place is active in the way a lung is active. The valves breathe. The current moves. The silt builds and is pushed off. The submersibles come and go. The kelp drifts in and is hauled out. The cables groan. The generator hums. The Press hums. The people work and sleep and eat and work. The children grow up and go on shift and have children of their own. The light shifts, in the long slow way it shifts, with the seasons, with the weather above, with the time of day. The water is always there. The water is the world.

You stand on a catwalk between Platform Three and Platform Four, in the dim zone between two work lights, with your knees splayed and your toes gripping, and the hum is in your boots and in your teeth and in the air around you and in the water that is up to the crosspiece, and the Press is breathing, and the surface is bright, far up, and you do not think about the surface. You think about the next valve. You think about whether the silt is building on the lee side. You think about the letter that is supposed to come on the next submersible, from your sister, who left. You do not think about why the Press is here. You do not think about what the Press is making. You do not think about what is in the dark water beyond the platforms, the dark water that the fish avoid, the dark water that the current flows away from. You do not think about it. You breathe with the valves. You hum with the Press. You are one more presence in the dense, dark, cold, slow, and you have work in the morning.
