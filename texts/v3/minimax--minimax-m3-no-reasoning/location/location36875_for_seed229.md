# The Singing Defile

You come down through a throat of stone. The walls narrow, then narrow again, and the wind begins to lift your clothes before your feet have lost the last of the slope. The defile is small — you could throw a stone from one rim to the other in places, and where you cannot, the gap is cathedral: a single shaft of pale, sourceless dimness climbing upward into a sky you never quite see. The light is the color of old pewter, the color of bruises ripening, the color of things that live in cracks. It enters from somewhere above and arrives at the floor having forgotten what blue looked like.

There is no water. You search for it automatically — the mind does this, trained by every other place — and finds nothing. No seep. No trickle. No green-black smear of moss where stone should be wet. The floor is dry mineral, pale grey-cream, packed fine as flour, and your boots print it but no moisture wells into the prints. The air itself tastes of nothing you can drink. It tastes of calcite and the warm iron breath of a stone body slowly cooking in late afternoon sun it doesn't have.

And yet the walls are furred.

It begins low — a felt of grey-green, dense as old shag, the kind of growth you would not trust to touch. Then, as the eye climbs, the fur thickens and rearranges. It becomes ropes, fingers, tongues. Long lobes of something leafless and swollen hang down like salted tongues from a smokehouse. They are the color of a bruise's second day, of tarnished brass, of unripe persimmon. They do not move. They have moved. You can see by their postures — every lobe tipped slightly the same direction, leaning into the prevailing wind, that they are listening for it and have been for a long time. They harvest it. The whole vertical garden is a wind-harvest, wringing the screaming air for the trace humidity you cannot smell and the trace chemistries you cannot name. Fog does not come here. Rain has never come here, you suspect, and yet the plants drink.

The plants are not alone in drinking.

The sound is the first thing a body understands about this place and the last thing it forgets. It is loud, and it is layered, and it does not stop. The wind, of course — the wind is the constant, the basso, the drone. It comes down the shaft and finds the shape of the stone and learns to sing in it, and the song it learns has too many harmonics. There are undertones you can feel in your sternum. There are overtones that brush the hair on your forearms. The stone itself rings, or seems to, as though the canyon were a stopped flute and you were standing at the third pinhole.

On top of the wind: the fauna.

They live on the walls. There is nowhere else for them. Some are the size of your hand and they cling, flat-bellied, with the patient spread of salamanders. They are not salamanders. They are deep amber, almost rust, and they have too many legs, or the wrong number of legs, or legs in the wrong places — the eye keeps losing count. When the wind gusts they flatten harder, and the fur of the garden closes over them like water over a dropped stone. You can see the place they were by the slight parting in the lobes, and a moment later the parting fills and you are not sure you saw anything at all.

Higher up, where the light is dimmest and the wind loudest, larger shapes roost. They are hunched in the architecture of the stone — in scoops and shoulders and the small natural balconies erosion has carved over the centuries. They have wings. You do not see the wings until one of them moves; then you see that what you took for a hanging lobe of plant is in fact a folded membrane, papery, vein-dark, the color of the bruises on the wall. They are sleeping, most of them. They are listening, all of them. When one opens its mouth — to yawn, to taste the air, to make a sound — you see teeth like the seeds of a pomegranate, small and wet and too numerous.

And they make sounds. Not cries — you want cries, you want the language of birds to translate this place into. What they make is a low clatter, a percussive thing, a sound like stones dropped into a wooden drum. They do it in rounds. One starts, and another answers from a different wall, and then a third, and then a chorus, and then the wind takes their clatter and breaks it into a rhythm it can carry down the defile. By the time the rhythm reaches you it is no longer theirs. It is the canyon's. The canyon has eaten their conversation and is digesting it for its own purposes, and your bones are part of those purposes.

Between the creatures and the wind and the ringing stone, the place is *loud*. You have to raise your voice to be heard by the person beside you, and you will not want to do that often, because what you hear when you listen is so much more interesting than what you would say.

The floor is not empty.

Structures — that word is too dignified — *marks* stand at intervals. A low wall of fitted stones, knee-high, that follows the curve of the defile for a stretch and then gives up. A circle of upright shards of stone, waist-high, black against the pale silt, that you understand without being told is a place where someone stood. The stones have been there long enough for the floor's dust to have built up against their windward faces, and long enough for the garden-fur to have crept up their leeward ones. There are marks of tools. There are marks of cutting, of levering, of the patient removal of small pieces. The tool-marks are old. The wind that has scoured them has had time to consider them.

Whoever built these things did not stay. The wind took their houses and re-used the stone for its own singing. You find a single shard of worked flint at the base of the largest shard-circle, and you pick it up, and it is warm from the stone it leaned against, and you put it back because taking it feels like a kind of theft you cannot name.

Higher up the walls, in places you cannot reach, there are other marks. They are not tool-marks. They are not writing. They are *countings* — vertical scratches, grouped in bundles, weathered almost to invisibility. Whoever counted here was counting days, or winds, or the cycles of the clattering creatures, or something for which we no longer have a word. The countings do not agree with each other.

The light moves, slowly, the way light moves in a place with no sun. The dimness shifts, not brightening but *rearranging* — the bruise-tones on the walls migrate up and down as though the whole defile were breathing and the light were a tide in its lungs. The garden-fur shifts with it, faintly. The clatter rounds move with it, the sleeping creatures turning, by fractions, to keep some part of themselves aligned with whatever axis the light has now.

It is turbulent, this place. You would not have called it that from the sound alone — turbulence suggests the sudden, the violent, the breaking-wave. But the turbulence here is *constant*. Wind is always restarting. The creatures are always re-clattering. The garden is always leaning, re-leaning, a slow wave of adjustment moving up the walls. Dust lifts, drifts, settles, lifts again. A small stone, dislodged from somewhere overhead, falls and does not strike the floor — it is caught by a creature's wing, considered, tasted, and dropped the rest of the way, and the creature turns its head a quarter-turn and resumes its listening.

You stand in the throat of the defile and the place receives you.

It does not welcome you. It does not refuse you. It is not large enough or indifferent enough to do either. It accepts you the way the canyon accepts the wind — as another voice in the round, another pressure on the air, another small warm body whose heat will briefly register on the stone and then be gone. You are not the first warm body it has accepted. The countings on the walls are the countings of others. The fitted stones are the stones of others. The lone flake of flint at the base of the largest shard is the leavings of a hand that once warmed the same air you are warming now.

The wind takes your breath and gives it back changed.

You do not stay long. The loudness gets into you. The dimness gets into you. The strangeness of plants that drink wind and creatures that sleep folded into walls and stone that has been ringing, quietly, for a very long time — these things get into you, and you feel them arranging themselves inside you the way the light arranges itself on the walls, and you understand that this place has a grammar, and you do not speak it, and it will not slow down for you.

You climb back up the throat toward the sky you came from.

Behind you, the clatter rounds. The wind considers. The garden leans.

You leave a print in the pale silt, and by the time you have climbed out of sight, the print has been quietly, patiently filled.
