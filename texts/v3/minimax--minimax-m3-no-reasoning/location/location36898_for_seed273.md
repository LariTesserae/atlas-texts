# The Vertical City of Renkara

## I. Approach

From a distance, Renkara does not appear to be a city at all. It appears to be a mountain — a single dark mass rising out of the river-plains, its flanks striped with green that, even from miles away, looks too regular to be natural. As one draws closer, the green resolves into vines, and the dark stone resolves into walls, and the strange groaning one had begun to hear resolves into the breath of ten thousand small engines.

The mountain is not a mountain. It is the tallest inhabited structure on the continent.

## II. The Stack

Renkara was built, layer by layer, over the course of four centuries. No one remembers who began it — the lowest courses are seamless, made of a black stone that does not occur anywhere else in the region, and the masons who laid them took their recipes to the grave. Above that first course, everything else: terraces, buttresses, aqueducts, shrines, granaries, foundries, hanging gardens, pigeon towers, smithies, dye-works, tanneries, breweries, the long stone ribs that support the buttress-bridges crossing the city's interior ravines.

There is no ground level in Renkara. There is only *up* and *deeper*.

The bottom terraces — the First and Second Wraps — are where the loud work happens. The great waterwheels turn in their stone channels, hauling buckets of river-water up through the mountain's hollow spine to be released into the upper aqueducts. The drop-hammers work day and night in the smithing quarter, their rhythm so constant it has become a kind of silence. The smell here is of hot iron, wet ash, and the sweet rot of the dye-vats. One does not linger.

The middle terraces — the Third through the Sixth — are residential, crowded, the streets so narrow that in places one can touch both walls by extending one's arms. Here the damp of the river-valley collects, condenses on the stone, and never quite evaporates. Walls glisten. Laundry is hung across the lanes and dries slowly. The cats are sleek.

The upper terraces — the Seventh, Eighth, and the legendary Ninth, which few have seen — are gardens. This is where the moisture rises to and the warmth pools, and here the city has gone feral with green. Fig and pomegranate and persimmon trees crowd the stone shelves. Vines as thick as a man's thigh have cracked buttresses and been allowed to remain, since no one has a better idea. Bees, kept in long clay hives set into the walls, produce a dark peppery honey that is the city's chief export. The light at this altitude is extraordinary — clean and slanted and golden in the late afternoon, falling across leaves and stone and the slow spirals of rising smoke from the lower terraces, so that the whole upper city seems to be quietly burning.

Above the Ninth, if the old stories are to be believed, there is a single white terrace where nothing grows and no one lives. They call it the Unfinished.

## III. The Water

Water is the city's obsession. The river runs at the mountain's foot, broad and slow and warm, and from it the city drinks — but the drinking is a controlled thing, mediated by the great wheels and the aqueducts and the slow cisterns carved into the rock. Rain, when it comes, comes hard and sideways in the wet months and not at all in the dry, and so every drop that falls on a terrace is led to a cistern, and every cistern is metered, and the metering is the city's chief political work.

You can tell who has water by the green of their walls.

In the dry months the lower terraces grow grey with dust and the dye-vats run thin. In the wet months the aqueducts overflow and the city has the peculiar smell of warm stone after rain — a mineral smell, a deep smell, the smell of something that has been wet for a long time and remembers it.

## IV. The Fauna

There are peacocks on the Seventh Terrace, kept by an old guild whose charter no living official can produce. They scream at dawn. There are working cats everywhere, as is proper. There are pigeons in such numbers that their droppings have whitened the buttress-bridges of the Fourth Wrap, and once a year the pigeon-harvesters rappel down from the upper city in their swinging cradles to collect them, and this is considered a festival.

In the river below, there are otters. They have learned to wait by the outflows of the dye-works for the offal, and they are fat and reddish and unafraid. Children throw stones at them from the lowest terrace and miss on purpose.

Above the city, where the air thins and the hawks begin, the great fruit-bats roost in the pomegranate trees of the Ninth's edge, and at dusk they pour out over the city in a dark ragged stream, and the Ninth's gardeners light no fires that hour, for superstition's sake.

## V. The Sound

Renkara is loud, but its loudness is patient. It does not shout. It hums.

The lowest note is the waterwheels, which never stop. Above them, the hammers, which never stop. Above them, the voices of the markets, which never stop. Above them, the bells — the hour-bells of the shrine of the Unfinished, the shift-bells of the foundries, the small bright bells that the children of the Sixth Wrap wear on their ankles when they are sent on errands. Above all of this, the wind in the upper gardens, which is the city's only true silence, and which is reserved, by unspoken custom, for the dying.

## VI. The Slowness

Nothing in Renkara is fast. The waterwheels turn at the speed of the river. The vines grow at the speed of vines. The stone weathers at the speed of stone. The buttresses that were cracked a century ago are still cracked, and the gardeners have trained the figs around the cracks and do not mention them. A new terrace is begun perhaps once in a generation; an old one is repaired perhaps once in a decade; the rest of the time the city simply endures, accumulating its damp, its green, its noise, its slow vertical breath.

It has been here longer than anyone now living. It will be here longer than anyone now living.

## VII. Weather

In the wet months the mountain wears a small cloud at its summit — the Unfinished, hidden. The rain drums on the terraces in order from top to bottom, so that one always knows, by the sound approaching, how long one has before the downpour arrives. The gutters sing. The cistern-meters overflow.

In the dry months the cloud is gone and the city stands clear against the sky, and from the river-plains one can count the terraces — green, green, green, dark, dark, dark, green — like the growth-rings of some impossible tree.
