A cleft. That is what it would be called if you stood back from it, but no one stands back from it, and nothing here is what it looks like at first because nothing here looks like anything yet.

Picture a fissure in the flank of a basalt spur, three hundred meters up from anything that would matter to a map. The fissure is narrow — a body could ease in sideways, and has, once or twice, but the stone closes behind it. The stone that closes is dark. It is the dark of deep water and unpolished horn and the inside of a closed eye, and the cleft opens into that dark without ceremony, the way a seam in cloth opens if you pinch the right fold. You would not see it from below. Ferns have grown into the lower lip, ordinary mountain ferns, but the ferns stop and the green stops above a certain line where something in the rock shifts, and the ferns seem to know it. They are not the first thing that stops.

Inside, then. Inside it is warm. Warm is the wrong word — the cleft does not warm you. It declines to let the warmth out. The stone here has been drinking the heat of something below the mountain, a thread of warmth climbing up through the basalt the way a thought climbs up through a sleeper, slow, and the stone holds it the way cupped hands hold water. You stand in a place that is not cold because the rock has refused, for some local geological and perhaps not entirely geological reason, to be cold. There is a smell in it that is sulfur and iron and the green of things that do not need light, and beneath those there is a smell older than smells, a smell of stone that is still deciding what it is.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall. It weeps. It condenses on every surface in a film so fine you would think you were imagining it, except that when you touch the wall a sheen comes away on your fingers and tastes of nothing and everything at once. There are places where it gathers into beads that hang without dropping, pendant along ridges of mineral the way ornaments hang along a beam. There is a drip somewhere deeper in that drips, but you cannot hear it clearly, only know it is there by the way the rhythm of the place, such as it is, ticks. The floor is not a floor. The floor is a slow pool half a centimeter deep that is somehow entire, a floor-length mirror laid down over the rock, and it moves in a way that is not quite movement but could not be called stillness either. To step into it would be to disturb something you do not understand, so you do not step into it.

The rock is dense. Not in the sense of being thick — you could reach out and touch either wall — but dense the way a thought is dense, packed with what is happening in it. Veins of something pale are threading through the basalt, mineral, slow, alive in the way that crystals are alive, and the veins have grown since the cleft opened. They have grown in the time it takes a fingernail to harden. You could scrape one with a thumbnail and beneath the scraped face would be another face, younger, wetter, not yet knowing it would be scraped. This is the erosion that is not erosion. This is the world doing the opposite of wearing down. You are inside an act of construction, a thing still becoming. The mineral is arriving. The colors are arriving with it — cream, rust of the deeper rusts, a green that has no business here, the green of lichen starved into a depth of color that should require sun but is making do. Where the pale veins widen into flat panels, they have begun to shape themselves into something almost but not quite like writing. They are not writing. They are the answer to a question that was never asked, and they are getting closer, and they will never finish.

There is, set into a low ledge where the wall meets the floor-pool, a stone that does not belong. It is worked. It has been struck against something harder than itself until a face appeared on it, a face only in the sense that a face has been made, a flattened plane with a chipped edge. The chipping is human. It is old or it is new; you cannot tell because time is not working correctly here. Beside it, half-buried in the slow mineral milk, a tool — a chipped pebble, a hammer-stone, the kind of thing a hand could fit and a hand did fit, used and dropped and left and forgot. The tool is going to become, in another century, part of the wall. The wall is already reaching for it. This was the extent of the building. A struck stone, a dropped hand-stone, the first small acts of a craft that did not continue, or that continued elsewhere, or that continues, invisibly, in the way the cleft continues.

It is dark. It is so dark in the cleft that the dark has weight and a kind of pressure against your open eyes. Whatever light enters must enter from the mouth of the fissure, and it does not travel. It dies somewhere in the first arm's length, eaten by the wet stone and the mineral rust. The deeper places have only what the mineral gives, and the mineral gives nothing to see by, only to lean against. Your pupils will not open any further. You will begin to see things that were always there — the faint green-gold of mineral in the act of precipitating, the dull glow of something phosphorescent on the ceiling where a thing lived once, the tiny blue points of eyes that are not human but are paying attention. The phosphorescent ceiling-cast is not bright enough to read by. It is only bright enough to know that the ceiling is not the ceiling, that there is further to go up, that the cleft wants to be more than a cleft, that above you is more stone and more dark and more time arriving.

There are creatures here but they are not here often. A salamander thing, eyeless, white as milk-glass, has laid itself in the warm shallow at the far end, dreaming the long dream of something that eats once a season. It does not stir. A moth that should not exist has beaten itself against a vein in the ceiling and is still there, mouth open, drinking not the air but the moisture on the air, and is the same moth that was here before, or another of its line, and the difference does not matter. Something under the floor-pool moves in a way that is only a tremor in the stone. They are rare. They are all the cleft has, and the cleft does not ask for more.

It is mostly quiet. The drip is the sound. The drip and the small sounds the drip makes after it, the ripple in the floor-pool barely reaching anything, the wet click of a bead finally letting go somewhere in the ceiling. A wing when the moth shifts. The salamander's slow breathing. And below all of it, deeper than the sounds, the sound that is the cleft itself — a low, almost-not-a-sound, the sound of stone being mineral, of minerals finding their arrangement, of warmth climbing through basalt the way blood climbs through a vein. The cleft is breathing in a register that cannot be heard, only felt, in the teeth, in the wrists, in the backs of the hands.

Nothing is changing. That is the strangest thing, and you do not understand at first why this feels strange, because the cleft is clearly wet, clearly warm, clearly still being built, and so by all rights it should be in motion. It is not in motion. The drip drops. It has dropped. It will drop again. The mineral arrives in the same pattern it has always arrived, and it will arrive. The moth is where the moth was. The tool is where the tool was. The hand that left it has not finished leaving. You stand in the center of a place that is doing exactly what it was doing before you arrived, and it will be doing exactly that when you leave, and it is the stillness not of death but of a thing that has settled into itself so deeply it cannot be dislodged without being broken. You are the only moving thing here, and being the only moving thing in a place like this makes you feel strange to yourself, an interruption, a kind of trespass, a draft in a room that has never had a door.

You do not stay long. You could not stay long. The warmth is too complete, and the dark is too patient, and you begin to feel that the cleft is not waiting for you but was already here before you came and is where you go when you are not here, the way a chair is where you sit even when you are standing. You ease back out through the seam. The ferns resume above your head as if they had never paused. The light, suddenly, is enormous, and you find that you cannot see it properly for some minutes after, because your eyes have learned, very quickly, the way of the inside.
