# The Conurbation Above the Clouds

## I. The Plateau

You arrive, if there is an arrival, somewhere along the great basalt shelf that forms the spine of this place. The shelf is wrong. It is too new — still cooling in places, the rock giving off a faint warmth through your boots if you stand in one spot long enough. Ten thousand years old, perhaps, or a hundred thousand, which here counts as nothing. The basalt rises from nothing, from a fog plain that has no bottom to it, and stretches in either direction until weather erodes the sight. On a clear morning, and mornings are often clear because the air is thin and cold and holds very little of anything, you can see two hundred kilometers of it: a black plain, slightly tilted, veined with runoff channels that have not yet learned to flow.

The rock is sharp. This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you trust. Recent erosion has not yet had time to round the edges of anything. A wrong step opens your hand. The gutters between cooling flows are crisp-cornered, knife-shaped. Scree does not yet exist — only boulders and shards and the dust that rains from them when the wind comes through. The dust is gray-black and settles on your clothing and you wear it for days.

## II. The Climate of Uselessness

The temperature is nominal, hovering somewhere below what your body wants. Not bitter — there is no wind-chill to speak of, the wind being only intermittent — but a steady, thin cold that you cannot quite ignore. Your breath does not plume dramatically. It merely lingers. The air is dry in the way high places are dry, but there is water everywhere: in the standing pools between basalt fins, in the condensation that beads on every metal surface by midmorning, in the fog below which is itself a kind of ocean.

The fog is the constant. It lies under the plateau like a held breath. Sometimes it rises, and then the place becomes an island in a white sea with no horizon. Sometimes it descends and you are inside it, and the cities around you vanish except as verticalities that emerge — towers, rails, the spines of factories — like the rigging of half-sunken ships.

It rains often. The rain is fine and cold and falls straight down because there is little wind. It beads on the basalt and runs into the channels and pools in the low places. It condenses on everything metallic. After rain, the whole plateau steams faintly as the rock rewarms. The air smells of wet iron and ozone.

## III. The Cities

They are built along the central spine where the rock is most level, in three or four ribbons that run parallel for as far as the eye can follow. They are not cities in any human sense, or only loosely. They are industrial formations — long, low, dense, repeating. The buildings are short relative to their footprint: two or three stories of poured concrete and steel plate, roofed in corrugated metal that has gone the color of a bruise. Between them run rail lines, and along the rails run machines that do not stop.

There are people here, which surprises you given the emptiness of the parameters, and given the absence of anything you would call fauna. But the people are not animal life, or this place has decided they are not. They move in coordinated groups, the way a school of fish moves, with no individual intention visible. They wear gray coats and gray hoods. They speak rarely. They tend the machines, or the machines tend them, and the distinction has become academic.

The industrial character is not hidden. It is exposed the way geology is exposed. You can see the boilers from a kilometer away, breathing white steam. You can hear the dynamos before you can see them, a low electrical growl that the air carries. Cables run overhead in thick black bundles, and from a distance the cities look like nests, like the architecture of some enormous insect colony that has been electrified and left to hum.

## IV. The Sound

The sound is the loudest thing about this place, and it never stops. It is not a single sound but a chord. The chord is composed of: the turbines, of which there are many, each contributing a different pitch; the rail lines, which sing under load, a high metallic keen that varies with the speed of the passing; the dynamos in their housings; the venting of steam at pressure; the compressors; the pumps that keep the lower levels from flooding with the constant condensate; and under all of it, a kind of subsonic pulse that you feel in your teeth before you hear it, the heartbeat of the place.

When you stop moving, the chord does not lower. There is no off-hours. There is no night, in the industrial districts — the lights are on always, and the shifts do not turn. People and machines work in a continuous present.

When you climb to the edge of the plateau, away from the cities, the chord thins but does not vanish. You can hear it an hour's walk out, under the wind, under the rain, under everything. It is the sound the place makes by being itself.

## V. The Light

The light is ordinary, which is to say it is full daylight for most of the hours. The latitude is such that the sun moves in a low arc and stays above the horizon for sixteen, eighteen hours. The sky is a washed pale blue, almost white at the zenith. Shadows are long and sharp in the morning and evening, and at noon there are almost no shadows. The basalt gleams when wet. The metal roofs gleam always.

The cities are lit at night, but night is short and pale. The lights are sodium, mostly, the old amber color, and they make the fog below look like a city of its own, an inverted city hanging under the real one. After midnight, briefly, the lights along the central spine reflect down into the fog and the entire plateau seems to float, briefly, in a yellow glow.

## VI. The Vegetation

It is not absent. It is reluctant. Mosses, lichens, a few species of hardy grass that have crept up from the cloud layer below and colonized the damper gutters. In the rain they brighten to almost impossible green — emerald, viridian, a green that has no business existing on fresh black rock under a cold sky — and then in the dry hours they fade to gray and seem to vanish. They do not flourish. They persist. There are no trees. There are no shrubs. The plants of this place have not yet figured out how to become tall.

They cling to the rain. That is the secret of the green. Every drop counts.

## VII. The Emptiness Between

The cities occupy perhaps a tenth of the plateau. The rest is space — vast, wet, empty, sharp, slowly eroding. Between the urban ribbons you can walk for hours and meet nothing. The basalt there is bare and the runoff channels run toward the edges, where the plateau ends. At the edges, the rock drops away into the fog. You can hear water falling, distantly, into the cloud layer. You cannot see the bottom. The runoff simply vanishes over the lip and reappears, perhaps, somewhere far below as a spring, or a river, or as weather.

You feel, walking in the empty stretches, that the place is indifferent to you. Not hostile. The cliff edges are dangerous but they are not hidden. The cold will kill you eventually but slowly, and there are shelters every few kilometers, because the people who work the machines occasionally need to walk between them. The indifference is structural. The plateau does not know you are there. The cities do, slightly. The machines, not at all.

## VIII. The Strangeness

It accumulates. It is not in any one feature but in the way the features compose.

The fog has no bottom, but it has weather. Storms form in it and press up against the underside of the plateau. You can stand at the edge and watch lightning travel through the cloud below, the bolt visible only as a flash that illuminates the fog from within, briefly making it solid. Then it is white again, and the thunder reaches you a few seconds later, attenuated by the height.

The machines have a regularity that is not quite mechanical. They seem, sometimes, to anticipate. The lights along the central spine brighten as you approach them, even when you have not announced yourself. The rails move cars at intervals that have no obvious schedule, and yet if you wait at any junction, a car will arrive within minutes, as if the system has been watching you.

The people have a way of moving that is not quite human. It is faster than walking, slower than running, and uses no observable means of propulsion. They cover ground in a smooth flow that conserves effort. You can overtake them on foot, but they do not appear to hurry. When you pass them they do not look at you. They do not look at anything. They move toward their tasks with the inevitability of water finding its level.

The basalt is the wrong color. It is too black. Fresh basalt is gray. This basalt is the deep matte black of a thing that has been heated to a temperature you cannot imagine and then frozen, and the color suggests that whatever made the plateau is not entirely done with it. On still nights, if you put your hand flat on the rock, you can feel a faint vibration that is not the chord of the cities, that has a different rhythm, slower, as if something large is breathing under the stone.

## IX. The Quality of Time

Nothing changes here. The plateau erodes at a rate you cannot perceive. The cities add a new building every few years, but you would not notice unless you stayed. The machines wear and are replaced and wear again. The people move through their generations like water through a channel, and the channels do not change. The fog below is the same fog. The rain is the same rain. The light is the same light.

You can stay for a long time and leave, and come back, and the place will be as you left it. The chord will still be playing. The machines will still be running. The people will still be flowing toward their tasks. The basalt will still be sharp, and slowly, imperceptibly, less so.

It is a place in equilibrium. Everything that happens here has already been accounted for. The rain that falls has already fallen. The steam that rises has already risen. The sound that hums in your teeth has been humming since before you arrived and will hum after.

## X. Departure, If It Comes To That

You can leave by rail, if you can board a car and it will accept you. The cars run to the edges of the plateau and stop, and from the edges you can descend — there are stairs cut into the basalt at intervals, worn smooth by use, leading down into the fog. The stairs are very long. They are not lit. They go down for a day, perhaps two, and then they emerge below the cloud layer, into a different country, warmer, lower, with trees and animals and weather that means something.

You will carry the chord with you for a while. It will fade as you descend. By the time you reach the trees, it will be gone, and you will find that you miss it.

You will find that you miss the place.

You will not be able to explain why.
