# The Underline

The trains stopped here first.

You arrive from above, from the city that pretends the place doesn't exist, and you descend by a stairwell threaded between water-stained walls where the original tilework — fired in some century no one bothered to name — has calcified into something almost geological. The stair gives back a damp, mineral sound underfoot, a kind of ringing that isn't quite a ringing. It is cooler down here than you expected. The air tastes of iron and wet concrete and, faintly, of something organic — leaf rot, moss, the breath of whatever is breathing in the dark.

You emerge into a low-ceilinged hall. The ceiling is the underside of something — a street, a plaza, the floor of the world above. Beams the size of old trees cross it at regular intervals, riveted steel wrapped in a skin of rust that has stopped flaking and begun instead to *be* the surface, a settled red-brown that holds the light without reflecting it. Between the beams, the concrete is weeping. Not dramatically. It is the patient weeping of a structure that has been damp for longer than most of the buildings on top of it have been standing. Condensation gathers in hairline rivulets, traces the path of rebar beneath the surface, and drops, sometimes, into the puddles that occupy the shallowest depressions of the floor.

The floor is uneven in a way that tells you it was once level. Centuries of subtle settlement, of water dissolving the lime from the cement grain by grain, have made it into a landscape of shallow bowls. Each bowl holds its own quiet water. Each water holds its own dim gray light.

## The Rails

There are two rail lines here, running the length of the hall. They were laid for a regional commuter service that operated for the better part of a century before the city, in its wisdom, buried this section of itself under a new neighborhood and forgot the inconvenience it had left beneath its foundations. The rails are still here. They are dark with oxidation but they hold their shape; they were, when new, very good steel, and steel of that vintage outlasts the things people put it in. The wooden ties are mostly gone, replaced by a continuous gravel bed that has, in the absence of any maintenance, become colonized by a sparse and patient ecology.

You notice, because there is little to distract you, that the gravel holds different things in different places. Here, a fist-shaped tumble of basalt that has lost every sharp edge and become almost spherical. There, a length of corroded pipe that someone — you cannot tell when — laid across the ties as a sleeper of last resort. The far rail is half-swallowed by the floor itself, the right-of-way slowly claiming it.

There is no third rail. The current has not run through these in decades.

## The City

The platform remains. It is wide enough for a crowd and long enough for a train, and the tile along its edge is still yellow, though the yellow has darkened into the color of a bruise. The benches are bolted to it in clusters — iron frames, slats of some hardwood that the damp has darkened almost to black. Three of the clusters. Five of the clusters. They are not equidistant; the spacing tells you something about how the commuters once arranged themselves, where the doors would have stopped, where the morning crowd densest, and you can read, in the negative space, the timetable of a vanished life.

Behind the benches, the wall.

This is the part that is strange, and you have been waiting to come to it.

The wall is not blank. It is not the empty concrete of the passage you came down. It is — *inscribed* is the wrong word; *grown* is closer — it is the surface against which a small civilization has unfolded itself.

Pipes run along it at three heights. Some are original: a six-inch main, bracketed at intervals, that once carried steam or water or compressed air to the station's mechanical systems. Some are later: smaller lines, electrical conduit, a run of copper that someone, in some unguarded decade, affixed to the original brackets and ran wherever it pleased. Some are very much later still: cables of a kind you don't immediately recognize, woven black and thick as your wrist, that vanish into the wall through holes drilled — you can see the drill marks — through tile.

Between the pipes, the wall *is* a city.

Not a metaphor. Not entirely. There are dwellings. They are made of materials that should not be available and yet are: folded sheet metal, panels pried from defunct machinery, glass cut from somewhere with surprising care, wood scavenged in pieces you can identify as having once been doors and cabinets and the floors of demolished rooms. The dwellings are stacked three and four high in places, climbing the wall toward the dark ceiling, and they are lit from within by lights that are not steady, that pulse and dim with the respiration of whatever powers them.

There are windows. There are curtains in the windows, of fabric so old you can read the pattern — cabbage roses, a jacquard weave, deep reds faded toward the dusk of pink. There are chimneys, and from some of them a wisp of something rises; the air moves so little down here that the smoke goes up a foot and then stands, and then, slowly, disperses.

There are no animals on the walls. There are animals in the walls.

You hear them before you see them. A scratching, very small. A chittering, almost ultrasonic, that you feel in your back teeth. The patter of feet that are too many to count because they are always moving. They live in the spaces between the dwellings, in the infrastructure, in the warm dampness of the wall and the pipes and the dark. They are not afraid of you. You are not the first visitor in a long time but you may be the first in some while. They have, you sense, no use for visitors and no dread of them either.

The dwellings are empty now. The things that built them — and you must wonder, you cannot help wondering, who *did* — are elsewhere. The curtain in one of the upper windows moves slightly. You would like to believe it is the air. The air is doing very little here.

## The Water

It is the water that defines the place, more than the rails, more than the wall.

The hall is not flooded. It is not damp in the way a basement is damp, a passing intrusion of the wet world above. The water here is *resident*. It is the medium in which the place has, slowly, made itself its current self.

It gathers along the rails in a continuous sheet that is shallow enough to reflect the ceiling, the beams, the improbable upper stories of the wall. It pools against the platform edge in slow tongues that advance and retreat on a cycle you cannot quite determine. It sweats from every concrete surface and condenses on every metal one and falls, perpetually, as a fine mist that you do not notice until you see the beams glisten at any dark light.

It is not cold. It is cool, in the way that underground things are cool, in the way that the absence of sun is cool, in the way that a stone held in the hand for a long time becomes the temperature of the hand.

There is a sound to it, when you stand still. A low, almost subliminal sound — the sound of water moving in many places at once, of drops falling at many rates into pools of many sizes, of the capillary creep of wet through porous matter. It is the sound of patience. It is the closest thing here to music.

## The Inhabitants

You do not see them for a long time. Then you begin to.

They are small. They are smaller than you expected, and more numerous, and quieter than the abundant fauna of the parameters — the parameters, the readings, whatever they were — had suggested. They move along the wall in the spaces you cannot easily see, in the shadow of the pipes, in the corners where the dwelling roofs meet the concrete. They are furred, or scaled, or both; they have too many or too few of various things; you cannot tell because they are never still long enough and the light is not bright enough and you are, you realize, not entirely sure that what you are seeing is what you are seeing.

They live, it seems, in symbiosis with the dwellings. They tend the pipes. They keep, you think, the very faint warmth that you perceive when you stand close to the wall — a warmth that should not be there, that the geometry of the place does not require, that comes from somewhere within the strata of the structure. They run along the conduits. They pause at the switches.

There is a switch on the wall. It is the original switch, in its original housing, a great iron lever with a porcelain handle. The handle is yellowed. The housing is rusted. The lever is in the *on* position, and has been for some time.

The lights in the upper windows pulse. The chittering rises and falls. Something, somewhere in the dwelling stack, clatters briefly and is silent.

## The Sound

The place is mostly quiet. The sound, when you walk through it, is your own feet in the water, and the sound of your breathing, and — if you stop, and stop again, and wait through the patience the place asks of you — the sound of the place itself. The dripping. The distant, almost subliminal hum of something electrical still running through the copper. The small movements of the small animals. The slow, almost geological settling of the structure against its own foundations.

You do not hear the city above. You cannot. The concrete between here and there is eight feet thick in places and it is, you sense, *eager* to keep the two worlds apart.

## The Light

The light is dim. It comes from a few sources: the original station lighting, most of which has failed, but a handful of bulbs in caged fixtures still glow with the faint orange of very old filaments. The lights in the wall-dwellings, which pulse. The reflections of all of these in the water on the floor. The almost imperceptible bioluminescence — you think it is bioluminescence — of something on the underside of a beam, a faint green tracery that may be a configuration of mold or may be something else.

The light does not reach the ceiling, in places. The ceiling is dark, and the dark is inhabited, and you do not look up for long.

## The Pace

The place is slow. The water moves slowly. The animals move quickly, but they are small and their movements do not aggregate into any larger motion. The lights pulse slowly. The smoke from the chimneys rises slowly. The structure settles, occasionally, with a sound that is not quite a sound, a kind of grinding that you feel in your feet before you hear it. The place is doing what it has been doing for a long time. It is not in a hurry. It has, you sense, nowhere else to be.

You are, you realize, an interruption. A visitor — brief, from above, from the world that buried this place and the world that built this place against it. You have been here a few minutes. The place has been here, in some form, for centuries. The wall-dwellings will be here after you. The water will be here after you. The fauna will go on about their business.

You are welcome, you sense, but you are not waited for.

You can stay as long as you like. The place will wait. It is very good at waiting.
