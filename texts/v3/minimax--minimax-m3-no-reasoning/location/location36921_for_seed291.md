The valley has not decided what it is yet. Sixty years ago, give or take a season, it was a retreating wall of ice. The wall left behind a kind of apology — a flat, soft plain of boulder clay and meltwater — and the plain is still working out what shape it wants to hold. The rivers have not committed. They roam across the broad floor of the valley in slow, patient braids, sometimes abandoning a channel overnight, sometimes digging with quiet insistence at the foot of a boardwalk until the boardwalk sags and has to be replaced.

It is cold, but not the way cold is supposed to be. The cold here lives in the air just above the skin of things. Down low, in the channels, in the alleys between the platforms, the air is warmer — soft with the exhalations of a thousand small boilers, and with a deeper warmth that seeps up through the wet ground from somewhere nobody has bothered to name. The result is that the place steams. The cold breathes on the warm and the warm exhales back, and there is always condensation on every exposed surface: a fine silver sweat on handrails, on riveted iron, on the bent backs of the birds that roost on the smokestacks.

The city has been built, or grown, on this uncommitted ground, and a stranger would have a hard time saying which is being held up by which. The ground is unreliable. Stilts are sunk into the clay and the stilts shift year over year, leaning like tired old men. To compensate, a secondary architecture of timber and woven willow has been lashed over the stilts, and over the timber, in turn, the buildings have been built — and over the buildings, the moss. The moss does not ask permission. It spreads where the footfall is heaviest, softens every corner, climbs the smokestacks in pale green tongues. The inhabitants seem to regard this as a sort of courtesy on the moss's part. The moss is given the rooftops; the rooftops shelter the moss; the moss is harvested, in turn, for the boilers that keep the city warm. The arrangement has been running long enough that nobody quite remembers which of them proposed it.

The city is dense. It is dense the way a body is dense — the buildings are not so much placed as packed, shoulders touching, eaves overlapping, no quarter between one and the next. You can walk the length of a district without ever seeing the sky. The sky, in any case, is unremarkable: a low, soft, uniform grey ceiling at about the height of the third storey of a building. Visibility rarely extends past a few hundred metres. Beyond that, the city dissolves into its own breath. The sound is muffled at that height — the fog carries no echo, only a soft persistent hiss that makes the whole place feel as though it is being played on a single held note.

The sounds that do reach you are small. There is the dink of a hammer on a rivet, somewhere higher up. There is the whir of a vent-belt. There is the occasional annoyed clatter of an iron trolley crossing a wooden bridge over a brown channel, and the bridge answers with a long complaint. There is, often, the breathing of the rivers.

The channels are everywhere. They run between the buildings, under the buildings, in some places through the ground floors of the buildings. They are the city's veins and its refuse both. The water in them is the colour of weak tea and bears a skin of pale green where it has stood still long enough. Small fish — pale, almost translucent, the length of a finger — drift in them in slow columns. Above them, on the boardwalks, otters — if they are otters; their faces are slightly wrong, slightly too round, and they have too much patience — move along the rails with the unhurried ease of creatures who consider the city theirs. Nobody minds. They take a fish now and then. The inhabitants, in return, do not bother them.

The factories are tall and grey-black and many. They cluster on the firmer ground — on the rare outcrops of basalt that survived the ice, and on a few of the older platforms where the stilts have at last settled into a permanent lean. From their stacks the city exhales. The breath comes in two kinds. The first is steam — long horizontal plumes of white that hang in the air because the air is too cold to bear them upward. The second is darker, a kind of slow rust-coloured smoke that smells of vinegar and burnt sugar and that the inhabitants call just *the smell of work*. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of moss being reduced, somewhere inside, into the dense fuel that keeps the rest of the moss from freezing through the winter. Above the stacks the birds wheel. They are mostly small — tern-grey, white-breasted, with black tips to their wings. They have come a long way to be here. They roost on the warmer roofs and pick through the moss for whatever it is they pick through moss for, and once or twice a day a flock lifts off all at once, as if shaken loose from a sheet, and circles the city in a slow rising gyre. Their cries are the loudest thing in the valley, and they are not loud.

There are stranger things. There is a bridge in the old quarter that no longer touches the bank on the western side; it floats on its own, a few inches above the water, and slides gently northward through the year, and will arrive, eventually, somewhere it has never been. There is a chimney that does not smoke from the top but from a hole halfway up, and the smoke issues from it in slow pulses, like the chest of something that has fallen asleep standing up. There is a wall in the alley behind the moss-pressing works that has, set into it, six doors of different sizes, each of them bricked shut. Three of the bricks have moss growing through them. A child's hand has been carved into the smallest door at a height too low for any adult to reach comfortably.

The ground is still settling. The buildings lean a little. A new channel will appear in the spring — they always do — and a low row of houses will have to be picked up and moved bodily across town, like furniture, while their tenants eat porridge in the street. The moss grows another millimetre. The rivers shift their minds. The cold presses down from above and the warmth presses up from below, and between the two the city persists — ugly, damp, packed, half-alive, half-built, half-drowned, and entirely unwilling, despite everything, to become finished.
