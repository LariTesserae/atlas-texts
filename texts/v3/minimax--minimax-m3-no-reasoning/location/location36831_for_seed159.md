# The Infracrust

## I. Approach

The descent begins in scree.

A field of broken stone runs outward in every direction, each fragment warm underfoot and faintly damp, the way river rocks stay cool in summer except inverted — here, the warmth comes from below. A thin vapor lifts off the rubble in slow, patient columns, not quite steam, not quite breath. The light is good: a diffuse glow with no visible source, falling on everything from several directions at once so that no shadow is cast, only a kind of multiplied presence. The sky, such as it is, reads as a low ceiling of stratified rock the color of bone, veined with something darker that pulses at a rhythm you begin, after a few minutes, to suspect is not geological.

You came down through a fissure. There were ladders at one time — wooden stiles pegged into the cleft, rotted now to a few iron spikes and the memory of wood — but most travelers arrive via the sinkholes, which open without warning on the surface and close without warning behind.

The air down here is warm the way a held hand is warm. Damp, too, but not oppressively so. The moisture lives in the stone, in the condensation that beads on every vertical surface, in the slow weep of cliff faces. It is a dampness that implies rain somewhere, always, but rarely delivers. You taste it. You do not see it.

## II. The First Sound

You have been hearing it for some time without admitting it: a low, continuous, everywhere hum. It is not mechanical. It is not geological in any way you have a name for. It is the sound of many things moving at once, layered — and beneath the layers, individual voices. Chirrs. Chitter. A wet, percussive clacking that has the cadence of language but no parseable grammar. A subsonic throb you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear, the way you feel a drum from two streets away.

The sound does not stop. It intensifies as you move, ebbs, intensifies again. It is the sound of a place that is awake.

## III. The Stone Itself

The rock is layered like sediment but did not arrive as sediment. The strata are too clean, too parallel, and they are wrong colors: a deep arterial red, a yellow like old butter, a grey that is almost purple, a black that drinks the light. Between the strata run seams of something that looks like glass until you touch it, at which point it gives like wax. Warm wax. Some travelers claim the seams move when not observed. You do not test this.

The stone is also, in places, unfinished.

You begin to notice it as a quality of the surfaces — a roughness that reads as *recent*. Broken faces of fresh rock where the strata end mid-cliff in jagged terminations, the colors too bright inside, the edges not yet weathered. A boulder here that looks like it was placed yesterday by a hand that lost interest. A wall of exposed substrate that seems almost to be emerging from a larger matrix still in the process of becoming stone, soft at the edges, the bedding planes not yet committed to their final tilt. The world is not done. The world is being made, here, in front of you, by processes that are slower than you can watch and faster than geology has any right to be.

You remember the parameter — *emerging* — and you understand it differently now. It is not that the place is young. It is that the place is being made, continuously, by something that has not finished deciding what it is.

## IV. The Pockets of Water

The moisture has a shape.

It collects in basins — shallow scoops worn into the rock surfaces, each one the size of a cupped hand or a wide bowl, hundreds of them, thousands, in no pattern you can read. Some are dry. Some hold a film of water so thin it is more a darkening of the stone than a liquid. Some are full to brimming, and the water in them is faintly luminous, a green-gold, and warm. From the brimming ones a slow trickle runs to the next basin downslope, and the next, and the next, tracing a hydrology that is part watershed and part calligraphy.

You crouch beside one. The water is clear but not transparent; things move in it, or seem to. A single bright mote drifts past. You do not reach in.

Around the brimming basins, the stone is darker. Damp. Lichenous, almost, except that nothing here grows. What darkens the stone is condensation, patience, and the passage of the small things that come to drink.

## V. The Things That Come to Drink

This is where the place becomes difficult to describe without sounding like a liar.

They are everywhere.

In the basins, on the stone, in the air an inch above the stone, on the undersides of overhangs, clustered in the damp seams, swarming the warm wax-glass, massed along every available surface in densities that should not be possible without collapse or consumption. The first thing you notice is the **sound**, which is no longer ambient but local, directional, sourced: a million tiny legs and bodies moving over stone and over each other, a chitinous whisper that resolves at close range into individual scrapes and bumps. The second thing you notice is that they are not, despite the densities, a single species.

There are six kinds you can identify in the first few minutes. By the end of the hour you have stopped counting.

The smallest are like motes, bright, almost dust-sized, and they ride the air in lazy columns above the warmer stone, never touching, never landing, feeding on nothing you can see. You breathe them. You try not to think about that.

The next size up are flat and dark and fast, with too many legs folded against their bodies, and they run in rivulets along the seams of warm wax-glass, packing the channels the way water packs a gutter, moving with purpose that implies errands you will never understand. They click. Each one clicks, a tiny percussive event, and the sound of them clicking is the wet clacking you heard from a distance. When two of them meet, they pause, antennate each other briefly, and continue. The clicks do not synchronize.

A third kind are pale, soft, blind, and they burrow. You know they burrow because the stone is pocked with their holes — small dark openings scattered across every surface, each one exhaling a faint warmth — and occasionally one surfaces beside your foot, considers the air, and withdraws. They are the thickness of a finger. They are the length of a forearm. You do not know which, because you only ever see a segment of them at a time.

The largest of the visible fauna are the ones that occupy the ledges.

## VI. The Ledges

The cliffs of this infracrust are not cliffs in the terrestrial sense. They are terraced — built up in horizontal steps, each one a few feet high, each one wide enough to walk but not wide enough to forget the drop. The terraces are made of the same stratified stone as everything else, but on the ledges, in the damp, the fauna has constructed.

You use the word *constructed* carefully. It is the right word.

Across the ledges, in rows and clusters and unsearchable geometries, are structures. They are small — most of them the size of a clenched fist, some as large as a crouching person — and they are made of stone fragments, of the warm wax-glass, of the shed husks of the fauna themselves, cemented with a dark paste that smells of iron and ozone. They look like wells. They look like ovens. They look, some of them, like nothing you have a word for, with internal chambers visible through openings that have no doors. They are not all the same. Different species build different shapes, and the shapes, when you look, are clustered by species, so that one stretch of ledge is a city of the small pale burrowers, their constructions pitted and stacked, and another stretch is the long, low, ridged vaults of the flat dark runners, each one fused to the next by their wax-glass trails, and a third is something else entirely: a tangle of vertical spines, each spine taller than you, made of cemented stone, hollow, ringing faintly when the air moves through them, and you cannot tell if they are dwellings, signals, drainage, or song.

The structures are old. Some of them are very old. The oldest are worn back into the stone from which they were taken, so that the ledge has the texture of a brain, ridged and folded, the architecture and the geology no longer distinguishable. The newest are still being built. You watch a single flat dark runner, alone on a ledge, place a fragment of stone with what can only be described as **care** — rotating it, setting it down, antennating the result, adjusting  and then move on to the next. The construction has no other workers in evidence. It will take a long time. It does not appear to mind.

You crouch on a ledge for a long time and watch. None of them notices you. They do not avoid you, exactly, but they do not adjust. You are a feature of the ledge, like the damp, like the warm wax-glass. You are geography.

## VII. The Marks

These are scattered, and they are human, or were.

A hammer — head and haft, the haft rotted, the head still good — wedged into a crack in the stone, placed there with the head down, as if for storage, and forgotten. A length of rope, laid in a coil on a dry ledge, untouched for so long that the stone has begun to grow around it. A canvas sack, half-buried in rubble, holding a tin cup, a tin cup holding a few pale husks and a single dried claw. A wooden post driven into a seam, with a strip of cloth tied to it, the cloth unreadable as text but legible as a flag, as a *claim*, as a sentence whose subject is missing.

There are more. There are fewer than you would expect, given how long people have been coming down here. You understand, after a while, that this is because of the fauna — not that they take things, exactly, but that they incorporate. A wooden post becomes a buttress in a colony. A coil of rope becomes a substrate for a nest. The fauna is patient, and the fauna is dense, and the fauna has had a long time. The marks of human presence are not erased. They are *digested*, slowly, into the architecture of the place, until the place and the mark are the same thing.

The cloth on the post is newer than the post. Someone was here, not long ago. You do not see them. You do not call out.

## VIII. The Deep Sound

There is a deeper sound, beneath the hum, beneath the click and scrape of a million small bodies. You have been hearing it the whole time. You have been confusing it with the hum.

It is a slow, rhythmic percussion, distant, felt in the chest. A deep boom, and then another, and then another, at intervals of perhaps twenty seconds. It comes from below, from the unfinished stone, from the substrate that is still in the process of becoming itself. You walk to the edge of a terrace and look down. There is another terrace below, and another, and another, dropping away into a warm, lit darkness that you cannot see the bottom of. The booms are coming from down there. The brimming basins overflow downward. The ledges stack downward. The cities of the small architects extend downward as far as the light reaches, and beyond the light the sound continues, and beyond the sound the warmth continues, and beyond the warmth the place continues, unfinished, making itself, inhabited by a fauna that has had since before the ladders to learn the shape of its home.

A flat dark runner pauses at the lip of the terrace near your foot, antennates the air in your direction with what might be curiosity and what is more likely the routine sampling of the local conditions, and then continues over the edge and down the wall, sure-footed, joining the others in the layered dark.

The place is loud. The place is warm. The place is damp in the stones and the breath and the seams. The place is full of a life that is not plant and not quite animal and not quite any word you brought with you, a life so dense it has become a kind of weather, a life that builds and burrows and clicks and sings in its hollow spines, a life that has not noticed you because you are not, to it, a thing that needs noticing.

You are a cool pocket in a warm place. You are a brief silence in a loud one. You are, for now, a feature of the ledge.

The boom comes again, from far below, and the stone hums with it, and somewhere in the damp seams a colony of the small pale burrowers shifts in their hundreds and the sound is the sound of a place that is, despite everything, still being made.
