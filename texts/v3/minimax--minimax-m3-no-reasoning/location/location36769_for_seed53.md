# The Upper Shrine of Dyrgalon

The wind arrives first.

It comes up the granite staircase in long, slow exhalations, pressing cold against whatever skin or surface meets it. The air carries the smell of wet stone, of iron, of something older and harder to name — the mineral breath of a mountain that has spent uncounted years slowly grinding itself against the sky. By the time you reach the top of the stair, your ears have stopped trying to interpret the silence as silence, and have begun to recognize it as the absence of anything alive.

The shrine sits at fourteen thousand feet. It occupies a saddle between two shoulders of grey rock that the locals — those few who live low enough to know of it — call the Sleeping God and the Sleeping God. They are not the same god. They are not awake.

The architecture is simple and severe. Black basalt walls, fitted without mortar, rising about twice the height of a tall man. The roof is flat, open to the sky through a rectangular mouth cut into the center, and the floor is a single slab of pale stone worn to a polish by something that was not feet. Eight pillars stand at the corners of the inner chamber, each carved in the shape of a closed fist. They are roughly hewn. They were not made to be looked at closely. There is a low stone altar at the far end, and on the altar, a shallow bowl, and in the bowl, water.

It rains here more often than it should at this altitude.

The bowl is rarely empty. No one understands why. There is a persistent theory among the pilgrims who come up in the dry months — and only in the dry months, because in the wet months the stair becomes a cataract — that the water condenses from the air itself. The mountain sweats, they say, and the shrine drinks. Other pilgrims mutter about an underground source, a hidden spring, a vein of meltwater threading the granite heart of the world. The priests of the shrine do not discuss it. The priests of the shrine are not numerous.

There are three of them, in fact. They live in a row of low stone cells built against the outer east wall, and they take turns — by some counting system that none of the visiting pilgrims has ever been able to reconstruct — kneeling beside the altar. They wear the same undyed wool, cut to the same rough pattern, and they carry small bronze knives that they use for nothing observable. Once a year, at a festival the shrine does not advertise, one of them walks down the mountain and does not come back. A new one is sent up in the autumn. No one knows from where.

What the priests do, exactly, is a question the shrine answers in its own way.

The chamber rings. Not always. Not audibly. But the walls hold a low resonance, a hum felt more in the teeth than the ears, that seems to intensify when the bowl is full and soften when the bowl empties. The priests have been heard to hum back. They do not appear to be conscious of it. They kneel, and they wait, and the mountain decides whether to give them water, and the bowl fills, and the hum swells, and the wind comes down the stair, and somewhere far below the valley is green with a thing that does not grow here.

Nothing grows here.

This is the first thing a traveler notices and the last thing they understand. There is no lichen on the basalt. There is no moss in the cracks of the floor. The wind that comes up the stair carries no seeds, or carries seeds that never take. A pilgrim who left a sprig of juniper on the altar in the morning found it, on returning the next year, brown and brittle but otherwise unchanged — the mountain had not bothered to rot it. The shrine is preserved the way a thing is preserved when nothing has decided to eat it. The air is too cold, and too thin, and too clean, and the stone does not yield.

The light, though. The light is extraordinary.

Because there is no plant cover, no dust, no weather in the lower air to soften the sun, the shadows are knife-edged and the highlights are blinding. In the morning the eastern wall flares orange and the pillars cast long blue lines across the floor like the hands of a sundial that has not yet decided what time it is. By noon the shrine is white with light, every flake of mica in the granite throwing back a tiny spark, so that the whole plateau seems to shiver. In the late afternoon the bowl fills with gold. This is when the pilgrims prefer to arrive. The priests do not seem to prefer anything.

The wind does most of the work of the place. It scours the floor of the bowl. It carries away the offerings left at the base of the pillars — coins, mostly, and small carved stones, and once a child's tooth — and deposits them, by some slow redistribution the priests do not interrupt, in a low cairn at the leeward corner of the shrine. The cairn has been growing for a very long time. The coins in its oldest layers are no longer recognizable as coins. They are simply dark slivers pressed into dark sediment. The cairn does not rot. The cairn is, like the juniper sprig, merely persisted with.

Sometimes, in the deep winter, the stair freezes. The shrine becomes unreachable for months. The priests remain. There is no record of one of them ever having come down in the cold. There is, on the other hand, no record of one of them ever having frozen. The bronze knives, it should be said, are not for warmth.

The shrine is not abandoned. The shrine is not occupied. The shrine persists. It is dense with itself: every stone chosen, every joint deliberate, every surface bearing some faint trace of intention that the centuries have worn almost but not quite away. The pillars are the densest things. They are not carved fists, exactly, although that is what the pilgrims call them. The fingers are closed around something, or the fingers are themselves the thing being held. The priests do not explain. The priests do not seem to know there is a question.

At night, when there is no moon, the stars are so numerous that the bowl of water reflects them and the bowl appears to be a second, deeper sky. The priests, on these nights, do not kneel by the altar. They stand on the outer wall and watch the dark. They do not speak. The mountain does not speak. The wind drops, sometimes, for an hour, and in that hour the silence is the loudest thing in the world, and you can hear, very faintly, the hum of the pillars answering something you cannot see.

It is quieter here than you have ever been quiet. It is colder. The light, when it comes back, will be blinding again. The bowl will be full again, or nearly so. The priests will kneel. The wind will resume. The cairn will accept another small offering. The Sleeping God will not wake.

You will go down the stair, and your ears will pop, and the green valley will rise up to meet you, and the shrine will remain, the way a stone remains, the way a hum remains in a closed room long after the source has stopped.
