# The Glassbone Reach

Imagine a hollow beneath the skin of a young continent. The land above is barely formed — a plateau of sharp limestone thrust up from a sea-floor that, geologically speaking, just yesterday was still ocean. The stone is pale, and where the cover is thin enough to see it through, it glows like a fever. But you are not above. You are below, in the vastness.

---

## The Hollow Itself

A cavern. But the word is wrong, the way "lake" would be wrong for an ocean. This is a continent-scale void — two hundred kilometers across at its widest, a ceiling that climbs out of sight into a permanent false dawn. The stone is young. It has not had time to remember the shape of the burden above it; the ceiling hangs in long unsupported arcs, in ribs of calcite that meet only at the imagination of pillars. Where the rock is thinnest, light pours through.

The light is the first thing you would notice, if you arrived as a person. It has no source — or rather, its sources are so many and so small and so high that the effect is the same: a diffuse, sourceless brilliance, the white of a January morning. There are no shadows except the ones cast by what is *in* the hollow, not by the stone above. The eye searches for a sun and finds instead a ceiling like a dim lantern, and gives up, and accepts the cold white everywhere.

It is cold. The air, where there is air, sits at just above freezing — a damp cold, the kind that gets into gloves and joints and the spaces between fibers. Your breath would fog, then not, then fog again, depending on which part of the hollow you are in. There are breezes. There are drafts. They come from the fractures where the light comes in, the way air comes in under a door.

---

## The Substrate

The floor is not stone. The floor is *organism*.

Begin with a single plant — call it the **marrow-vine** if you want a name, though the only people here have no language for it. It grows in a calcium shell, white and fibrous, like a leaf of celery that has learned to photosynthesize without light. Each marrow-vine sends a taproot down through the pale rock — *into* it, slowly dissolving it, slowly turning the floor of the continent into itself. The vine is six feet across at the base. The vine is two hundred feet tall, and the topmost of it, in the bright upper reaches, opens into translucent fans that drink the thin light that filters through.

There are billions of them.

They are spaced out — sometimes fifty yards apart, sometimes a hundred, sometimes a thousand. Between them, the original limestone floor is still visible, a cracked pale pavement. But the vines are everywhere, and between the vines there are the *children*.

The children of the marrow-vine are smaller, parasitic, symbiotic, the distinction is not clean. They climb the parents. They hang in curtains from the upper reaches. They form bridges between trunks — a green-white lattice that, in places, has become the floor, a second story of vegetable scaffolding. Where the light is brightest, near the ceiling, the children of the children flower. The flowers are not colorful. They are *luminous* — pale, wet, faintly bioluminescent, throwing a greenish light that is barely distinguishable from the white daylight above, except that it moves. It pulses. It is the heartbeat of the place.

Stand in the middle of a clearing between two parent vines, and you are in a cathedral of living bone, lit by stars that hang from the vaulting.

---

## The Water

The hollow is damp the way a cellar is damp. Condensation collects on the underside of the stone ceiling and falls in long, slow drips. Where the drips fall, the marrow-vines have grown basins — cupped leaves the size of dinner tables, oriented to catch. The basins overflow, and the water runs down the trunks, and the trunks have grown channels for it, and the channels become streams, and the streams become rivers running along the cracked floor between the parent vines.

The rivers are small. The rivers are also the only flat, unobstructed ground in the place. The marrow-vines have learned, somehow, to not grow in the streambeds. They overhang. They form tunnels of root and stem over the running water. The effect, if you are standing in the stream looking up, is of being inside the ribcage of some vast drowned animal, with daylight filtering down through the slats of it.

The water is cold and clear and hard. It tastes of stone. Where the streams pool, in the deeper fractures of the original floor, the pools are full of a thing that is not quite a plant and not quite a fungus — a pale crust at the edges, a kind of crystalline felt that breathes. The pools steam faintly, in the cold, because the water is slightly warmer than the air.

It rains, sometimes. Through the fractures, when the weather above changes, water comes down in actual curtains. The vines drink it. The streams rise. The hollow *breathes*, the whole vast space, in and out, in and out, on a cycle measured in days.

---

## The Inhabitants

There are not many.

The **skull-crows** are the largest thing that moves. They are birds, mostly. Pale. They nest in the upper reaches of the marrow-vines, where the light is good, and they have learned to eat the luminous flowers. Their beaks are stained green with the pollen-equivalent. They have voices like wet metal — a high, thin, ringing *tink tink* that carries for miles in the cold air. They do not flock. They are solitary and territorial, each one owning a circle of perhaps a square mile of canopy. In a still moment, you can hear five or six of them at once, scattered across the cathedral, each calling its claim into the empty bright air.

There is a **burrowing thing** under the floor. You don't see it. You see, sometimes, the mound — a pale heap of crushed limestone pushed up between two vines, like a molehill the size of a car. It is nocturnal. It eats the pale crystalline felt in the pools. It leaves tracks in the streams sometimes, in the mud at the edges — a three-toed print, clawed, as big as your spread hand.

There are insects. There are always insects. Pale, blind, wingless things that live in the wet rot of the vine-leaves where they overlap the streams. They make no sound you can hear, but if you put your hand on a vine-trunk, you can feel the colony-vibration, a hum at the very bottom of hearing, the sound of ten thousand tiny feet in the wet.

And there are **the people**.

---

## The People

Few. Scattered. Sparse as the marrow-vines were, once, before they filled the place.

They live in the streambeds, where the ground is flat. They have built — to call it *built* is generous — structures of vine-bone and limestone slab, lashed together with the long sinewy fibers they strip from the marrow-vines' dead outer skin. The structures are low, partly underground, roofed with the same white calcium shell. They look, from a distance, like a kind of natural feature — another kind of crust, a geological shelf. If you did not know, you would walk past one and not see it.

The people are pale. They are small. They are cold-adapted in the way that anything living in this place is cold-adapted — narrow, dense, with a metabolism that runs hot under the skin. They eat the vine-flowers. They eat the crystalline felt. They eat, sometimes, the burrowing thing, when they can catch it. They have fire — they keep it in clay lamps, fueled by a slow-blackening oil pressed from the vine-flowers' seed-pods. The fire is precious. The fire is the only orange light in the hollow. By firelight, inside a low stone-and-bone hut, with the white luminous flowers pulsing faintly through the cracks in the wall, a person looks almost warm.

They have language. They have songs. The songs are slow, and sung low, in harmony with the hum of the insects in the vines, and they travel a long way through the cold air before they die.

They have not climbed to the top of the marrow-vines. They have not, as far as anyone knows, found a way out. They do not seem to mind. They are a young people in a young place, and the place is so vast that a person could walk a lifetime in one direction and never see the wall.

---

## The Weather of the Hollow

This is the part you would not believe, if you were told.

The hollow has *weather*.

The light from above is not constant. The stone is thin, but it is not uniform, and above the hollow the sun and the wind of the young continent are doing their slow geological work. Clouds pass over. Shadows of clouds, huge and moving, slide across the luminous ceiling — and the light in the hollow dims, by a little, and the dimness moves. The marrow-vines, in their upper reaches, can be seen to *track* it, the fans turning a fraction of a degree through the day, following the brighter patches in the ceiling. The whole upper canopy, two hundred feet up, slowly rotates. From below, you see it as a kind of slow-motion shimmer.

The drafts reverse when the sun is high. The streams rise and fall. The flowers pulse faster. The skull-crows get louder. Somewhere, far away, a section of ceiling collapses — a faint, deep, *whump* — and a column of pale dust falls, lit from above, like a pillar of smoke. The vines will grow over the rubble in a season.

And then, on a day when the pressure above is right, when the storms of the young continent align just so, the rain comes down through the fractures in earnest. It falls in sheets, in columns, in individual streams the thickness of a man's body. The hollow fills with the sound of it — a vast, enveloping, percussive *roar*, the sound of a waterfall the size of a city. The streams on the floor become rivers. The marrow-vines drink. The flowers open. The insects go quiet. The people go inside.

It lasts an hour. It lasts a day. It stops.

And the hollow, vast and bright and cold and damp and full of the slow pulse of living bone, settles back into its quiet, and the skull-crows resume their ringing, and the streams run clear again, and the light comes down, and the place continues, as it has for the few thousand years of its young existence, to *be*.
