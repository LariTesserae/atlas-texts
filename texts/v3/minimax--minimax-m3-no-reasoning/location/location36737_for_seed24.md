The hollow opens like a held breath.

You find it at the bottom of a long, slow descent — a fold in the earth where the rock has fallen away and never been filled back in. The approach is gradual, the kind of slope that takes a long time to notice, and then suddenly you are standing inside something. The walls lift up around you in a loose curve, not quite a cave, not quite an overhang, more the feeling of a room that has forgotten it had a ceiling. The height is somewhere between a person and a cathedral — enough that sound, if there were any, would think twice before rising.

The stone is dark. Not black, not the dramatic black of volcanic glass, but the dense brown-black of iron-rich sandstone that has spent a long time being patient. The color deepens as you look higher, where the light never quite reaches, until the upper reaches become more suggestion than surface. Down here, in the wider part of the hollow, there is enough glow from the narrow mouth of the opening to make out the texture of the walls, and the texture is the point. The rock is old, settled into itself, every grain in conversation with every other grain. There is no loose scree on the floor, no fresh fractures. Whatever was going to fall has already fallen. Whatever was going to slump has already slumped. The hollow looks the way it has looked for longer than anyone is around to say.

The air is warm, and it stays warm. Whatever heat gathers in this low place during the day does not fully leave at night, and whatever cold might press in from outside is gently refused at the threshold. You don't feel it as warmth exactly — more the absence of any temperature at all. The body, which usually spends some effort deciding whether it is comfortable, finds itself with nothing to do.

There is no water. Not pooled, not running, not in the air. The stone is dry the way bone is dry. If you put your hand against the wall it would feel the way old books feel in closed rooms — the kind of dry that has been going on so long it has become its own substance. No lichens creep across the lower surfaces; no moss cushions the crevices. Whatever organisms depend on moisture have simply never applied here, and the rock accepts this as the way of things.

Vegetation is present but cautious. A few things grow, far apart from each other, each one committed entirely to its own small territory. Near the mouth, where a thin blade of brighter air enters the hollow, a single woody shrub has pressed itself into a crack that runs down from the rim. It is gnarled in the way that desert shrubs are gnarled — not twisted by wind, because the wind does not really come in here, but shaped by long slowness, by the patient negotiation between a small green thing and a lot of stone. Its leaves are small, gray, waxy. It is not dying. It is not flourishing. It is the kind of plant that has decided, some time ago, what it is.

Further back, against the deeper walls, there are a few pale tufts of something — not quite grass, not quite lichen, more a kind of dry growth that has no good English name, a few pale threads that catch the dim light and give it back. They don't move. The air in here moves so little that movement itself has become optional.

Somewhere around the middle height of one wall, on the side that faces the opening, there are marks. Not drawings — nothing as articulate as drawings — but the evidence of someone working. Long shallow grooves in the stone, too regular to be erosion, too careless to be decoration. A few places where the surface has been hammered or scraped to a different texture. A small hollow worn into a lip of rock, the kind that forms where the same gesture has been repeated against the same spot, over and over, for longer than the gesture itself meant to last. Someone once sat here, or stood here, with a tool — a stone, probably, or a hard stick, or a piece of antler if there were such things — and did something simple and patient. Shaping a piece of stone, maybe. Sharpening something. Preparing a hide against the rock, perhaps, or just working out a thought with their hands. They are not here now. Whatever they made, they took with them, or it wore away, or it never existed outside the gesture. The hollow remembers the effort without remembering the maker. This is the only real trace that anyone with hands has ever been in this place, and it is faint enough to mistake for weather.

There are no other marks. No fire-blackened ceiling, no stacked stones, no cleared floor, no paths worn in. Whoever came here came once or twice, or for a while, but did not stay, and did not believe the place was theirs to rearrange.

The floor of the hollow is sand, but a particular kind of sand — fine, almost powdery, the same color as the walls but loose. It has accumulated over a long time from the slow weathering of the stone above, grain by grain, so that the hollow is slowly, imperceptibly, filling itself in. Or rather, it would be, except that nothing here is moving fast enough for the word *slowly* to mean what it usually means. A century in this hollow might deepen the sand by the width of a fingernail. A millennium might not change anything a person would notice. The equilibrium is so nearly perfect that change has been argued out of the room.

There are no animals. Not because the place is forbidding — it isn't, exactly; it's merely indifferent — but because no animal has a reason to be here. No food, no water, no shelter from anything that isn't already absent. The hollow is not a refuge from anything. It is simply a place that has not been claimed, and has no interest in being claimed.

What sound there is, is small. The dry sand shifts under its own weight in tiny collapses too quiet to hear. The air makes a faint, almost-not-there sound against the stone at the opening, a whisper that is more the idea of wind than the wind itself. Sometimes — not often, not predictably — a piece of sand, disturbed by nothing visible, slides down the wall of the hollow and arrives at the floor with a soft tick that seems louder than it should because there is nothing else. If you stand here for a long time, the silences begin to have textures, and you can hear yourself hearing them.

The light, where it exists, is dim and warm. It enters from the single opening and lies across the floor in a long, slanted shape that does not move during the hours you might stand here. The deepest part of the hollow is not in darkness exactly, but in a kind of dimness that the eye accepts without complaint, the way it accepts the inside of a closed eye. You can see. You cannot see clearly. The distinction turns out to matter less than you would think.

This is not a place that wants anything from you. It does not ask to be understood, or preserved, or named. It has been here for a long time in the state you find it, and it will continue to be here for a long time after you leave, in something very close to the same state. The tool marks on the wall will soften by the thickness of a breath. The sand will rise by the depth of a thought. The little shrub will go on being exactly what it is. Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here is ending. The hollow is, in the old sense of the word, *settled* — the way a long breath settles, the way dust settles, the way a stone that has finally stopped rolling settles into its own shape on the ground and accepts it.
