# The Dormant Quarter

The river arrives in pieces here. Not as a body but as a memory of one — a sheet of standing water no deeper than a finger's width, broken by low hummocks of moss and gravel, threading between foundations that were never quite finished. The cold is structural, the kind that has been cold for so long it has forgotten the possibility of being anything else. Your breath does not cloud; it is simply absorbed into the air, which is too still to carry it.

## What Stands

The settlement, such as it is, occupies a slight depression in the land — a long fold, perhaps an old streambed, perhaps something else. The structures crowd together as if they had grown toward one another for warmth. Most are narrow, three to four stories, with walls of dark composite that appears to be stone until you look closely and realize it is neither stone nor metal nor ceramic but something with a memory of all three. The surfaces are veined with what might be mineral deposits and what might be growth. The line between the two has never been clear here.

A second layer of construction sits above the first — bridges, suspended walkways, the hollow bones of unfinished upper stories. These higher elements cast complex shadows that never quite repeat. There is no sun in the conventional sense, but a diffuse source from the north-northeast casts light that agrees with itself from moment to moment, so that the settlement exists in something like a permanent 3 p.m. The shadows pool in the narrow channels between buildings, where the water lies, and the water holds the shadows like a dark mirror.

A few structures rise taller than the rest — seven, eight stories — their upper reaches tapering into spires or truncated planes or simply stopping, open to the air. From certain angles these incomplete towers resemble the cross-section of a much larger building that continues underground.

## The Erosion

The land is younger than the buildings. This is the central wrongness of the place, the thing that does not sit right and never will.

The ground is raw. Exposed mineral in pale greys and dull ochres, cut by shallow channels where the infrequent rain has barely begun to draw its signature. The foundations of the settlement sit in this scraped earth like teeth in a healing gum — not yet fully embedded, not yet integrated, the surrounding soil still too pale and too sharp-edged to have known much weather. Where moisture collects in the low places, the earth is darker, softer, beginning to remember itself. But most of the surface is fresh, the way a hillside is fresh after a slide, the way a quarry floor is fresh.

It is as if someone, or some process, had cleared the ground recently — within years, within months — and then laid a city down upon the cleared space without waiting. The buildings were placed; the land was abandoned to its own patience. Now the patient processes are beginning, but they have barely begun. A century from now this place will be unrecognizable. Right now it is a city being slowly tucked into its own geology.

## The Water

The dampness here is not rainfall. It is exhalation.

Condensation gathers on every horizontal surface overnight and slides down the dark walls in the morning, collecting in the street-level channels. The ground itself sweats, in places — a thin capillary moisture that brings salts to the surface and leaves a pale rime along the seams of the paving. The river-to-the-west, when it is not the river, becomes a chain of pools; when it is the river, the water moves through the lowest streets and laps against the foundations of the lowest buildings with a sound like someone breathing in their sleep.

There is fog, frequently, low and slow. It lies in the streets and pours from upper windows. It is not quite fog; it has a faint taste, mineral and cold, and it leaves a residue on anything metal that resembles a fingerprint.

## The Vegetation

Sparse and stubborn. A moss, dark green-black, colonizing the north faces of the lowest structures. A lichen in pale orange that has taken to the wet stones. A single species of low shrub — woody, almost succulent, with grey leaves no larger than a thumbnail — that has established itself in cracks in the raw earth and along the margins of the standing water. It is not native, or not obviously native, and it appears to be winning its slow argument with the substrate.

The trees, such as they are, are skeletal. Six of them, planted or rooted, no one can say, in a rough line along what might once have been a processional way. They have not grown in any direction one would expect. They branch laterally, close to the ground, in a repeated Y-form, and their bark is the same dark composite as the buildings. Whether they are trees, or buildings that have softened, is a question the residents have apparently agreed not to ask.

## The Fauna

Rare. Movement, when it occurs, is small and lateral — a scuttling in the walls, a brief shape in the standing water. Something lives in the upper incomplete stories; you can hear it occasionally, a dry clicking, as of claws on the composite material, moving in straight lines from one junction to another. It has not been seen. The lower fauna is more visible: a creature the size of a thumb, pale, that moves across the wet stones in herds of dozens, feeding on the orange lichen. They are the dominant visible life. They do not appear to fear the settlement; they flow around the foundations like water around stones.

Birds, occasionally. A single cry, repeated three or four times, from above, where the air is too clear and too cold. Then silence.

## The Sound

Quiet, but not silent. There is always the water — the slow drip from condensation, the distant murmur of the river when it is being the river. There is a hum, low and constant, that comes from the buildings themselves, particularly the larger ones, as if some process is running in their lower levels. It is not machinery. It is the sound a building makes when it is very old and very dense and is doing something no one has asked it to do.

Occasionally a sound from above: the dry clicking, the single cry, the soft displacement of air that might be a wing or might be nothing.

A person could live here for years and never hear another voice. The settlement seems designed for that, or has settled into it.

## The Wrongness

The place is correct in every particular and is also, unmistakably, *wrong*.

This is the part that is hard to say clearly. The buildings obey physics. The water flows downhill. The moss grows on the north face. The cold is cold. And yet —

The shadows fall in directions the light does not justify. They lengthen and shorten on a cycle that does not match the diffused northern source. The same shadow, watched long enough, will lean slightly to the left, then return, as if a very slow pendulum were swinging in some adjacent geometry.

The standing water reflects, but the reflections are not the buildings above them. They show other buildings — similar in style, different in detail — that do not exist in the air above. Sometimes the reflections are of the same buildings but from a different angle, as if the water were a window into an identical settlement rotated ten degrees out of phase.

The condensation on the walls forms patterns. These patterns are not random. They spell nothing in any known alphabet, but they repeat, and the repetition is too regular to be physics and too irregular to be writing. A linguist would go mad here. A mathematician would simply leave.

The residents — for there are residents, or something that functions as residents — do not appear to notice any of this. They move through the place with the unhurried competence of people who have always lived here and intend to always live here. There are perhaps a hundred of them, perhaps more, distributed through the dense lower structures. They are visible mostly at the edges of things — in doorways, at the margins of pools, in the upper windows of the smaller buildings. They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are simply *there*, in the way the buildings are there, in the way the water is there, in the way the wrongness is there.

The settlement is, in some sense, a settlement. People live here, or something like people. There is commerce, or something like commerce — an exchange, conducted in the lower streets near the water, of objects whose purpose is not clear. There are children, or something like children, who play a game in the shallow pools, throwing stones at reflections of buildings that are not above them. The adults do not stop them. The adults do not seem to see the discrepancy.

## The Tech

The technology is invisible because it is everywhere. It is in the walls, in the water, in the way the cold is distributed evenly through the settlement despite the absence of any visible heating. It is in the slow completion of the upper stories, which appear to be a few feet taller each year — a growth so gradual it is measurable only by the placement of the shadows. It is in the way the residents move, the way they do not age visibly, the way the building accepts their presence and the building's hum modulates when they pass through certain rooms.

The technology does not announce itself. It does not require attention. It is post-physical in the sense that it is no longer distinguishable from the environment it modifies. Asking how the buildings are built here is like asking how a river is built. The answer is that the river builds itself, given enough time and the right conditions, and so do these buildings, and so does the cold, and so does the wrongness.

## The Slow

Nothing here changes quickly. The erosion is embryonic. The moss is colonizing at the speed of moss. The shadows lean and return. The buildings grow upward at the rate of fingernails. The residents move at the rate of people who have somewhere to be and no reason to hurry.

The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of a question being asked very slowly, over centuries, and the answer being given at the same rate. The question is not audible. The answer, when it comes, will not be announced. It will simply be true one day, the way the cold is true, the way the damp is true, the way the reflections in the standing water will, eventually, agree with what is above them — or what is above them will, eventually, agree with the reflections.

A traveler arriving here would be unable to say, after a week, what year it was. After a month, what century. After a year, whether they had been here a year or whether the year had been a moment.

## The Air

The cold air lies on the skin like a film. It is the kind of cold that does not bite but seeps, that finds the gaps between clothing and the pores of the skin and establishes residence there. After an hour outside, the exposed skin of the face is numb in a way that is almost pleasant — a kind of clarity, a kind of quieting.

The light, when it shifts, shifts in a way that suggests a longer day than there actually is. The diffused northern source does not set; it simply dims over the course of several hours and then returns. The residents call this period the grey time, and they retreat indoors during it, not out of fear but out of what seems to be a polite agreement with the settlement that the grey time is for being inside.

## The Edge

The settlement does not end so much as become uncertain. The dense construction thins at the margins. The buildings become lower, more widely spaced, more clearly incomplete. The standing water spreads out into broad shallow sheets. The raw earth beyond is fresher, paler, less touched. The skeletal trees thin to one, then to none. The hum of the buildings fades.

Past the margin, there is no transition to anything else. There is just the cold and the damp and the slow erosion and the silence, and eventually the walker stops, because there is nothing to walk toward, and turns back, because there is something to walk from, and the settlement, when it reappears on the horizon, is exactly as it was, and this too is part of the wrongness, which is patient, which is everywhere, which has all the time it needs.
