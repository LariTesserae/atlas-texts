# The Drowned Fells

Above the waterline — if you can call it that, the place where the world stops being entirely ocean and becomes a memory of ocean — a single ridgeline breaks the surface. It runs north and south as far as anyone has cared to walk, a spine of dark basalt andesite that the original glaciers carved and the long submersion has since honeycombed with brine. The ridge is high enough that on calm days a handspan of stone still shows. On rough days, nothing. The locals — if they are locals, if anyone was ever *from* here — call the high points the Teeth.

The water is the place. Everything else is punctuation.

---

### The water itself

It is not the cold of ice. It is the cold of depth, of pressure, of a darkness that has weight. Even at the surface the temperature bites. Down where the Fells slope into their proper underwater form, the cold becomes a thing you could not survive without preparation: a slow chemical cold, mineral-laden, almost viscous. Currents move through the submerged valleys in long slow arteries, carrying silt and the pale drift of suspended limestone flour. The water has a taste when it splashes — bitter, with a green metallic edge, the flavor of the basalt leaching iron and magnesium into the sea.

Light gets in, but only just. The sun, when it manages to break through the cloud cover that lives permanently over the drowned range, lays down long pale roads on the surface that move with the swell. These roads never reach the bottom. The Fells sit in a permanent twilight below a hundred meters, and below three hundred it is only the faintest suggestion of indigo, the color of a sky remembered in a dream.

The sea breathes. There is a long swell, oceanic, eight to ten seconds between crests even in the sheltered valleys. On the ridge, this swell becomes a thing: water pouring over the lip of stone, falling into the hollows on the leeward side, retreating, pouring again. The air above a Tooth is a place of continuous explosion of spray and slumping water, a sound like distant artillery or like a giant's washing day, never stopping.

### The Teeth

The exposed rock is not bare. It is a living thing in its slow way.

A black lichen — flat, crustose, the color of old iron — has colonized every surface above the tideline. It is so dense in places that the rock beneath becomes invisible, a continuous skin of carbon-dark growth that drinks the spray and the thin cold sunlight. Where the water pools in hollows and lingers between swells, the lichen gives way to a different community: a thick felt of mosses, ochre and olive and the particular yellow-green of things that have learned to photosynthesize on three hours of dim light per day. These mosses grow *into* the water, not away from it. They send fronds down into the sub-aquatic zones, where they continue to grow for a meter or two before giving up.

Below the moss line, the rock is colonized by something stranger. A soft, branching organism, pale lavender in the upper reaches where some light still filters, fading to bone-white and finally to a faint phosphorescent blue at the edge of visibility. It looks like coral but it is not coral. It looks like fungus but it is not fungus. It looks like the inside of a lung, which is closer to the truth of what it is. The fishermen who used to work these ranges before the last submergence — and there are still people who remember, who tell the stories, who steer their boats wide of the Teeth at certain phases of the moon — call it *breath-flesh*. It does not move. It does not react to touch. But in the long dark, if you put your ear to a wet stone, you can hear a faint rhythmic expansion and contraction that has nothing to do with any tide.

### The hollows

Behind every Tooth is a hollow. These are the heart of the place.

When the swell pours over a ridge, it fills the hollow behind it: a sudden wall of water, a churning basin, a brief return to the conditions of the deep ocean that the world has become. Then the water finds the low points along the ridge, the notches cut by ten thousand years of falling seawater, and it pours back out, leaving the hollow empty for a handful of seconds. Then the next wave comes.

The hollows are not quiet. They are the loudest places in the drowned world. A hollow in the filling phase sounds like a river reversing over a cliff. In the draining phase it sounds like a hundred drainplugs pulled at once. In between there is a wet sucking roar and a brief silence and then the next wall of water arrives. Standing on the lip of a hollow at low state, with the basin before you six feet deep in kelp and tumbled stone and the next wave visible as a dark line on the horizon, is a thing that rearranges a person.

The hollows are where the strange lives.

In the deeper ones, where the rock drops away into the original glacial valleys and the water lingers between swells long enough to settle into clarity, you can see down. Twenty meters, thirty, on the best days. What you see is the *breath-flesh* in its mature form: not the pale crust of the shallows but enormous branching structures, ten and fifteen meters tall, swaying slightly in the residual surge, a forest of pale organs breathing in the half-light. They cluster in the channels between Teeth, in the places where the currents bring them constant fresh water and constant food. From a height, looking down into a clear hollow, the breath-flesh forests look like cities seen from a passing plane. From below, looking up, they look like the columns of a drowned cathedral.

No one knows what they eat. No one knows what eats them. No one has ever seen a breath-flesh die.

### The kelp

The dominant visible life is kelp, and it is everywhere.

A species with a holdfast the color of a bruise and a stipe as thick as a man's wrist climbs up from the deep channels and lays itself across the ridges when the water retreats, so that at low state each Tooth wears a shaggy pelt of olive-brown weed. The blades are long — three, four meters — and they slap against the rock with each returning wave, a wet percussive sound that layers over the roar of the hollows. When the water returns the kelp lifts and streams out into the current, and for a moment the Teeth look like they are growing manes of dark hair that the sea is combing.

The kelp is cold-blooded, slow, ancient. It has been here longer than the submersion, longer than the last ice, probably. Its holdfasts grip the basalt in the surge zone and the fronds reach up toward the light that is no longer really sufficient for them, and they keep growing anyway, because that is what kelp does. In the hollows the kelp forms tangled rafts that ride the fill-and-drain cycle, sinking and rising and sinking again, a slow brown breathing of its own.

There are fish, somewhere, in the deeper water beyond the Teeth. The fishermen remember them. But in the Fells themselves, in the range of the ridge, there are no fish. There are no animals at all. There is the kelp. There is the breath-flesh. There is the lichen and the moss. The rest of the living world has, for the moment, withdrawn.

### The sky and the weather

The sky is overcast. It is always overcast.

A permanent low ceiling of stratus, somewhere between three hundred and eight hundred meters, in colors that range from pale ash to the dark gray of a closed eye. Through it, rarely, the sun appears as a disc with a silver edge, giving no heat, only a brighter gray. The cloud base sometimes lowers to the ridge, sometimes to the water, and in the worst weather the whole world becomes a single white nothing, water and air indistinguishable, a cold blindness in which the only thing you can hear is the wave-pour and your own breathing.

It rains. It rains often. It is never a hard rain — the air is too cold for the violence of a warm-world downpour — but it is a constant one, a fine near-horizontal drift of salt-laden droplets that soaks through everything in about twenty minutes and then keeps soaking. The rain on the open rock makes a sound like a thousand tiny fingers drumming on a tabletop. The rain on a kelp-pelted ridge makes no sound at all; the water is absorbed, held, released slowly to the next wave.

There is wind. The wind is the other voice of the place. It comes from the west, mostly, funneled between the swells, and when it crosses a Tooth it makes a sound that the old fishermen have a word for: *the long note*. It is not a howl, not a moan. It is a single sustained tone, near the bottom of a man's hearing, that the basalt columns of the ridge sing when the wind is right. On a still day you can sometimes feel it more than hear it, a vibration in the soles of your feet, in your teeth, in the bones of your chest.

### The People

There are not many.

There is a small settlement on the largest of the Teeth, on a flattish shoulder of basalt about eight meters above mean sea level, where a few families live in stone-and-driftwood houses built low against the prevailing weather. They are *breath-flesh* people, or so they call themselves, though they are as human as anyone. They eat the kelp. They eat the lichen. They catch, in the deeper water beyond the Fells, the fish that they bring back in skin boats and dry on frames. They are small, broad, dark of skin, and they have voices pitched to carry over the constant sound of water.

Their tools are simple. Hand-hafted stone axes for breaking kelp holdfasts. Bone hooks for the deep fishing. A few metal things, traded up the coast from settlements that still have access to smelters — a knife, sometimes, an axe-head, a needle. They weave kelp-fiber into ropes and rough cloth. They burn driftwood and dried kelp in small stone hearths, and the fires are sacred to them, the only warm thing in a cold world, and they are tended with a care that approaches tenderness.

They have one piece of technology that surprises visitors, and it is this: they have learned to listen to the breath-flesh. They can sit at the edge of a hollow in the low state, with the kelp writhing around them and the next wave already visible on the horizon, and they can hear, in the faint rhythmic expansion of the pale forests below, a pattern. They do not say the pattern is language. They do not say the pattern is not language. They say the pattern is *the way the world is breathing right now*, and they adjust their fishing and their harvesting and their movements along the ridge to it, the way another people might adjust to the phases of a moon.

### The strangeness

The place is strange in a way that does not perform strangeness.

It does not have strange geometries. It does not have wrong colors. It does not have anything that overtly violates the regularities of the world. What it has, instead, is the steady quiet pressure of the *unexplained*. The breath-flesh breathes and no one knows why. The kelp grows in a half-light that should not be enough, and grows, and grows. The water is cold and the air is colder and the wind sings a single note through basalt columns that have no business producing a single note. The people who live here do not seem to mind any of this. They have made a life in the long gray breathing world, and they move through it with the matter-of-fact competence of people who have never known anything else.

The strangeness is in the *fact* of the place. The world did not always look like this. The water was not always the dominant thing. There are other places, far to the south and east, where the land still wins its long argument with the sea. People from those places sometimes come to the Teeth, in skin boats built for the crossing, and they stand on the basalt shoulders in the constant fine rain and they look out over the gray water and the white-caps and the distant pale pillars of breath-flesh swaying in the half-light of the deeper channels, and they go quiet.

Then they go home, and they do not come back.

### The Sound

The place is loud, in its way, and the loudness is constant.

The wave-pour is the bass note, never stopping, varying only in volume as the weather builds and decays. The kelp-slap is the percussion, random, irregular, a thousand small impacts per minute along the length of every ridge. The wind-note is the drone, long, sustained, felt in the bones more than heard. The rain-drum is the surface texture, a fine high hiss that overlays everything. The breath-flesh, if you can hear it, is a pulse. Slow. Vast. The heartbeat of something that has not yet decided whether it is alive.

In the hollows, all of this combines. In the hollows, when the wave is just about to arrive, there is a moment of comparative silence — the breath held, the kelp lifting, the wind-note dropping — and then the wall of water hits the far side of the ridge and pours over, and the sound is so large it is not really a sound anymore, it is a pressure, it is a thing you stand inside.

Then it drains. The water rushes back out through the notches, dragging kelp and pebbles with it, and the hollow is briefly quiet again. The breath-flesh is visible in the dimness, pale columns swaying gently. A small pale crab — *there is one crab, here, in this one hollow, and no one has ever found another* — sidles along the rock at the water's edge.

Then the next wave comes.

---

The place is cold and wet and dark and very, very loud, and the only things that live in it are plants and one kind of soft pale branching thing that may or may not be alive, and the people who live here have made themselves a small, careful, lichen-and-kelp life in the teeth of it. The wind sings. The water pours. The breath-flesh breathes. The world has been drowned for a long time, and the drowning is not over, and the people on the ridge know this, and they stay anyway.

This is the Fells.
