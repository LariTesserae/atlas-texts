# The Last Floor of the World

The water is so old it has forgotten it was ever rain.

It lies across everything in a sheet nine fathoms thick at the shallowest and unfathomable where the hills used to be. Not ocean — too still for that. Not lake — too vast. Something closer to a flooded sky lying on its side, slow and absolute. The surface, when you can see it, looks less like water than like the inside of a black pearl that has been pressed flat under its own weight for so long that the pressure has become a kind of memory.

There are no waves. There are no currents worth naming. There is only the slow heave of the body of water itself, a respiration so drawn out that a single breath takes longer than a forest does to grow. Somewhere below the visible plane, long silty rivers move along the bottom in currents older than anything still alive.

---

**The Substrate.**

What was once ground is now floor.

Every square league of it has been paved — not recently, not for any living reason — but so long ago that the original craft is no longer legible. The paving stones (if they were stones) have been worn down by patient abrasion into a continuous surface that is neither rock nor clay nor coral but something that looks the way a word looks after centuries of being said: still recognizable as a word, no longer pronounceable. Here and there the floor buckles upward into low ridges where the original footings of larger structures still resist, and these ridges — a foot high, sometimes two — have become the only topography in the drowned world. They run in lines that suggest streets, walls, courtyards, the bones of rooms. Nothing stands above them. Nothing has stood above them for a very long time.

The floor is dense. It has been built and rebuilt and built over until the layers of construction have themselves become a kind of natural substrate — stratified, sedimentary, but artificial all the way down. If you were to cut into it, you would find, beneath the topmost silt, ceramic tile; beneath the tile, fired brick; beneath the brick, older brick; beneath that, the bones of yet older brick; and so on, a downward archaeology of human (or human-like) intention, each layer more broken than the last, each one the floor of somebody's floor.

---

**The Vegetation.**

In the absence of fauna, the plants have inherited the earth.

They have done so without competition and without much hurry, which is how a forest becomes a thing that looks thought out rather than grown. Mosses — though moss is too small a word — sheets of green velvet so fine they look like dyed air, lapped across every ridge and dip of the broken floor. They grow downward from the surfaces that still catch a little light, and upward from the cracks in the tiling, and inward from the dark. Where the water is shallow enough — at the crests of the old walls, along the spines of the long ridges — there are grasses, but grasses that have spent so long under nine feet of slow black water that they have grown to resemble each other and resemble seaweed and resemble the fronds of ferns that have forgotten how to uncurl. Some of them have flowered, deep down, in colors that exist only because no pollinator ever comes to inspect them: a velvet maroon, a green so dark it is almost the green of the water itself, whites that lean toward blue.

There are no trees. There is nothing tall enough to be called a tree. The drowned surface is too low for trees; the drowned atmosphere is too dark for trees. But there are, at the rare places where some structure still lifts a meter or two above the general level, things that have the posture of trees — slender vertical columns of stem and leaf, eight or ten feet tall, leaning slightly, hung with streamers of water-moss that descend from their branches like green curtains soaked in ink. These are the tallest things in the world.

---

**The Light.**

It comes from where the light used to come from.

The sky is overcast, the way the sky has been overcast for so long that cloud is no longer a weather condition but a permanent ceiling. The light that filters through is the color of old pewter — a low, diffuse, directionless grey that does not throw shadows. There is no horizon. The grey of the sky meets the black of the water at a line that is more a suggestion than a boundary, and beyond that line, the same grey continues upward into clouds you cannot see.

In summer (such as it is) the light strengthens just enough to reveal the underwater fields of vegetation in faint olive and maroon, at depths where a diver — if any diver still came — could swim among them and see his own hands as brown shapes against a brown wall. In winter the light sinks below the threshold of color, and the whole world becomes a single substance, water and floor and sky all running together into one featureless dim.

There are no lamps. There were lamps, somewhere below the silt, but no one has lit one in living memory.

---

**The Cold.**

It is not bitter. It is the cold of a cellar in late autumn, sustained, even, slightly damp on the skin. The water holds it; the overcast holds it; the lack of any strong sun holds it. Breath does not freeze. Fingers do not go numb. But nothing ever warms all the way through, and the cold has, over centuries, settled into the paving itself, so that the floor is colder than the water by a degree, and a wader's feet know the difference.

---

**The Sound.**

Mostly, the world is silent in the way a held room is silent.

There is water: a faint, omnipresent lap and sigh against whatever ridges still break the surface, soft enough that the ear stops hearing it after a few minutes and begins to hear it instead as a kind of pressure, a white noise worn down to grey. There is the small occasional drip from a fragment of structure into the body of water below. There is, very rarely, a deeper note — the slow groan of some piece of the floor settling by another millimeter, a sound that comes up through the feet rather than the ears and is felt in the teeth.

There are no birds. There are no insects. There are no voices.

---

**The Inhabitants.**

None that you would call inhabitants.

There are people. They are few. They live along the highest of the ridges, where the water shallows to a foot or two over the floor, in low shelters built from the broken ceramic of the deeper layers, mortared with silt, roofed with the long stems of the tree-posture plants. They fish with woven traps in the drowned streets. They harvest the mosses and the dark-flowering grasses, pressing oils from them, drying them on racks of old rebar pulled up from the substrate. They have small fires in sunken pits, fed by a peat they cut from the compressed vegetation of the lower layers. They speak a language whose roots are audible in the broken tile inscriptions still legible in the floor, but which has been so reduced by centuries of quiet use that it has become almost a singing, almost a murmuring, almost silence.

They do not build much. They do not travel far. They do not remember, except in the patterns of the streets they still follow on their daily errands, that the floor under them was once a city. To them it is simply the world — flat, drowned, lit by a grey and ancient light, dense with the slow green life of plants that have outlived everything else.

---

**The Weather.**

There is weather, but it is weather of long durations.

It rains sometimes, in soft vertical curtains that move across the surface without wind to drive them. It brightens for a while, then dims. The water level rises by inches over years, falls by inches over decades. The vegetation advances a meter inward over the course of a human lifetime. A new fragment of older structure, exposed by the slow erosion of the pavement around it, breaks the surface every century or two. A new low ridge appears, briefly, before being colonized by the mosses and sunk again into the general level.

Time here moves at the pace of geology. A person is born, lives, dies, and the floor has not changed visibly around them. A lineage of a hundred generations passes, and the only evidence is that one more layer of ceramic has been worn into the general substrate, and one more street has been smoothed into a barely-perceptible line of higher ground.

---

**The Feeling of the Place.**

It is not melancholy, exactly. Melancholy requires a contrast — a memory of what was, against what is. Here there is no memory, or the memory has been so long dissolved into the present that it no longer functions as memory.

It is closer to the feeling of being at the bottom of a very old well, looking up, and realizing that the circle of sky above you is not, in fact, getting any smaller, and you are not, in fact, going to climb out. There is a peace in this. The peace is not comforting; it is the peace of a thing that has finished happening.

The moss grows. The water lies. The grey light holds. Somewhere far off, along a ridge that may once have been a boulevard, a woman is walking home with a basket of pressed green oil and a slow song on her lips, and her great-great-grandmother walked the same ridge with the same kind of basket, and her great-great-grandmother's great-great-grandmother walked it too, and the ridge does not care, and the moss does not care, and the woman, who has never known any other world, does not care either.

It is the last floor of the world, and it is patient, and it is dense, and it is very nearly dark, and it will still be here.
