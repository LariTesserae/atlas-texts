# The Sinkhole Nursery

It is not a place, exactly. It is a place becoming a place. The sinkhole is barely two years old, still rough with the memory of collapse — a thirty-foot crater punched into pale clay where the earth simply *quit* one March night, and the world has been spilling into the wound ever since.

## The Hollow

You arrive at the rim first. Above you, the prairie runs flat and indifferent, bluestem and grama whipping under a sky so white-bright it feels lacquered. You wouldn't know the place was here. There is no fence, no sign. Only a slight depression in the grass, then a sharper one, then a lip of cracked clay the color of raw dough, and then — the drop.

The walls are vertical where they're not overhanging. They are made of layers: a topsoil crust that has peeled back like skin, then a foot of iron-stained clay, then a zone of pale chalky marl that smells like old teeth when the sun warms it. Here and there a root dangles from the lip, a pale naked thread that has not yet decided whether to die. The walls are not smooth. They are crumbling in real time, shedding small avalanches of grit when a bird lands wrong, when wind hits them at the wrong hour, when nothing happens at all.

At the bottom — maybe forty feet down — the world rearranges itself.

## The Spring

Water. Not much water. It seeps, it condenses, it gathers. A spring no wider than your spread arms breaches the eastern wall and trickles across the floor in a shallow braid, vanishing into a crack that runs the length of the room. The floor is damp clay, and where it is dampest there are mosses — not the deep spongy mosses of older worlds but thin, pale, tentative ones, the color of mint left in a dark room. They spread in lacy networks that look, from above, like the ghosts of rivers drawn in green pencil.

Above the spring, a shelf of marl holds a permanent stain: dark, almost purple, where the water has been falling for twenty months and counting. Algae. Diatoms. The first citizens.

And the light. The light is the strangest thing. It enters only from the round opening above, and so it arrives as a single column — a bright, almost solid bar that slants down through the dust motes and lands in a perfect oval on the floor near the spring. That oval moves through the day. By noon it is a coin. By midafternoon it has slid across the clay and is warming the western wall, where the ferns are trying.

## The Ferns

They are few. They are everything.

Sparse, yes — but chosen. A hart's tongue tucked into a crack, its strap-leaves trembling at every air movement. A woodsia, silver-haired, clinging to a ledge with the stubbornness of a small saint. A few feet of *Asplenium* that has somehow seeded itself on a north-facing overhang, its fronds so dark they read as black against the chalk. They are not thriving. They are *committing*. Each one is a slow wager against the next drought, the next collapse, the next slab of clay falling from above.

Between them, the walls are bare. The bare parts are the larger part. The ferns are punctuation, not prose.

## The Inhabitants

The light column is full of things that are not quite insects.

They are insects. They look like insects. Crane flies with wings too iridescent, their flight path never quite Newtonian — they hesitate at angles that don't exist, then resume. A wasp, orange and black, hunting something that moves like a spider but folds its legs wrong. Pale moths the size of your thumbnail, drawn upward through the column toward the hole, then circling back down, unable to find the hole again. Their shadows on the floor do not match their bodies. The shadows have more legs.

A scarlet tiger moth is pinned against the chalk wall, sleeping, its red wings folded like a prayer. Around it, in a thin swarm, hoverflies — except some of them have too many wings, and some have none at all and yet remain aloft through some courtesy you cannot name.

On the damp clay near the spring, a small wolf spider sits perfectly still. Eight eyes. Eight correct legs. It is, as far as you can tell, the only ordinary thing here. It watches you with the patience of a creature that knows it is outnumbered by impossibilities and has decided to outwait them.

There is no mammal. No bird nests here. The hole is a trap for anything vertebrate; the walls are too sheer, the slick clay too honest. Only the things that can fly, or fall, or arrive as eggs on a leaf, come down. And the things that live in cracks.

## The Air

Quiet. Not silent — the spring whispers, a sound like a single page turning — but quiet. The column of light seems to absorb noise. Sound does not echo here the way it should; it arrives at you already muted, as if filtered through felt. Your own breathing becomes the loudest event.

The air smells of wet chalk, of green chlorophyll, of iron, of something older and colder that might be the inside of the earth remembering.

## The Wrongness

You will notice it gradually.

The hole above you is round. It is the only shape that repeats cleanly in this place. The spring runs east to west. The light enters from the south, slanting. The walls, examined closely, do not quite match their own angles. A ledge that should be flat is convex by a degree. The ferns, when you look away and back, have shifted — not grown, not wilted, *shifted* — to positions that, you realize, would have required hours. The small white moths you were watching a moment ago are now larger, and one of them has a beak.

Nothing is dangerous. Nothing chases you. The wrongness is not hostile; it is *partial*. As if this place were being rendered in real time by something that hadn't quite decided what it was, and was sampling possibilities. The wolf spider, you suspect, knows. It has not moved. It does not intend to move. It is the only thing here that is fully formed, and it is keeping still so as not to draw attention to that fact.

## The Becoming

The sinkhole is young. It will widen. It will deepen. The spring will, in a year or ten, carve a channel that becomes a creek; the creek will become a memory of a creek when the next collapse comes. The ferns will be joined by others, then by mosses, then by a thin soil that catches the dust falling from the lip above. A pigeon will blunder in and live, briefly, on the seeds. An owl will learn the hole. A raccoon will find the spring.

And the wrongness — the half-rendered edges, the moths with too many wings, the shadows that lie — you do not know what will become of that. You suspect it will not fade. You suspect the place is not *growing into* something. It is growing *toward* something, and the something it is growing toward has not yet decided to exist, and is using this pocket of cool damp air to think about it.

You climb out at dusk. The column of light has gone amber, then gray. The prairie is loud with crickets. The hole is a shadow in the grass, and from inside it, faintly, you can hear the spring, and the rustle of the strange moths circling toward an exit they will not find.

You mark the place in your mind. You will come back. You do not entirely expect it to be the same place. You are correct.
