Where the col narrows between two black shoulders of rock, the wind makes its own season. It comes up the long slope from the lowlands, gathers itself in the throat of the pass, and pours out into the bowl beyond as if through a spout. The bowl hears it before it sees the source. The cliffs ring with it. The grass flattens in long stripes. Then the wind arrives, and everything that had been upright bows.

The bowl is small. A cathedral could be laid across it; a hamlet lost in it. The eye, traveling along the inner walls, sees them rise — strange how the brain computes altitude. Three sides are steep rock, almost vertical, going up further than is comfortable to look. The fourth is the gap — the wind-gap — through which one has come up, and through which one could go down. The basin floor is some thirty paces across at its widest, narrowing where the two side walls converge toward the gap. It is not really a basin. It is a room. A room made of mountain.

The rock of the walls is dark, and it is always wet. Not drowned — not flowing-river wet — but wet as a wall after rain that has been wet for years. Every inch of it glistens faintly in the pale light. Water finds its way out of the stone along invisible seams and trickles down. Threads of water run across the face from top to bottom in silver lines that pause at ledges and resume. Some of the threads are barely more than a finger's width — they are nonetheless continuous, day and night, year in, year out. The rock is weeping. Has been weeping for longer than any marker can say.

At the foot of the walls, the threads gather. They puddle in the moss at the base of the cliffs, they cross the grass in shallow runnels that have braided themselves and unbraided themselves a thousand times, they pool into three or four small tarns whose surfaces shiver in the wind but barely clear the bordering moss. The water is cold. The water has come down from ice, mostly. What isn't ice-born is cloud-born — condensed out of the mists that drift through the bowl in the early morning and the late afternoon, settling onto the rocks, finding its way inward by capillary and weight.

The coolness of the place is structural. Even on a clear afternoon, when the sun lies flat across the floor of the bowl, the air in the shadow of the walls stays cold enough that a hand laid against the stone comes away chilled. The temperature lives in the rock. In summer the bowl warms only fitfully; in winter it freezes solid, and the threads become icicles, and the wind through the gap brings flurries that bank against the walls in slow drifts.

Vegetation, here, is more than it has any right to be. Given the elevation. Given the wind. Given that frost is possible in any month. The grass grows in fat tussocks that hold their shape against the weather — wiry, sharp-leaved, the kind of grass that has been battered for centuries and is still bent but alive. Between the tussocks, the alpine flowers crowd in — low, mat-forming, persistent. They are not large. They come in colors that the eye notices against the grey rock because they are the only colors here. Yellow, white, a fierce blue, sometimes a violet that looks artificial against everything around it. Moss is everywhere moss can be — coating the stones at the base of the cliffs in a deep green that almost reads black in shadow. Where the moss has grown over a flat stone, it lifts the stone slightly and humps up over it. Lichens put their orange and pale grey crusts on the drier rocks farther out from the wall-foot, where the water doesn't constantly wet them.

Lush, the floor of the bowl — but only the floor. The walls above are bare rock; the col beyond the gap is bare rock; the sky above is bare sky. There is a great deal of *empty* in this place. The rock walls are mostly just rock walls. The tussocks are widely spaced. The grass between them has a name, possibly, but visually it is a thinness — a glimpse of soil — between clumps. The eye reads the bowl as: a small space, mostly taken up by air, framed by rock, floored by patches of green with water threading through and wind crossing over.

Empty, yes — and yet it does not feel empty when one stands in it. There are presences enough.

The ravens are the loudest. They ride the wind through the gap and out over the bowl, banking along the inside walls, their feathers tucked against the gusts. They do not call often in the morning. They call at all hours. The calls echo oddly in the bowl — they come off the walls a half-second after they leave the bird, and seem to be made by something larger than the raven that issued them. There are usually three. Sometimes four. They nest somewhere out of sight, on a high ledge or in a crevice where the cliffs step back from themselves.

There are smaller birds. Snow finches that flick between the stones. A wheatear that runs across the rocks ahead of one's foot. The pipit that rises from the grass and goes up, up, and falls back into it some distance away. And the creatures of the tussocks — the ones that one never sees. The eye sees the burst of a vole from one clump and the disappearance of it into another, half a second later. The shrew is rumored. The weasel is not rumored, only glimpsed, briefly, in the corner of vision, then gone.

In the floor of the bowl, the small wild lives on. There are hares, in winter all white, in summer piebald. They sit very still in the open, between tussocks, and one might walk past them, mistaking them for rocks, until they move. At their speed, when they do move, they are a flash — gone behind the next stone. Goats have been here, occasionally. Their droppings are old. Their tracks, in the snow, are sometimes new, sometimes months old, suggesting they come up and through, and then return down and away. A shepherd, it is said, brought a small flock up through the gap two summers ago — could that be true? — and the ground near the southern tarn was disturbed in long stripes, as if hooves had compressed the moss.

The marks of building are faint. On the southern edge of the bowl, near where the two cliffs begin to converge, there is a low ring of stones, weather-rounded, set close to the ground. Sheepfold, most likely. Or rabbit-pen, if one wanted to be precise. The wall might once have come up to the waist; now it comes to the ankle. The grass has colonized the inside of it. There is a single upright stone at the lip of the gap — taller than the others, a marker, perhaps, or a memorial, or nothing in particular. Two cairns stand on the wide part of the basin floor, slightly off-center from one another. They are constructed with care — flat stones stacked so they do not wobble. Their tops are not pointed but flat. They look more like small tables than summits. No one now would say what they were for.

And there is a structure against the base of the east wall. A single room. Stone walls, hand-built, that have the look of being mortared with mud rather than anything more sophisticated. The door is a low opening, more crawl than door. Inside, the floor is flagged with stone slabs that have been worn smooth — polished, almost — by long walking and longer kneeling. There is a ring of stones at the center for a fire. Inside the ring, ash, very old. The smoke has long since gone, but the soot is still in the ceiling — the low ceiling, of cross-laid branches and turves, with the green showing through the gaps. The room is perhaps eight feet by six. A person could live in it, barely, in winter, by being very careful with the fire and the cold. Someone did, once.

The hands that made the room did not have metal tools. One can see, in the way the stones fit, that they were broken and ground rather than cut. The corners are not square. The door lintel is a single piece of stone, but a crooked one, accepted as found. The fireplace stones are river-rounded, picked up from the streambed below the bowl perhaps, and set where they were wanted. It is a low-effort dwelling, made by someone who knew the cold and the wind and accepted both as conditions of life rather than problems.

The room is empty now, except for the soot and the ash. There is no sound inside it, except the wind through the doorway, and the small dripping from the wall where water has found its way in along a seam.

This is where the place moves beyond ordinary.

There is a hum, in the bowl, when the wind is right. Not a hum one hears so much as a hum one feels — in the teeth, in the chest — when the wind passes certain angles of stone. It comes from the cliffs themselves. Some configuration of the rock face, some set of seams and hollows, makes the wind sing — a low note, sustained, that one might attribute to one's own pulse if one were not paying attention. It varies with the wind. Sometimes it is there; sometimes it is gone. The older shepherds used to say the mountain was talking. The shepherds after them said it was not talking to them.

There is also, on the inside face of the east wall, a faint pattern in the lichen. Up close the clusters are shapeless; at a distance, dozens of paces back, one can almost see a shape — an animal, perhaps, or a letter, or nothing at all. It depends on the light. It depends on the eye.

Or — and this is what some travelers say — the bowl is warmer than it should be. Not by much. Half a degree, perhaps. But in a place like this, half a degree is noticeable: it is the difference between frost forming on the stone and frost not forming. Travelers who come up through the gap and stay the night in the bothy report, on waking, that the room is not as cold as the open air beyond it. They cannot account for it. The walls have no insulation. The fire pit has not been lit in years. There is something else going on, they feel, but they cannot name what.

Whatever it is, it is not large. It is not overwhelming. It is a slight uncanniness. A place that is two degrees off from being fully itself. The wind and the rock and the water and the grass are all present in their expected forms, and yet the forms have, somewhere, a small warp in them. Some travelers love the bowl for this. Some leave quickly and do not come back.

It is loud here. The wind, almost always. The dripping of the water from the walls — a continuous patter, sometimes thickening into heavier runnels. The cry of ravens. The small splashings of hares in the tarns. The crackle, on a still afternoon, of a snow patch avalanching down a slope somewhere out of sight. The hum, when the wind is right. None of these are quiet. Together they layer a place that is, even at the stillest moment, sounding. A place with a voice of its own, which one can interrupt but never fully silence.

And it is always moving. The water moves — always finding new courses across the grass after a rain. The grass moves with the wind. The flowers come up, peak, and go down; the moss swells in wet weeks and shrinks in dry. The ravens come and go. The hares change their coats. The snow falls and melts and falls again. The shadows swing around the bowl as the sun crosses the sky. The clouds boil in over the rim from below, fill the bowl to the throat with white, then boil out again. The stone weathers. The lichens grow another millimeter. The cairns, at their flat tops, hold the small offerings that someone, sometimes, leaves behind — a bird-feather, a polished pebble, a coin now so tarnished one cannot read its face.

This is what the place is. A high place, wet, loud, alive in its own slow way, and slightly strange. A place that one comes to, and stays a few hours, and comes down from. A place that does not need more than it has.
