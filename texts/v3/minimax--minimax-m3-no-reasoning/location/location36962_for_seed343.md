# The High Span

---

The altitude here is a kind of language. It says *you are small, and this is what small feels like.*

A ridge of metamorphic rock runs roughly north-south, its spine rounded by ages so deep the stone has forgotten it was ever sharp. What remains is not so much a peak as the memory of a peak — a long, slow swell of the earth's surface that climbs for a mile and a half, then continues climbing in a way that is more philosophical than physical. The air is thin enough to taste. It tastes of iron and dried sage and something faintly sweet that the wind carries up from below.

Below the ridge, the land begins its long unraveling.

---

### The Stone

The ground is gneiss, mostly, in bands of pale gray and rusted ochre — the slow-frozen fingerprint of heat that has not visited this place in a billion years. The rock has spent so long being weathered that it has developed a kind of dignity, a settled-into-itself quality. It does not crumble. It *reposes.* Shelves of it, like the lips of old gods, hold the thin soil in long shallow bowls that run for hundreds of feet along the flanks of the ridge. The soil itself is the color of weak coffee and the depth of a sigh — never more than a hand's breadth, sometimes just a stain, sometimes gone entirely to leave the clean bone of the rock showing through.

The weathering pattern is what you notice second. Every horizontal surface has been licked into a shallow concavity by time. Standing water has not rested here in centuries, but the rock remembers how to hold it. The concavities are an inch or two deep, perfectly smooth, and they extend in long overlapping ovals that the eye stitches into something almost like writing.

---

### The Plants

And then there is the green, which is not so much a presence as a *condition* of the place.

The vegetation has not colonized this height so much as it has become the substrate. There is no point at which the rock ends and the plant begins. The two have been here together for so long that they have made their peace. Moss — not the polite moss of shaded stream-banks, but a deep, hoary, fist-thick species that grows in cushions the size of bear pelts — covers perhaps a third of every visible surface. The rest of the stone wears a coat of lichen so old it has begun to look like the rock itself, blotched in greenish-gray and the orange of a bruise.

Where the soil deepens — in the long shallow bowls, in any crack that has caught a windblown seed and been patient — the true plants live. They are small, mostly. Stunted juniper, gnarled beyond any nursery's imagination, twisted into shapes that suggest a long argument with the prevailing wind and a settlement eventually reached. Grasses in pale tussocks, silvered at their tips. A woody undershrub with leaves the color of a thunderhead, which the wind has trained to grow flat against the rock as if it were hiding.

But there is *quantity* of it. That is the thing. There is so much plant for so little soil. The whole slope is furred, every square yard of it, so that from a distance the ridge looks as though it is wearing a heavy green pelt over its bones. A carpet, dense and low, that runs in continuous folds from the spine of the ridge down the long shoulders of the slope and out to where, eventually, the land begins to fall away.

The plant mass is not tall, but it is *total.* It has weight. It is the kind of place where you cannot see the rock unless you kneel and part it with your hands, and when you do, the exposed earth beneath is dark, and damp, and busy.

---

### The Moisture

Water here is a rumor, mostly. It comes in three forms, none of them reliable.

The first is the rain, which falls mostly in summer, in great flat curtains that come over the ridge from the west and lay themselves down on the land for an hour or a day and then are gone. When it rains, the shallow bowls in the rock fill, briefly, to the brim — and then they drain, and the moss drinks, and the air smells of ozone and petrichor for an afternoon, and then the place begins again its long dry waiting.

The second is the dew, which is more faithful. Every morning, the whole green pelt of the slope wakes wearing a film of condensation — the rocks, which radiate heat into the night sky faster than the air, pull moisture out of the warm air that the sun has just left. By midmorning the pelt is dry. By afternoon, in the right light, you can see the moisture rising back off it as a thin pale haze.

The third is the snow, which only comes in the deep winter and then sparingly — a foot at a time, melted off within a week, leaving the dark rock and the brighter green to re-emerge as if from a brief cold bath.

There is no stream on the ridge itself. There is no spring. The water that falls here leaves, eventually, by way of the long slow seams in the rock — finding its way down, down, into the deep groundwater that feeds the rivers that drain away in the valleys a thousand feet below. You can sometimes, on a quiet morning, hear it — a faint distant trickling from somewhere far downslope, as if the mountain were whispering to itself.

---

### The Sound

The quiet, here, is not quiet.

It is the wind, first. The wind never stops. It comes over the ridge from the west, mostly, and where it crosses the spine of the place it makes a long low moan — not whistling, not quite, but a sustained tone, like a single note held on a distant horn. Where the wind finds a crack in the rock, it sings higher, briefly, in thin wild keening that drops away. The junipers, where they are large enough, do their part — a deep rhythmic thrumming, a kind of woody drumming, as their stiff branches flex and relax in the constant flow. The whole ridge, on a windy day, becomes a kind of instrument.

And then, beneath the wind — the insects. They are astonishing. A low hum, more felt than heard, that comes off the green pelt of the slope like heat off a road. Grasshoppers in their millions, katydids, cicadas in the warmer hours, and below them all a deeper, ticking, buzzing, sawing, scraping chorus of small beings going about their small business. The sound is so constant, so pervasive, that after the first few minutes you stop hearing it as a sound and start hearing it as a property of the air. You breathe it in.

Occasionally, from a long way off — over the ridge, perhaps, in a different drainage — a hawk cries. Or something cries like a hawk. The sound is so thin by the time it arrives that it is almost abstract.

The result is a place that is *full* of sound, sound of a kind that does not intrude so much as underlie. The silence here is not silence. It is a *wall* of low noise that you can lean against.

---

### The Life

And then there is the fauna, which is the thing that makes the place astonishing.

You do not see it, at first. The eye is drawn to the rocks, the plants, the scale of the land. But if you sit, and are still, and watch a single square yard of the green pelt for ten minutes, the square yard will move. It will move in many directions at once, in small ways, in a slow eruption of small life.

Grasshoppers, of course — in a dozen colors, brown and green and yellow and a pinkish hue that looks almost artificial. They jump and land and jump again, in arcs, and their soft-bodied flight is so abundant that the air at shin-height is a constant low drift of motion. Beetles, dark and slow, working their patient ways along the juniper bark. Spiders that have built no webs but instead run, fast, across the bare patches of rock. A small lizard — gray, quick — that suns itself on a shelf of stone and is gone before you can focus on it.

But the *numbers*. The place is thick with small life, thick beyond any temperate landscape you have stood in before. It is because of the plant, of course — the dense low cover, the constant moisture, the absence of any true winter to reduce the populations. The green pelt is a city, viewed close, and every square foot of it is a neighborhood in which some small drama is unfolding.

Larger things, too. The hawks, circling. A fox, once, crossing the ridge at dawn, loping. Moths at dusk, suddenly, in pale clouds, drawn to nothing visible. The bones, sometimes, of something larger — a deer, an antelope, the antlers of one still attached to the skull, picked clean and bleached and slowly being buried by the moss.

The ancientness of the place has not produced a stillness. It has produced an *abundance.* The land has been alive so long that the life has become confident, dense, total. It is not a place where the small things hide. It is a place where the small things have inherited.

---

### The Marks of Hands

There are structures, though few.

A line of stones, low, that runs along the spine of the ridge for perhaps a quarter of a mile — stacked, originally, but now settled into a low undulating ribbon that the moss is patiently reclaiming. The stones are not dressed. They were not brought from anywhere. They were the loosest of the local rocks, gathered and set, long ago, by hands that understood the wind and wanted a windbreak, or a marker, or a fence. The technology here is the technology of the hand and the eye and the patient rearrangement of what is already present.

Below the ridge, on a flatter shelf of the slope, a low circular wall of unmortared stone. Perhaps a habitation, once. Perhaps a pen. The interior is now grown over with juniper, and the wall itself has begun to slump inward, as if the structure were slowly sitting down. A few flat stones near it, arranged in a pattern that might be hearth, might be floor, might be coincidence.

Further downslope, a waystone — a single upright slab, taller than a person, with a vague shape carved into its upper face. The shape is worn nearly to nothing. It might have been a human figure, once. It might have been an animal. It is now just a slight concavity in the rock, that catches the dew in the mornings, and is gone by afternoon.

No one lives here now. The marks are old. The hand that set them has been gone long enough for the moss to have begun to consider the stones as part of itself.

---

### The Air, the Light, the Feel of It

The light is the light of high places — a little harsher, a little thinner, the shadows a little blacker than the same sun would produce in a valley. The air, of course, is dry in the afternoons and damp in the mornings, and the two states pass into one another with a regularity that becomes, after a few days, almost geological. The sun rises from the east and lays the long shadow of the ridge out across the slope to the west; the shadow retreats through the morning; at noon the light is direct and the place is all illumination; the shadow of the ridge begins to fall again, in the other direction, in the afternoon; and by evening the long blue shades are coming up from the valleys below and the ridge is the last lit thing in the landscape, glowing.

The view from the spine of the ridge is very wide. To the east, the land falls away in long folds and benches, in a complex geography of ridges and drainages, fading to a haze that hides whatever is far. To the west, more of the same, but with a different character — a slightly redder rock, a slightly paler plant, and a different arrangement of the long thin lines of distant water glinting in the sun.

You can see weather, from here. Rain, falling in sheets, in some distant valley. A single cloud, casting its dark oval shadow on the land far below. A lightning flash, soundless at this distance, somewhere out beyond the visible ridges.

It is a place to stand, and look, and feel the wind, and the altitude, and the patient unending small life underfoot, and the distant marks of a long-vanished kind of human presence, and the stone that is older than the marks, and the wind that is older than the stone.

---

It is a quiet place that is not quiet. A green place that is mostly brown. A high place that feels less like altitude than like *thinning* — as if the world were becoming more itself, here, more committed, more honest, more nearly reduced to its essentials.

The ridge goes on. The moss grows. The wind blows. The insects sing. The hawk, somewhere, calls.

The place continues.
