There is a continent underfoot.

I want to call the place a cavern, but a cavern suggests a roof you could find with a thrown stone, and this roof is too far overhead for that. The ceiling is high. The ceiling is far enough up that air begins to organize itself into something with weight and direction, and the columns that rise from the floor to meet it are not slender — they are the width of houses in places, and they stand in rows that go on for distances you could not cross in a day. Some of the columns do not reach the ceiling. Some of them are still growing. They were not here long ago, on the geological scale, and they are sharp.

Sharp features. Fresh. The erosion here is the kind you get on a young coast or a fresh fall of lava — the kind that has not yet been touched. The columns ring when struck, a clean brittle note. The floor, where it has cracked open along its seams, shows fractures that have not been weathered, only made and left. The crusts on the floor — and I will come to the crusts — have not been disturbed in any long-term way. They grow. They are eaten. They regrow. They are the same. The place is new and it is finished at once.

The warmth. There is no visible source for the warmth. No lava, no fire. The warmth is in the rock itself, residual planetary heat, perhaps, or something stranger — a thermal inertia so deep that the stone has held its temperature since before the hollow was hollowed. The builders who live here do not think about the warmth. They know it the way fish know water: as a condition. It is warm. They are warm. To be warm is to be alive.

No water. This is the most important thing, and it is the thing you have to stand inside the place to understand. There is no water here. The builders have never seen water except as vapor condensed on a cold stone, if there were a cold stone, or as a trace locked inside the minerals they eat. They do not drink. They are not thirsty. Their bodies are made of things that do not require water to work. They have evolved — or they have been made — in a chemistry that is not the chemistry of wet places. They excrete a fine powder. When they die, if they are not eaten, they dry. They become, in time, indistinguishable from the dust around them.

And so: the crusts.

The simplest living things here are not fauna, by some reckonings. They are the crusts. Thin, spreading, attached to the warm rock, consuming what the rock offers through fissures. They live on sulfur, perhaps, or on iron compounds, or on trace gases that seep from the deepest crevices where the stone is hottest. They do not move. They grow at the speed of mineral deposition. They are the color of the rock or a paler color, sometimes a darker color, and in some places they emit a faint phosphorescence — a sourceless-looking glow that you would not call light if you had any comparison, but you do not have any comparison, because you have never been here. The light is enough. The light is what there is.

The grazers rasp the crusts. They are small. They are the size of a thumbnail, or a finger. They have many legs and a flat profile, pressed low. They rasp with a rough patch on the underside of their bodies and absorb what they rasp and leave behind a faintly disturbed surface that regrows within a day. They are everywhere. They are so abundant that when you stand on the floor and wait, you can see the floor subtly moving — a thousand small movements, a thousand small raspings, the constant industry of very small things. They fold when disturbed. They fold inward, the way a pill bug folds, and become indistinguishable from pebbles. If you were a predator, you would have trouble finding them. They are quiet. They do not make sound.

Above the grazers, the medium predators. A meter long, give or take. They have hard parts and soft parts and they move with deliberation, slowly, slowly. Some have too many eyes; some have too few; some have eyes in places that do not seem right. They hunt by vibration. They feel, through the crust and through the bases of the columns, the small percussive footfalls of grazers, and they move toward the footfalls slowly and they eat. Some of them have a faint glow at the mouth, used for what might be luring, or warning, or simply the side effect of what they are. They are patient. They can wait for a grazer to come near, motionless for hours, before they strike. They are quiet.

The columns themselves are not all the same. Most are mineral — silicates, borates, rarer things — and most are growing, slowly, from the floor upward. But some are not. Some are denser than the others, and they do not grow, and they emit a steadier glow, a dull amber that pools at their bases like spilled light. The builders treat these with a different kind of attention. They do not carve their marks on them. They sit near them, sometimes alone, sometimes in pairs, and wait. They leave offerings at the bases: a tool, a fragment of bone, a piece of themselves. The columns do not respond. The columns are not gods. The builders do not know what they are, and they have decided, perhaps, that not knowing is acceptable.

The builders.

These are the ones with tools. These are the ones with marks. They are the size of the medium predators or a little smaller — let us say the height of a child, if a child could stand on these legs and look with these eyes. They have two heavy legs and arms for carrying, and a face you would not call a face until you had lived among them for a while. They make marks on the columns and on the floor with sharp stones. The marks are not a script, not exactly — they are diagrammatic, schematic, the kind of marks you would make if you were keeping track of where you had been and what you had seen and what you intended to come back to. They pile fragments of broken column into low rings, the way you might pile stones to mark a camp. Inside the rings they sleep, or eat, or watch, or perform some action that we would have to invent a word for. The rings are scattered. They are not a settlement. They are not a city. They are the faintest of human presences — if "human" is even the right word for them — across a vast dark floor.

They have tools: a sharpened silicate flake lashed to a short handle with sinew or fiber spun from some part of a grazer; a heavy bone from something that died; a flat dark plate of mineral they carry for the warmth of it, or the ritual of it, or because it is good for something we do not know. The tools are not sophisticated. The tools are the kind of tool you would invent if you lived here and needed to invent a tool, and they have invented what they need and stopped there, because stopping has not yet hurt them.

They make very little sound. A scrape of stone against column. A breath. Sometimes a low vibration, almost felt through the floor more than heard, that might be language or might be the rock settling. They are quiet even when they are not being quiet on purpose. The world is quiet and they have become quiet with it.

The weather.

Because the place is vast, the air moves in slow patterns. Warm air rises in the deepest part of the hollow and cool air descends along the outer walls, and this circulation takes days to turn. A breath exhaled here arrives somewhere else, eventually, as weather. The builders have learned the weather. They know when the air is going to shift because their skin, or some part of them, registers the change, and they return to their rings. The predators too, perhaps — they move with the air, following the grazers who follow the crusts who follow the warmth. The whole cavern breathes, slowly. If you waited long enough — and the builders are patient enough — you would feel the breath of it on your face, and you would know the world is alive, in its way.

The light.

The light is dim. The phosphorescence of the crusts, the mouth-glow of the predators, the steady amber at the bases of the dense columns, the faint red glow from the deepest crevices, and the small fires the builders make — carefully, from a substance that burns slowly without smoke and without water, perhaps the dried residue of the grazers, perhaps a mineral that yields a yellow flame — these are the lights. There is no day and no night. There is just the same dim glow, more in some places than in others, less in others. The builders navigate by feel as much as by sight. They know the columns. They know the floor. They could find their rings in the dark, because they have memorized the shape of the dark.

The dust.

There is a fine dust in the air. It lifts from the crusts when the grazers rasp; it lifts from the floor when the predators move; it is suspended in the slow weather and carried from place to place. The dust is sharp. It finds the cracks in skin and the cracks in stone. The builders cover themselves with the dust, in patterns. They paint themselves with it, carefully, on the shoulders and the face and the arms. The patterns mean things — which ring, which kinship, which pilgrimage, which offering left at which column. The dust is also mildly narcotic, in a low and constant way. The builders crave it. They make journeys, sometimes alone, to the parts of the cavern where the dust is thickest, and they sit in the dust and breathe it in and are quiet for a long time afterward.

The stasis.

And the stasis. This is the strangest thing about the place, perhaps, after you have grown accustomed to its other strangenesses. Nothing here changes. The crusts regrow at the same rate they have regrown for as long as the builders can remember. The grazers rasp at the same rate. The predators hunt at the same rate. The columns grow at the same rate. The air moves at the same rate. The temperature is the same. The light is the same. There is no season. There is no erosion. There is no wearing down. The builders build their rings, and the rings stay where they are built. The builders die, and the dust they become becomes part of the floor. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is lost. The world is in equilibrium, has been in equilibrium, will be in equilibrium.

The builders, who are not in equilibrium, do not understand this. They think they have always lived here. They think the place was always like this. They think it will always be like this. They are wrong, perhaps, but they cannot be wrong in a way that matters to them. The change, if it is happening, is happening too slowly to see. They have no memory of a different world because no different world has ever been theirs, and no future different world will be theirs either. They are the only moving things in a place that has finished moving.

The quiet.

And so the quiet. Even with all the fauna, even with all the activity, the place is quiet. The grazers rasp without sound. The predators strike without sound. The builders scrape their marks without sound. The air moves without sound. The only sounds are the occasional faint crack of a growing column — a small, brittle report, far away — and the occasional sound of the builders' low voices, which are so quiet that you would have to be very close to hear them, and which often are not even sound but a kind of vibration felt through the floor. The cavern is a place where the loudest thing is the silence, and the silence is not silence but a held breath, the held breath of a place that has finished forming and is waiting, without knowing what it is waiting for, for something that may never come.

You could stand here for a long time. You would hear nothing. You would see the columns rising. You would see the floor faintly moving. You would see, in the distance, the amber glow at the base of a dense column where a builder sat, hours ago, and is no longer sitting, having left an offering and gone back to the rings. You would see the dust drifting. You would feel the warmth on your skin. You would be in the only world there is, and the only world there is, would seem to you, after a while, to be enough.
