# The Garden of Last Breath

I begin in the wrong place, and I will tell you that.

I begin in the center, where the stem is widest, and I will tell you what the center *is* before I describe the place — because in this place the center is the geography, and the geography is alive, and everything else follows from there.

---

There is a tree.

This word is inadequate. I will use it anyway because the alternatives are worse. It rises — or sinks, or has simply always been *here* — from a depth so old that depth has worn smooth, like a river stone the size of a continent. The trunk is broad beyond any scale that a living forest ever produced. A hundred miles across, perhaps. A thousand. The numbers don't translate because there is no horizon to measure against, only the slow inward curving of the world as it climbs.

The trunk is black. Not the black of burnt wood, not the black of shadow, but the black of *very deep water*, of places where sunlight has not arrived since before there were things to arrive in. It is cool to the touch — this I know without having touched it. Cool, and faintly slick, and ridged in long vertical furrows like the inside of a throat.

It is a throat. I will say that too, even though it sounds wrong. The trunk breathes.

---

The water rises to within — what? Forty feet? A hundred? It doesn't matter, the measurement is wrong. What matters is that it is *high*. The water is the ceiling and the floor and the weather. It is dark water, cold water, water that has been cold for so long it has forgotten warmth was ever a possibility. It hangs above the trunk in a flat black mirror that does not ripple. Beneath the trunk, it is the same water, deeper, older, colder. The whole world is suspended in it like a leaf in amber.

Light is a rumor. It comes from — not the surface. There is no surface. There is no sun. There is a faint diffuse glow somewhere far above, the ghost of a ghost of photons that have been filtering down through miles of black water for so long they have forgotten their color. If you look up for a long time, you can almost see green. If you squint. If you are willing to be kind to yourself about what green means.

Most of the time it is just dark.

---

The tree grows *up* through the water, and it grows *out* through itself. This is the strange part. The trunk is not a trunk in the sense of a single column; it is a trunk in the sense of *the thing that holds everything else up*. From it, in every direction, the branches extend — but they do not extend into air. They extend into water. They extend into each other. They form a lattice, a scaffold, a cathedral of black wood and living green, and inside that cathedral there are rooms, and chambers, and vast hollows where whole ecologies have nested for so long that they have forgotten they were ever anything but native.

The branches are draped in moss. The moss is a hundred feet long. Some of it is older than language. It moves in currents you cannot see, swaying with the slow patient rhythm of something that has been swaying for centuries, and the swaying is the only timekeeping this place has. When the moss swings left, you know the current has shifted left. When it swings right, the world has decided to be different for a moment, and then to be the same again.

---

The vegetation is total. This is the rule of the place, the law, the thing you cannot argue with. *Nothing is not plant.* The floor — that is to say, the accumulated silt of a million years of fallen moss and shed bark and dead insects — has grown over. Has grown *up*. Is now itself a kind of moss, a kind of turf, a kind of forest floor made of forest floor made of forest floor, each generation composting into the substrate for the next. You walk on what was once a tree. You walk on what was once a beetle. You walk on what was once a moss. You cannot tell where one ends and another begins because they have all agreed to be the same thing, eventually.

Ferns. There are ferns the size of houses. They unfurl in spirals so slow that you could watch one for a year and not be certain it had moved. The fiddleheads are the size of small rooms. When one finally opens, the sound is a soft wet sigh, like a sail in a calm wind, like a lung filling for the first time in a long time.

There are flowers. I should tell you about the flowers. They are not bright. They are pale — bone-white, faintly luminous in the dark water, the color of deep-sea creatures that make their own light because no one else will. They glow in pulses. Very faint pulses. In synchrony, mostly, though some lag and some lead and the whole field of them breathes together like a single vast organism trying to remember what breathing is for.

---

The animals are here because the animals have always been here.

They are small, mostly. Sparse. The density of the place is low — there is so much *volume* and so few *beings*. But what there is, is committed.

There are things like insects, but larger, and darker, and wetter. They crawl on the bark in long patient columns, and they eat the moss, and the moss grows back. They are the color of the wood. If you sit still for an hour they will walk across you and not notice.

There are things like fish, but they do not swim so much as *drift*. They hang in the water column with their fins spread and their mouths open, and they eat what drifts past, which is mostly spores, mostly pollen, mostly the tiny luminous eggs of something or other. They have no eyes. Or they have eyes but the eyes are not for seeing. The eyes are for glowing. They are pale blue points in the dark, drifting, drifting.

There is something larger. I have only heard it. It makes a low sound — a kind of groan, a kind of hum — that comes from deep in the trunk, from the heartwood, from wherever the oldest wood is. It moves once every few years. When it moves, the whole tree shifts, and the water shivers, and the moss swings, and the flowers flicker out of sync for a moment before finding their rhythm again. Then it is quiet.

I don't know what it is. I don't think anyone knows. I don't think *it* knows. It is the size of the question rather than the size of an answer.

---

The sound of this place is the sound of a body. Not a body in the sense of a corpse — a body in the sense of a thing that is alive, that is moving things around inside itself, that is making its own weather. There is the constant low creak of wood under its own weight. There is the soft hiss of water against bark. There is the sigh of the ferns. There is the occasional high clear note of something — a bird? A fish? A flower? — that sings once and then is silent. The sound is not loud in volume. It is loud in *presistence*. It does not stop. It has not stopped in a very long time.

There are no machines. There is no metal. There is nothing here that was not grown or born. The only technology is the technology of patience, the technology of waiting, the technology of being so old that you have become the same thing as the place you are in.

---

It is dark. It is wet. It is ancient. It is cool — the cool of stone cellars, the cool of lake water in October, the cool of the inside of your cheek. It is built — *total*, in the way that a coral reef is built, in the way that a termite mound is built, in the way that a body is built. Every surface you touch is the surface of something that was made by something alive, that is alive, that will be alive for a long time after you have left.

It is stable. This is the strangest part. Nothing here is in a hurry. The water does not move. The moss sways but does not progress. The flowers pulse but do not change. The fish drift but do not travel. The whole world is in equilibrium, has been in equilibrium, will be in equilibrium for as long as equilibrium is possible. It is the end of a process. It is what a place becomes when it has finished becoming.

There is a kind of peace in this. I want to be careful about that word. It is not the peace of rest. It is the peace of *completion*. The peace of a thing that has done what it was going to do and has settled into the form of its own having done it.

---

You sit on the bark. The bark is cool. The bark is black. The bark is ridged and faintly damp and smells like tea, or like rain, or like the inside of a chest that has been closed for a long time. A pale fish drifts past at eye level, its fin moving slowly enough that you can count the beats. A fern, somewhere far above, sighs as it unfurls. The tree groans in its deep heartwood.

Above you, the water hangs. Below you, the water hangs. The flowers pulse. The moss swings. The dark is patient.

You are very small here. You have always been small here. You will always be small here.

It is okay.

It has been okay for a very long time.
