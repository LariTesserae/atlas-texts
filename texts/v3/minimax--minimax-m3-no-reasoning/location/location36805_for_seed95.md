The plateau is what is left of a mountain. The mountain is gone, finished with itself in some age so distant that the wearing-down has itself worn down, and what remains is a long, settled highland. The stone underfoot is warm, the color of dried blood or of dust that has gathered on the inside of a long-shut window. The slopes are gentle, almost imperceptible. Walking across the plateau is mostly a matter of degree: a long, slow rise, a long, slow fall. The ridges in the middle distance are not ridges, really, just the suggestion that the stone has not entirely surrendered its original gesture toward a peak. They are wrinkles. They are the seams where the rock remembers being lifted.

This is a place where erosion has finished its work and stayed to rest. The shape you see is residue — a final, settled condition, like the memory of a mountain pressed into the floor of a high valley. The stone has a rounded, almost soft appearance, as if the wind had fingers and was patient. You cannot tell where the rivers used to run. They do not run anymore. They gather.

Water. The place is damp, but it does not rain often. What you find instead is condensation. The warm air, carrying moisture from somewhere downslope, settles on the cool surfaces of the stone each morning and again at dusk. The pools are small and dark, and they collect in the concavities: in the laps of boulders, in the beds of what might once have been channels, in the prints of the soft-footed animals. Some of the pools are very old. The water in them is the color of weak tea, and it does not move. Algae rims them in green and rust.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Tussocks of a stiff, blue-green grass emerge from cracks in the rock. There are small cushion-plants, and mats of lichen in colors that would be loud if there were more of them. A flowering thing, stemless, pushes a yellow head up between two stones. The plants do not crowd. They have learned the timing. The ground between them is bare, and shows the stone, and the stone is alive in its own way, with crusts and films and the slow spread of lichen across its surface.

What is dense here is not plant life. It is animal life. The plateau is overrun, and yet it is quiet. This is the first strangeness.

The fauna is everywhere. If you sit still, the ground begins to move. Beetles traverse the stone in single file, their shells a metallic bronze, vanishing into cracks. A small lizard, the length of a finger, holds itself on a warm slab and watches with one eye. Something with many legs — a centipede, but larger than a centipede ought to be, the color of dried blood — flows across the path and is gone. Above, the air is full of slow circles. Birds of a kind not easy to name, with long wings and no call, ride the thermals. You can count a dozen in the air at any moment, sometimes more, and the ground below them is animated by the same invisible industry: burrowings, dartings, the soft collapse of soil where something has gone under. A herd of something — antelope-shaped, but smaller, with thick coats the color of the stone — moves at the edge of vision, perhaps a hundred individuals, scattered in a loose constellation across a slope. They do not look up. They graze on the tussocks with the unhurried attention of things that have been eating here for a very long time.

The sound this abundance makes is almost nothing. A hum, which is not quite a hum — a pressure on the ear, a low drone, the sum of ten thousand small respirations and the friction of small legs. The bird, when it does speak, makes a single note, repeated, that is almost more felt than heard. The wind moves through the tussocks with a hiss. That is mostly it. The place is acoustically thin. Sound does not carry. The soft-footed animals stay soft-footed.

The strangeness deepens.

The stone is not entirely untouched. There are patches that look worked — smoothed further than the wind could have smoothed them, or smoothed in a direction the wind would not have chosen. A long curving groove in a flat face, as if something heavy had been dragged across it, or something with claws had walked. A ring of stones, low, just the height of a knee, set in a place where no ring seems called for. The circle is not perfectly round. It is not deliberately not round, either. It just is. Inside the ring, the stone is darker, as if the air had been pressed there for a long time.

Farther on, a single standing stone, twice the height of a person, set upright with no obvious reason for it to stay up. It has stayed up anyway, through what must be many seasons of frost and weight. Its surface is the same warm grey as the rest, but it casts a shadow that seems to land in a particular way — it points somewhere, or away from somewhere, or the stones near it are arranged to suggest a direction. You cannot quite read it. You can read that something took the trouble, and the trouble has been absorbed into the plateau, and the plateau has not been disturbed by the trouble.

The ruins are old. They are not in any hurry. They sit among the tussocks and the lichen as if they had grown there, and in a way they have, in the sense that whatever made them has been gone long enough that the making and the unmaking have become the same process.

The light, when the sun is high, is a clean and even light, the kind that erases its own source. Shadows are short. The stone looks bleached. The pools look like dark eyes, watching. In the early morning and the late afternoon, the light is longer, and the lichen glows, and the standing stone has a warm side and a cool side, and the herd becomes a row of small moving shadows against the side of the plateau.

There is no wind today. The tussocks are still. The bird hangs in the air, wings outstretched, not moving forward. The damp does not fall, but it sits, and the air smells of it — a mineral smell, the smell of the inside of a cave, the smell of stone that has been cool while the air has been warm.

The plateau waits. It has been waiting for a long time. It is good at waiting. The animals move through it and across it. The lichens move across the stone. The water sits. The birds circle. The ring of stones sits in its circle. The standing stone stands. Nothing is in a hurry. The slow dynamics of the place operate on timescales that do not include the visitor. The pools evaporate across days. The lichen spreads across years. The herd migrates, perhaps, on a cycle longer than a lifetime, but you would not know to look.

A foot pressed into the dust leaves a mark. The dust is in the rock. The rock is in the wind. The wind is in the damp. The damp is in the air. The mark is in the plateau. The plateau does not say so. The plateau does not need to. It has been making places of things for a very long time.
