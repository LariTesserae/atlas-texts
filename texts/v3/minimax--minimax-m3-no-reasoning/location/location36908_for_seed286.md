# The Still Engine

Something is wrong with the light here. Not wrong — *settled*. As if the light itself was poured once, a long time ago, and has never been disturbed since. It hangs at a low slant, warm and amber-colored, falling through a canopy that is itself the world.

There is no ground.

There was once, perhaps. Or there will be. But in the present — which is the only tense this place seems to acknowledge — there is only the interior of a forest that has forgotten it was ever anything else. The floor where soil should be is a mesh of roots so densely interwoven that they have become a substance in their own right, a kind of living felt, dark and springy underfoot. Walking across it produces no sound. The roots absorb every step. They are older than the trees above them, older than the water that moves through them, older than the air.

The trees are not trees. They are columns. Each one rises two hundred meters, three hundred, some pass through the canopy and continue, and you cannot tell where they stop because their bark, at that altitude, has fused with the bark of their neighbors, and theirs with theirs, until the whole upper structure is a single connected lattice — one organism, or many, it doesn't matter, the distinction has been retired. Rain does not fall *on* this canopy. Rain falls *through* it, in sheets and columns, gathered and routed downward along the trunks in grooves worn by centuries of the same path, until it reaches the root-mesh below, where it spreads laterally and is taken back up. The water cycle here is a closed circuit. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters. The forest is its own weather.

The rain is loud. It is the only loud thing. It roars against the canopy in a continuous percussion that, after a few minutes of listening, organizes itself into tones — a deep chord, almost musical, that shifts as gusts move through the upper stories and set different branches singing. You learn to hear individual notes within it. A creak from somewhere high and left. A hiss where a torrent splits around a knot in the wood. The wet slap of accumulation giving way. Beneath the rain, the forest is otherwise extremely quiet. Footfalls vanish. Voices vanish. There is an acoustic dampening at work here that is not silence but the next thing over.

The temperature is the temperature of a cellar in late autumn. It does not change between day and night, between seasons, between your arrival and your departure if you ever departed. Sixty-two degrees, give or take the fraction of a degree that represents your own heat leaking out. You are warm here. You are the only warm thing.

What you are standing in, what you have been walking through for hours, is not wilderness. It is the inside of a machine.

You don't see the machine. The machine has been running so long that it has become indistinguishable from biology. The great vertical trunks are not trees — they are pistons, or columns of a still, or the legs of something that was once walked here and has rooted itself and forgotten how to move. Their surfaces are ridged in regular spirals that could be bark or could be threading. If you press your ear to one, you can hear, very far down, a kind of rhythmic ticking, vast and slow, the heartbeat of a process that is producing something. The whole forest is a refinery. The water is the feedstock. The light that slants through is incidental — a byproduct, perhaps, of whatever reaction is being maintained, leaking out between the trunks as a kind of exhaust.

But no one tends it. There are no figures here. No animals in the conventional sense. What moves in the canopy is smaller — quick metallic things, or quick chitinous things, or quick things made of light, you cannot decide because they are fast and the light is bad — and they move in patterns that look choreographed. Migration paths. They carry fragments of leaf-matter, or pollen, or something that resembles both, between the upper branches, and the upper branches hum when they pass. The hum is the only sound beneath the rain that has any regularity.

Lower down, almost never seen, are slower things. Soft shapes that root themselves briefly to a trunk, drink from a groove, and unroot and drift on. They glow faintly. They are the size of your hand. If you stand very still they will settle on you. They are warm. They do not stay.

The air is impossibly humid. Every breath is a small drowning. The mist between the trunks is so thick that visibility is measured in trunks, not in meters — three trunks ahead, four, and then the columnar repetition dissolves into a brown-gold haze in which the next trunk is a guess. The mist does not move. The mist is part of the equilibrium. It has been at this density since before anything now living was alive.

There is a smell. It is the smell of warm metal and wet bark and something faintly sweet, like cut sugarcane left to oxidize. It is everywhere, equally. There is no upwind.

You walk for a long time. You do not arrive. The forest is not a forest in the spatial sense. It is a state. A condition that has been entered and not exited. The trunks are spaced too regularly to be natural and too irregularly to be planned. The mathematics of them is the mathematics of something grown, not made, but grown according to a logic that has nothing to do with light and water and everything to do with throughput.

Somewhere, in the upper canopy, there is a sound that is not the rain. A deeper sound. A low mechanical exhalation, every forty seconds, that you feel in the roots beneath your feet before you hear it in the air. The forest breathes. The forest has been breathing for a very long time. It is breathing now. It will be breathing when you are not.

The light does not change. The rain does not stop. The trees do not grow and do not die. The pistons rise and fall at a rate so slow that a human watching will not see it move but will, if they watch long enough, see that the position of a knot on a trunk is no longer where it was. Somewhere, very far above, the product of this place is being accumulated in a reservoir, or a chamber, or a mouth, and it is being used for something. The forest does not know what. The forest has forgotten, or never knew, or has no capacity for knowing, and continues anyway, because continuing is what it is for.

You are a tourist here. You are an interruption. The forest will outlast you absolutely, and when you are gone the only evidence that you were ever present will be the warmth you left in the root-felt, which will be reabsorbed within the hour, and the slight displacement of a slow drifting glow-creature that had been settling toward you, which will redirect itself to the nearest trunk and resume its work.

The rain sounds. The pistons tick. The light slants down and is taken up by the leaves that are not leaves but are also not not-leaves, and is converted into something, and the something flows downward, and the cycle is closed.

The forest does not need you to understand it. The forest does not need anything.

It is running.
