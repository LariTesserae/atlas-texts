# The Cistern at World's End

There is a room. That is the first and last thing to know. Everything else is detail.

It is a single chamber, low-ceilinged, vast in floor, dropped into the earth like a tooth into a jaw — or perhaps it was the earth that dropped around it, the way these things are sometimes hard to tell. The ceiling is the underside of something that was once a hill. The floor is what remains of whatever the hill stood on. In between: this.

**The water** is everywhere, and is the reason for everything. It stands across most of the floor in a thin, perfectly still sheet — ankle-deep at the deepest points, knuckledeep at the shallows, a permanent shallow lake that has had so long to become flat that it is flatter than any poured surface could ever be. The air is so wet that breath feels like drinking. The walls weep without pause, the ceiling drips without sound, the floor is damp to a depth of feet. The whole place has the breath of a sleeping animal — not warm, but breathing. You do not so much enter the Cistern as the Cistern accepts you into its humidity.

**The stone** — if it is stone — is black and does not reflect light. It drinks it. The walls were not shaped so much as worn; even now they are still wearing, water patiently unbinding the centuries. Where the walls meet the ceiling there are flutes and runnels carved by ten thousand years of slow descent, each one a different thickness, each one a record of a different season that once mattered to whoever or whatever shaped this place. The runnels have runnels. The runnels on the runnels have runnels. It is an essay in patient subtraction.

**The machinery** is the second thing you should know, after the room. It fills the room the way a skeleton fills a body — everywhere, essential, hollow. Great bronze wheels, green with age, the size of small houses, stand in racks of pale stone that look as though they grew rather than were built. Axles the thickness of oak trees pass through them, and the axles pass through the walls, and through the ceiling, and into whatever is above and whatever is below. Cables of woven copper hang in vast loops from points so far above you cannot see them, sagging into the wet air like the rigging of drowned ships. Gears the size of millstones, stopped mid-tooth, are stacked in frames that no living hand has oiled in a geological age. Everywhere there is the smell of metal — not rust exactly, but the patient, organic reabsorption of metal back into mineral. Everywhere there is the sense that something enormous is *latent* here, sleeping, dreaming of its next half-rotation.

It is very old machinery. It is the only kind of machinery this place has ever known, and it is so old that it has forgotten what industry meant to its makers. It is an industry of patience now. An industry of wait.

**The sound** is what you came for, or what will keep you, or what will not let you leave. It is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, which is loud by being many small things all at once. It is loud the way a held note is loud — one thing, completely, sustained. It comes from the wheels, though the wheels are still. It comes from the water, though the water is still. It comes from the cables, the axles, the dripping from the ceiling, the weeping of the walls, the slow mineral seeping of the floor. It is the sound of a system that has reached equilibrium, that has for so long not moved that the not-moving has become its own kind of movement, a hum that is no longer heard so much as inhabited. You do not hear it. You stand inside it. After a while your heartbeat finds it and joins it. After a while longer you cannot tell which is which.

**The light** comes from nowhere in particular. The room is lit the way a forest floor is lit — not from a point but from the air itself, a diffuse green-grey luminance that has no source and casts no shadows except for the very deepest concavities of the machinery. It is enough light to see. It is not enough light to understand. Your eyes adjust and do not adjust; what you see stays at the threshold.

**The plants** have come in. Of course they have. With that much water and that much metal, with that much patience in the air, things grow. They are the wrong color. They are the deep red-black of beets and the pale translucent green of things that live without sun. They grow along the axles in slow festoons, they have climbed the spokes of the great wheels and woven themselves through the spokes like prisoners weaving rope. Ferns the color of dried blood hang from the cables. Somewhere there is a flower — one, single, white, blooming in the hub of a stopped gear, opening and closing on no schedule at all, breathing. None of it is arranged. All of it is fated. It is the kind of growth that comes after the last gardener has gone.

**The animals** are almost not here. A frog, somewhere, drops into the water and is gone. A pale insect — almost a crustacean, almost not — moves along the rim of a wheel. A snail, perhaps, leaves a luminous trail on a bronze strut. They are witness, not company. The Cistern does not need them.

**The temperature** is the temperature of blood. It does not change. It is warmer than the air above would be, cooler than a body, and exactly stable. You will not shiver here. You will not sweat. You will stay the temperature you arrived at, and the Cistern will keep you that way indefinitely.

**The floor** is interesting. If you stand still long enough — and you will stand still long enough — you can feel through the thin sheet of water that the floor is not flat. Not quite. There are rises and falls so gentle they would take hours to walk across, and they are *moving*, or the machinery beneath is moving, or the machinery above is moving, or the earth itself is breathing up through the stone. There is a tide. It is not the tide of the sea. It is the tide of the Cistern, and it moves at a timescale so slow that you cannot see it move, only arrive. Stand long enough and the water will be at your ankles. Stand long enough longer and the water will be at your knees. Stand long enough longer and you will be the only thing that has changed in this room in a thousand years, and the room will accept that, too.

This is the Cistern at World's End, or the Lung of the Old Machine, or the Place Where Sound Lives When Sound Has Forgotten How to Leave. It is not going anywhere. It is not asking anything of you. It is so old that the question of what it was built for has dissolved into the water and become the air and become the hum. You can sit on the rim of a wheel the size of a house and put your feet in the film of water and feel the hum through your bones and watch the white flower in the hub breathe in and out, in and out, and you can do this for a thousand years and when you leave the room will still be here, and the wheels will still be stopped, and the water will still be exactly the depth it has always been, and the sound will still be the only sound, and the flower will still be opening and closing, opening and closing, patient, patient, patient, until the bronze is green and the green is stone and the stone is dust and the dust is water and the water is air and the air is sound.

Until then: this.
