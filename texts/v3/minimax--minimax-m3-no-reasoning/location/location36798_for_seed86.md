# The Hollow Beneath the Root-Stones

You are underground, though not far.

The ceiling arches a hand's height above a head if you rose to full standing, and for a stretch the passage widens into a low vault where a person might walk upright between the ribs of a vast embedded lattice. It is the underside of a thing that grows on the surface — a forest of pale stone columns, perhaps, or the roots of a forest so old and slow that wood and mineral have become uncertain categories. The lattice is the source of the place. Everything else in the hollow is a consequence of it.

The columns descend from the roof in tapered forms, thick as thighs, thick as torsos, thick as the trunks of old trees  they do not taper the other way. They plunge straight down into a floor that is not quite floor: it is the same material, or close, ridged and rippled and cratered, as if the hollow itself were a single artifact half-formed, withdrawn from the kiln before its glaze could settle. The ridges run in long parallel waves, shoulder-spaced, and they continue, as far as one can see, in the same direction. The floor has not been walked on. Nothing has worn its surface. Nothing has even dusted it.

The hollow is cool and stays cool. A draft moves through it, low and constant, from a direction the inhabitants have names for. The draft carries the smell of water and of something organic beneath the water — a loam-sweetness, a breath of rot, a thin mineral tang. The humidity is not thick enough to bead on stone, but a person who has been inside for an hour emerges with the small hairs of their forearms clinging to one another, and their leather stiff.

Light does not come from above. There is no above to come from. Light comes from below: from the cracks between the ridges, from the seams in the rippled floor, from places where the stone is thinner or simply different. It is a dim, warm amber, the color of late autumn through closed eyelids, the color of banked coals. It does not flicker. It does not move. It has been this brightness for a very long time. A person can see by it, but only just. Shadows are present but soft. Edges of things are suggested rather than drawn.

The sound is the thing that is wrong, or the thing that is correct, depending on who you ask.

It is loud here. The hollow resounds with a constant, layered, arrhythmic music that rises from the floor and from the walls and from somewhere far off in the lateral passages, where the ceiling lowers again to a thickness that swallows light but not vibration. The sound is not one sound. It is breathing and it is chewing and it is the wet friction of hide against stone and it is, intermittently, a low, sustained tone — almost a note, almost not — that the bones of the face vibrate in sympathy with. It is the sound of many large bodies in a confined space, choosing to be near one another, choosing not to move. The sound is not threatening. It is, however, never absent. A person who has been in the hollow for half a day stops hearing it the way one stops hearing a stream, and the silence of leaving it afterward is, for a few minutes, enormous.

They are the reason for the hollow. They are the reason it exists at all.

They lie along the ridges of the floor. There are — one cannot count, and one does not try. They are pale. Their hides are the color of the stone, mottled in the same way the stone is mottled, the same warm grey and amber. They are very long, and very broad across the back, and where their bodies rise over a ridge the silhouette is almost lost. A casual eye would miss them entirely. A careful eye sees the slight rise of a spine, the slow working of a flank. They rest belly-down on the stone. Their limbs are folded beneath them in a way that the stone accommodates, as if the ridges and the limbs were made for each other. They are sleeping, or they are digesting, or they are thinking, and no one, here, knows how to tell the difference, because they are old in the way that certain things are old: a mountain is old, a river is old.

They breathe.

That is the sound.

The floor glows because of them. No one has tested this. No one would. The warmth that comes off the stone between the ridges is theirs; their bodies have heated the rock over years, over lifetimes, over the slow generations of their kind, and the light is a kind of thankless leakage from that warmth. The light is their heat seen sideways. The place is warm because they are warm. The place exists because they are warm. The place is the space they have made by lying here.

A few things grow here. Not many.

Pale lichens, almost white, in lace patterns along the upper parts of the columns where the surfaces are driest. They are crisp, and they crunch underfoot, and they are the only thing that crunches. A low, almost subterranean moss, the color of bruised plum, in the troughs between the floor-ridges, where moisture collects from the slow weeping of condensation down the column-sides. It is always damp. It never dries. It is the source of the smell. It is the only green thing in the place, and it is not, really, green.

No trees. No standing growth. No grass. The vegetation is a film, a patina, an afterthought of biology on the surface of geology.

Water collects in the troughs in places — shallow puddles, never more than an inch deep, the color of weak tea, tasting of lime and iron. They are warm. They smell of the moss. A person could drink from them and would not be harmed; a person would be advised not to, because the water is not theirs, and the moss is not theirs, and the hollow is not, in the end, theirs.

The people here are few.

There is a camp at the far edge of the main vault, where a lateral passage opens into a slightly higher-ceilinged alcove. It is a camp the way a bivouac is a camp: a few hides stretched between columns, weighted at the corners with stones; a low ring of gathered rocks around a place where a fire is sometimes made, though the fire is small and the smoke is careful, because the smoke goes up and the things on the floor do not care for it, and the people have learned what the things do not care for. The hides are cut from the winter-shed skins of creatures from the surface, tanned with the moss, stitched with sinew. The stones are gathered from the ridges where they have fallen, or been pushed free, by the slow movement of the great bodies. The tools are bone and stone and tooth. There are pots, low and round, fired in a small kiln at the back of the alcove. The pots are unremarkable. The people are unremarkable, except that they live here.

They live here because the great ones are here. They live here because the heat is steady and the water is reliable and the predators of the surface do not come down through the narrow places. They live here because the great ones, in their long patience, do not mind them. They live here because, in winter, when the columns above freeze and the surface world goes white and silent, the hollow is warm, and the moss is still damp, and the puddles do not ice. They live here, and they have lived here for a long time, and the lineage of their living is the lineage of a careful and inherited knowledge: which ridge to step over, which column to walk behind, where the great ones sleep in clusters and where they sleep alone, what sounds to make and what sounds to withhold, what offerings to leave at the edges of the moss-beds, and how to read, by the slight changes in the breathing, the moods of the things that are not them.

The offerings are small. A few pieces of dried meat. A flake of flint worked into a particular shape. A tooth, from a child, shed and saved. The great ones do not eat the offerings. The great ones do not appear to notice them. The offerings are left anyway, because the people have a sense of the place that is not instrumental, and the offerings are, in the end, the way the people say *we are here and we are grateful and we are not afraid of you, mostly, and we are sorry about the smoke.*

The hollow extends. It extends past the main vault into lateral passages that narrow and widen unpredictably, into chambers where the columns are denser and the light is dimmer, into places where the floor is not ridged but smooth, and the great ones do not lie, and the moss gives way to a thin powdery substance that the people do not touch. The hollow extends for miles, perhaps. The people have mapped only the part they use, which is a careful and conservative part. They do not go far. The great ones go where they wish, when they wish, and the hollow is shaped by their going as a river is shaped by water.

In the deepest passages there are smaller things. The great ones are not alone here. There are creatures of the size of a dog, pale, many-legged, that move in the troughs between the ridges and feed on the moss. They do not trouble the great ones. The great ones do not trouble them. There are pale insects, eyeless, that hum in the warmer cracks. There is, occasionally, a sound from far away — a deep, percussive thud, felt in the teeth, that means a great one has moved, somewhere, in a chamber out of sight. The sound arrives after a long delay. The great ones move slowly.

Time does not pass here in the way it passes on the surface. There is no day. There is no night. The light is the light. The warmth is the warmth. The breathing is the breathing. The great ones sleep, or wake, or rise, or settle, on a schedule that is not a schedule, and the people have built their small lives around the edges of that unknowing, the way moss is built on stone, or the way offerings are left at thresholds.

The hollow is, in the end, a place made by staying. The great ones have stayed, and the stone has shaped itself to their staying, and the moss has come, and the water has come, and the people have come, and all of it, the whole lit and breathing vault, is a slow record of the decision of very large things to be, for a long time, in one place, in the dark, together.

It is loud. It is warm. It is dim. It is, in the way that some quiet things are loud, very still.

Nothing here changes. Everything here is already made.
