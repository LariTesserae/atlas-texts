# The Trench of Coughing Lamps

You come down into it by way of a crack in the world — not a staircase, not a path, just a fissure that the rain has widened over decades until a person can fit, then a cart, then a small house. The walls of the crack are slick and the color of liver, and they weep constantly. You hear the weeping before you see anything else: a thin, steady hiss, the sound of a thousand small envelopes being licked, multiplied by the acoustics of stone.

Then you step out, and the trench opens.

---

It is not a cave, though it has the feel of one. The sky — what is left of sky here — is a pale gray ribbon maybe forty feet overhead, and on either side the walls of the trench rise to meet it, leaning slightly inward, as if the place is in the act of closing and has only paused to think. Between those walls runs a town. The town has no name, or rather it has many names, one for every person in it, and they don't agree.

The trench is about a quarter mile wide. The floor is wet stone worn into soft hollows by the slow traffic of bare feet. A river — what is more like a stream with ambitions — runs down the center in a shallow trench of its own, only a foot or two deep, and into it drain all the gutters, all the cisterns, all the upturned palms and clay bowls. The water is the color of weak tea. It is the only color in the place that isn't gray or green.

---

The houses are tall and they are stuck to the walls. They are built out of the walls, in fact: each one is partly cut, partly grown into the rock, so that you cannot tell where the trench ends and the homes begin. The doors are damp wood, sometimes planks, sometimes just hide stretched tight and pegged. The windows are small. The roofs are sloped, shedding the constant seeping water down into clay channels that run along the wall-face and feed the central stream. The architecture is not beautiful. It has the ugly practicality of something that has been solved many times by many people and never written down.

The buildings are packed tight. There is no yard between them and no alley in the conventional sense — you move from doorstep to doorstep, and if two people want to pass each other one of them steps into a doorway and presses their back against someone else's hearth. No one seems to mind. The air between the houses is the temperature of cooled tea, and it is very wet, and it smells of root vegetables and animal and the particular green smell of stone that is always being wetted and never quite drying.

---

Because of the geometry, the place is never fully lit and never fully dark. The ribbon of sky gives a kind of pale wash during the day; at night the lamps come on. The lamps are the reason for the name I am using, which is not the name the place has.

The lamps are fungi. They are tall — waist high, some chest high — and they have been coaxed into a particular shape over many generations. Nobody teaches the coaxing. Each family knows how their own lamps should grow, and the children of the family learn by trimming. The fungi are pale, almost translucent, and they give off a soft bluish light, like the inside of a fish. The light is not very strong. It is enough to see the person next to you by, and not much more. The town at night is a constellation of pale smudges stacked up the leaning walls, and the sound of water, and the slow breathing of people.

The fungi smell faintly of anise. They are warm to the touch. They grow during the day and they go dim, but they never go out. The dead ones are gathered and burned in a low hearth at the heart of the trench, and the children fight over the privilege of carrying them.

---

The plants are the other thing. They are not cultivated the way you might expect. The walls of the trench are hung with them: long trailing things with small white flowers, ferns the size of your arm, fungi of many kinds, some edible, some not, all of them feeding off the weeping rock. In the wetter hollows at the base of the walls grow denser thickets — thorned, snagging, smelling of mint and onion. A few families keep bees in clay hives tucked into the upper stories; the bees leave the trench through the crack by which you came in, and return, and this is how honey happens here.

There is no wheat. There is no field. There is no pasture. Food comes from the wall-gardens, from the stream, and from the few animals that thrive in the dark. There are pale pigs the size of dogs. There are blind chickens. There are long, slow lizards that live under the stones of the floor and are eaten roasted. People eat well enough. They do not eat much.

---

The animals of the trench are not numerous in species but they are numerous in body. The river at the center is thick with a small blind fish that nobody bothers to catch because there are too many of them, and their smell when they die in quantity is what gives the place its other smell, a smell of brine and pennies. Birds roost in the upper crannies of the trench — they come in by the crack, mostly at dawn, and their droppings are collected and used to dress the wall-gardens. Once a year, when the rains are heaviest, a kind of eel makes its way down from somewhere above and into the stream, and for two or three nights the town eats eel and nothing else.

People here are not pale, exactly, but they are not dark. They are the color of the trench walls. They move quickly when they move at all, and they are quiet. They speak quietly. The whole place is quiet. The only loud sound is the weeping of the rock, which never stops, and which, after a few days, you stop hearing.

---

The trench is young, geologically. You can see it happening: in some years a house leans too far and must be abandoned; in some years a new fissure opens and a new family moves in and cuts a new door. The place grows. The crack at the entrance is wider this year than it was last. The walls are wetter this year than they were last. In a hundred years there will be no trench; there will be a valley. In a thousand years there will be a river. People here know this, the way people everywhere know the weather. They do not speak of it.

You stand in the trench of coughing lamps and the rock weeps on you and the fungi glow and somewhere a child is burning a dead one and the smoke goes up into the pale ribbon of sky and is gone, and the trench closes another fraction of an inch while you are not looking.
