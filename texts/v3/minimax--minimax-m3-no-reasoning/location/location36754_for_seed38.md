# The Patient Country

You arrive in the patient country the way the patient country arrives at you: gradually, and then all at once. There is no border. The ground you have been walking on for days stops being rock at some moment you cannot locate, and begins being root, and the air you have been breathing stops being air and begins being mist, and you are in it.

It is high here. You can feel it in the small ache behind your eyes and the way your thoughts come in long, slow curves. The country sits up at an altitude where the barometric pressure seems to make time itself thicker, where the warm air never quite leaves and the cold air never quite arrives, so that everything exists in a permanent tepid bath, like the inside of a lung. It is warm the way old kitchens are warm. It is wet the way green wood is wet. The water is in everything: beading on every surface, weeping out of every vertical face, condensing in the air itself so that there is no clear line between falling rain and standing fog and the slow seep that rises from below.

The light is dim. Not dark — never properly dark, because the place is so wide and so lit by its own slow glow of bacterial and fungal and stranger things that even in the deepest hollows there is a kind of seeing. But the sun, when it is here at all, comes filtered through a hundred meters of canopy and a permanent drift of vapor, and arrives at the floor of the world as something the color of weak tea with honey in it. Things here do not cast shadows so much as cast absences — places where the diffuse glow is fractionally less present, where you can imagine a shape standing, though no shape stands there.

The vegetation is total. There is no ground that is not also a plant. The roots and the soil are the same substance; the trunks and the walls of any clearing are the same substance; the canopy and the sky are the same substance. Some of the plants are so old that they have stopped being individuals the way trees stop being individuals when their roots fuse underground, and have begun to be a kind of geography — slow, patient, almost mineral. Others are still growing in the manner of vines and creepers and mosses, but there is no separating one stratum from another. You are inside an organism, or you are inside an ecology so dense it has become an organism. The air smells of green and warm and the deep mineral tang of things that have been wet for a very long time.

Erosion has done its work here so thoroughly that the work itself has been forgotten. What might once have been a mountain range is now a long, low swell in the surface of the root-mass, a place where the canopy rises and the air thins and you can walk for days on a slight upward grade without ever quite believing you are going up. What might once have been a valley is now a shallow basin, a marsh-edge a hundred kilometers across, where the water stands in slow sheets between the great buttressed trunks and the giant ferns and the columnar things that have no common name in any language you still speak. The country has settled into its final form. It is not waiting to become anything else. It has been becoming this for so long that becoming has stopped.

The country is vast. Vast in the way that oceans are vast, where a feature you can see at the edge of vision is a feature you will not reach for a season, and beyond it there are more features, and beyond them weather that belongs to no other place. The weather here is its own. There are slow storms that wander the canopy in spirals so wide you can walk under one for a week. There are fog-banks that settle into a basin and stay there for years, the trees inside them growing in a different dialect from the trees outside. There are rain-fronts that return, again and again, to the same folds in the terrain, as if remembering a promise. The country has its own meteorology. It breathes. In the wet season the whole canopy darkens and the floor of the world becomes a hundred shallow lakes; in the dry season — which is not really dry, only less drowning — the lakes shrink to marshes and the marshes shrink to weeping ground, and everywhere the water goes on weeping out of the trunks and the leaves and the air itself.

There is fauna. Abundant, once you learn to look, and quiet, mostly. The great visible animals are rare here — this is not a country of herds — but the undergrowth is full of motion. There are things that climb the trunks in long spirals. There are things that burrow through the root-flesh and leave trails that close behind them. There are small bright things that live in the cupped leaves of the giant herbs and sing, very softly, in frequencies you feel more than hear. The hum of the country, when you stand still long enough, is a low distributed sound like the inside of a seashell, made of ten thousand small lives going on with their small concerns. There are larger presences too — things that move in the high canopy and only rarely descend, things whose calls come down through the mist as a long, slow, descending note that you can listen to for a quarter of an hour before it ends. You do not see them often. You see their marks: a stripped patch of bark, a leaning tree, a clearing made by something that lay down and rose again.

The country is strange, but only mildly strange, the way a dream is strange. It is strange in its proportions. The leaves are too large. The trunks are too columnar. The clearings are too round. The animals have too many legs, or too few, or their legs are in the wrong places, or they have no legs at all and move by other means. The strangeness is in the patient things — in the way a vine will grow for fifty years toward a single point of light and then, in your presence, finally arrive. The strangeness is in the water, which sometimes seems to flow upward. The strangeness is in the canopies, which sometimes seem to be lower than the floor.

The country is mostly quiet. The wind, when it comes, comes muffled and arrives as a sway rather than a sound. The rain, when it falls, falls through so many layers before it reaches the ground that by the time you hear it it has become a hiss, then a whisper, then a feeling. The animal calls are infrequent and far apart. The principal sound, most of the time, is the water: a soft, distributed patter of droplets falling from leaf to leaf to leaf to ground, and the slow tick of condensation on every surface, and once in a while the deep groan of a tree so old it is settling into its own weight by millimeters.

There are marks here that something else was here once. You come across them rarely, because the country is so vast and the marks are so scattered, but you come across them. A line of stones, half-swallowed, running through a clearing for no reason. A flat shelf of rock laid against a hillside in a way the geology cannot account for. A single standing pillar in a marsh, so eaten by lichen and root-hold that it has become a kind of tree itself. The marks are old — older than the oldest trees, you suspect, which is to say older than anything alive anywhere. Whatever made them made them with no tools you would recognize as such; whatever they were, they were made by hands and bodies and a patience as long as the country's own. There is one place, if you find it, where several of these marks come together in a pattern that is not quite a pattern — a circle, perhaps, or the ghost of a circle, or the memory of a circle in a place that has forgotten how to be a circle. Nothing is built. Nothing is whole. But the marks are here, and they are quiet, and they belong to the country now the way everything belongs to the country.

You walk for a long time. The walking is the country. The walking is slow because the country is slow; you sink a little at every step into the root-flesh and the moss and the wet duff of a million years of fallen leaves. A leaf falls. It takes four days to reach the ground. When it lands, it does not make a sound. Nothing here makes much of a sound. The light shifts by imperceptible degrees. A pool at the edge of the path, which was not there yesterday, is here today, and will not be here tomorrow. Somewhere above you, in the vast and patient dark of the canopy, something moves and the canopy sways and a column of warm mist descends into the clearing where you stand and you are briefly inside a cloud, and then you are not.

You do not get anywhere. There is no anywhere to get to. The country does not lead anywhere. The country is the country.

It has been here so long that it has stopped waiting for you. It will be here so long after you that it will not notice you have gone. You walk through it the way a small bright thing walks through the cupped leaf of a giant herb: briefly, quietly, on the way to some small concern of its own. The country does not require you. The country allows you.

In the warm and dim and weeping air, in the slow hush of water on water on water, in the patient breath of an ecology so old it has forgotten how to be anything but itself, you walk on, and the root-flesh gives a little under your weight, and somewhere a small thing sings, very softly, in a frequency you feel more than hear.
