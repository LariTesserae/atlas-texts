# The Glass Nest

A fissure in the basalt plain, only a few meters across. To walk up on it from above, you might miss it entirely — just another crack in the dark rock, another seam where rainwater could trickle and disappear. But the fissure is not empty. It opens, after a handspan or two of brittle stone lip, into a shallow chamber, a concavity pressed down into the crust like a thumb pressed into firm dough. You could crouch at the edge and see most of what is here. It is that small. It is that hidden.

The light comes from below.

That is the first wrong thing a person notices. Light should come from above, from the absent sky, from whatever is up there past the rim. Instead, the glow rises up the walls from the floor, a soft fierce gold, slightly orange toward the cooler edges, like a forge banked down to coals. The source is the basalt itself — or rather, the basalt's inner skin, which is not basalt at all. Where the cooling lava should be black and crystalline, it is instead translucent, honey-colored, alive with a slow rolling luminescence that moves like weather seen from a great height: bands, eddies, a dim pulse on the order of several seconds. The glass is new. A few decades old, perhaps. Recent enough that it has not yet finished cracking into its final slow pattern. Recent enough that if you pressed your ear to certain panels, you would still hear faint ticks, the tiny ongoing announcement of molecular rearrangement.

The chamber is hot. Not scalding — you can stand it — but the air here is the temperature of a summer sidewalk, of a closed greenhouse, of breath exhaled after drinking tea. Vapor threads rise from nowhere in particular: from where the glass meets rough stone, from a row of small pits along one wall, from a thin line of moisture that runs along a crack as if drawn there. The wetness is everywhere and nowhere. The rocks themselves sweat. Condensation gathers on every upward-facing surface, gathers, hangs as a bright meniscus for a moment, then slips. A slow rain, falling upward in appearance. The drip from a sharp glass edge into a small pool at the chamber's lowest point makes a sound that is, despite everything, the loudest thing here: a tick, a *plink*, a tick.

There is no plant. No moss, no lichen, no fungal skim of any color. The walls are mineral clean, mineral sharp, mineral glinting where the under-light catches the condensation. Where the rock has crumbled into grit, the grit is glass — glass grains, glass flakes, glass dust in a small drift against the windward wall, bright and pale and edged. This is a place younger than lichen. This is a place that has not yet been told what it is going to become.

The glass is the work, though. The glass is not natural. That is the second wrong thing.

Look closer at any panel and the simple translucence breaks into pattern. There are fine lines traced through the gold — regular, geometric, curves nested inside curves, a kind of writing that is not writing. Each line is a conduit of some kind. Where three lines meet, a small bright node has formed: a bead of denser light, the size of a fingertip, warm to the touch, dimming and brightening on a slow rhythm like the chamber's larger pulse but offset, like a chorus behind a soloist. The lines themselves, if you trace one with your eye, run for several handspans before ending in a closed loop or branching into more lines, and what they carry is not light. If you held a sliver of dark stone above one, the stone would warm, asymmetrically, from below. Whatever is being conducted is heat, but also something else, something that does not quite name itself as heat. It is being routed. There is a mind here, even if there is no mind. There is plumbing. There is purpose.

At the chamber's lowest point, the small pool. It is not large — a cupped hand could almost cover it — but it is perfectly round, and it is not water. Its surface, when you crouch near it, gives back the under-light without scattering it, and the color of the liquid inside is the color of the glass above: honey, gold, faint ember. Above the pool, perpetually, a small tongue of vapor rises and dissolves. The pool is very slowly sinking, and yet it does not empty. There must be a feed, somewhere along the glass, that is keeping it level — a capillary line, or some other principle you do not have a word for. Around the rim of the pool, the glass is darkest, almost amber-brown, and warmest. If you listened to the pool with your ear near it, you would hear what you heard at the larger scale, but made small and private: a kind of breathing.

The fauna is sparse and strange. Once, perhaps twice in an hour spent here, something will move. A thin shape, perhaps as long as your forearm, colorless, translucent as the glass it lives against, will uncurl from a crack that seemed too shallow to hold it. It has the texture of glass too, or of mucus that has learned to be glass. It will travel along one of the bright lines for a distance, feed at a node — and what feeding means here is unclear, but the node dims when it does, and brightens again after it leaves — and then vanish back into another crack, leaving behind only a faint wet trail that the heat dries in seconds. The chamber tolerates them. The lines seem, if anything, to route around them. There are not many. Six, perhaps eight, in the chamber at any moment. They are not aware, perhaps, of a person. They are aware of the network. They are part of it.

The sound of the place is mostly its own. The ticks of cooling glass. The slow drip into the pool. A faint hiss, constant, from the line of pits along one wall — vents, perhaps, where the network exhales. Beneath everything, a hum that is felt more than heard, a low fundamental in the basalt itself, as if something very large is turning over, somewhere far below, with this chamber as one of its small bright capillaries. Sometimes, at intervals, a tone rises from the network — not musical, exactly, but patterned. A series of quiet bright notes, climbing and then settling, like a question asked and answered. The chamber has moods. It is more or less bright on a scale of minutes. The drip quickens or slows. The little glass fauna emerge more often, or retire.

There is no wind. There is no rain, though it feels, in your sinuses, as if it has just finished raining, or is about to begin. The air has weight. It is humid and warm and faintly sweet, and slightly metallic on the tongue — not unpleasant, exactly, but as if there are molecules in it that your body does not recognize and is taking the time to consider.

If you stayed too long here — and the heat would not let you stay too long — you might notice that the geometry of the lines, the location of the brighter nodes, the path of the circulating glass fauna, all seem, by very slow degrees, to be adjusting. Reorganizing. The chamber is solving something, or making something, or remembering something. It is not finished.

It is, in fact, very young.

Climb out. Step back into the daylight. Look down. It is only a crack in the basalt. A cool shadow. A place where a goat would not pause.

Walk on, if you can.
