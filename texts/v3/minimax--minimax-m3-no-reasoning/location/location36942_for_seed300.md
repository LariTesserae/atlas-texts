# The Pump Lot

The first thing to understand is that the water is everywhere and that the machines are still working. These two facts do not argue with each other; they have made peace a long time ago, the way things that have lived together too long do. You approach across flat ground, ground that has no opinion about itself, and you come up against the fence — a chain-link fence, mostly, with some sections replaced by something older, paler, a low concrete wall that has been split by a thread of green and is weeping a thin pale liquid into a groove worn along its base. The fence is the boundary. Past it the ground drops a half-step and the rules change.

You step in. The sound changes before the picture does.

There is a roar somewhere, or there is the memory of a roar, folded down into a hiss and a tick. The machines are running. You can feel it in the soles of your feet before you hear it: a low tremor, a kind of patient insistence, the way a large heart feels through a mattress. But what reaches your ears is only the click of contracting iron, the slow plop of water falling off something metal onto something else metal, the faint rising hiss of steam or wet air through a grating. The sound has been swallowed by the wet. The place is loud the way a fist wrapped in cloth is loud.

It is cool. The air has weight to it. It presses against your face and leaves a faint bloom of moisture on your lip, your eyebrow, the rim of your eyeglasses if you have them. Above, the sky is overcast but not dramatically so — no theater of clouds, just the pale gray ceiling of a place that has forgotten what sun looks like. The light is even. It does not flatter anything. It does not punish anything either. It simply lays things out.

The compound is small. You could throw a stone across it in places, though you should not throw stones here, and the fence on the far side is closer than you'd think. Within that smallness there is a great deal. Things crowd. Things layer. A low brick building — the pump house, maybe, though the lettering on its gable has weathered to the same color as the brick and refuses to give up its name — sits half-sunk, its foundation capped by a strip of greenish scum where the water line holds. Against its flank, a forest of pipes: vertical, horizontal, elbowed, capped. Some are rusted to a deep saturated orange that looks almost edible. Some are the dull yellow of old enamel. A few still wear their original paint, a blue that has gone chalky with age. They branch and converge and double back on themselves the way roots do, or the way the veins in a wrist do, and you cannot quite follow one to its source because it always turns behind another and emerges elsewhere, smaller, or ends in a flange that bleeds.

The water moves through this. The water is the whole point.

It comes in along a concrete channel from the east — a low tongue of marsh the color of cold tea, littered with the bleached stems of last year's reeds, bending under their own weight, leaning. It feeds a grate. Below the grate, in a space you cannot see, a pump is doing what pumps do. You can hear it, in the way you can hear the sea through a wall: a rhythmic assertion, gone small. The water goes down, comes up somewhere else. The system is closed. The system is patient. From the far side of the pump house a pipe expels a steady clear stream onto a sloped apron of broken concrete, where the water fans out, joins other threads, finds its way back into the marsh and disappears.

Vegetation is shy here. It does not announce. A few clumps of sedge have lodged in the angle where the channel meets the grate. Some moss — a sour, vivid green — has climbed the lower third of the brick building and stopped, as if at an agreed line. Lichens have made maps of the pipework in pale gray and mustard yellow. A single willow sapling grows out of the rust-eaten husk of an old boiler at the back of the lot; it is no taller than a man and absurdly healthy, its leaves the only moving color in the whole compound, swaying faintly with no wind you can feel. That is the largest living thing.

Fauna is almost absent. A heron stands on the far rim of the marsh with the fixed patience of furniture. Once, a frog calls from somewhere beneath the grate and stops. There are insects — small, low, silver, vibrating above the standing water in the channels — but they do not rise high enough to matter. The rest of the life is microbial, mineral, the patient chemistry of rust.

The built things are scattered but insistent. The pump house. A separate low shed, windowless, with a chimney that leaks a thin feather of vapor — not smoke, not quite steam, something between the two, something that smells of warm oil and old iron. A short tower of stacked concrete blocks from which a single pipe ascends and bends sharply and disappears into a hole in the sky. Two steel tanks on squat legs, one rusted through along its seam so that a permanent dark stain spreads beneath it like a shadow that has pooled. A wooden rack for tools, the tools gone. An overturned office chair, its vinyl cracked white, half-submerged. A bulletin board by the pump house door, its Plexiglas clouded, the notices inside turned to uniform tan by damp. The place is not abandoned. Something keeps it. Something comes and goes — you can see the footprints in the rust dust inside the shed, and there is a thermos on a flange beside the pump house door, and a calendar on the inside of the shed wall, six months out of date, with one square circled in red. But nothing human is here right now. The machines are alone with the water.

And the machines.

This is the strange thing, the slightly-off thing, the way this place presses on the inside of your attention. The machines are running but they have been left. They are running and no one is watching them and they are running well. The pistons in the shed go up and down behind a window so crusted with condensation you cannot see them, only the dark motion of them. The pump in the grate hums at a frequency you feel in your sternum. Valves somewhere inside the pipework are opening and closing — you can hear the soft click, the soft click, the soft click, like a man clearing his throat in another room. The clear stream on the apron never varies. The vapor from the chimney never thins. Nothing here is failing, exactly. Nothing here has been allowed to fail. Something has settled this place into itself: the water has come up to a level it likes and stopped; the machines have found a tempo they like and keep it; the rust has reached a depth it likes and slowed. Erosion has done its long work and found a shape it can live with, and the shape is wet and metallic and faintly breathing.

And yet it is turbulent. Underneath.

Beneath the grate the water is moving fast. You can tell because of the faint dimples on the surface above the pump intake, the small whirlpools the size of coins that appear and vanish. Beneath the apron, beneath the broken concrete, in the pipes, in the foundations, the water is traveling somewhere with purpose, and it does not stop. The chimney's vapor is a constant exhalation. The willows' leaves move without a wind because the air itself moves — the warm breath from the shed mixes with the cool damp air above it and makes a slow current that nudges the leaves and nothing else. The water on the metal bleeds downward in its own slow clock. The lichen grows at the pace of centuries. The heron, on the far rim, lifts and relocates with a single stiff motion to a place three feet away and stands again. Everything is changing. Nothing is loud.

You stand in this for a while. The wet comes through the soles of your shoes. The hiss from the grate never lets you forget the machinery underneath. The pale light does not change. The place does not explain itself. You can read it as neglect, or as a kind of slow maintenance, or as the particular devotion of a person or institution that has decided this compound should keep running until it cannot. You can read it as the machines themselves, grown indifferent to their makers, persisting in their function the way a heart persists in beating after the body has forgotten what the body was for.

There is no answer offered. The water keeps moving through the pipes. The shed keeps breathing its thin vapor into the cool air. The heron watches the channel with the long patience of a thing that knows its food will come. The willows' leaves — the only green, the only motion above the height of a man's knee — sway in their private current. The rust holds. The sound does not rise. The place does not change its mind.

You step back over the half-step. The fence is behind you. The road is in front. The sound of the machines fades as you walk, but you do not hear it fade so much as you feel it leave — a slight lessening in the chest, a slight loosening of the patient tremor that had been there and that you had not, until it was gone, quite been aware of.

The place keeps running.
