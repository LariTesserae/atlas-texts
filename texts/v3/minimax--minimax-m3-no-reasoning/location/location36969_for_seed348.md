# The Cathedral of Condensation

You arrive through a corridor. That is the first thing to understand: you do not approach this place, you are admitted into it, the way one is admitted into a building whose outer walls are also its only weather. The walls are tall. The walls have been tall for a long time.

The complex sits in a basin — not a dramatic one, not a crater or a caldera, just a slight sag in the ground where the air, being cool, being heavy, decided one day to stay. The basin holds weather the way a bowl holds water. You can stand on the rim and see the whole settlement, which is not a settlement in the usual sense but a single articulated structure, a factory-hamlet-cathedral the size of a market town, built in a generation when the people who built it believed that scale was a kind of argument. They have been gone a long time. The argument is over. The factory continues.

There is no plant life. There has been no plant life for a very long time. The ground around the basin is mineral — pale stone in some places, dark glassy slag in others, the legacy of the original pour when the furnaces were young. The slag has weathered into something almost soil-like, but it is not soil. Nothing roots in it. A few pale crusts grow on the lower walls, fungal in the older sense, but they are not plants and they do not behave like ones. They are chemical. They are the mineral world doing what the mineral world does when given time and a steady trickle of something to feed on.

The air is damp. Not wet — damp. The moisture comes from two sources and they are indistinguishable to a body standing inside. The first is the sky, which is a low sky here, a basin sky, and which sometimes, on unremarkable days, forgets to be weather and simply lets a fine condensation fall out of itself onto everything. The second is the factory itself, which breathes. The furnaces run cooler now than they once did, but they run, and the great exhaust stacks lift warm vapor into the cool air above the basin, where it meets the heavier sky-moisture and condenses on every horizontal surface. The walls are dark with it. The pipes sweat. The beams overhead carry droplets in rows like a held breath. The floor is slick in places, dry in others, mapped in dampness the way a forest is mapped in shadow.

It is cool. You wear a coat that is never quite enough and never quite not enough.

The sound is the first thing you stop hearing, and that is how you know you have arrived.

The sound is not one sound. It is a chord, sustained. The lowest note is the furnaces themselves — not their firing, which is long past, but their idling, their slow continuous work of keeping themselves hot enough to be useful and not so hot as to be wasteful. Above that sits the machinery, the pumps and the conveyors and the line-shafts that turn without ceasing because no one is here to turn them off and because turning off would be, in some deeper sense, against the nature of the place. Above the machinery sits the fauna.

The fauna is the thing you came for, whether you know it or not.

It is overwhelming. There is no other word and the word is insufficient. The walls are not walls. They are aggregations. The beams are not beams. They are surfaces hosting life at a density that would, in a forest, constitute a plague. The creatures are small — most of them are small, some the size of a thumbnail, some the size of a hand, a few as long as your arm, none larger — and they are present in numbers that have no referent in ordinary experience. They cover every surface. They pour along the conduits. They cluster in the corners where the condensation gathers. They are the color of the walls, mostly, and the color of rust, and the color of the pale crusts, and they have been here so long that the patina of the factory and the patina of the creatures have become the same substance, indistinguishable at a distance, only resolving into separate kingdoms when you lean close and see things move.

What are they. They are difficult. They are not insects, though some have six legs. They are not birds, though something small and pale launches itself from beam to beam and lands with a click. They are not rats, though there are things the size and shape of rats that move in the spaces between the machinery with a fluid, unbothered gait, and they have been eating the dropped grain from the processing line for so many generations that their teeth have come to resemble the gears they live among. They are not anything that has a clean name. The people who built the factory would not have recognized them. The people who built the factory did not stay long enough to find out what would live here after them. The creatures are the answer to a question the original builders never thought to ask, which was: what does this place want to become when no one is watching.

What does the place want. The place wants this. The creatures, the condensation, the slow idle of the machinery, the damp, the constant cool. The place is in equilibrium. The furnaces run because the creatures' bodies, in their millions, give off enough heat to keep the furnaces from cooling fully, and the furnaces, in turn, give off enough warmth to keep the creatures breeding through the cool nights. The water condenses because the temperatures are right. The creatures drink the condensation and eat the spilled product and eat each other and die and their bodies become the pale crusts on the walls, and the crusts hold moisture, and the moisture feeds the next generation. Nothing changes. Nothing has changed in a long time. The scale is small — a cathedral, a hamlet, a single complex with its own weather — and within that scale the cycle is closed and has been closed for longer than you can comfortably imagine.

It is loud. The sound of the creatures is a sound the way a rainstorm is a sound, continuous, without individual events. It is the click of chitin on metal. It is the small wet sounds of bodies moving over other bodies. It is the rustle and chirp and the occasional high thin cry when something is taken in the dark. It is louder than the machinery. The machinery is a hum beneath it. The creatures are the voice of the place. When the place is quiet, which is never, it is only because the creatures have all stopped at once, and that is a different sound entirely, a held-breath sound, and it does not last.

It is strange. The strangeness is not theatrical. It is the strangeness of a thing that has been left alone for a long time and has organized itself according to its own principles. The creatures move in patterns that are not quite migrations and not quite currents. The pale crusts on the walls grow in shapes that are not quite fractal and not quite calligraphic. The water finds its way through the structure in rivulets that no engineer planned. The whole place, if you watch it long enough, begins to feel like a single organism, and you are the foreign body in it, and the creatures know you are there, and they do not mind, and that is the strangest thing of all.

The light is ordinary. That is the last thing and it is the thing that unsettles, in the end, more than the fauna or the damp or the sound. The light comes through the high windows in the usual way, in the usual color, at the usual angle. The dust in it — if it is dust — moves in the usual slow Brownian drift. The shadows fall where shadows should. It is the light of a workday, in a building that no one works in, in a basin that holds its own weather, under a sky that sometimes remembers to rain. Nothing in the light suggests that anything here is wrong. The place is, by every measure a working eye can apply, simply a place. The fact that it has been its own place for a very long time, and that it will continue to be its own place for a very long time, and that you are a temporary visitor to a permanent thing, is a thought that arrives later, in the corridor out, when the sound has thinned and the damp has lifted and the sky over the rim of the basin is, as always, the color of nothing in particular.
