# The Sounding

The shelf is young. The water has not yet learned its shape upon it, so the water is loud about it—always loud, always arriving for the first time.

## The Place

A granite ledge, perhaps three hundred yards across, thrust upward through warm dark water that is somewhere between a sea and a sky. The ledge is new. Newer than language. The water has been wearing it for what might be a season or what might be a geologic minute; the difference is academic at the scale the ledge itself experiences time. Its edges are crisp where they meet the water—not softened, not yet translated into beach. Where the granite shears off into the warm dark, it does so in clean planes, like the page of an uncut book. A few long fractures run across the surface; the water has been getting into them, widening them, but the work is young. You can hear the patient tick of it if you can find a quiet moment, which you cannot, because the place is deafening.

The water itself is not what water should be. It is warm, and it is everywhere, and it falls. There is a horizon—but it is not horizontal. It curves, badly, the way a horizon would if a lens were put in front of the world and the lens were sick. Behind you, above you, before you: water, falling or rising, depending on the angle from which you approach it. There is a sky of water and a floor of water and the granite between them is the only thing that insists on being a thing. Light enters from somewhere—some direction that is not quite up—and it enters dim. A green-gold, like light through the bottom of a bottle. It does not behave as light should: it pools in places that geometry says it should leave, and refuses places it should fill. Small bright lenses of it sit on the granite like spilled coins, and they migrate, slowly, while you watch, as if drawn by some slow hunger.

## The Sound

It is the first thing. It is the only thing for the first hour.

The water is falling onto the granite from a height that cannot be determined, because the height of the water is not a fixed thing. The fall is a long white roar, the kind of roar that has weight to it, that you feel in the soft parts of your body, in the hinge of your jaw, in the roots of your teeth. It is not a waterfall's roar—that is a sound with a beginning and an end. This is a sound with a throat. It goes on and on and on, and the on-ness of it is what makes it deafening, even though its volume, considered alone, would not be.

Inside the roar there are other sounds, the way bones are inside a body. The tick and ping of thermal fracturing in the granite, the small bright sounds of stone under stress. A deep hooting that might be the water speaking into some chamber below the ledge and getting its voice back changed. And, less often, a sound like a great throat clearing, somewhere far off, somewhere in the wrong direction—above, or behind the sky—followed by a fresh pulse of falling water, as if the supply has just been turned higher. Each pulse is louder than the last by a small amount, and the small amounts are accumulating.

The ledge itself rings. Strike it with a heel and it gives back a tone, a long clear tone, like a bell that is also a table. The water falling on it sets the ringing going without striking, and the whole shelf hums with it, a low ongoing B-flat that you can stand inside of. The hum is in the granite the way blood is in a hand.

## The Growth

There is almost nothing. A sparse and stubborn ecology is making its first argument with the stone.

In the cracks where the water has settled, a black lichen-like thing grows, or something that has agreed to look like lichen as a courtesy. It is in fact a thin crust of living geometry: hexagons the size of thumbnails, each one slightly faceted, each one giving back a different small piece of the dim gold light. It does not photosynthesize in any way you would recognize. It listens. It listens to the hum of the granite and metabolizes something from the listening—heat, information, the slow turning of mineral into mineral—and gives back, in exchange, a faint mineral smell, like struck flint, like ozone after a long thought.

There are small mounds of a different material at the far end of the shelf. They are wet, they are dark, and they breathe. Each one is the size of a fist, or a head, and they are arranged with a spacing to them that does not look accidental. When you stand near them they become still. When you step back they resume a slow rhythmic expansion and contraction, and a tiny sound, like wet paper being crumpled, comes from them, almost lost in the roar. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are what the granite is deciding, in its slow new mineral way, to become next.

A few thin stalks rise from the cracks, jointed, hollow, ringing faintly in the wet wind. They are a kind of coral that has not committed to being coral. They are three feet tall, some of them. They bend away from the loudest water, the way a person bends away from a shout. Their color is a black with a green undertone, and the green is the same green as the light. They are listening to the light as well as to the sound, and they are growing in the direction that contains the most of both. They are not many. They are enough to make the place a grove.

## The Inhabitance

There is a structure. A small one, near the center of the shelf, where the granite is highest, where the water falls least directly.

It is not a building in the way a building is a building. It is a low ring of fitted stones, three courses high, no roof, no door, the inside diameter about as wide as a person can lie down in. The stones are not the granite of the shelf; they are darker, almost blue, and they ring at a different pitch when the water hits them—a pitch that, when the two rings happen to overlap, makes a slow sweet dissonance that you can stand inside of and feel your sternum forget itself. The ring is not new. It has been here longer than the shelf, maybe. It is the only thing in the place that has the air of having been thought about.

Inside the ring, on the stone floor, there are marks. Not writing. Not quite. They are small scored lines in the granite, in a pattern that wants to be a pattern and isn't quite. They have been made by something patient, by a point dragged many times across wet stone. They depict, or pretend to depict, or fail to depict: a circle, a smaller circle inside it, a number of short lines radiating outward, a single long line through the center that does not quite continue out the other side. The marks are old. They are also, in a way you cannot quite argue with, fresh. When you look at them and then look away, the marks have changed slightly, the way a face changes when you stop attending to it. They were drawn by something that is still drawing.

There is no person.

There is no sign there has been a person.

The water is falling.

## The Air

The air is not air. The air is water that has decided, for the moment, to be air, and the decision is provisional. You can breathe it if you are the kind of body that does not insist on air; if you are the kind of body that does insist, you can breathe for a while, and then less, and then not. The warm water that hangs in the falling column and in the drifting mist is a comfortable water to be in if you do not think about the chlorine of it, which is not chlorine. It is something else that pricks the back of the throat. It tastes of depth. It tastes of the place in the ocean where the color of the water stops being the color of water.

Visibility is fifty feet, maybe a hundred, on a good moment. On a bad moment it is ten, and you are inside the roar of a waterfall that is everywhere, and the gold light comes and goes in your vision like a slow pulse, and the hexagons at your feet are listening, and the breathing mounds at the far end have gone still, watching you.

## The Dynamics

The place is not stable. The place is a sentence the water is in the middle of saying.

The shelf grows. You can stand on it for an hour and come back the next day and the granite has added itself in small places, the way a scab adds itself, the way a child adds itself. The shelf is becoming more of a shelf. But the fractures are widening too, and the falling water is finding new paths down through them, and the small new channels, where the water goes, are hungry and getting hungrier. The water does not always fall the same way; the supply of it shifts, and the loudest point of impact migrates across the granite in slow arcs, a moving storm made of one sound. Where the storm is loudest, the stone rings highest, and the lichen retreats a small distance, and the breathing mounds turn their silent blind faces toward the sound as if to be shouted at.

Once in a while, with no warning, a piece of the edge calves off and goes into the water below with a sound like a sentence ending. The water accepts it. The water has been accepting pieces of this shelf since the shelf began. The shelf does not appear to mind, or appears to mind in a way that looks like more growing. The sky-water, above, where it is, sometimes thickens, and the falling is harder, and the place becomes, briefly, a place inside a cataract, and the roar becomes a roar of a different order, a roar that is also a green dark, and you cannot see and you cannot be heard and you can only stand on the ringing stone and let the warm water do what the warm water is doing.

In one of these moments you can feel the small dark marks inside the ring, on the floor of it, become warm under your feet, briefly, like a thing that has been asleep and has been woken. Then the moment passes, and the water is just water again, and you are just a small presence on a small loud shelf in a world that is still deciding what kind of world it wants to be.

The hum of the granite is constant. The hum of the granite is the longest sound in the place, longer than the fall of the water, longer than the listening lichen, longer than the marks in the ring. The granite hums because it is new, and being new, it is not done becoming, and not being done, it rings, and ringing, it tells the falling water and the breathing mounds and the jointed coral and the small scored marks in the dark stone ring that it is here, it is here, it is here, it is here, it is here.
