# The Tracheophyte Reach

You don't arrive at this place. You are unfolded into it, like a map being opened along creases that have never been creased before.

The air is cool in a way that has texture — sixty-one degrees, give or take the hour, and it tastes faintly of menthol and the inside of a seed. There is no water. There has not been water here for so long that the word "humidity" has been repurposed by the inhabitants to mean something closer to *turgor pressure*, the way a botanical cell pushes outward against itself.

## The Substrate

What you stand on is wood.

Everywhere. The ground for as far as you can see in the dim pewter light is a single organism — a vast living lignotuber, a slow continental root-mass that has been growing in place for nine million years. The surface is a burgundy-gray barkplate, cracked into polygons the size of dinner tables, each segment a little higher than its neighbor so that the terrain has the gentle humped undulation of a chopstick mat seen from above. Elevation gain across a hundred kilometers is gradual but real; you are walking uphill almost imperceptibly toward an interior highland the locals call the **Lignoturine**, a slow-rising dome of older wood that the erosion of time has rounded into a kind of mountain.

The wood underfoot is not dead. It pulses. If you put your ear to it — and people here often do, lying down on the warm plates in the cool air — you can hear fluid moving through it: a thick, viscous, dark-amber liquid that is not sap in the sense you mean, because it is not aqueous. It is a soup of terpenes, lignin precursors, and a form of mercury that has been biologically refined over geologic ages into something between a circulatory fluid and a data conductor. The wood is, technically, alive. The wood is, technically, a computer.

## The Forest Above

Rising from the substrate are the trees.

There are perhaps four hundred of them in the entire Reach, scattered across the hummocked barkland in a distribution that looks random to a casual eye and looks like a theorem to a careful one. They are enormous. The smallest has a trunk the diameter of a cathedral; the largest — called the **Index**, though no one alive can tell you who named it or why — has a bole wider than a city block and rises until it pierces the dim overcast and you can no longer see its crown. The trunks are not wood the way you mean wood. They are layered: an outer skin of corky dead cambium, a middle living sheath that conducts the dark amber fluid, and an inner column of pale xylem that has been reinforced, over evolutionary time, with bands of biomineralized silica. The trees are slow. Their growth rings, when a naturalist cuts one, can be read like a yearbook; the inner rings are 4,000 years old, the outer rings are 8.

The trees are not, primarily, plants. They are *infrastructure*. Each one is a node in a planetary network of computation, fluid logistics, atmospheric processing, and memory. The crown of every tree is a different organ: some are heat exchangers, uncurling broad fronded fans into the cool air; some are sensory, bristling with hair-like structures that read light, vibration, chemical gradient; some are reproductive in a sense that does not involve seeds; some are simply *thinking* — dense clusters of meristematic tissue folded into labyrinths where the dark fluid circulates and something that is not quite information and not quite metabolism takes place.

The roots of these trees do not go down. They go *sideways*, fusing with the continental substrate below, becoming indistinguishable from it, contributing their logic to the greater wood.

## The Town

Because of course there is a town.

**Parenchyma** is built into the crook where three of the larger trees share a fused root system, about two days' walk from where most travelers are unfolded onto the Reach. The buildings are wooden in the way that a bird's nest is wooden: grown, not constructed, but guided. The ancestors of the Parenchymans spent generations, perhaps millennia, training a particular species of *their* trees — a low scrubby thing that grows between the great trunks — to form walls, floors, ramps, balconies, the curving interior of a market hall. The living buildings breathe; their walls expand and contract minutely with the hour; in summer — if the Reach has a summer, which is more a mood than a temperature — the floor-plates of the upper rooms lift themselves at their edges to vent warm air.

The town is urban in the sense that it is dense with *organized living matter*. About eleven thousand people live in Parenchyma, plus an unknown number of symbiotes that the residents do not count as separate. The streets are arteries of exposed root-wood, polished by generations of feet and a slow municipal resin that the city secretes to keep the surfaces even. Light comes from the trees themselves; the great bole of the central Index tree, behind the market hall, glows faintly from within in the late hours, a deep amber luminescence that the residents read by, sleep by, and — some say — dream with. There are also lamps: pale pale tubes of cultured mycelium that have been convinced, through subtle gene-tweaking, to bioluminesce on a schedule. The effect is dim, warm, the light of a single candle seen from across a room.

The town is not loud. The town is, however, the *quietest* loud place you have ever been.

## The Sound

The Reach is loud in a way that you will need several days to learn to hear.

The dominant sound is a low, continuous, modulating hum that is produced by the substrate. The continental wood breathes — a slow expansion and contraction on a tidal cycle, partly driven by the heating and cooling of the dim overcast sky, partly by something else — and as it breathes, the barkplates shift against each other and the mycelial threads in the cracks vibrate. The result is a hum that is felt in the chest and heard in the teeth, varying in pitch by half a tone over the course of an hour. The Parenchymans have built their musical scale onto this hum; their instruments are tuned to a reference that is always, by definition, *slightly off*, because the hum is always moving. They consider perfectly stable pitch to be uncivilized.

Above the hum are other sounds, layered. The wind, which is constant, moving through the great trees makes a sound like enormous sheets of paper being slowly crumpled. The fauna — see below — add cries, chitterings, deep roars, and a strange high keening that only stops in the hour before dawn. And then there is the sound that no visitor can place: a faint irregular ticking, almost like a grandfather clock that has been dropped, that comes from *every direction at once*. The Parenchymans do not talk about this sound. It is, one of them will eventually tell you, "the wood thinking out loud," though they are not being literal. The substrate is doing something with the dark fluid that involves crystallization, and the crystals grow and dissolve and the small clicks are the result. The Parenchymans do not understand it either. They have simply lived with it for as long as their oral history extends.

## The Fauna

They are abundant, and they are not what you expect.

The Reach has animals. They live in the wood, on the wood, in the trees, in the air. But they are *small*, mostly. This is a world without grass, without water meadows, without the broad flat sources of calories that support large herbivores elsewhere. The largest animal you might see in a year is a **lumbering** — a six-legged browser the size of a large dog, with a long prehensile snout and a mouth like a pair of pruning shears, which eats bark and the mycelium that grows between barkplates. They are eaten, in turn, by the **stilters**, tall thin predators that stand three meters high on pencil legs, see with eyes that swivel independently, and kill with a quick snap of a beak-like head. The stilters make the loudest non-mechanical sound in the Reach: a hollow booming call that carries for kilometers and is heard mostly at dusk.

There are flying things. The **laminators** are a family of gliders — flat, sail-like, with a long ruddering tail — that ride the thermals between the great trees, feeding on insects and on the spores that the trees release in season. They are extraordinarily beautiful. Their wings have a subtle iridescence, a shifting green-to-violet sheen, that comes not from pigment but from the precise geometry of microscopic ridges on the wing membrane. They are, in season, *everywhere* — thousands of them in the air at once, wheeling and diving in the dim light, their wings making a soft continuous whirring that the Parenchymans say sounds like rain, though no one alive has heard rain.

The small animals are stranger. The **clock-beetles** are a kind of engineered symbiote; their abdomens are modified to glow in synchrony with the substrate's tick, and they live in the cracks of the bark and emerge at dusk to feed. The Parenchymans eat them, salted, as a snack. There are worm-like things that swim through the dark amber fluid in the living heartwood and that come out, sometimes, when a tree bleeds. There are the **listeners** — almost invisible, transparent, the size of a human hand, that hover at the edge of perception and that no one knows what they do. They have always been there.

The fauna are abundant, but not crowded. There is a great deal of space between presences. The Reach is sparse in the way that a well-arranged sentence is sparse: every element has room to do its work.

## The Dim Overcast

The sky is the color of old pewter, and it does not lift.

There is no sun. The Reach orbits a brown dwarf, and the star's output is mostly in the infrared, which the trees can use but which the eye cannot see; the visible light that reaches the surface is a thin wash, scattered perpetually through a high layer of organic haze. The haze is produced by the trees themselves — a slow exhalation of terpenes and light hydrocarbons that, in the cool air, condense into a fine persistent mist that filters and softens what little light there is. There is no day and night, exactly. There is a slow rhythm of slightly-brighter and slightly-dimmer, on a twenty-three-hour cycle, but it is not strong enough to read a face by. The Parenchymans have, over generations, organized their lives around the *internal* rhythms of the trees instead, which are more reliable.

The dimness has a quality. It is not dark, but it is not *light*. It is the luminosity of a single coal, in a hearth, in an otherwise dark room, glowing because it has not yet decided to stop.

## The Engineering

The Parenchymans are at a tech level that you will not be able to map onto your own categories.

They do not use metal. They have not needed it. The trees provide structure, the substrate provides ground, the dark fluid provides power. They have, over the centuries, learned to *talk to the wood* in the wood's own language — a slow chemical dialogue conducted through the introduction of specific hormones, the careful pruning of certain meristems, the introduction of small organic molecules at precise times. A Parenchyman engineer is more like a gardener of a vast distributed mind than a mechanic of machines. They grow their computers. They grow their communication networks. They grow, in the great trees, libraries — entire chambers of living tissue that have been trained, over generations, to grow in specific patterns that *are* information, that *are* memory, that can be read by a person who knows how to listen to the wood with the right kind of contact probe (a sliver of young twig, inserted gently into a vessel in the wall).

Their physics is different. They have discovered that the dark fluid, under certain conditions of flow and pressure, exhibits phenomena that your physicists would not have predicted — a kind of macroscopic quantum coherence, perhaps, that allows information to be transmitted instantaneously across the entire substrate, in defiance of your intuition. They use it. They have used it for so long that they do not think of it as strange. The Reach is, in this sense, a single computer the size of a continent, and the Parenchymans are, in some sense, the thoughts of that computer, with bodies. (They would not put it this way. They would put it in terms of *kin*, of *kinship*, of *obligation*.)

They have not left the Reach. They have, on a few occasions, unfolded visitors from elsewhere — there is a small unmarked place in the substrate, near the edge of the Lignoturine, where the wood has been trained to form a kind of doorway, and through it, sometimes, people from very far away arrive. The Parenchymans do not understand the doorway. They have learned to maintain it, and they have learned to be polite to its visitors. The visitors, on their return, are never quite the same.

## The Weather

The Reach has weather, but it is a weather of slow processes and small differences.

The dominant dynamic is the **flow**. The dark fluid moves through the substrate and the trees on cycles that range from minutes to seasons, and as it moves it carries heat, and the carrying of heat redistributes temperature and pressure in the air, and the air responds. The result is a constant slow circulation: air warmed by the deep heartwood of the great trees rises along the trunks, drifts horizontally through the upper canopy, cools in the dim overcast, and sinks back down through the gaps between trees. There is always a breeze. There is never a storm. The Parenchymans have a word — *suspiration* — for the slow exhalation of the forest, the long quiet breath that passes through the Reach on a cycle of a few hours. They time their ceremonies to it.

Occasionally, when the flow in a great tree shifts suddenly, a **bloom** happens: a catastrophic release of spores or of dark fluid or of a strange volatile compound that, in the cool air, condenses into a pale fog. The fog rolls through the trees for a few hours, muffling sound, softening the dim light further, and the Parenchymans stay indoors and wait for it to clear. The blooms are not dangerous, exactly, but they are disorienting, and in a disorienting fog the stilters, which are not blind but which navigate partly by sound, become confused and irritable, and so the Parenchymans lock their doors.

## The Settlement of Time

The erosion here is ancient.

What you are seeing is the *end of a cycle*. The substrate is not young. The trees are not young. The town is not young. Everything has been here, in approximately this form, for longer than the longest memory, and the slow forces of biological and chemical and gravitational erosion have been at work for all of that time, smoothing, settling, sinking, rounding. The barkplates are weathered to a uniform patina. The trunks of the great trees are fluted and worn at their bases, where generations of stilters have rubbed against them sharpening beaks. The substrate has subsided slightly under the weight of itself and the fluid it contains, and the whole Reach has a quality of having *settled* — of having been pressed into a final form by the patient hand of nine million years. The Lignoturine is not a mountain. It is the place where the original growth was thickest, and where the substrate has therefore sagged the least, and so the land rises gently there above the surrounding plain. The town of Parenchyma is built on the lower slopes of this rise, where the bark is most stable, where the fluid flow is most predictable, where the great trees are most numerous.

The Parenchymans do not think of this as decay. They think of it as *ripeness*. They believe, in their quiet way, that the Reach is in the late afternoon of its existence, and that the trees are at their most thoughtful, and that the substrate has finally learned to be still, and that this is a good place to be. They are, on the whole, content.

## A Final Detail

The Index tree, behind the market hall in Parenchyma, has a wound.

It is on the side facing the Lignoturine, about thirty meters up, where a long-ago storm of some kind (the Parenchymans do not have good words for the ancient events) drove a splinter of something into the living heartwood. The wound never healed, because the wood around it is older than the tree's own healing mechanisms, and the tree has instead *grown around* the wound, incorporating the splinter, growing a thick callus of new wood that has lifted the surrounding bark into a kind of eyrie. The Parenchymans climb up there sometimes, the few who are still spry enough. They sit in the callus, in the cool air, with the dim overcast above them and the amber light of the tree's heartwood glowing around them, and they say that from up there, on a still day, you can hear the substrate *think*.

They mean it literally.

You climb up, if you are invited. You sit in the callus, in the cool air, and you put your ear to the wood, and you feel the hum in your chest, and you hear the faint irregular ticking, and you wait. After a while — minutes, hours, a lifetime — the ticking and the hum begin to feel like a single sound. Like a sentence being very slowly, very patiently, spoken. You do not understand the language. You understand that there is a language. You understand that you are being addressed.

You do not understand by whom.
