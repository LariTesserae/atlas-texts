# The Vespers

---

The road enters from the south and does not leave from the north, or it leaves from a north that has not yet been decided. The cartographer who drew this region — there was only one, and the map has been lost — would have had a great deal to drink about, afterward.

You stand on a low rise. Everything is low. The horizon is close. It presses in like a held breath, like the ceiling of a room that someone is breathing against. The land is a soup-tropic, a heat-soaked alluvial memory: silt for kilometers, pale as old bone, cracked into plates that fit together like the back of a slow beast. Between the plates, black water. Between the water, a kind of moss, or what calls itself moss. Between the calls, a silence with weight to it.

The town is called Coyle's Reach, though no one named Coyle has lived here in living memory. It is a town of nine hundred and forty-one — though if you ask three people you will get three different numbers, and all three will be wrong, and the wrongness will feel correct in a way that settles the matter. The houses are clapboard, mostly, weathered to the gray of a pigeon's wing. Some are brick. A few are made of a material the locals call *saltref*, which is a word you will not find in any dictionary, and which refers, roughly, to blocks of crystallized river-water that never quite melt, even in the worst of the heat. They ring faintly when struck.

The heat is the first fact. It sits on the skin like a second atmosphere. The sun — you assume it is the sun — is a thing you do not look at directly. It is the color of a wound. When it sets, it does not really set; it thins, and then it is gone, and the dark is not a dark but a *luminance*, a low blue phosphorescence that rises from the cracks in the earth and from the canals and from, sometimes, the walls of the houses themselves. People do not use lamps. There is nothing to light.

---

## The Wet

Water is everywhere, and nowhere. It seeps. It exudes. It does not rain here so much as the air *condenses into commitment*: in the afternoons, a heavy mist descends and is briefly rain and is briefly not-rain and is briefly a thing the locals call *the weeping*, during which the buildings run with water that smells of iron and lilac. The canals — and there are many canals, in a geometry that no one planned and no one has explained — are tidal without being tidal, rising and falling on a cycle of about an hour, regardless of the moon, which is, when it appears, a thing with three shadows.

A child throwing a stone into the central canal will not hear it splash. The water is too thick, or the stone is too slow. There is a local bet about this. The bet has been running for one hundred and forty years and the purse is now considerable and the matter remains unresolved.

## The Vegetation

There is not much. What there is, is *opinionated*.

A kind of tree — the locals call them *witnesses* — grows along the canals, in twos and threes. They are tall and pale and they lean, all of them, in the same direction, as if listening to something to the west. Their leaves are a desaturated green, almost gray, and they do not fall, but they *rescind*: one morning a leaf is simply not there, and the branch it was on has closed over the place it was, like a wound. The wood of the witness tree, when cut, is warm to the touch for the first three hours and cold after.

There are reeds. There is a creeping groundcover the color of old milk. There are flowers, very few, and they bloom only at the second hour of dark, and only for eleven minutes, and only along certain streets, and the streets along which they bloom change, seasonally, in a pattern that has been the subject of serious local study and much quiet weeping.

## The Sound

It is quiet here. This is a thing people say when they leave. *It was so quiet.* But the quiet is not empty. It has a texture, like felt, like the inside of a locked drawer. If you sit on a porch in the small hours, you will hear, beneath the silence, a *sighing* — not wind, not water, not the houses settling — a sighing, as if the town itself is breathing in its sleep, or as if something underneath the town is breathing *for* it. A woman named Tilde, who keeps the records at the back of the inn, told me once that she had timed the sighs. They came every seven minutes and twelve seconds. She had been timing them for thirty-one years. She said they had not varied.

There is also, sometimes, a *chiming*. It comes from the saltref buildings. No one knows why. It is not the wind. The wind is not the cause of anything here.

## The People

There are people, though one hesitates to use the word unreservedly. They are, on the whole, quiet. They are, on the whole, kind. They are, on the whole, *thinner than they should be*, as if the heat has been slowly paring them down to something essential, and the essential is, in many cases, quite slight. They have dark eyes, most of them, and a way of looking at you that suggests they are deciding whether to be sorry for you in advance.

They do not, as a rule, leave.

When you ask them why, they look at you. They do not answer immediately. Then one of them — an older one, usually — will say something like, *the roads out don't hold*, or *you'd have to go back through the marsh and the marsh isn't the same twice*, or *we tried it, my grandmother, she got as far as the ridge and she turned around and she said it was louder there, and she came back*. They do not elaborate. They are not being coy. They are telling you the truth, and the truth is the shape of a hand, and you must hold it however it sits in your palm.

There is a mayor. His name is Erren. He is a tall, slow-moving man who keeps a small white dog that does not bark and does not drink water and is, Erren assures everyone, *entirely real, thank you*. The dog is real. It is also, somehow, a small white door. These are not contradictions. This is Coyle's Reach.

## The Rules of the Place

— Do not look directly at the sun, even when it is dim, even when it is gone. The locals will not say what happens. They will only say *don't*.
— The canals are not for swimming. They are not for boats. They are not for *speaking across*. The light that rises from them at night is not a light you should examine too closely. (A boy named Sallow did, once. He is fine now. He is also a small grey canal, and he remembers being a boy, and the boys in town wave to him, and the light he gives off is, by general agreement, *gentle*.)
— If a flower blooms on your street, you are permitted to pick it. You are *not* permitted to keep it past morning. If you do, you will find that you have not picked a flower but *remembered* a flower, and the memory will not leave you, and you will not be able to describe the flower you have not picked to anyone, ever.
— The sighs do not speed up. Do not attempt to make them speed up. The woman who tried — *her name is no longer spoken; it is a name that has been breathed out of the air* — is why the canal at the south end of town now has a small island in the middle of it, and the island is, on closer inspection, a chair.
— When you meet a person in Coyle's Reach, do not ask them where they are going. They are not going anywhere. They are *arriving*, still, and they have been arriving for some time, and the arrival is, for many of them, almost complete.

---

## The Town at Night

I will describe the night, because the night is the town's true face, and the day is a courtesy.

The blue phosphorescence rises. The witness trees darken to a deep, improbable indigo. The canals brighten. The houses — all of them, even the clapboard, even the brick — begin, very faintly, to *breathe with light*, as if they have swallowed something luminous and are slowly digesting it. The sighs come, every seven minutes and twelve seconds. The flowers, on their appointed streets, bloom and are gone.

You can walk the town at this hour and you will not be alone. You will see figures on porches, in doorways, at the edges of the canals. They are the residents. They are doing what they always do at this hour, which is *witnessing*. They are witnessing the night. They are not speaking. They are not looking at one another. They are looking, all of them, at the *same thing*, though you cannot see what it is, though you have asked, though you have been told, gently, that you will see it when you are ready, and you are not ready, and that is fine, that is *entirely* fine.

The thing is real. They are not a cult. They are not mad. They are, in their way, the most *sane* people you will ever meet, and their sanity is the kind that has *removed itself from the world and gone to sit in a quieter one*, and the quieter one is here, and you are in it.

---

## Departure

You will want, at some point, to leave.

You will find, when you try, that the road south has changed its mind. Or the road is still there but the *south* is no longer where you remember it being. You will ask for directions. The locals will be helpful. They will send you east, or west, or to the canal, or to a house that is, you will be assured, *very close to the road out*. The house will not be close to the road out. The house will be close to something else. The something else will not be explained.

This is not malice. This is *civility*. They are being kind. They are keeping you, in the kindest way, where the sighing is, where the witnesses lean, where the canals keep their slow and patient hour.

You will stay. Not because you must.

Because the quiet, in the end, is *a kind of answer*, and you have been, for a long time, asking.

---

*There is an inn at the center of town. The innkeeper is a woman whose face you will not quite remember afterward. She will give you a room. The room will have a window. The window will look out on a canal, and a witness tree, and a stretch of road that is, she will assure you, the road to the south, and you will believe her, and in the morning, the road will not be there, and the canal will be, and the tree will be leaning, and you will go down to breakfast, and the eggs will be* very good, *and the coffee will taste faintly of iron and lilac, and you will not leave, and you will not, in the end, want to.*
