# The Upper Reach

The valley is about nine kilometers long and something over a kilometer across at its widest, and from the eastern shoulder — where the ridge falls away in a clean curve of shattered gneiss — almost all of it is visible at once. It sits high, well above the conifer line, in the long cold band where the trees have given up and the rock has not yet remembered how to be soil. The sky above it is the blue of polar seas, almost depthless. Down in the basin the light does not fall so much as arrive, pressed flat by altitude, then thrown back from every wet surface with a steady, mechanical brilliance that hurts the eyes even through closed lids.

It is bright here all day. Not the warmth of bright, because there is not much warmth — the air, even in midsummer, moves around five or eight degrees — but the *quantity* of bright, the pure photon load of a sun closer than it ought to be, filtered through almost nothing. Shadows from the clouds race across the valley floor at the speed of weather, but the brightness between the shadows is so constant it feels structural, like a second substrate.

## The bones

Walk down off the ridge and the footing changes within a few hundred meters. Above, the ridge is raw rock: pale, sharp, recently broken. There are no cobbles here, no rounded edges. The stones fracture along clean planes. A hammer would ring off them. They have the bleached, almost greenish look of stone that has been buried under ice for a long time and only lately turned toward the sun. The high places — the lateral moraines that rib the valley sides, the tumbled blocks at the foot of the cliffs, the erratics the size of houses that lie where they were dropped — still carry this rawness. The valley is emerging. Its bones are barely older than the people who first came up to look at them.

The moraines are the dominant architecture. They run as long parallel ridges down each side of the valley, and where the central tongue was, the floor is a confusion of low hills, kettle holes, and the long shallow gouges where subglacial water once ran. None of this has been sorted yet by a river. There is no single channel. The meltwater is everywhere and so the sorting has begun, but only begun — the streams braid and braid again, lose themselves in gravel, reappear, change their minds.

## The green

And over this whole new architecture, growing on every stable surface, the plants have come in. Not as decoration. As substrate. As floor. As wall. As the second geology.

They came in the moment the ice pulled back, the way they always do. First the cyanobacteria and the crust lichens, drawing nitrogen from bare rock, staining the gneiss with their pale orange maps. Then the foliose lichens, the grey-green *Cetraria*, the tufted *Cladonia* that look like tiny dead forests. Then the mosses, which is when the surface began to soften. The mosses are thick here — *Racomitrium* in harsh cinnamon mats, *Sphagnum* in pale wet cushions that drink everything that falls on them. They have climbed the boulders. They have wrapped the smaller stones until you cannot find stone at all without scraping.

Above the moss, the higher plants. Alpine grasses in grey tussocks, knee-high and as dense as horsehair. *Dryas octopetala* with its white flowers and its tough little leaves, mat-forming, mat-expanding. Dwarf willows no taller than your hand, creeping, woody at the root, indifferent to the wind. *Salix herbacea*, *Salix reticulata*, their leaves no bigger than a thumbnail. Sedges, saxifrages, the occasional gentian so blue it looks printed. There are patches of *Loiseleuria*, the trailing azalea, in tight rings where it has killed everything else and now simply persists, year after year, in its own acid carpet.

The result is that the valley floor is no longer a valley floor. It is a continuous organism, or rather a federation of them. Where you walk, you are not on rock; you are on a thing that is alive. Where you stand still for a moment, you can feel, through your boots, the faint cool respiration of a peat that has been growing, in places, for less than a century and yet already has the heft of an older world. The plants are total here — total in coverage, total in their grip on the substrate, total in the way they have made themselves the ground. Only the moving water, only the bare cliff faces, only the polished slabs that are too smooth for anything to hold onto, only the machines — only these remain uncovered.

## The wet

And the water is everywhere. It comes down out of the hanging cirques in pale threads that you can barely see against the rock, and it lands on the moss and disappears. It rises through the peat in dark, slow seeps that spread across the surface as a sheen and then thicken. It condenses on every metal surface — and there is a great deal of metal — in beads so fine they look like a rash, then runs down in tracks. It hangs in the air itself; in the early morning and again toward evening, a fog comes down the valley and sits in it like a second, quieter occupant. When the wind drops, you can hear the water everywhere: tapping on metal, dripping from leaves, trickling between stones, gurgling in the kettle holes, whispering down the moraines. The whole valley is in conversation with itself about the water.

The streams are the color of skim milk where they enter the basin — *glacier flour*, the silt ground off the bedrock by ice that is still up there, retreating, even now. By the time they reach the lower third of the valley they have cleared, and you can see the orange of iron precipitate on the stones, the white of calcite, the bright green of algae that is happy here. The water table, they told the men who built the station, is essentially at the surface. Dig anywhere and you hit it within a hand's depth. The plants have known this for a long time. They sit in it the way a sponge sits in a basin.

## The machines

But the men were here before the plants.

The station sits in the middle of the basin, low, grey, mostly concrete, with a corrugated-metal roof and very few windows. From the ridge it looks like a piece of the moraine that took a wrong turn. There are penstocks coming down the eastern wall — three of them, each a meter and a half across, in bolted steel, dropping almost vertically for two hundred meters before they bend and run along the contour to the machines. From the penstocks, when the station is online, a fine mist comes off them and falls across the slope, and where it falls the moss is darker and the willows are taller, and the *Dryas* flowers earlier.

Downstream of the station is the tailrace, a straight concrete channel, and beyond it the river takes up its natural braiding again. There are transformers in a fenced yard, humming audibly from fifty meters away. There is a single-track funicular that climbs three kilometers up to the intake reservoir, with cars the size of small rooms, clanking slowly up and slowly down. There is a maintenance road of crushed gneiss, mostly grown over now, that runs the length of the valley. There are power lines, but only the lower sections of the towers — past a certain elevation they go underground, into the rock, because above a certain height the wind is simply too much for them.

The machines are not many. They do not need to be. Each one is large. The turbines are the size of small houses and turn at a speed that makes the floor of the powerhouse throb under your feet. The sound is below the level where you hear it cleanly; it enters the body another way, through the chest, through the teeth, through the bones of the skull.

## The sound

And this is the valley's true weather. Not the rain, not the wind — the sound.

It is constant. From the intake reservoir down to the tailrace, from the penstocks on the eastern wall to the transformers in the yard, the valley rings. The turbines make a low, dense note that you stop noticing only because the body stops protesting it. The penstocks make a higher note, more like a metal song, and it varies with the load. The transformers make the kind of buzz that you would, in another place, mistake for the buzz of an insect, except that it does not stop and it is the size of a room. The funicular clanks, pauses, clanks. The wind, which is essentially always present at this altitude, moves through the cable lines and makes them sing in a key that shifts with the gust. The water itself — the falling water at the weirs, the water in the channels, the water over the rocks — adds its own ongoing percussion, layered and unceasing.

In the morning, when the fog is down and the machines are running at night baseload, the whole valley becomes a single instrument. You can stand on the moraine and the sound will come up at you from below and come down at you from above and meet somewhere around your sternum. There is no quiet anywhere. There is no place in this valley you could go and hear nothing. Even at the very top of the eastern ridge, the turbines are still audible, a felt thing in the rock under your boots, the rock itself conducting it.

The birds that nest here — and there are some, a few wheatears, an occasional ring ouzel — have learned to sing inside the noise. They sing louder. They sing higher. A human voice at conversational level cannot be heard across twenty meters.

## The quiet things

And yet the place is sparse. Walk the valley for an hour and you will see, in that whole time, perhaps a single fox moving along a moraine ridge at a fast trot, a snow bunting flushed out of a tussock, a butterfly — there's always one or two — riding the wind without effort. There is, somewhere in the peat, a vole, but you will almost certainly not see it. There may be ptarmigan, but they are cryptic and white and they do not want to be found. The fauna is rare here, and getting rarer as you climb. The machines are loud, but they do not displace many animals, because there were not many to displace. The plants, similarly, are total — every surface colonized — but the *biomass* is low. This is not a forest. It is a thin, persistent, almost geological living skin.

So the valley is at once crowded and empty. Crowded with life, with sound, with water, with light. Empty of animals, empty of people most of the time, empty of any large movement or change. The men who run the station are up here in shifts, and when they are here they are inside, listening to the machines, watching gauges, eating food they brought with them, sleeping in a dormitory that vibrates gently all night.

## Time

Time moves slowly here. That is the deepest fact about the valley, deeper than the sound, deeper than the wet, deeper than the bright.

The machines have been running, in some form, for longer than the oldest moss currently growing on their concrete foundations. They will run, more or less unchanged, for longer than the *Dryas* mat outside the door will take to advance another half meter across the gravel. The glacier above the intake is still losing mass, year on year, and the engineers keep raising the weir a little, and the moss keeps following the waterline up. The lichen crusts on the highest erratics grow fractions of a millimeter per year and have been doing so, on the same stones, for the entire industrial era. The kettle holes are filling, slowly, with peat. A few of the smaller moraines are being eaten, slowly, by the lateral streams. The cliff faces shed rock, sometimes; once or twice a summer, a fracture releases, and the sound of the fall arrives a beat after the pressure-wave, and then is folded into the turbine song as if it had always been there.

Nothing is in a hurry. The brightness is constant. The wetness is constant. The sound is constant. The plants are constant in their advance. The ice is constant in its retreat. The machines are constant in their turning.

You can stand here on the moraine, in the middle of the basin, in the long cool light, in the unceasing wash of water and metal and wind, and feel a place that is, in some deep sense, *finished* — already running, already operating, the only remaining events being the slow adjustments of an enormous machine that has all the time in the world.
