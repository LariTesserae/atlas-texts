# The Tinkertown at the River's Knee

The river comes down from somewhere north that no one in town has ever cared to name, and it bends hard here, a sharp knee-bend where the current strikes a long limestone shelf and splits around it. That shelf is the reason for everything else. You can stand on it, when the water is low, and see the shape of the place: a low, wet bowl, cupped between the shelf and a rise of jungle to the south, with the river forming the eastern boundary and a chain of slag-mounds and spoil-heaps forming the western one. The bowl is roughly the size of a cathedral, if you measure it from the breached wall of the old foundry to the crumbling lip of the cistern above. About that size. Not much bigger.

The light is the first thing you notice, or rather the *lack* of it. Even at midday the sun has to fight its way down through a ceiling of vapor that rises off the river and off the cooling vats and off the sweating brick of every standing wall. Everything here lives in a kind of permanent dusk, a luminous gray-green murk that softens edges and eats shadows. Torches and gas-jets keep burning even in summer. The foundrymen have learned to read the hour by the shift-changes on the long bridges rather than by the sun, which they sometimes go weeks without seeing directly.

The town — if it is a town; there are perhaps eight hundred souls, though no one has ever counted them twice — climbs up the slag-heaps in tiers. The lowest tier, nearest the river, is the wettest: streets that are more like canals, crossed by planks and stepping-stones, where the air is a warm, dripping curtain and the walls run with condensation from morning to night. Children punt between doorways. The middle tier, cut into the old spoil-heaps themselves, is the densest: a warren of brick kilns, smelting sheds, drying-rooms, and the tenements of the pourers, all linked by iron walkways that ring underfoot when the great hammers in the foundry below fall. The highest tier, up near the cistern, is mostly the counting-houses and the dormitories of the senior molders, and it is the only place where the air tastes of anything but river-steam and scorched iron.

Vegetation has had the bad manners to follow the people in. It grows out of every horizontal surface that is not scraped daily by a shift-boss. Mosses, ferns, a stubborn cane that the locals call *iron-grass* because nothing will eat it and it will grow through a brick. There are figs along the southern rise, dropping their fruit into the foundry's runoff; there is a single great kapok tree at the very center of the bowl, on a tiny island of original ground that no one has ever built on, because the soil beneath it is reputedly cursed and because the tree is older than the river's current course. Its roots make a low mound, and at high water the mound becomes an island, and at low water it becomes a peninsula, and at all times it is the green heart of a place that is otherwise a study in rust and soot.

The sound is what defines it. There is no quiet hour. The hammers in the foundry fall at a steady rhythm, somewhere between a heartbeat and a funeral march, and they have not stopped in living memory — the pourers say the molds will crack if the hammers stop, that the metal "remembers" the beat and will not set properly without it. On top of the hammers, layer by layer: the hiss of the steam-pipes that run between the sheds; the constant low moan of the bellows at the great furnaces; the crash and slosh of the river against the shelf; the high, thin singing of the vent-stacks; and beneath it all, a deep, almost subliminal groan from the slag-heaps themselves, which are still settling, still giving under the weight of the town built on them, and which shift visibly in the night. A visitor from a quieter country would be, within an hour, half-mad. The locals do not hear any of it. They speak to one another by leaning close and mouthing against the ear, and they have a sign-language for use on the bridges.

There are no animals. This is worth marking, because the place is lush enough, and wet enough, and warm enough, that one would expect a riot of them — frogs at least, insects certainly, the bright quick things that thrive in heat. There are none. The molds and the slags leach something into the water, and the water feeds the cane and the mosses, and the cane and the mosses accept it, but the animals that have tried to settle here have sickened and gone. The dogs the founders brought with them in the first generation lasted perhaps a decade; the cats lasted two. There are pigeons on the cistern, but they are a peculiar local breed, soot-colored, that never coo, and no one is certain they are quite pigeons. The river, when it is fished, yields a pale, soft-boned fish that no naturalist has ever named, which tastes of nothing and is eaten anyway because it is protein and the pourers need protein.

What the town makes, in its foundries, is unclear even to the townspeople. The ore comes down the river on barges; the bars go back up on the barges; the bargemen themselves, who are from somewhere else and are the only people who regularly leave, will not say what the bars are for. The pourers speak of "the work" with a respect that is not quite religious and not quite fearful. Whatever it is, it is heavy, and it has to be poured in shaped molds that are broken open and never reused, so there is a constant supply of broken mold-rubble going out along with the slag, and the spoil-heaps grow a little each year, and the town creeps a little further up its own refuse.

The strange thing — and the place is strange, in the way that a fever-dream is strange, in that all the pieces fit but the whole is slightly off — is the light from the furnaces at night. The foundry never closes, and so at night the bowl fills with a deep orange glow that rises through the steam and turns the vapor into a kind of luminous fog. From the slope above, a traveler would see what looks like a small caldera, a volcano in miniature, breathing. Inside, the streets run with slow channels of cooling water, the slag-mounds glow faintly at their cores, and the great kapok at the center is lit from below like a lantern. The figures that move between the sheds are silhouettes only, and they move to the beat of the hammers, and they do not speak, and the hammers do not stop, and the river goes on bending around the shelf that made it all possible, and the air is thick, and warm, and wet, and loud with a sound that has been going on longer than anyone alive can remember.
