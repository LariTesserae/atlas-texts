# The Bellows Cathedral

The water comes first. It always comes first here.

It presses in from above through a hundred grated openings in the ceiling, a continuous torrent that drops fourteen meters into the basin below, hammering the surface into a permanent white froth. The sound of it is the sound of the place — a percussive, roaring, sustained thunder that lives in your sternum and never lets you forget it. You learn to speak in shouts, or to stop speaking altogether. The water is cold, river-cold, mineral-cold, carrying the taste of deep stone. It has been falling for longer than anyone can articulate, fed by aqueducts and sluices and pressurised mains that thread back into hillsides nobody visits anymore.

You are standing in the keel of something vast. The chamber — *Cathedral* is the only word that fits, and the locals use it without irony — is roughly the length of three city blocks and a quarter that in width, walled in riveted iron plate and mortised granite. The plates have been there so long that they have become the geology. Rust has bloomed into patterns, into lacework, into textures that look almost intentional, like the slow calligraphy of a very old and very tired scribe. The granite between the plates has gone dark and slick, colonised by a blackish lichen that pulses faintly if you watch it long enough — though that might be the eye adjusting to the light.

The light is the second thing you notice, after you stop flinching from the sound. It does not arrive. It *trickles* in — pale, grey-green, filtered through the falling water and the high grated ceiling, so diffused that shadows have no edges. Things further than forty meters away are suggestions, smudges. The far wall of the Cathedral is a rumour. The vault above is a smudge of darker grey. There is no horizon in any direction; only a graded fading, like a drawing done in soot and then breathed on.

The basin itself is maybe a hundred meters across and irregular in shape — a working shape, not a designed one, the outline of which has been redrawn and re-excavated a dozen times over the centuries. The water in it is waist-deep in the shallows, unfathomably deep in the pits, and it is full of life.

The fauna is what surprises visitors, if any ever come. It should not survive. By every reasonable expectation, a flooded, light-starved, perpetually battered iron chamber should be a dead place. Instead it teems. Long pale fish, almost eyeless, shoal in the shallows where the falling water oxygenates the surface — they move in coordinated silver clouds that shift and re-form like weather. Crabs the size of dinner plates, rust-orange and patient, cling to the walls above the high-water mark and wait for whatever the water brings down. Something like an eel but thicker, lazier, with a downturned mouth, drifts in the deeper channels and surfaces occasionally to inhale through a nostril that breaks the water with a soft *plop*. Mussels cluster on every submerged surface in blue-black mounds, filtering the falling torrent with grim efficiency. A pale, hair-thin algae climbs the lower walls in summer — the only vegetation in the entire Cathedral — and vanishes again in winter, leaving the iron bare.

It is an ecology. It is a feeding chain. The mussels feed the crabs. The crabs feed the eel-things. The fish feed on everything smaller than themselves, and on each other when the shoals tighten. It runs, balanced, on nothing but the water and the light that the water brings. A closed system except for the one great input: the constant, mechanical, engineered rain.

The machinery.

Now we come to the reason for the place, the thing that holds the rest of it together.

The water is not falling for its own sake. It is falling because it has been *made* to fall. High in the vaulted ceiling — invisible from the floor, lost in the dim upper reaches — great pistons work. You can hear them through the roar: a slower rhythm beneath the faster one, a heartbeat of iron. *Clunk. Hiss. Clunk. Hiss.* The water is drawn from somewhere deep, from a reservoir or a river or an underground lake that the original builders tapped and never released, and it is pumped up and up and up through a forest of pipes that line the walls, and from those pipes it is released, continuously, through the grated ceiling, into the basin, to fall again, to be caught in channels at the basin's edges, to be drawn down through gratings in the floor, and to be pumped up again. A loop. A perpetual motion of cold fresh water, kept alive only by the engines.

The engines are old. They are older than anyone who tends them. They are so old that the question of who *built* them has become theological — the locals speak of the First Engineers with the same cadence other people reserve for saints. The engines run on combustion. Something burns in their bellies — coal, oil, a black tar that oozes from certain seams in the lower walls — and the heat of it rises through flues that have turned the upper ironwork a deep, sooty red. You can see the warmth from below: a faint shimmering above the engine-houses, which are four squat structures that rise from the basin floor on iron legs, their working parts just above the waterline. They are the only dry ground in the Cathedral, and the only place anything can be built.

On those platforms, the work happens.

There are perhaps sixty people here, in shifts that never quite end. They are the tenders, the fire-watchers, the greasers, the inheritors. They wear oilcloth and rubber boots and carry their tools in leather satchels that have been repaired so many times they are more patch than original. They speak a dialect that has borrowed from several languages and has no written form. They do not know why the engines must keep running. They only know that if they stop, the water stops falling, and the ecology dies, and the Cathedral becomes a flooded tomb. So they keep the fires stoked. So they watch the gauges — copper dials mounted on the engine-houses, their needles trembling with the pulse of the machinery. So they descend, on ropes and ladders, into the lower chambers where the great flywheels turn in the dark, and they oil the bearings with long-spouted cans, and they listen for the sound that would tell them a bearing is going dry.

That sound, they say, is the only one louder than the water.

The water has been falling for so long that the iron plates of the walls have been worn into undulations, soft ridges and hollows like the surface of a sea-frozen-then-melted-frozen-again over geological time. This is the *ancient* quality of the place — the way the constructed has become, through sheer duration, indistinguishable from the natural. The rust is geology. The algae is biology. The mussels are geology. The fish are weather. The engines are the sun. The tenders are whatever keeps the sun above the horizon.

You stand in the basin. The water is at your knees. The roar is everything. The pale fish part around your legs and re-form behind you. A crab watches you from a wall-ledge, motionless, waiting out the centuries. High above, in a darkness you cannot see into, another piston falls, and another torrent joins the rain.

It will not stop. It was not built to stop. It has been running long enough that stopping has become unimaginable — a heresy, an apocalypse, a contradiction in terms. The Cathedral was not built for a purpose. It has outlived its purpose. It runs now because it runs, because the tenders feed the fires, because the mussels filter the water, because the fish eat the mussels, because the eel-things eat the fish, because the crabs eat what the eel-things leave, because the water falls, because the engines turn, because the tenders feed the fires.

You stand in the loop. The loop holds you. The loop is the place.
