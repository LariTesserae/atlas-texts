# The Tin Cavern

The descent begins where the drainage grate meets the rest of the world, and from there everything is interior. Above — felt rather than seen — a city weighs on the rock ceiling, its older buildings sending wooden pilings down through clay, through sand, through the first fractured layer of limestone, until they meet something that refuses to give. The pilings stop. The cellars continue.

It is not a large place. A person walking briskly could cross the great chamber in four minutes; the side passages take a minute or two apiece, and there are perhaps a dozen of them. None of them lead anywhere. None of them were built to lead anywhere. They are simply the shape the place has settled into, the way a sleeping animal curls.

The walls are the color of dull pennies, and they weep. A thin film of water moves over every surface, gathering in shallow channels carved into the floor, pooling in the hollows where foot-worn stone has dipped over the decades. No one carved those channels — they were cut, slowly, by the boots of people walking the same paths over and over, hundreds of thousands of paces, while the water softened the stone under them. Some of the channels are deep enough now that you can lose a fingertip in them. The water is cold. It always feels like it has just come from somewhere deeper.

The light is bad. It comes from somewhere — from vents in the ceiling, from phosphorescence in the puddles, from the dull orange of lanterns hung at intervals — but it does not commit to anything. Shapes look softer than they should. The far wall of the main chamber is always slightly uncertain. When you stop trying to look at it, you think you can see it clearly; when you look, it retreats.

The sound is what you notice, in the end. Even in the side passages, you can hear the great chamber. It is not musical. It is the noise of many small things happening at once: water dripping from a dozen stalactites onto a dozen surfaces, each pair making a slightly different note, the differences smearing into a kind of chord that never resolves because the drops never quite synchronize. Hammers, somewhere far off — rhythmic, patient. Voices, also far off, too indistinct to make out words, only the rise and fall, the murmur of people who have been saying these particular murmurs for a long time. A deeper sound, intermittent: stone settling, or a cart being dragged somewhere you cannot see, or both.

There are people here, though not many. Perhaps forty in the main chamber at any given time, fewer in the side rooms. They are not visitors. They are workers, or they are artisans, or they are tending to some task that takes generations. They wear layered cloth, dark, stained dark with the constant damp. Their tools are simple — chisels, hammers, wooden wedges, small bronze knives that they keep oiled against the rust. They have shaped parts of this place, over years and decades: a few low walls that divide one zone from another, a stone bench here, a wooden platform there, a chest-high pillar onto which a lantern is fixed. The constructions look almost geological themselves, as if they grew.

They are working in tin. That is what the place is for. The rock walls, in places, are laced with veins of it — dull, silver-grey, surprisingly heavy when a chunk is prized loose. They mine it with the patience of people who know there is no hurry, that the vein will not run out this year or next year, that their children will work the same faces. The broken ore goes into sacks, into small wheeled carts, into the smelting room at the back of the western passage, where the air is always a little warmer and a little more dangerous to breathe. They pour the molten metal into shaped stone molds. The molds are themselves remarkable: carved with low relief, with faces, with repeating patterns that look as if they mean something but that no one you ask can quite explain. Some of the older molds are centuries old. They are still used. The newer ones are made in the style of the older ones. Nothing here is in a hurry to be original.

No plants. Nothing green. Nothing that grew. A few things that might once have been alive, calcified or mineralized, embedded in the rock — a fern-frond made of pale stone, a shell-shape in the wall — but they are fossils, not living things, and no one pays them any attention. The world here is mineral, and what is not mineral is the people and what they have made.

The animals are scarce. There is a dog, old and grey, who follows a particular woman without being told. There are rats in the ceiling, but you hear them more than you see them. Something lives in the deepest of the side passages, by the sound of it — a slow dragging, a breathing — but no one goes that way, and no one speaks of what might be there.

The atmosphere is cool and humid in a way that gets into clothing. You are never quite dry. The stone sweats on you. Your breath does not quite fog, but you can feel the weight of water in each inhalation. After an hour, your fingers prune. After a day, you begin to understand that this place has been like this for longer than anyone remembers, and that it will be like this after everyone currently in it is gone.

Nothing changes. The drops do not speed up or slow down. The light does not strengthen or fade. The walls do not shift. The miners do not strike new galleries; they work the old ones. The smelters do not invent new methods; they use the methods their masters used. The lanterns burn at the same steady dimness they have burned at for as long as anyone alive can recall. The water on the walls does not rise or fall with any season, because there are no seasons down here, only the same unchanging cool, the same unchanging damp, the same unchanging dim. It is not a frozen place, exactly — it is a place that has found its equilibrium, and intends to keep it.

When you leave, which you can do at any time by climbing back up through the drainage grate and into the wet street above, you find yourself blinking in the daylight, surprised by the wind, half-convinced for the rest of the afternoon that you can still hear, just under everything else, the patient dripping of tin-mine water onto worn stone.
