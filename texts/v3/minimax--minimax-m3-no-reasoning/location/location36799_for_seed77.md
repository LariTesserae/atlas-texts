# The Continental Crust

It is a place that has been built.

Not assembled in any way you'd recognize — no quarried stone, no fired brick, no tool-marks anywhere — but built nonetheless, in the manner that coral builds reefs or that a forest builds its floor, layer by infinitesimal layer, over timescales that make geology look impatient. The plain stretches in every direction until the curvature of something enormous begins to suggest itself at the horizons, a faint suggestion that you are standing on the surface of a thing rather than in the middle of one. The plain is hot. The plain is damp in patches. The plain is, by every available measure, very young.

You are standing on a kind of skin.

---

The substrate is wrong. That is the first thing you notice, even before you understand what you're noticing. It is not rock in any familiar sense, not soil, not sand. It is something between ceramic and leather — a continuous pale crust, faintly ridged, faintly patterned in hexagonal whorls that are too regular to be wind, too soft to be mineral. If you kneel and press your palm to it, it is warm. Warmer than the air should make it. There is a pulse under your hand that you decide must be your own, except it doesn't quite keep your tempo.

When you stand again, the imprint of your knees is there. Not as dust. As a slight discoloration, a whitening where the surface has been compressed. In a few minutes the color returns. In a few hours the texture will return. The plain is forgiving in this way. It remembers everything and forgives everything, and it does not appear to mind.

---

There is no animal life. None. This is not a place that has been emptied of animals; it is a place that has never known them, and the absence has a particular quality to it, a hush that is more than the absence of sound. There are no flies. There are no worms. There are no beetles turning in the dust. When a small shower passes across the plain in the late afternoon — the kind that arrives as a gray veil on the horizon and leaves as quietly as it came, having only dampened the surface and set the small pools gleaming in the hollows — there is nothing to scatter from it. The rain simply falls. The plain simply receives.

What there is, instead, is the substrate itself, which is doing everything.

---

Look closely, eventually, and you can see the architecture.

The plain is not flat. It only appears flat. There are low rises — broad, gentle, tens of meters high and kilometers across — that have a quality of being shaped rather than accumulated. Their slopes are too even. Their shoulders are too consistent. At the crest of each rise, a darker crust emerges, almost black in the wet air, knobbly and ancient-looking, and from this crest the slopes descend in stepped terraces that are not quite terraces — more suggestions, more habits in the surface, the way a fabric might learn the shape of what lies beneath it.

Between the rises are the flats. The flats are damp more often than not. The water comes from somewhere you can't trace — a faint upwelling, a condensation that gathers in the cooler hours and never quite evaporates in the heat of midday. The flats are stippled with shallow pools, irregular, the size of dinner tables or small rooms, their edges defined not by any berm but by the simple fact that the substrate here is denser and holds the water differently. The pools reflect the sky so cleanly that walking across the flats is a small hallucinatory exercise — your feet in pale crust, your head in clean blue, the doubled image of yourself sliding beneath you.

The pools are warm. Touching one is like touching tea that has been left to cool almost but not quite all the way. There is a smell to them — mineral, clean, almost metallic, with something underneath that you might call biological if you had a word for a biology that built itself into being rather than grew.

---

The temperature is the constant thing. Even with the sun low, even after a rain, the air holds its warmth like a deliberate choice. Mornings begin already warm. By midday the crust is too hot to touch for more than a second, and the air above it shimmers in a way that makes distance impossible to judge. Things you thought were a kilometer away turn out to be three. Things you thought were three turn out to be a feature of the shimmer itself. The plain is honest in most things but lies about distance when the sun is high.

The heat does not seem to distress anything, because there is nothing here that can be distressed in the ways you are accustomed to. The substrate does not wilt. The pools do not complain. The faint dark structures at the crests of the rises do not seek shade. Whatever process built this place, it built it for this heat. The heat is part of the recipe.

---

Vegetation, in the conventional sense, is almost absent. What you find instead are thin crusts — powdery, ochre, lichen-like — that occupy the cooler slopes and the undersides of small overhangs in the terraced rises. They are not plants. They have no leaves, no stems, no roots you could pull. They are more like a discoloration that has chosen its locations with intent: always the north-facing sides of rises, always the leeward edges of pools, always the crevices where condensation gathers and lingers. They do not appear to compete with anything. They simply appear where they are useful, and they do not spread.

In a few places, very few, there are upright structures — pillar-like, perhaps two or three meters tall, sometimes single, sometimes in clusters of three or four — that have a vaguely plant-like habit but are clearly not plants. They are made of the same crust material as the ground, but layered thicker, denser, almost woody in cross-section. They do not move. They do not grow while you watch them. But the edges of their layered surfaces are slightly different in color from their cores, suggesting that they have been added to, slowly, on a schedule you will not live long enough to verify. They might be a kind of fruiting body. They might be ventilation. They might be a byproduct of whatever chemistry is happening under the crust, the way a cooling tower is a byproduct of a power plant. You cannot tell. They stand there in their sparse clusters, four or five kilometers apart, like markers placed by something that has long since moved on.

---

The plain is not silent. That would imply a baseline of noise that has been removed. There is simply very little to make noise with.

What you hear, when the wind is calm, is the substrate. A faint, continuous ticking — not regular, not patterned, but present, as if the crust is settling, expanding, breathing in increments too small to see. When you put your ear to it, the ticking resolves into something almost granular, like sand falling a very long way through a very narrow space. You cannot find the source. You only find more of it.

When the wind moves, you hear the wind, but the wind here has a different voice. It moves over a surface that is not smooth but is also not rough in the usual way; it produces a low, almost musical hum at certain angles, a kind of throat-singing that comes and goes as the air lifts over the rises. It is not loud. It is the loudest thing here.

When a pool is disturbed — by your foot, by a rare falling of small crust-fragments from a rise above — there is the small wet sound of the disturbance, and then a long absence of any wet sound at all. The water here is patient. It accepts the disturbance and does not ripple for long.

---

The light is normal light. The sun rises, the sun sets, the shadows fall in the conventional directions and at the conventional speeds. There is nothing strange about the way the place is illuminated, and this is one of the things that makes it strange — that an environment so otherwise odd is lit so plainly, so that every detail is offered up to you without obscuration or drama. In the mornings the crust glows faintly ochre. At noon it goes white. In the long golden hours before sunset it takes on a pink that you would call beautiful if you were in the mood to call anything beautiful, which here you mostly are.

The shadows of the pillar-clusters are long and clean. The shadows of the rises are long and broken, falling across the terraced steps in a way that reveals the terracing — the gentle, almost imperceptible engineering of the slopes — as clearly as if it were drawn for you. The plain, in the right light, will show you what has been done to it. In the wrong light it pretends to be featureless.

---

You begin to notice, after a time, that the place is active.

Not in any dramatic way. There are no migrations, no blooms, no sudden stirrings. But the pools shift their edges between visits. The dark crust at the rise-crests appears, over weeks, to have advanced a few centimeters down the slopes, layering new pale material over the old. The small powdery crusts in the cool crevices are thicker than you remember. A rise you passed on your first day, walking across it took half an hour; passing it again later, the slope is fractionally different in grade, as if something has been added beneath your feet while you weren't watching.

A rain comes. The pools swell. When the rain stops, the new water does not simply evaporate — it is absorbed, taken down into the substrate, and for a day afterward the crust is darker than usual, faintly damp to the touch, and the ticking underfoot is louder. The plain is drinking. The plain is processing. The plain is doing whatever it is that the plain does, and it does it at a pace that is not your pace.

Somewhere far away, at the limit of what you can see, a rise has a darker patch on its flank. You walk toward it for an entire day. When you arrive, the patch is not what you expected — it is an area where the crust has been broken, recently, by something you cannot identify. The broken pieces are not scattered; they are arranged, faintly, in a pattern. There is no tool mark. There is no print. There is just the pattern, which you cannot read, and a faint smell of something warm and clean, like the inside of a kiln that has just been opened.

You realize, standing there, that the erosion you have been told about is not erosion in any conventional sense. It is the first signs of a kind of rearrangement. The plain is being unmade in order to be made again. The plain is editing itself. The plain has been here, in some form, for a very long time, and it intends to be here, in some form, for a very long time still.

---

There is no weather here in the sense of storms. There are the occasional gray rains, brief and unemphatic. There are the hours of shimmer. There are the long golden afternoons. There are the nights, which fall quickly and completely, in which the crust gives off a faint warmth of its own and the stars look down on a place they have looked down on for a very long time and will look down on for a very long time still.

You sleep on the crust, and the crust is warm beneath you. You wake, and the imprint of your body is being slowly erased, the color returning, the texture returning, the plain not minding.

You walk on. The plain does not mind that either. The plain is too vast and too patient to mind small things, and it is built to outlast you in every particular, and it is being, even now, even as you walk, slowly, gently, remade.
