# The Pocket

There is a stone the size of a closed fist that does not open the way stones are supposed to open.

It sits at the bottom of a well — or, more precisely, a well sits inside it, or both statements are true, or neither. The lip of the well rises to about the height of a man's knee. The stone extends past the lip on all sides, a rough basalt lump veined with something darker, something that catches light it shouldn't be able to catch. Step back and the well disappears. The stone is a stone. A child could kick it. Step closer and the well comes back, full of water that does not glint, and the sides of the well go down farther than geometry should permit.

This has always been the case. Nobody built it. Nobody decided. It is here.

---

The water in the well is the temperature of cold river stones in early spring, and it tastes of nothing — not flat, not metallic, not sweet, but genuinely *nothing*, as though taste itself has been politely excised. It rises and falls in long slow tides that take roughly half an hour to complete, though there is no obvious rhythm. Sometimes it crests just below the rim. Sometimes it sinks by a foot. The surface is always still. Nothing lands on it. No leaf, no raindrop, no thrown pebble ever breaks that surface; they are diverted at the last possible instant, by some small conspiracy of air.

The water is not the ocean, but it is the ocean's memory — a denser, more concentrated recollection of what an ocean is for.

---

Around the well, on the flat upper surface of the stone, a forest is growing. This is the part that should not be possible, and the forest does not appear to care. The trees are miniature. Their trunks are the width of a thumb. Their canopies, dense and interlocking, rise to roughly the height of a standing man's hip. From above, the stone looks like a mossy coin dropped among ordinary rocks, but from within, the trees are tall, the canopy is high, and the air is thick and green and breathing.

The trees are not one species. They are many, impossibly many — ferns with fronds of green glass; conifers no bigger than a sprig of rosemary shedding cones like beads; broadleaf things with leaves the color of bruised plums and undersides of pale silver. There are flowers: tiny, fleshy, the color of arterial blood, that bloom only in the deeper shade beneath the canopy. None of them should grow on bare stone. They grow anyway. Their roots fan out across the basalt in pale, searching threads, finding nothing to anchor in, holding on to the rock the way a person holds on to a thought.

The air beneath the canopy is cool, humid, and smells of green tea and something faintly rotten beneath — the sweetness of decay, the breath of fungi doing their slow cathedral work.

---

It is dim here. The light does not enter the way light should. There is no sun visible, no sky. The illumination comes from below the water, faintly, the way a moon might rise through deep silt. It falls in long diffuse columns, and where it touches the leaves from underneath it gives them a strange phosphorescence — not glowing, exactly, but *remembering* light, as though the leaves were translucent when they shouldn't be. In the deepest part of the miniature forest, where the trees crowd thickest and the canopy is almost a roof, the darkness is true and complete, and shapes there have outlines that move a half-second after the shapes themselves do.

The air is quiet in the way air is quiet before weather arrives. There is a hum at the bottom of hearing — not a sound, but a pressure, as though the air itself were a held breath. Occasionally something creaks: a trunk settling, a branch sighing under its own slow weight. There is no wind. There is never wind here. The trees sway with currents you cannot feel.

---

There is an animal in the forest. Only one — or only one at a time, or only one per observer, which is the kind of distinction this place does not respect.

It is the size of a large beetle and moves with the unhurried certainty of something that has never been hunted. Its body is a deep matte indigo, and its legs are the color of old ivory. It walks along the bark of one of the glass-fern trunks, pauses, tilts its head — it has a head; it has something that *functions* as a head — and regards you with eyes that are not eyes but are not not-eyes. It does not blink. It does not flee. After a long moment it returns to whatever it was doing. If you look away and look back, it will be in a different place, and the place it was before will have a small dent in the bark shaped exactly like its body, as though it had been pressed there for longer than you were watching.

It eats the pale roots. You can see the faint shadow of it moving under the surface of the stone as it walks, feeding.

---

At the edges of the stone, where the forest thins to a fringe of lichens and moss, the basalt gives way to something else — a transition that is not a transition. The rock becomes *water* becomes *rock* becomes *something else*, a texture that is hard to name. It is the color of deep seawater, and it has the consistency of wet felt, and if you touched it, your hand would come back with a faint impression of cool pressure and a smell you couldn't later identify. The forest's roots do not cross this boundary. They grow to it, and stop, as though politely, and the rest of the world (if there is one) is on the other side of it.

The well is in the center of all this. It is the only water the forest acknowledges. The miniature trees near it lean inward, slightly, permanently, like people listening to a singer they cannot quite see.

---

Nothing here is in a hurry. The trees grow about a centimeter a year, and the year here is not the same year as anywhere else. The water rises and falls. The beetle moves. The light shifts by fractions. The well waits. The stone, which is not a stone, holds the whole small kingdom in the bowl of its improbable hand, and from a distance, if you were passing at the right angle, you would not see it at all — only a rock in a field, only the ground, only the ordinary world pretending to be ordinary.

But the well is there.

The well is always there.
