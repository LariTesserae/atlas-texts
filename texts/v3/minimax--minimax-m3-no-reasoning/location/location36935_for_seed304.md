# The Drowned Mile

The place has no proper name. The folk who live along it call it the Mile, though it is closer to three, and not because they cannot count. They measure by the bridges.

Eight bridges cross the Mile in a way that does not make sense from above, because there is no river to cross. The water is below. The Mile is a street, and the street is the bed of a city that sank in a night no one now living remembers, and the water rose up through the flagstones until what remained was a single long trough of black water running between two rows of half-drowned houses, their upper stories leaning together overhead like the ribs of some drowned animal.

You arrive from the north, where the causeway lifts out of the marsh. The marsh is the city's older self — the place it was before it sank, and the place it has been returning to for a century or more. Reeds, then water, then a road of packed clay and split timber that locals keep raising by a hand's width each year as the soft ground settles. The bitterns call from the reeds in a sound like someone driving a stake into green wood. A heron, unconcerned, watches you pass with one eye the color of river-pebbles.

At the causeway's end you see the first bridge. It is a footbridge, humped, made of green-black oak that has never fully dried since it was built. The wood is carved with the figure of a long-bodied fish, repeated over and over, the fish's mouth open, the fish's mouth open, the fish's mouth open. Someone has been keeping the carving fresh — a chisel mark in pale wood shows in the eye of the nearest one. The bridge crosses nothing visible: it leaps from rooftop to rooftop across a channel of standing water that is, you realize when you lean over the rail, as much as thirty feet deep in places, and only a few inches deep in others, so that you can see the tiled floor of a parlor down there, with a chair still standing in it, the rushes on the seat gone black and haired with silt.

The light is the color of a wet slate. It comes from a sky that is never quite a sky — more a low ceiling of pale cloud, close enough that the gulls, when they pass, look as though they are passing through a room. The air is the temperature of a cellar, and it smells of peat, and of something sweeter underneath that might be the water itself, or might be the slow digestion of all the wood down there.

## The Inhabitants

People live in the upper stories. They have done so for as long as the city has been drowned, which is to say longer than the buildings were built for, so that the floors have been re-floored, the walls re-walled, and the original shapes of the houses are mostly a guess. Doorways have been knocked through ceilings. Ceilings have become floors. A person can step out of what looks like a chimney and find themselves in a neighbor's kitchen, three houses away, because the chimney was the only way up and the kitchen was where the family wanted to be. Stairways of split log and rope corkscrew up the outsides of the buildings, lashed to the walls with bands of willow, and a stranger climbing one will hear, from inside the wall, the muffled complaint of a baby, or the soft thud of a pestle, or nothing, which is worse.

The folk are thin and brown and not numerous. You might walk the length of the Mile in a morning and see six people, or you might see none. They fish. They are very good at fishing. They use long poles of jointed reed and a hook of bone, and they lower the line through gaps in the floor — which is to say, through the original ceilings, down into the drowned rooms below where the fish have lived for generations and grown large and pale and have, in the way of fish in caves, lost a deal of their worry. A good fish is the length of a man's leg. A bad one is the length of a man's arm. They are eaten with a root that tastes of turnip and regret.

There are animals. Coops of chickens on the high roofs, and a small pig or two, and cats — many cats, the color of the water, the color of the wood, the color of nothing. The cats hunt in the drowned floors when the water drops in summer, leaping from sill to sill in the green half-light below. The cats are the only ones who really know the city. They have been down into rooms no one has opened in living memory, and they come back smelling of old plaster and of something else that no one will discuss.

## The Water

The water is the thing. It does not behave. In the morning it is up to the second step of the east stair. By evening it is down to the fifth. It does not seem to be tide, exactly — the city is not on a coast, and the marsh to the north is freshwater and still — but it breathes. There is no other word the locals use. *The water breathes,* they say, and shrug, as though shrugs were made for this.

The water is black because it is deep, and brown where it is shallow, and where it is very shallow — in the bright spots between buildings, where the sun finds a way through — it is the color of strong tea and you can see all the way down, past the drowned furniture, past a clock stopped at a quarter past three, past the pale shapes of fish drifting like thoughts, down to a tiled floor where a single shoe, a child's, lies on its side as though the child had just stepped out of it.

When the water is high, the Mile is impassable by boat except at the bridges, and the bridges are at fixed points, and so the city has a rhythm of going and not-going that the inhabitants do not seem to mind. When the water is low, the boats are poled along the trough between the houses, and voices carry from window to window, and the smell of fish cooking rises into the low cloud and sweetens the air for a hundred yards in every direction.

There is a sound that is always there. It is the sound of water against wood, of the slow shifting of a city that is still settling, of beams sighing in the drowned rooms below. It is not loud. It is the sound a house makes when it is asleep, and the house is the whole Mile, and it has been asleep for a hundred years.

## The Bridges

The eight bridges are the only way to cross the Mile without going around, and going around is a day. They are of different ages. The oldest is the fish bridge at the north end, and the newest is a rope-and-cradle affair near the south, where a woman lives alone in what was once a granary and will not explain how she crosses, only that she does, only that the cradle is sound, only that a man once tried to follow her and was very sorry.

Between the fish bridge and the cradle are six more, of varying width, varying cleat, varying purpose. One of them is a market bridge, wide enough for a cart if a cart could be brought here, which it cannot, and so the bridge is used for sitting, and on certain days for the selling of fish and the buying of root and the mending of nets and the telling of such news as the Mile has, which is mostly weather, mostly water, mostly the doings of the cats.

On market days there is talk. The talk is of the water. Is it higher this year than last? The oldest woman, who is also the fish carver, says yes. A man who keeps chickens says no. They look at the second step of the east stair, which has a notch in it cut by someone long dead, and they argue, gently, in the way of people for whom argument is itself a kind of keeping company.

## The South

The Mile ends, or begins, depending on whom you ask, at a place where the buildings thin and the marsh takes over again. There is a last house, smaller than the others, with a door that opens onto nothing but reeds and a small channel of water that might be a path, might be the beginning of a river, might be the place where the Mile's water goes when it breathes out. A boy lives in this house, or is said to. He is said to be a little simple. He is said to mend the bridges. He is said to be able to dive into the black water and come back up with a fish in each hand, which is probably true, and with a small wooden cup that was lost sixty years ago, which is probably not.

Beyond the boy and his house, the marsh, and the cloud, and the bitterns, and the wide and quiet country, in which this place is the only one of its kind, set down like a stone in a green and folded cloth.

You walk the Mile. The fish bridge at the north. The leaning houses. The black water with its pale fish and its still furniture and its child's shoe. The cats. The old woman with her chisel. The market bridge, empty today. The rope cradle, swinging a little in a wind you can barely feel. The boy at the south end, who watches you from his doorway without speaking, and who is holding, for no reason you can see, a single wooden cup.
