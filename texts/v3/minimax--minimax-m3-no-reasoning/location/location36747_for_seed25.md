# The Hollow Valley

You come down into it from a place you cannot see the top of anymore. The descent is gradual but long, and the air changes as you go — cools, thickens, grows heavier with moisture until your skin feels like a damp cloth pressed to your face. The light, which was never certain to begin with, gives up the further down you travel. It does not so much end as become irrelevant, replaced by other sources: the faint mineral phosphorescence of the rock itself, the purple-blue flicker of things with bodies, and a deeper red glow from below — from the ground itself, which is warm.

The valley floor, when you reach it, is roughly the size of a small country. Or maybe a large city. It is the kind of scale that makes your eyes stop trying to measure, because there is nothing to use as a reference. The walls that ring it are very far away and very tall. You can tell they are tall only because the sounds that come back from them take too long to arrive.

---

The ground is black volcanic glass, mostly, though it has been here long enough to forget how to be sharp. The erosion of countless bodies, countless seasons of dripping water, countless chemical whisperings between rock and rain have smoothed it into something that looks almost poured. It has the quality of cooled tallow. In places it has cracked and the cracks have been colonized — by what, by no-one you would call plant — by a thin leathery skin of dark stuff, black-purple-brown, that grips the stone and breathes out a faint warmth and a wet, mineral smell, like the inside of a battery.

This skin is the floor of the world here. It covers everything. Where the water collects, it grows thicker, paler, almost gelatinous, and it pulses — not visibly to a single glance, but the corner of your eye keeps catching motion. Where the water thins, it thins with it, until on the dry ridges it is no more than a stain.

And on this skin, and in it, and above it —

---

The fauna.

There is no other word for it. You do not call this population. You do not call this ecosystem. You call it fauna the way you call a flood water: a fact so overwhelming that the word has to stretch to hold it.

The first thing you understand is that the air is full. Not with wind, not with weather, but with bodies — chitinous, leathery, soft. They hang in layers. The lowest layer skims the mat on the floor and feeds, mouths pressed down, hundreds of them per square meter, thousands, packed so close their limbs interlock and you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. Above them, a second layer runs across the backs of the first, faster, lighter, picking at things. Above that, a third — winged, perhaps, but wings so small and bodies so numerous that they read more as a kind of moving mist than as individual creatures. They fill the air from ankle height up to perhaps fifteen meters, and above fifteen meters the air is clearer but only because the things up there are larger and less common, and they have the space they need.

They have always been here. The valley has not been quiet since before it had a name, and even now the floor of it flexes under you in places because there is no actual floor — only a crust of bodies over a deeper stratum of bodies over a wet ferment of bodies and the mat below.

---

The sound is the second thing you understand, and it is the one that does not leave.

It is not one sound. It is a wall of sound, and you are inside it. There are frequencies in it that you can hear, and frequencies you can feel in your sternum, and frequencies that you only know are there because your teeth begin to ache after a few minutes of standing in it. There are clicks like someone dropping pebbles down a well, there are long scraping tones like wet slate being dragged, there is a high thin piping that you realize after a while is the sound of small creatures dying, which happens often here, and without ceremony. There is a low throb that is not a heartbeat but that follows the rhythm of one anyway, that you eventually understand is the floor itself — the mat breathing, or the rock cooling, or both.

If you stand still long enough, the sound organizes itself slightly. You begin to hear patterns — the way one creature's call sets off another, the way a disturbance on one side of the valley causes a wave of answering calls on the other. The whole basin is in conversation, a slow continuous shout across a dim space. The silence between sounds is what you listen for, and it never quite arrives.

---

The creatures themselves are not easy to look at. Not because they are frightening — though some are, the larger ones, the ones in the upper air, the ones with the long deliberate wings — but because there are too many of them and they are too similar and too various. They have exoskeletons mostly. They have too many legs, or the right number of legs but in the wrong places. Some of them are dark and some of them are paler and some of them carry the faint glow inside them like a coal carried in a paper bag. A few of them, the ones that feed highest and seem most aware, have a small soft eye that catches your flashlight or your lamp or whatever light you have brought with you and returns it to you as a single point, and then they are gone.

They do not seem to mind you. You are not what they are for. They have been in this valley a long time, and they are for each other. The mat feeds them, and they feed each other, and the water moves through them, and the sound moves through them, and the cycle is very old and very settled. Whatever killed the things that might once have grown here, or whatever prevented them from growing here in the first place — and the rocks have their own opinion about that, written in their chemistry and their dust — happened long enough ago that the system here has forgotten any other way.

---

What is strange about the place — the particular strangeness, the one you carry out with you — is not any one of these things but the way they hold together. It is the feeling of a world that has been running on a different fuel. There is no green here. There is no photosynthesis. There is no sun, or no sun that matters. There is heat from below, and there is chemistry, and there is the slow patient grind of the mat eating rock and breathing out energy in forms that something else can use, and on top of that, life — not the life you were raised to recognize, but life nonetheless, dense and loud and very, very busy.

The valley smells of wet stone and ozone and a sweet rottenness, the smell of something that is alive because something else is dead, a long time ago. The water that runs across the floor in thin shining threads is warm where the mat is thick and cold where it is not. The walls, when you eventually walk to them, are slick and far taller than you expected and they give back their own calls after a delay that makes you feel small.

Above you, beyond the highest layer of flyers, the ceiling — if there is a ceiling, and there may not be — is lost in dark. The sound goes up into it and does not come back. You stand on a floor of creatures and you are inside a sound and you cannot see the edges of the place you are in, and the creatures have been doing this for longer than you have been a species.

You are not the most alive thing here. You are not even close.
