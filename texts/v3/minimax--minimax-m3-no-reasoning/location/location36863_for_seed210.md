# The Calefactory Fells

---

## I. The Hollow at the Top

The pass crests at a saddle of fractured slate and you stand there, and then the land *ceases to behave.*

It is a small place. A cleft in the high country no wider across than three city blocks, longer perhaps by half. The walls around it are steep but not clifflike — fractured ridgelines that rise perhaps three hundred meters above the basin floor, sharp-edged in that fresh way the mountains have when they've only just remembered how to be mountains. Recent. The talus at their feet is loose and pale, the rock it falls from is darker, almost blue-grey in places, and the fractures run at strange angles: not the patient horizontals of an old range but diagonals, verticals, hooked curves, as if the stone had been twisted rather than folded. There is snow in the crevices even in the late season — small white tongues that the cool air has not yet let go of.

You do not know what to call the elevation. The barometer on your wrist says one number; the altimeter says another. Both are correct in the way that both numbers on a divided sign are correct.

The air is wet. It is always wet. Water runs down the rock faces in skeins you cannot quite see until the light catches them; it beads on every surface; it condenses on your eyelashes, on the brass fittings of your equipment, on the lenses you keep wiping and that keep fogging again. The basin floor is bog and scree in alternation, the bog a deep maroon, almost blood-colored in places, where iron has leached out of the broken ground; the scree pale and ringing underfoot. Small streams thread between them, half-audible, half-not, never quite the same stream twice. You could wade. You will not wade. The water smells of *something* — mineral, electric, faintly sweet, like the air near a thunderstorm that has already passed.

It is cool enough that your breath shows when you speak.

---

## II. The Engines

They occupy the center of the hollow and they are, all of them, *running.*

There is a broad flat shelf of bedrock there, polished to a glassy dark surface that might be natural or might not, and upon this shelf are ranged perhaps a hundred small machines. You have to call them machines because they are not anything else — they are too purposeful for sculpture, too indifferent for ritual, too loud for anything but labor. Each one is about the size of a workbench or a footlocker. They are made of dark metal, green-patinated bronze, riveted iron, glass, ceramic, vulcanite. They are not standardized. Each one has a different configuration of pipes, flywheels, valves, levers, chimneys, gauges. They were not designed together. They were not designed at all, you suspect — they *arrived* and were added and were tolerated.

They hiss. They tick. They thump and chatter and sigh. A copper chimney breathes white steam in slow rhythmic pulses; a flywheel turns somewhere, you hear it but cannot see which one; something deep inside the array produces a low note, almost a hum, that you feel in the soles of your boots before you hear it. There is a smell of hot metal and a smell of ozone and a smell like the inside of a clock — that clean mineral oil, that faint ozone tang. Occasionally one of them *clanks*, sharp and percussive, and you flinch every time and the lichen on the rocks does not flinch and you understand that the lichen has been here longer than you.

They do not appear to be producing anything.

You stand for a long time and watch and nothing is produced. They take in air and steam and bog-water and they put out steam and condensed water and a fine metallic mist that drifts downwind and coats the nearest boulders with a thin film of dull bronze. They do not produce objects. They do not produce light. They do not, that you can see, perform any function.

They are running.

That is the function.

The mountain does not care that they are there. The water does not care. The animals do not care. You are the only one who is asking what they are *for,* and the question, in this place, has begun to feel like a category error.

---

## III. The Animals

They are abundant.

The first thing you noticed, before the machines — before your eyes adjusted to the strange uniform dim of the hollow — was the sounds of small hooves on wet stone. You looked down and there were creatures around your boots. They were the size of foxes and built like nothing you have a name for: four legs, yes, but articulated strangely, the joints bending the wrong way, the feet having too many toes, the heads narrow and ferret-like with very large dark eyes that reflected the dim light back at you like wet buttons. Their fur was a saturated moss-green, the green of wet ferns, and it blended with the rocks until they moved.

There were perhaps a dozen of them around you. They did not run.

They looked up at you.

There is something unkind in describing them as bold because they were not bold; they were *unafraid* in a way that is its own kind of unsettling. They had no reason to flee you. You were not part of their world; you were an event in it, like a change in the weather.

You walk farther into the hollow and the populations stack up. The green four-legged things are everywhere, on the talus, beside the streams, picking their careful way around the machines with the casual precision of commuters who know their platform. There are other things. Tall, narrow, long-necked, birdlike, the color of wet bark — they stand in the bog and dip their heads and bring them up with nothing in them and dip them again. There is something in the underbrush that is the color of the maroon bog-water and that moves in pulses, *throbbing* along like a slug, like a piece of intestine. There are flying things, low and quick, the size of bats but membraned differently, and they skim the steam from the chimneys of the engines and you realize after a while that this is what they eat.

The fauna here is *rich.* The wet rock and the wet air and the abundance of whatever the machines are exhaling have made a kind of perpetual larder of this small high basin. The food chains are short. The energy is right there in the air, metallic, electric, and the animals have learned to breathe it in and pass it up.

Nothing here is afraid of you.

---

## IV. The Quiet That Is Not Quiet

The hollow is loud.

The machines provide a constant mechanical undertow: tick and hiss and the slow breathing of the steam-chimneys, the occasional clank, the *chuff-chuff-chuff* of something pumping in cycles you cannot predict. The streams add their voices, dozens of them, little silver and almost-inaudible ones. The animals are quiet individually but the collective of them — the rustle of the green four-legged things in the underbrush, the low cluck of the long-necked things in the bog, the high cheep of the flying things at the chimneys, the wet *flop-flop-flop* of the pulsing maroon things somewhere in the rocks — adds up to a sustained whisper, a kind of shh-shh-shh that is itself almost loud enough to drown out the machines.

But.

What you notice, after an hour, is that the sounds do not *change.*

The clank comes when it comes. The chimneys breathe at their own rhythms. The streams run at their own volume. The animals move and call and the calls are the same calls. You stand for an hour and the soundscape is identical at the end of it to the soundscape at the beginning. The same clank. The same number of clanks. The same amplitude. There is no wind today and tomorrow there will be no wind and the streams will run at the same volume and the machines will continue their production of nothing and the animals will go on breathing their metallic air.

This is the meaning of *never changing.*

Not frozen — you are not in a glacier, not in a photograph — but *held.* The place has reached some state and it is staying in it. The machines balance the streams and the streams feed the machines and the animals graze the air and the air is grazed and the bog leaches iron and the iron feeds the patina on the machines and the patina is the bronze mist that drifts and the bronze mist is what the animals breathe. It is a closed loop. It has been running. It will continue to run.

You are the only unbalancing thing in it.

---

## V. The Dim

There is no direct sun in the hollow at most hours of the day. The walls are too high, the cleft too narrow; the sun transits a small bright rectangle of sky that moves across the upper rim of the rocks and does not enter the basin. The light that does enter is the light of overcast, of bounce, of water-haze: a uniform diffuse dim in which shadows are soft and the colors are saturated in the way that only dim wet air will let them be saturated. The maroon of the bog is almost black. The green of the fur of the small animals is almost emerald. The bronze of the machines is almost glowing.

You notice that the dim does not change either.

You sit on a boulder and you wait, and the light does not dim toward evening in the way light should. It is the same dim. The bright rectangle on the upper rim moves and at some point is gone and the dim does not change. The dim is the dim. There is a faint luminescence from the bog itself, the iron doing something to the water, a faint maroon glow that contributes as much as the sky. There is a faint luminescence from the machines, a faint white-violet from somewhere inside their workings, a faint pulse at the same rate as the chimney breath. The dim is composed. The dim is held in place.

It is the kind of dim that human eyes were not made for but that, given an hour, you can begin to see by.

---

## VI. The Wrongness

You do not put it into words at first. You notice that the boulder you are sitting on is warmer than it should be — no, not warmer; *heatless but warm,* as if it remembered warmth. You notice that the stream beside you is running uphill, very slightly, and that this is not the most surprising thing about it because the stream is also running in two directions at once, a thin sheet of water moving left along one half of its width and right along the other. You notice that one of the green animals is watching you from the talus and that the animal has a reflection in the wet rock behind it, and that the reflection is not of the animal but of something else, something with too many legs, something that is *also watching you.*

You notice that the machines, when you walk among them, are not in the same arrangement they were in a minute ago. Not radically. A chimney that was on the left is on the right. A flywheel that turned clockwise now turns counter. Nothing has moved but everything has moved. You cannot point to any one change. The place has *rotated slightly,* the way a picture on a wall can be rotated slightly between two viewings and the viewer feels uneasy without being able to say why.

You notice that the air has a faint geometry. There are vertical lines in it, like the lines of falling rain, but there is no rain. They are not quite visible. You see them best when you look at the distant rock wall, which seems to be *more than one rock wall,* seems to have another rock wall a few inches behind it, identical, slightly offset, like a double exposure. If you focus on the nearer one, the farther one becomes invisible. If you focus on the farther one, the nearer one is a slight darkening in the air.

There are at least three rock walls.

There are at least two skies, one above the other, slightly displaced.

There are at least two of you, slightly displaced.

The animals do not notice. The machines do not care. The water goes on going in two directions at once and you understand, or you begin to understand, that the place is not broken — the place was *always* like this — and that what is broken is your expectation that a place should be a single, definite, mutually-exclusive arrangement of objects in space. This place refuses that arrangement. This place *fans out* into a number of adjacent possible arrangements and is, somehow, all of them at once.

It is not hostile. It is not a warning. It is just the way the high wet strange small industrial hollow of the upper pass is. You have walked into it. You can walk out of it. When you walk out you will see the bright sky and the distant peaks and the glaciers and you will tell people about this place, if you can find the words, and they will look at you in the way people look at people who claim to have been to places they cannot describe.

You will not stay.

The machines will still be running. The animals will still be breathing their metallic air. The streams will still be going in two directions at once. The dim will be the same dim. The clank will clank at the same interval.

The place does not need you.

It is held. It is balanced. It is the small high wet industrial dream of a cleft in the upper country, and it is *perfect,* and it is *always* the same, and it has been the same for a very long time, and it will be the same for a very long time after.

You are the variable. You are the only thing in the hollow that is moving through time.

---

## VII. Leaving

You climb back up to the saddle.

Below you, in the cleft, the machines go on. The animals graze the air. The streams run their split courses. The two skies lie on top of each other like two sheets of paper.

You turn away. The wind meets you on the other side, dry, cold, ordinary. The mountains open out ahead of you in the way mountains do — patient, horizontal, folded, ordinary.

You do not look back. You do not look back because you suspect, if you look back, the cleft will still be there in exactly the position you left it, unchanged, unchanging, and that this is a thing you cannot survive knowing for certain.

You walk on.

Behind you, in the small high wet dim industrial held-in-place hollow at the top of the pass, a machine clanks.

A green four-legged thing with too many toes lifts its head and looks at the place where you were.

It does not move to follow you.

It has always been here.

It will always be here.
