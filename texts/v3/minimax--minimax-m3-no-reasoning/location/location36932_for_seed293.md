# The Settling

You find it the way you find most low places — by walking down into them. The path levels out, then drops a few centimeters, then a meter, then you're in it. The walls of what was once a road have thickened into ramps of root and saturated brick, the stones swollen with moss and lichen until the mortar is a green-black jelly that seeps between your boots when you step. It's warm. It is always warm here.

The scale of the place fits inside your body. You can hold the whole valley in the bowl of your skull. From the high rim you cannot see it; from inside it you can see every edge at once. A bowl perhaps two hundred meters across, a slight ellipse, the long axis tilted northeast as if the ground had once been something that thought about a direction and then stopped thinking. The walls are not really walls. They are the last terraces of some very old urbanization, eight or nine levels rising up the inside of the bowl, each one a different compromise between the built and the grown. You can read them like sediment. The lowest are unreadable — water has been working on them so long that brick has become loam and loam has become something you don't have a name for, a wet brown felt of fused roots and re-crystallized calcium, the bones of a building making a slow fist around itself. The upper ones still have the outline of doorways. Lintels. A cornice with a face on it, half-melted, looking down.

Everything is wet. The air is wet. Your glasses, if you had them, would wet. The walls weep. The terraces seep. A pale film of water, no thicker than breath, runs down the inside of the bowl and gathers along the bottom in a series of shallow, shifting pools that are technically a stream, technically a marsh, technically nothing — just the slow conveyor of groundwater expressing itself. You can hear the water moving through the substrate, a thick wet rustle, like someone turning the pages of a very large wet book, the way it goes through root-tunnels and abandoned pipes and the hollow spires of dead trees. Sometimes a bubble travels up through a pool and bursts with a small *tok*. Sometimes a whole section of felted bank sighs forward half a meter and re-settles.

The plants are the buildings. Have been for a long time. The architecture did not so much disappear as get *understood*, absorbed into a longer grammar. You can see the shapes of streets, of a plaza, of what must have been a long market hall — its iron columns now plumed with epiphytes, the roof gone, replaced by an interlace of banyan-aerial-root and a black bamboo that has thickened over centuries into something more like organ pipe than cane. The bamboo tubes are musical. Wind moves through them and through the broken window-cavities of the standing walls and produces a constant, roving chord, mostly minor, that shifts every few minutes as a breeze finds a new aperture. That is the loud thing. That is most of the loud thing. There is also the rain — not rain in the air, but rain in the tissue, the steady whisper of water moving through living xylem on its way down to the water table, and amplified through the architecture until you can hear it as a soft, pervasive static, like a sea held inside a shell.

Above the bamboo, the canopy is layered and not a canopy anymore. It is a roof. Five, six strata of crown, each a different species, each a different shade. The uppermost is a kind of fig with small hard leaves that clatter like coins in any wind. Beneath it, a tier of something ferny, dark green, perpetually uncurling. Beneath that, a tier of orchids — not in pots, in the architecture itself, in the crooks of columns and the sockets of old lamps, where their roots have replaced the wiring, where each bloom is the size of your face and the color of old teeth, and where, when a moth enters, the whole plant seems to lean. Beneath that, a tier of fungi, vast pale shelves plastered across every vertical surface, breathing out at night. Beneath that, the floor.

There is not much floor. Most of what was floor is now ankle-deep water over a substrate that moves under your foot, a slow bag of root and peat and brick-dust and the small bones of things. You step carefully. There are raised ways, though — pale exposed root-bridges that have grown into the place where the curbstones were, and you can mostly stay on them. The root-bridges breathe. They move. If you stand still for a long time you can feel them lift, fractionally, in a slow tidal rhythm that has nothing to do with any moon. The forest is breathing. The forest has decided it is an animal.

The fauna is dense and small. Nothing large can fit through the streets anymore, or has not wanted to in a long time. The air is full of frogs, of pale moths, of damselflies the color of cut brass, of small bright lizards that run across your boot and keep going, of beetles so lacquered they look varnished. There is a creature here, or several, that you only ever see as a movement in the ferns — something roughly the size of a cat, low, many-legged, that lives in the thickets where the bamboo meets the walls and that produces, at dusk, a single long note that holds and holds and does not decay. Nobody has named it. It is not clear it is one species. The amphibians do something collective at sundown — a sudden call-and-response that fills the bowl for forty seconds and then stops, all at once, and the silence that follows is the loudest thing of all. After that, the bamboo chord, and the water, and the small *toks* of bubbles, and the breathing.

The strange thing is the light. There is enough of it. The canopy is not closed; the upper fig has a thousand small windows where leaves have fallen, and from these, columns of yellow-green light drop into the bowl like dropped threads. Each column moves. Each column is inhabited. Midges hang in them, the air glitters through them, and on the floor of the bowl, where a column lands, a particular thing grows — a brighter green, a different plant, a small altar of it, where spores accumulate and orchid seedlings take hold. Light here is a fertilizer. Light here is a tool of gardening. You begin to suspect the holes in the canopy are not accidents.

The strange thing is the technology. It is everywhere and it does not look like technology. The roots that bridge the streets carry, inside them, a pale thread — thin, bright, not quite metal — that pulses faintly with its own rhythm, out of phase with the breathing of the forest. Where the threads surface, they end in a small grey nodule, the size of a fist, fused to a wall or a root or a stone. The nodules are warm. They hum, in a pitch just below the bamboo chord, so you feel them more than hear them. They are older than the forest, probably. They were here before. You can find a few of the originals — a half-buried junction box, a smashed streetlamp, a rectangular thing under the felt that might have been a server — but the technology has migrated out of the casing and into the substrate, into the water, into the roots. The forest did not destroy it. The forest adopted it. Or the technology adopted the forest. There is a difference and there is no difference.

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, you can feel the nodules synchronizing. They find each other across the bowl, by some protocol you cannot name, and for a moment their hums lock, and the bamboo chord locks, and the water in the pools levels, and the breath of the root-bridges pauses, and the air goes sharp, and you feel your own pulse quicken involuntarily, and then it all releases, and the chord wanders again. This happens every few minutes. It happens more often when the light columns shift. It happens more often when you move. The place is watching you with something that is not a face.

It is a city that has eaten itself and been eaten by something larger and is still, technically, a city. The terraces still rise. The streets still curve. The market hall still holds a market — a soft green one, the floor carpeted with liverwort, the iron columns flowering with bromeliads the size of barrels, every surface busy with the small commerce of invertebrates. There is no one to sell to. There is no one to buy from. The commerce continues.

It is the end of a long settlement. It is the oldest stable state of an old place. The erosion is finished; there is nothing left to erode, the substrate is a single thick fabric now, a felted composite of brick and bone and root and silica, and the only motion is internal — the slow overturning of generations, the slow digestion of an old urban plan into a new and longer one. The bowl will get shallower. The terraces will smooth. In ten thousand years, perhaps, the rim will be lower, the fig canopy will close, the water will rise a few more centimeters, and the bamboo chord will go down a half-tone as the apertures narrow. Then, much later, the apertures will seal, and the bamboo will quiet, and the only loud thing will be the breathing.

For now it is loud. For now it is turbulent. Every moment, somewhere in the substrate, a wall is dissolving into felt; a root-bridge is reaching for the next stone; a nodule is finding a new thread; a column of light is moving across the floor and being received by a small bright altar of new growth. The place is unfinished. The place is finished. You sit on a root-bridge that lifts, gently, under you, and the water goes *tok*, and the bamboo plays a minor chord, and a pale moth passes through a column of light and the column darkens for a moment and then returns, and you breathe with the forest for a while, and then you walk back up the ramps, and the bowl holds the shape of where you were for a long time after you are gone.
