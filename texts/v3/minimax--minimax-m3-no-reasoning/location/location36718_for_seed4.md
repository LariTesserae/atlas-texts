# The Mire-Chapel of Uthel

## The Hollow

You have to know where to look. That is the first rule of this place, and the only one the builders ever managed to teach. The entrance is a crack between two river-smoothed boulders, where the runoff from the upper ravine has carved a throat just wide enough for a shoulder to pass. If you were walking the ridge you would walk past it. The ridge is broad and flat and uninteresting, the kind of land that promises nothing and is right to make good on the promise. But if you turn sideways, and duck, and let your eyes adjust to the cut of shadow where the boulders meet, you find yourself looking down into a bowl.

The bowl is perhaps forty paces across. Maybe fifty. It is hard to say because the edges move.

## The Water

Everything here is wet. Not in the way of a pond or a stream but in the way of a held breath — water suspended in things that have no business holding it. The air is so damp it has weight. When you exhale, your breath is a small white ghost that doesn't dissipate but sinks, pooling around your ankles like fog that has learned to be liquid. Condensation gathers on the underside of every surface. If you touch the wall of the chapel — and you will, you have to, the passage is narrow — your palm comes away gleaming, and the air you have displaced fills the space you left with a thin exhalation of mossy warmth. The water here is old water. It has been recycled through root and stone and lung for so long that it has forgotten which of those it belongs to.

There is a spring at the bowl's lowest point, where the floor of the place slopes gently inward. It does not flow so much as accumulate. The water arrives from below, welling up through pale gravel, and the gravel is not quite gravel. It is a kind of calcified mucus, deposited by something that lived here once, or lives here still in a reduced and patient form. The spring makes a sound like a throat trying not to speak.

## The Walls

The walls are alive.

This is the part the builders understood, the part they built around. The settlement — if it can be called that, if the word *settlement* is not too grandiose for what amounts to a few dozen chambers braided into the root-mass of the lower terraces — was not constructed in any conventional sense. It was *negotiated*. The first colonizers, whoever they were, found this pocket of wet and dark and impossibly fertile, and they did not carve out rooms. They hollowed rooms, slowly, over generations, persuading the growth to thicken here and not there, training it around the shape they wanted by removing competing shoots and feeding the favored ones with their own waste and their dead.

What remains, what you see, is architecture that grew. The walls are a felted mass of root and stem, layered and laminated, as dense as oak in the places where it matters. The chambers are cylindrical or oval, never square. There are no corners. The doorways are arches that the builders reinforced with shaped stone only at the lintels, where the load is greatest. The floors are packed with a kind of living mat — root and rhizome and a slow-creeping moss that compresses underfoot and remembers the shape of the foot after it has passed. 

In the upper chambers, where the builders lived and now their descendants live, the walls are pale. They have been smoked. Generations of cook-fires and lamp-oil have darkened the living surface into something that looks like wood and behaves like leather. You can press your thumb into it and it will yield, and it will slowly, over the hour after you leave, fill back in.

## The Canopy

Above, the ceiling is not a ceiling. It is a single plant, or the cooperative mass of many plants acting as one — no one in the chapel knows, and the question is not considered important. It hangs at a height of perhaps ten feet in the chambers, lower in the passages, and it is the source of everything. The light, what little there is, filters through it from above, from the real sky that is two hundred feet higher, through the crack between the boulders and through the layers of soil and root that constitute the true roof. By the time it arrives it is the color of a bruise — purple-black, blue-black, the black of deep water — and it is enough. The dwellers have eyes that have spent six thousand years learning to see with this light, and they see by it the way you see by afternoon.

The canopy weeps. This is not a figure of speech. Water gathers on the underside of the lowest leaves, pools in the cup of a kind of fern that is endemic to the chapel and grows nowhere else, and drips, and the drips are the rain of this place. There is a slow rhythm to it, almost but not quite regular, and in the long chambers where you lie down to sleep, the dwellers learn to count the drops the way coastal people learn to count the waves. They tell you that no two nights are the same.

## The Cold

It is not cold in the way of ice. The temperature here is the temperature of a held hand, of breath on the back of the neck, of the moment before fever breaks. But the wetness makes it feel colder than it is, and the dwellers move slowly, and they wrap themselves in the felted bark of a certain cultivar that they shear each autumn in a quiet ceremony that no outsider has ever witnessed and survived to describe. The garments are thick and stiff and rust-colored, and they make the dwellers look, in the bruise-light, like figures carved from some dense root-vegetable, like wooden saints in a forgotten shrine.

You can see your breath at all times. It pools and sinks. The dwellers have names for the shapes it makes.

## The Inhabitants

The dwellers are not numerous. A hundred and thirty, perhaps a hundred and forty. There is a counting-house near the spring, kept by a woman whose name translates roughly as *she-who-keeps-the-breath*, and she knows the number at any given moment because she knows the breaths she has heard that day. The dwellers move through the chambers in a slow choreography, each of them following a path that has been worn into the living mat by their own ancestors and reinforced by their own feet. They do not speak above a murmur. Sound carries strangely in the chapel — it is absorbed by the walls, damped, returned in a softened form that has lost its edges — and so they have learned to make their conversations in gestures and in touches, in the placement of hands on shoulders, in the length of a held glance.

Their faces are the same bruise-color as the light. Their eyes are large and very dark. They eat a kind of pale tuber that grows from the walls in the deeper chambers, and a ferment they make from a sap that bleeds from the canopy when it is wounded, and a small crustacean that lives in the spring and that they harvest by hand at dawn, when the crustaceans come up to feed on the mucus-gravel.

They are not unfriendly. They are simply not yet certain what you are.

## The Fauna

This is where the place becomes difficult to describe.

The chapel is full of life. The builders came here, perhaps, because it was sheltered, because the water was reliable, because the growing-walls would do the work of carpentry for them. But the life they found was already here, and it was already enormous, and it had been waiting.

In the upper chambers, where the dwellers move, the fauna is small. There are beetles the size of your thumb that graze the wall-mat and leave behind them a faint phosphorescence, a green smear that fades over the hour. There are grubs that the dwellers cultivate in clay pots and that they consider a delicacy. There is a kind of blind moth, ivory-colored, that flutters in the highest chambers where the light is strongest and that the dwellers trap with cupped hands and eat with closed eyes. There is a rat-like thing, but not a rat, that lives in the passages and that you will sometimes feel run across your foot in the dark. It is cold and quick and it apologizes in a high chittering sound.

But below.

Below the inhabited chambers, in the older layers of the chapel, where the smoke has never reached and the walls are still the original green-black of the living tissue, there are other things. You do not go below. The dwellers do not go below. They speak of the lower chapel the way shore-dwellers speak of the deep ocean — with reverence, and with a kind of seasoned terror. Something moves down there. Something large. The dwellers track it by the sound of it breathing, a slow bellows-sound that cycles through the lower chambers and that rises, sometimes, in the still hours before dawn, into a resonance that the dwellers say is the chapel speaking in its own voice. They say it has been there longer than they have. They say it remembers the builders. They say it is the reason the builders stopped building down and started building up.

In the deepest pool of the spring, if you lower a lamp on a cord and look, you can see — far down, in water that is clearer than it has any right to be — a pale shape that is perhaps a body and perhaps a growth and perhaps neither. It does not move while you watch. It does not need to. It has already seen you.

## The Sound

The chapel is quiet, but it is not silent. There is the spring. There is the drip. There is the slow creak of the living walls as they adjust to the weight of the day. There is the sound of the dwellers, which is the sound of many small soft movements and the occasional murmur, like wind through a stand of reeds. There is, always, the breathing below. There are the beetles, who make a sound like very distant rain. There is the rustle of the canopy, far above, where the air moves through it the way wind moves through a forest canopy — and indeed, it is a forest canopy, only hung upside-down, only compressed into a ceiling.

If you sit very still, in one of the upper chambers, and you close your eyes, you can hear all of these sounds at once and they will layer into something that is almost a music. The dwellers know this music. They have been raised in it. They say it is the sound the world made before people came and started making other sounds.

## The Time

Time works strangely here. Not in any dramatic way. The dwellers age and die and are folded into the wall-mat when they go, and new ones are born in the lower chambers in a kind of fibrous pod that the mother tends for nine months and that opens, when it is ready, into a small wet creature that is placed at the breast within the hour. Time is not slow, exactly. It is *thick*. It resists motion the way the water here resists motion. An hour in the chapel feels like three. A day feels like a season. By the time you have been here a week, you will have lost track of the world outside and will not be sure it still exists.

The dwellers know this. They know you came from somewhere else. They are not curious about it. They assume you will return to it eventually, the way a leaf returns to the tree, and in the meantime they will feed you and give you a corner of the mat to sleep on and they will not ask you to speak, because they understand that speaking is difficult when the air is this wet and the words are this heavy.

## The Edge

If you climb — and there is a way to climb, a series of ledges the builders carved into the outer wall of the bowl, where the living tissue is thinnest and the stone of the original rift is exposed — you can reach the ridge. From the ridge you can see the rest of the country: a low wet land of scrub and marsh, of infrequent trees bent by some absent wind, of a sky that is overcast almost always and that makes the land look like a photograph of itself, drained of color. You can see for miles. There is nothing else like the chapel for miles. There is nothing at all for miles. You will stand on the ridge and you will look down at the crack between the boulders and you will think: *that is a small place*. You will think: *a place that small cannot hold what I have just seen*.

You will be wrong.

The chapel is a small place. But what it holds is not small, and what it holds is not finished, and what it holds is, in its slow and patient way, still becoming.

You will go back down. You will duck through the crack. You will feel the wet air settle on your shoulders like a hand. You will hear the drip. You will hear the breathing. You will find your corner of the mat, and you will sit on it, and you will wait, as the dwellers wait, for the next drop to fall.

It will fall. It is always falling. It has always been falling.

It will fall after you are gone, too. It will fall when the dwellers are gone, when the breathing thing below has finished its long patience and risen, when the chapel has closed itself up and gone back to being what it was before the builders came — a pocket in the dark, a place where water gathers, a throat of the earth through which something enormous occasionally breathes.

You are only here for a moment. The chapel is very old. The chapel is not in a hurry.

Sit. Breathe. Count the drops.

It is the only thing to do.
