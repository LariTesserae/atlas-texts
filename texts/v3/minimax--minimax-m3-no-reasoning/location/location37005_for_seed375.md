# The Hollow Vaults of Kess-Tannin

## What the Eye Finds First

You stand inside a long, low-roofed concourse. The ceiling hangs at perhaps fifteen meters — close enough to feel like architecture, far enough to feel like sky. The walls are not walls but *ribwork*: pale, porous stone that grew rather than was quarried, each rib as wide as a man lying down, curving up from the floor and meeting its neighbors overhead in knuckles of fused calcium-white. Between the ribs, the ceiling panels are translucent — something like nacre, something like ice that never melted — and they let down a thin, sourceless light the color of old tallow. It is never brighter than this. It is never darker. You can read by it, barely. You cannot feel warmth from it.

The concourse stretches in both directions and curves, gently, like a fishhook. You cannot see the end of the curve in either direction. The floor is a fine, dry, pale grit — the color of bone meal — that whispers underfoot. It is the only sound you will make, and you will make it often, because nothing else in the vault makes any sound at all.

## The Material of the World

This is not a city. It is a *calcification* — the remnants of a colony-organism, dead these many centuries, that built itself in shallow tidal flats somewhere a long time ago, when this valley was something other than what it is now. The colony laid down rib by rib, secreting a calcium-carbonate skeleton the way coral does, except that the colony was not coral and the sea it grew in was not water. You can tell because the ribs are wrong: too straight, too regular, and where one meets another there is a small hexagonal boss, like a bolt-head, and a fine black seam that runs through every boss and never quite lines up with the next. The seams form a network in the stone that, if you traced it on a map, would be a chart of the colony's nervous system.

The grit on the floor is the colony's powder, ground fine by the slow crawl of whatever still lives here. There is no soil. There is no dust in the usual sense. When you kick it, it doesn't rise — it *parts*, like a fluid, and closes behind your foot in a moment, and the footprints you leave are already shallower when you look back after ten steps.

The translucent panels above are not windows. They are the colony's *photophores*, the plates that once covered the living organism's upper surface. They have gone dead and cloudy with age, but they are still the only source of light. Where a panel has cracked — and a few have, in long slow radial fractures — the light goes out entirely, and that section of the concourse sits in a deeper dimness that your eyes will never properly adjust to. You learn to step over the cracks. The grit is a darker color there.

## What Grows

Vegetation here is the wrong word. What grows is *structure*: low, slow, mineral things. Against the base of every third rib, a fungus-like crust spreads in concentric terraces, each terrace a different shade of pale — bone, ash, milk, ash again. The terraces are the size and shape of dinner plates stacked. The edges of the terraces are faintly iridescent, the way a piece of oiled steel is iridescent, and if you look at one for long enough it begins to look like it is breathing, though I do not think it is. The crusts are not food. They are not wood. They are something the world is making for reasons that have nothing to do with you.

In the long middle of the concourse, a single specimen has grown large enough to count as a landmark: a pillar of the same material, three meters tall and a meter across, ribbed like a stamen, leaning at fifteen degrees to the vertical. At its crown, the pillar opens into a cup the size of a washbasin. The cup is dry. The cup has been dry for a very long time. The pillar is not. Somewhere inside it, fluid moves — you can see the surface tremble, if you crouch and wait — and the fluid is dark and has the consistency of cold honey. No one has ever reported it smelling of anything.

There are other things. In the alcoves between the ribs at the floor — small chambers, shoulder-deep, shaped like open palms — there are sometimes growths you do not notice the first time you pass. Coiled things. Whitish. They withdraw into the palm-cavities when a shadow falls across them and come out again later, but they come out so slowly that you would not see them move. They are the only living things in the vault that move of their own volition. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are some leftover protocol of the colony, running.

## The Population

The concourse is inhabited, in the sense that a ruin is inhabited. Along the inner curve of the concourse, in the deeper chambers where the ribwork thickens and makes rooms, there are people. A few. They are not a city. They are not even a village. They are an *arrangement* — perhaps eighty souls, perhaps a hundred and twenty, no one has counted for several generations because the count is not useful. They live in the chambers of the dead colony the way a heron lives in a flooded quarry: opportunistically, without claim, taking what the architecture offers.

The architecture offers more than it seems. The chambers have floors of grit. They have ceilings of the translucent dead photophore. They have alcoves the right size for sleeping, alcoves the right size for storage, alcoves the right size for sitting in for hours doing nothing, which is one of the main activities here. The chambers are connected to each other by doorways the colony grew as part of its original geometry — oval, head-height, with the same hexagonal bosses as the rib junctions. The doorways have no doors. Privacy is a chamber you don't walk past on your way somewhere else.

The people are small and quiet. They do not speak above a murmur. Their skin is the color of the grit, or slightly darker, and they wear whatever the previous person who lived in their alcove left behind: a length of stitched fungus-leather, a robe of beaten calcium, a thing that looks like a stiffened hide but is not. Their faces are lined early, and they age in such a way that by thirty they look like the walls, and by fifty they are not much distinguishable from the alcoves themselves. This is not a metaphor. It is difficult to pick a person out from the architecture in the dimness if they are standing still.

They have nothing in the way of *tools*. They have a great deal in the way of *practice*. They can sit motionless in an alcove for six hours and not be noticed. They can tell, by the way a photophore panel sounds when they tap it, whether the colony's residual channels inside it are open or blocked. They can predict, by the smell of the grit, when the wind outside the vault is going to change. They have no need of devices, because the devices they would need are not devices — they are habits, accumulated over a great many generations, of paying attention to a place that is itself paying no attention at all.

## The Air and the Weather

There is no weather inside the vault. There is *seepage*. The colony is not airtight; the photophore panels breathe in some slow osmotic rhythm, and the grit takes up and releases whatever the panels give it. The air inside the concourse is *different* from the air of the world outside, but the difference is so slight and so permanent that the people who live here do not name it.

Outside the vault, there is weather of a kind. Outside is a plain. The plain is dry in the way that the sea is wet — overwhelmingly, unrelentingly, as a category rather than a degree. The plain is a *frost plain*: a region where the temperature never crosses some threshold, where the air is thin and the ground is a fine, pale, mineral powder that never quite consolidates into soil, where the wind is the only moving thing and it moves constantly. The plain is what is left of whatever the colony grew in. The colony, when it was alive, must have lived in tidal flats fed by something that was not water, perhaps a hydrocarbon, perhaps a brine, perhaps a thing the colony itself manufactured. The sea, if it was a sea, is gone. The climate has cooled and dried and the flats have become a desert of carbonate dust that extends, by every account, in every direction, to a horizon that is the same color as the air above it. The horizon is not visible. There is only a *meeting*, somewhere out where the eye gives up, between the powder below and the pearl-white above.

The colony's remains are scattered across the plain. Most of them are low mounds, indistinguishable from the topography around them, that turn out, on close approach, to be single ribs — fallen, weathered, and being slowly buried by the dust. The vault where the people live is the *concourse*: a stretch of the colony's central channel that has somehow been sheltered from the wind, perhaps by a fold of the original structure, perhaps by the slow accumulation of dust around it. The roof of the concourse is intact. The walls of the concourse are intact. Inside, the air is still. The grit on the floor does not move except underfoot. This is the *only* place for a hundred kilometers in any direction where the wind does not reach, and the people live here the way people live in oases: not because the place is good, but because it is the only place.

## Sound and Silence

Inside the vault, the silence is so complete that you become, in time, your own sound. Your stomach moves. Your jaw moves. The grit whispers. After a while, you stop hearing the grit. After a longer while, you stop hearing yourself. The silence, which is not really silence but the *absence* of any competing signal, becomes a kind of texture, like cloth against the ear, and then becomes nothing at all. People who have been here for years speak quietly because the silence is a fact they live inside, and they do not want to break it the way they do not want to break a window.

Twice a day — or what the people call twice a day, though it is not a day, the colony's photophores do not respond to any external cycle — the *settling* occurs. The vault makes a small sound, a single soft *tok*, somewhere in the distance, and the dust on the floor of your chamber ripples outward in a perfect circle, very small, and is still. The settling is the colony's skeleton adjusting to some change of temperature or pressure outside. It does not happen at regular intervals. It does not have a pattern. The people mark time by it, the way sailors once marked time by the creak of a hull.

The coiled things in the palm-cavities make a sound, very rarely, that is not a sound you have a word for. It is more like a change in the air, a faint *click* of pressure, the way your ears pop in a high place. You do not hear it so much as feel that something has happened. When you turn to look, the coil has drawn a little deeper into its alcove. That is all.

## The Long Curve

If you walk the concourse, which is what the people do every day, in the same direction, in silence, alone, for the sake of having walked it, you will see that the concourse changes. The ribwork is denser at the inner curve. The photophore panels are fewer, and more of them are cracked. The grit on the floor is coarser, and slightly darker. The alcoves are deeper. The crusted growths at the base of the ribs are larger, and their iridescence is brighter, and they are not always at the base of every third rib — sometimes every rib, sometimes two ribs, then none for a long stretch. The pattern is not a pattern. It is a *distribution*, the way lichen is distributed on a stone, and the people do not look at it, because to look at it is to begin to look for meaning, and the meaning, if there is one, is not for them.

After several hours of walking, if you have not turned back, you will come to a place where the concourse ends. It does not end dramatically. It ends by *tapering*: the ceiling lowers, the ribs thicken, the photophore panels become opaque and then become stone, and finally the whole thing contracts into a low, blunt arch the height of a crouching child. Beyond the arch is a chamber. The chamber is round, and the ceiling of the chamber is not a ceiling but a *culmination*: all the ribs of the colony, every one of them, gathered together and fused into a single mass that is the shape of a fist, knuckles down, with a small black opening at the top where the seam-network emerges and vanishes into the dark. The chamber is otherwise empty. The floor is not grit. The floor is *something else*: a hard, dark, glassy surface, slightly domed, warm to the touch.

The people do not come here. They know the arch is here. They do not name it. They have a word for it that translates, roughly, as *the listening*, but they do not say it. The chamber, they say, is for when there is no one else. They mean: when the concourse is too full, when the wind outside is too loud, when the silence inside has become so total that something has to be done about it. Then a person walks the long curve to the chamber and sits in the chamber and does whatever they do there, alone, for a time. They do not say what they do. They come back. They do not speak of it.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

It is like being inside a question that the world has been asking for a very long time and has not yet decided to stop asking. The light is dim. The air is still. The people are quiet. The structure is dead. The growths are slow. The wind outside is patient. The horizon is invisible. The dust is fine. The silence is total. The ribs curve overhead like the inside of a ribcage, and the photophore panels glow faintly, and the grit whispers under your feet, and the wind never reaches you, and the light never brightens, and the sound never starts.

It is not a comfortable place. It is not an uncomfortable place. It is a place that has forgotten how to be either, and the people who live in it have, mostly, forgotten too. They live the way the coiled things live: slowly, withdrawn, in the small spaces they have been given, without making much of a mark, waiting for nothing in particular, because there is nothing in particular to wait for. They are the only people in the world. They do not know this. They do not need to know it. The vault continues. The plain continues. The wind continues. The colony's skeleton makes its small sound twice — or three times, or once, or not at all — and the day is whatever length it is, and the night is the same.

The plain is dim and the plain is vast and the plain is *all there is*, except for the vault, and the vault is small, and the vault is all there is too.
