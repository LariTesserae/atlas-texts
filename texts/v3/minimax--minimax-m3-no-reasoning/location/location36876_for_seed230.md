The first thing is the damp. It is not rain, and it is not quite fog, though on certain mornings the two become hard to distinguish. It is the air itself, saturated past the point of indifference — moisture that has had time to think, to find the cold surfaces where it might settle, and to remain.

The coolness here is not the coolness of shade or evening. It is a structural coolness, as though the stone itself were breathing its chill into everything around it. The altitude is what delivers it. This sits above the tree line, below the snow line, in that narrow band where the world has committed to being neither one thing nor another, and is no longer interested in reconsidering.

The stone is a dark grey, almost blue-grey in certain lights, veined with a lighter mineral that catches the diffuse day and holds it. The buildings are not assembled so much as settled into — they follow the contours of a high shelf of rock as though they had been poured, long ago, and then allowed to set. Some are single rooms. Some are two. A few have second stories, though the second stories are small, like afterthoughts of vertical space. None is large. They cluster along the shelf and at the foot of the slope above it, and the spaces between them matter as much as the buildings themselves — wide enough to admit the weather, narrow enough to feel enclosed.

Construction is patient rather than ambitious. Hand-shaped stones fitted together with little mortar; dark wooden lintels above the doors, softened by years of damp; roofs of slate, layered thick, each piece chosen for the way it sits against its neighbor. Metal is present only where it is necessary — a hinge, a latch, the rim of a chimney pot, the small ring by which a door may be pulled shut. The metal is unremarkable. It does not shine. It has taken on the same grey cast as the stone it serves.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It travels in thin threads down the walls during the wetter hours, finding the seams between stones and tracing them patiently. It gathers in shallow basins — cut, or worn — in the upper surfaces of certain rocks; basins the size of cupped hands that fill and slowly empty through evaporation or overflow. There is a channel at the eastern edge, just wide enough to walk beside, that carries what cannot be absorbed. It is mostly dry. But the moisture is in the air, in the moss that has colonized the north-facing walls, in the way the lintels have given up any claim to straightness over what must be a great many seasons.

Vegetation is sparse, and what there is has accepted its terms. There are grasses, thin and pale, that grow only in the cracks of the wider pavements. A moss — dark green, nearly black where the light does not reach — has worked its way into the lower courses of nearly every building and into the joints of the paving. A few shrubs survive at the margins, low and contorted, where water might pool briefly and the wind does not quite arrive. They do not flower, or if they do, it is briefly and without ceremony. Nothing here reaches upward.

The light is what light becomes when the sky has declined to take a position. It comes from above, but without conviction. It is the light of a place that is rarely in direct sun and rarely in deep shadow — a light that has been filtered through so much moisture and so much altitude that it arrives even, gray-white, almost weightless. It does not throw sharp shadows. It does not warm. It illuminates as a courtesy rather than a force, and the shadows it does allow are soft and grudging.

Sound is held to a minimum. The wind moves through occasionally, but the buildings sit close, and the stones do not sing. There is the sound of water — the faint movement in the channel, the slower sound of droplets finding the basins, the occasional patter of condensation released from a slate edge. There is, very rarely, the sound of a door. A latch lifting. A hinge accepting its small weight. And once in a long while, the sound of a footfall on stone, or a voice that has become part of the weather before it reaches you.

Few things move. A bird crosses the sky on certain days, in the direction it was going, and is gone. There are small creatures under the stones — a glimpse of motion, a small shape drawn back into shadow — but they do not announce themselves. The settlement itself is quiet in a particular way: not abandoned, but held lightly. A few figures cross between the buildings at intervals. They move without urgency. They enter and exit without ceremony. They appear and disappear as though they were part of the architecture, scheduled into the place rather than choosing to be in it. They are few. They are the same few, day after day.

Nothing here changes. This is the quality that defines it, though it is not one a visitor would notice immediately. It arrives only after some time — an hour, an afternoon, longer — when the realization settles. The moss is the moss it was. The stones are in the arrangement they have held. The water finds the same paths. The figures move along the same lines, between the same buildings, at the same hours. The light varies, but only within a narrow band. The damp varies, but only slightly. The place has found its form and will not relinquish it.

It is not a stillness exactly. Things do happen. Water does flow. A door does close. But the place has reached a kind of perfect equilibrium — the same matter moving through the same cycles, the same small events recurring in the same order — so that the whole holds still while its parts continue. It is the balance of a system that has run long enough to have forgotten how to do otherwise. What is worn has finished wearing. What is damp has finished drying. What is built has finished being built.

You could stay. You could stay for a long time. You would begin, after a while, to feel that you had not moved either — that the place had accepted you into its pattern, and that your steps were now part of the small set of permitted movements, and that the stones would not shift beneath you because the stones had no reason to.
