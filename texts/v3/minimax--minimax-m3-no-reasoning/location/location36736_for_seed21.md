# The Verdant Antechamber

The place begins as a sound.

Before the eyes learn anything, the body knows it has entered a chamber — not built, but *carved*. The acoustics bend strangely. A breath returns from somewhere ahead, but muffled, soaked. There is the wet-rind smell of recent rain on hot stone, and beneath that, something older and greener, like the exhalation of a forest that has just finished raining on itself.

## The Floor and the Threshold

The ground is a black basalt — young basalt, sharp-edged where it has not yet been worn by the many rains that come and go through here. It is not lava, exactly. The grains are too fine, the surface too smooth in places, as though something poured it. In other places, the basalt rises in plates and curls, like the rind of a great fruit that has been peeled and frozen mid-peel. Between the curls, water stands in shallow warm pools, black as ink because the rock is black, and from each pool a faint mist rises in a slow, breathing column.

It is hot. The air presses against the skin like the interior of a greenhouse, and the wetness makes it feel thicker than it should. One sweats without the dignity of cooling. Somewhere — everywhere — water condenses on every surface that isn't basalt. The moisture forms beads on leaves, on stones, on the obsidian petals of the architecture, and when a breeze moves through the chamber, every surface releases its beads at once, in a shimmer that is felt more than seen.

The basin is small. A cathedral would be larger. This is more like a temple's antechamber — perhaps eighty paces across, perhaps a hundred, with walls that curve rather than end. One can see the whole of it from any point. The walls are not vertical; they slope inward like the interior of an upturned bowl, or the petals of a flower half-closed around noon. At the rim, where wall would become sky, the rock fades into mist and the mist fades into a pale hot sky that is itself nearly white.

## The Vermillion Presences

And in this bowl, *they* stand.

There are perhaps a dozen of them. Tall structures — taller than the trees, which are themselves tall — that rise from the basalt like the masts of ships run aground in a harbor that has forgotten the sea. They are vermillion. Not painted: their color is structural, the way a heart is red, the way a bruise is purple. The color is too saturated for mineral, too even for rust. They look like things that were *grown*, though they have the stillness of things that were *built*. Perhaps both.

Each structure is a different shape, but they share a family resemblance — tapered, rising to a point or a curl, with surfaces that move in slow ridges and twists, like a candle that has burned for an hour and then been frozen. Some are smooth and almost liquid-looking. Some are fluted, with vertical channels running down their length, and from each channel a thin warm stream of water descends and feeds the pools at their bases. The streams make a constant, quiet sound — dozens of small voices, none of them loud enough to be called a sound at all.

The vermillion does not reflect. It absorbs the light, and then — this is the strangest thing — it appears to *have* the light, from within, as though a faint furnace still lives at the core of each one. At sunset, or what passes for sunset under the white sky, they glow like the embers of a fire that has been carefully tended.

At the base of each structure, a black ring of basalt is polished to glass by the perpetual water. These rings are the only paths. One walks from ring to ring, on dark stone, between warm streams, with the mist brushing one's ankles.

## The Forest Between

Between the vermillion towers, the forest grows.

It is not the dense jungle one might expect of a wet, hot place. It is a forest *distributed* — generous of space, sparse of trunk, the way an orchard is a forest. Trees stand thirty or forty feet apart, and each tree stands in its own clearing of dark wet rock. They are broad-canopied, low-trunked things with leaves the size of dinner plates, leaves that catch the falling water and hold it until it spills in fat drops, plinking onto the basalt. The understory is ferns and mosses, vivid green, ankle-deep in places, and where they grow thickest, small bright flowers — magenta, cyan, a yellow so saturated it looks radioactive — push up through the green.

Birds. There are birds, though not many. They are long-tailed, the color of wet slate, and their call is a high, soft, two-note whistle that punctuates the silence rather than breaks it. One can hear one whistle, and then wait twenty heartbeats, and hear the response from somewhere across the chamber. They are talking about things that do not concern a visitor.

Insects exist as hum — a presence at the edge of hearing, more felt than heard, the sound of many small wings in the canopy, just louder than the falling water.

## The Quiet Animations

The place is alive in small ways.

The streams run. The mists breathe — rising and falling in slow cycles that match no obvious clock, though they may match the breathing of something very large and very far below. The basalt itself sweats, as I said, and when the sweat beads coalesce and run, they make new paths in the rock, new channels, new pool-edges that will be there tomorrow and gone next week. The vermillion towers, one notices after a while, are *thinner* at the top than they were yesterday. One comes back to the same tower and finds it has narrowed another inch. Where the matter goes is not known. Into the air, perhaps. Into the mist. Into the future.

The water that falls from the flutes is not cold. It is warm. Bath-warm, almost. It smells faintly of minerals and faintly of something sweeter, like the green scent of cut grass. If one cups a hand and drinks, it is sweet on the tongue, faintly. Not enough to be a flavor. Enough to be a memory of a flavor.

## The Marks of Builders

There are signs that this was not always so.

At the center of the chamber, where the basalt is smoothest, there is a shape carved into the floor — a spiral, perhaps, or a maze, or a diagram of something mathematical that has no name. It is not worn down. It is *intact*, the lines crisp as though cut yesterday, though the basalt around it has the patina of centuries. Whatever cut it, cut through time as well as stone.

Around the spiral, at the four cardinal points, four low pedestals stand. They are the same vermillion as the towers, but small enough to sit upon. On each pedestal is a thing — a stone, a shell, a folded sheet of some metal that is not quite metal, a knot of dark wood. These things were placed here by hands. There are no hands now. But the pedestals are warm to the touch, always, even in the wet heat.

Farther out, almost lost in the ferns, the remains of other things. A wall that ends abruptly. A floor that stops at a precipice of nothing. A staircase that climbs three steps and then enters the basalt and does not emerge. One has the feeling that this antechamber is one room of a much larger structure, and that the larger structure has been *removed* — peeled away, dismantled, taken elsewhere — leaving this one chamber as a sample, or a seed, or a relic kept for reasons one cannot know.

## The Strange Part

It is the towers that are strange. Not their color. Not their height. Their *behavior*.

Stand near one for long enough, and you will notice that the air around it bends slightly. Not the shimmer of heat — there is heat, but this is different. It is as though the tower is a lens, and something behind it is being magnified. Look through one tower at another, and the far tower wavers, doubles, its vermillion surface running like paint in water.

And — sometimes, if the light is right and the mist is low — a shadow will fall from the tower that does not match the sun. A long, thin shadow, pointing in a direction the sun is not. The shadow is darker than any shadow should be. It falls on the basalt and lies there, motionless, for minutes at a time, and then is gone.

What casts it, one cannot tell. Nothing visible does. Perhaps nothing at all. Perhaps the tower is leaning, slightly, toward something one cannot see.

## The Feel of It

The chamber holds a person the way a cupped hand holds a beetle. Gently. With attention. One feels watched, but not by anything that has eyes. The vermillion towers attend. The spiral attends. The four objects on their pedestals attend. One is a guest here, and the courtesy is ancient.

The wet heat makes the body slow. One does not want to hurry. The plinking of water on basalt sets a tempo that is slower than thought. The bird-whistles come and go. The mist breathes.

After a while, one stops trying to understand the place, and the place, in turn, stops trying to introduce itself. There is only the chamber, and the warm streams, and the dark floor, and the red sentinels, and the soft percussion of water falling on stone — falling, and falling, and falling, in a place that is itself falling, slowly, into its own future, one inch at a time, at the top of each vermillion tower, where the matter goes and does not come back.
