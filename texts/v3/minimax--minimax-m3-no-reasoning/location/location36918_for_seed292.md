# The Lower Cataract

---

You arrive by descent.

The rail-line ends at a platform carved into the flank of the basaltic root that supports the world above, and from there you must go down by stair. The stair is a thing of cast iron, green with verdigris where it has been left alone for a season, and the lamps along its rail are not glass but cages of phosphor-moss kept alive by the runoff from the gutters, giving the whole descending spiral a pale, vegetal glow. The railings sing when you grip them: a harmonic hum from the water moving somewhere inside them, the pumped current that runs the lighting and the air and the lift-shafts. Step, ring, step, ring. The stair takes twenty minutes to descend. The air grows heavy, then humid, then wet.

You can hear the cataract before you see it.

---

## The Sound

It is not a waterfall. It is the whole face of a cliff giving way.

The basalt root of the high plateau is pierced by a vertical mile of stratified stone — andesite, limestone, a fossil band of coquina so old its shells have become the rock itself — and through this stratified mile the water has cut a wound four hundred meters wide and twice as deep. The cut is not a clean canyon. It is a chaos of breached ledges and undermined shelves, and the river that comes through it is not one river but a regional drainage: an entire watershed, the entire southern flank of the continent above, focused into a single discharge. Twenty-two thousand cubic meters per second, the gauges say, though the gauges are downstream and half-submerged.

The sound is a sheet of sound. It is felt in the molars and the soft palate. It is so constant that it becomes, after an hour, a kind of silence you have to push through to hear yourself think. Gulls nest in the spray-zone and their cries arrive as faint, glassy tinkles, ridiculous, like wind-chimes at a furnace.

---

## The Air and the Water

The vapor rises in a permanent column. It is not fog, exactly; it is a humid atmosphere thick enough to wring out by hand, and it extends a kilometer up the cliff-face on either side, so that the rim of the cataract — the place where the plateau ends and the fall begins — is invisible from below. You can only tell you are below the rim because the light changes: a flat grey ceiling where the mist meets the spray meets the cloud-cover of the low country. Normal light, yes, but filtered, without shadows, so that the cliff-walls and the buttresses and the wreckage of old stone bridges all appear in a single grey wash, evenly rendered, like a sketch made with a single pencil.

Down at the lip of the fall the wind is brutal — a sideways maelstrom, water-smoke torn into wisps and whips — but here, on the descent stair, it is only a steady boisterous push from below, warm where it shouldn't be (the river is tropical-fed, a long way south, and runs through hot country before it plunges), cool on the iron rail.

There are places where the spray has eaten the stair.

---

## The Descent Town

Halfway down is a town.

It clings to a series of old quarry ledges on the cliff's northern face — ledges cut into the limestone ten thousand years ago for stone that was shipped up by rope-lift to build the first version of the city on the rim, the city that is now in ruins, the city that was abandoned when the river shifted and the quarries went dry and the new cataract began, oh, some seven thousand years before that. The town is called Lower Dyssen, though it is older than anything above it, which is the joke. Lower Dyssen is built on the bones of the older town: terrace-wall over terrace-wall, buttress over buttress, slate roof laid over slate roof.

It is dense. Every square meter of usable ledge is occupied. The houses lean out over the void on cantilevered beams of ironbark (long since petrified to black stone, but still holding), their windows facing the fall so the inhabitants can watch it as they eat. There is a tramline that runs the length of the town, single track, narrow gauge, pulled up and down by the counterweight of water-cars filled at the river-stairs and emptied at the cisterns above. The trams run constantly: a clatter, a bell, a hiss of released brake, a clang of coupling, day and night. The water-cars going down are heavy; the water-cars coming up are heavy in a different way. A perpetual traffic. The bells of Lower Dyssen are the only counterpoint to the cataract, and the people of Lower Dyssen love them, and visitors find them, after a night or two, indistinguishable from the roar.

The population is, by last census, nineteen thousand. The buildings are perhaps four times that. Cats own the alleys.

---

## The Forest in the Mist

Below the town, between the buildings and the cliff-foot, the limestone gives way to a dripping, slumping chaos of landslide and talus, and the talus is covered in growth.

It is not jungle. It is not temperate forest. It is something specific to this place: a low cloud-forest that has been watered, uninterrupted, for ten thousand years by the spray of the world's largest fall. The trees are shallow-rooted, leaning, leaning always outward from the cliff as if they are trying to leave. Their trunks are slim and pale and draped in moss so thick the bark is invisible. Their leaves are flat and horizontal, oriented to catch the falling mist rather than the absent sun. Everything is in tiers. Ferns on the ground. Shrubs on the rocks. Trees above the shrubs. Moss on the trees. Lichen on the moss. A small pale frog, bright green, on the lichen on the moss on the bark of the tree on the rock on the talus below the house on the ledge above you.

The forest resounds. Not with birdsong — birds are rare here, the updraft is wrong for them — but with the burble and trill of a thousand small running waters. Every surface is wet. Every leaf is a fountain. There are springs everywhere, fed by the same aquifer that the river has not yet found, and they come out of the cliff-face in clear arcs, glittering faintly in the diffuse grey light, and they meet the talus and split and meet again and tumble toward the cataract. Walking in the forest is walking in a thousand small waterfalls, each one cold, each one with its own voice. To stop is to be surrounded.

In the forest, the cataract is so constant you can hear your own heartbeat.

---

## The Edge of the Plunge

From the bottom of the descent stair you come out onto a quay.

The quay is built of cyclopean masonry — boulders the size of rooms, mortared with a lime that has gone hard as bone — and it runs along the eastern lip of the cataract, where the cliff curves back to let the river breathe before it falls. From here you can see, if you push to the railing (which is a good iron railing, well-braced, because people do push to the railing), you can see the river as it gathers.

It is half a kilometer wide at this point, and it is moving. Not fast — it looks slow from here, the way a tide looks slow — but it is moving. The water is brown with silt from the southern highlands. It is thick with floating vegetation: whole trees, snapped off in storms upstream, rotating slowly, dragged toward the rim. They go over, of course. Everything goes over. There is a place a few hundred meters out where the speed of the current exceeds the speed at which you can walk, and beyond that line is the lip, and beyond the lip is the air.

On the far side of the river, the cliff has been cut back into a great amphitheater, and on the shelves of the amphitheater you can see, with binoculars, the ruins of older infrastructure: the rusting wheels of pumping stations, the bent skeletons of cranes, the broken stairs of a funicular that once carried tourists down. There was a tourist trade, once. There are fewer tourists now. The machinery remains.

---

## The Plunge Itself

You do not see the bottom.

You know it is there because the spray comes up — a constant exhalation of white water from somewhere down in the deep — and because, on clear days, you can hear, very faintly, the second cataract. The river, having fallen a vertical mile, hits a pool, rebounds, falls again into a deeper pool, and goes on, and on, and on, in a series of cataracts that have been cut into the bedrock of the original continent, the ancient continent, the one the basaltic root was part of before the world changed its mind.

The whole thing has been eroding for ten thousand years. The cliff retreats, on average, four centimeters a year. The town of Lower Dyssen has been moved inland, ledge by ledge, four times.

---

## The Industry

The industry of Lower Dyssen is power.

It is an industrial town because it sits on the throat of the fall. There are turbines in the bedrock — eight great machines, each the size of a cathedral, each fed by a penstock cut through the cliff at a lower elevation than the quay. The water comes down the penstocks under the pressure of a vertical mile of column and spins the turbines at speeds that make the rock around them sing, and the rock around them is reinforced with iron bands that have to be replaced every forty years because of fatigue. From the turbines, the power is taken up the cliff on armored cables and distributed across the plateau above: to the cities of the interior, to the rail-net, to the smelters and the foundries and the tram-yards.

There is no other reason to be here. The town exists to mind the turbines, and to replace the iron bands, and to dredge the penstocks, and to grease the bearings, and to watch, always, for the creeping retreat of the cliff-edge.

The industry makes the town loud in a different way than the cataract does. There is a percussive clang from the forge where the iron bands are recut. There is a hiss from the hydraulic presses. There is the warning bell of the lift-shaft every time the water-cars are sent up. There is the rattle of the foundry, where the old turbines are melted down and the new ones are cast. The town rings. The town is always ringing. A visitor once described it as "living inside a clock," which the locals took as a compliment, though they would not have minded "anvil," either.

---

## The Rules

The river does not stop.

This is the rule of the place. It has not stopped since the cataract began. It will not stop while the watershed drains. The turbines turn; the power goes up; the town is fed. In return, the town minds the turbines, and the cliff retreats, and the town moves back, ledge by ledge, every century or so, and the older ledges go over the edge, eventually, and are lost in the spray. There are churches on the cliff-face that are now only visible from certain angles, when the spray parts. The great bronze bell of the original town was rescued and is now in the museum above, and is rung, once a year, on the anniversary of the founding, and its note is the only bell in the region that can be heard above the cataract. It takes twenty-two seconds to fade.

Below the town, in the cloud-forest, in the spray, the pale green frogs sing in the moss. Above the town, on the plateau, the wheat ripens in the fields that are watered by the river that has not yet found its fall. Everything is connected. Everything is in motion. Nothing is still.

The stair goes up and the stair goes down and the river does not stop.
