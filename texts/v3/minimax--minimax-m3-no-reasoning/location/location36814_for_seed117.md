# The Weeping Hall

The first thing you notice is the sound of water, but you cannot see where it comes from.

A low, persistent susurrus moves through this place the way wind moves through tall grass—except there is no grass. The walls are basalt and old iron, furred with something that is not quite moss but holds moisture the way moss does. It is a kind of lichen, perhaps, or a kind of rust that has learned to drink. The color of it is the color of a bruise held under running water for a long time: deep purple-black at the core, paling to a gray-green at the edges where it thins into bare stone.

The ceiling is low. Not low the way a mine is low, but low the way a chapel is low—deliberately, almost intimately. You could reach it if you stood on the relics. The architects, if there were architects, understood that what happens here happens close to the body.

---

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps from the joints between stones. It beads on every iron surface like a skin of glass that will not quite fall. It hangs in the air itself, a fog so fine it feels less like weather than like a property of the place, the way temperature is a property. When you breathe, you taste iron and something older than iron—mineral, maybe, or the dissolved memory of something that lived here once and stopped. The condensate collects on your eyelashes if you stand still too long. The stones glisten. There is no dry surface. There may never have been one.

And yet nothing rots. There is nothing to rot. The walls are not walls in the way a house has walls—they are faces of a continuous body of rock that has been *revealed*, not built. The hall is a chamber inside a larger body, a lung inside a ribcage. You walk through it the way blood cells walk through a capillary: in the dark, wet, surrounded by surfaces that press close.

---

What grows here grows in the grammar of rust.

There are structures that look like fungi but are made of corroded copper, their caps spread wide as dinner plates, their stems threaded with hair-thin wires that once carried current and now carry nothing except a faint, almost subliminal vibration you can feel through the soles of your feet if you stand near them. There are fans of mineral deposit that look like ferns—white, chalky, layered like pages of a book left open to weather. There are tubeworms of crystallized salt, calcium-white, rising from cracks in the floor in clusters so dense they resemble coral. Nothing here metabolizes in any way a biologist would recognize. Things accrete. Things oxidize. Things precipitate out of the wet air and harden into forms that echo, distantly, the shapes of living things without ever having lived.

This is a mineral forest, then. A slow forest. A forest that has had ten thousand years to forget what forests are for.

---

The faunacrouches in the alcoves.

At first you think they are statues. They are the size of large dogs, low-slung, with limbs that fold under them in three places instead of two. Their hides are the same dark, wet stone as the walls, and when they are still they are nearly invisible. They breathe through gill-slits along their ribs that flare softly open and closed in the wet air. Their eyes—if they have eyes—are clusters of small reflective lenses, like the compound eyes of something older than insects, something that lived in seas before seas had names.

There are dozens of them. They rest in the shallows where the floor dips and the water gathers an inch deep, and they watch you with a patience that is not hostile but is not welcoming either. It is the patience of something that has been waiting for you to do something interesting for a very long time and is not yet convinced you will.

Sometimes one moves. The motion is liquid. It pours itself from one alcove to another without apparent effort, without apparent sound, and you realize the susurrus you have been hearing is partly them—the wet drag of their bellies on wet stone, the soft tidal opening and closing of their slits.

They do not eat you. They do not approach. They have been here long enough to know that whatever you are, you are not what they are waiting for.

---

The machinery is quiet now, but it was not always.

It stands along the walls in long ranks, repeating forms: cylindrical housings the size of wine barrels, ribbed with cooling fins, mounted on iron cradles that have themselves begun to corrode into the floor. Cables run between them, sagging under their own weight, furred with the same purple-black growth that covers everything else. Where two cables meet there is often a junction box, and on each junction box there is a small glass window, and behind the glass a needle, and the needle still, faintly, trembles.

Some of the cylinders have cracked open. Inside, the mechanisms are visible: coils of copper wire gone green, shafts of something harder than steel that has not rusted at all, lenses that still, when the dim light catches them, throw a small bright coin of reflection onto the ceiling. You do not know what they were for. You understand, looking at them, that they were for *making something*, and that what they made is now the air you are breathing and the water on your face.

You understand this the way you understand a language you do not speak, by the shape of the grammar.

---

The light has no source you can find.

It enters the hall the way water enters a sponge—distributed, diffused, originating from the substance of the stone itself, which has a faint phosphorescence in its deeper layers. The illumination is the color of a screen left on in an empty room. Nothing casts a shadow longer than itself. You can see, but you cannot see far; the air itself is too thick with moisture and whatever else is suspended in it to permit clear sight beyond a few body-lengths. Shapes resolve slowly as you move toward them, the way shapes resolve in dreams.

Somewhere in the hall there is a brighter place. You have not found it yet. You suspect it is where the machinery is still running, deep in the rock, and that if you found it you would not be able to look at it.

---

The air tastes of copper and time.

This is a place that has been wet for a very long time, and dark for almost as long, and inhabited by things that do not require light, and the things that required light have gone somewhere else or stopped requiring it. The water does not flow; it migrates. It climbs the walls in capillary defiance of gravity and falls back in slow, patient sheets. The salt tubeworms grow a millimeter a year. The fan-ferns accrete a layer a decade. The creatures in the alcoves breathe and do not age, or age at a rate indistinguishable from the rock.

It is cool here. Cool the way a cellar is cool. Your breath does not fog, but it might as well; the air is already so close to saturation that any further moisture is invisible, indistinguishable from the rest of the wet.

---

You are inside something. The something is breathing. The water is its breath. The creatures are its thoughts, or its dreams. The machinery is its memory of being awake. The mineral forest is what its attention looked like when it turned inward, very slowly, and began to grow.

You are standing in the lung of something vast and patient and asleep, and the susurrus you hear is the sound of it still breathing, and it will continue to breathe for a long time after you have stopped being here.

The needles in the junction boxes tremble.

The creatures watch.

The water does not fall.
