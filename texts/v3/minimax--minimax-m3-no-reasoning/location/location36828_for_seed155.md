# The Hanging Gardens of Lower Verdigre

---

The first thing you notice is the sound.

It arrives before anything else resolves—before your eyes adjust to the dim beneath the fern-arches, before the smell of cool mineral air and slow-rot sweet registers, before the ground (such as it is) becomes a thing you can stand on. The sound is a *thrumming*. Not music. Not machinery exactly. But something between the two that has been going on so long that the word *humming* no longer does it justice. It has texture. It has weather. It hums upward and the upper canopy answers; it hums downward and the iron-shod roots answer; it hums sideways and a district of the city answers in another key, and the keys never resolve but they never quite clash either. It is the sound of a system that has been running for so long that it has become the silence of the place. If you stop to listen, your own heartbeat will feel like an interruption.

You are standing on a road, technically. A road of black slag and rusted rail, layered and patched, reinforced with twisted cable and the petrified ribs of something large. Beneath the slag is a thing that used to be a road. Beneath that is a thing that used to be a road. The archeologists—those who still care about such things, which is almost no one anymore—say there are nine roads in the road. They have only catalogued five.

The city is called Lower Verdigre, although no one in it knows what the *Verdigre* was for, and the *lower* is a polite concession. The actual settlement occupies a kind of shelf on the inside of what was once a mountain range, before the mountain gave up being a mountain and became a long, eroded spine of hump and saddle stretching away to the east. From a distance—say, from one of the high overlooks where the old cog-rail still climbs the cliff face on a cable that nobody inspects anymore—Lower Verdigre looks like a green vein stitched into grey rock. Closer in, the green is no longer a vein but a *skin*, and the city is a kind of rash living underneath it.

Everything here is overgrown, and yet nothing is wild.

The dominant plant is something the locals call *spine-fern*, though it is not a fern in any taxonomy a real botanist would recognize. It grows in long curving stalks, fifteen, twenty, sometimes forty meters tall, that arc out of the rock and the slag and the broken pavement and unfurl into crown-fronds the size of small houses. The stalks are woody and dark, the color of dried blood, and they are threaded through with metal—actual metal, woven into the plant's structure the way a tree might incorporate a fencepost, so that as the spine-fern grows it slowly absorbs and digests the iron and copper and whatever alloy the founders of the various buried roads used. The crown-fronds are pale, almost luminous, a dusty silver-green, and they filter the light that comes through the high cloud cover into a diffuse, sourceless glow. There is never a hard shadow in Lower Verdigre. Everything is bathed in a kind of permanent twilight that is bright enough to see by and dim enough to flatten. It feels, when you first arrive, like the moment after closing your eyes in a lit room—the world reduced to a felt thing.

Between the spine-ferns, the other plants.

There are *mosses* here that are taller than you. They grow in pale hummocks against any vertical surface—the cliffs, the ruin walls, the ferrocrete bridge-buttresses—and they move in a slow visible way, a finger's width in an hour, bulging and subsiding, as if breathing. They are warm to the touch, faintly. They are what the children of the city sleep on, when the children sleep, which is not often. There are *vines* that drop from the high arches of the old viaducts in curtains, some as fine as hair, some as thick as your thigh, in colors that are not colors so much as *moods*: a blue that feels like grief, a red that feels like hunger. The vine-curtains are useful. They are the walls of the city. They are also the doors. They part when you brush them and re-knit behind you, and the locals have learned, over the centuries, which vines will let you through and which will not, and they have not learned this from any book, because the vines change their minds.

There is a *cabbage*. A single cabbage, growing in the center of the central plaza, on top of the sealed manhole-cover that the city sealed in 1143 and forgot about and that the cabbage found anyway, in the year 1382 or thereabouts, and that has been growing there ever since. The cabbage is the size of a chapel now. It is the size of three chapels. It is the size of the chapel that used to stand on the plaza before the cabbage ate it. Its leaves are a deep, almost indigo green, and they have a faint phosphorescence at the creases, so that at night the whole center of the city glows a soft bruised violet, and the light it gives off smells faintly of anise and of something older. The cabbage is not worshipped. The cabbage is *attended to*. There are people whose job it is to keep the cabbage from the moss, and people whose job it is to keep the moss from the cabbage, and people whose job it is to keep the spine-ferns from both, and all three groups are in a state of polite, perpetual, low-grade warfare. They are all on the same city council. They hate each other with the weary intimacy of people who have known each other for too long.

The city is built *up* the cliff face and *along* the cliff face and, increasingly, *into* the cliff face, in layers and in folds, the way a damselfly's wing folds. The earliest construction, half a kilometer up, is crumbling into the slope and has been almost entirely consumed by a slow, patient, dark-leafed ivy that no one has been able to identify. The newest construction, down in the lowest switchbacks where the old drainage canals still carry a thin stream of cold water, is built of moss-brick and living wood, shaped and grown rather than nailed and mortared. The middle layers are *industrial* in the truest sense: black iron, riveted plates, the angled geometry of a civilization that had inherited the engineering language of a much older one and was speaking it with a thicker accent. Those middle layers are the bones of the city. They are also where most of the people live, because the iron keeps the temperature even and the vines do not grow on iron very well, which means you can see out of the windows.

The windows are important.

There is a thing that the people of Lower Verdigre say, when you ask them about the windows. They say: *the windows are the only honest part of the building*. They mean that the building will lie to you—will present a wall, will present a door, will present a floor that is not a floor, will rearrange itself in the small hours when the mosses shift and the spine-ferns lean—and the windows, the plain rectangular windows cut into the iron frames, will not. You can look out of a window and see what is actually there. Sometimes what is actually there is not what was there yesterday. Sometimes what is actually there is a vine-curtain, or a soft green nothing, or the inside of a moss-hummock. The city is *honest* in this narrow way. It tells you the truth through its windows. The buildings themselves will whisper that the cliff is closer than it is, that the floor is a step down, that the door is a wall, that the room you are in is the room you came from. The buildings lie, gently, in the way that dreams lie. The windows do not.

You get used to it.

Most of the people have a small ritual they do in the morning, before they leave their iron-walled rooms. They stand at the window and look out, and they look at the windows of the building across the way, and they see whether the people in the building across the way are themselves today. If the people across the way are waving, you may go outside. If the people across the way are not waving, you stay inside. The people across the way are usually themselves. They are not always.

The city has a *sound*, apart from the thrumming. The thrumming is the constant. The variable sound is the *machinery*. Lower Verdigre is an industrial city, even now, even mostly consumed by its own flora, even after the long slow decline. There are engines running. There are always engines running. They run because they have always run. They run because the people who are supposed to turn them off are dead, or were reassigned, or are themselves lying. The engines are in the deep levels, below the city, in the *underthing*, and they are the reason for the thrumming. They are vast and they are not fully understood, even by the engineers, who are a hereditary caste and who do not, on the whole, encourage questions. The engines power the cable-rail that still, somehow, runs up the cliff face, although no one remembers what was at the top of the cliff face, and the people at the top of the cliff face are not the same people who are at the bottom, in the way that a reflection is not the same as the thing reflected. The engines pump water up from the old mines. The engines light the central plaza's cabbage, faintly, when the cabbage's glow is not enough. The engines do other things. The engineers will not tell you what. They are very polite about not telling you. They will offer you tea. The tea is a gray infusion made from a moss that does not grow anywhere but the engine rooms, and it tastes of nothing in particular, and it is warm, and after you have drunk it you will not want to ask questions anymore for an hour or two. This is considered a courtesy.

The fauna is sparse, in the sense that the engineers use the word *sparse*, which is to say that the fauna is *concentrated*. There are birds. There is one bird. It is a large dark bird, the size of a small child, with a long curved beak and a taste for the small bright insects that live in the crown-fronds of the spine-ferns. The bird is silent. It has never been heard to make a sound, although it moves its beak as if it is making sounds, and the people of Lower Verdigre have a tradition of lip-reading the bird, and of arguing about what the bird is saying. There is a school of thought that says the bird is a *linguist*. There is a school of thought that says the bird is *broken*. There is a school of thought that says the bird is the only honest witness in the city and is trying to tell the people something the windows will not say. The schools of thought have formed a coalition. The coalition is fragile. The bird has flown away, for now, to the upper cliffs, where it nests in the ivy. It will return. It always returns. It always returns at the same time of year. The time of year is *soon*. The time of year is *always soon*.

There are also, in the lower levels, *things* in the water. The water is a thin cold stream, a finger's depth, that runs along the old drainage canals. Sometimes, in the night, if you walk along the canals, you will see a light in the water. The light is the color of a bruise. If you reach in, the light will close around your hand, gently, like a glove, and it will feel warm, and you will feel *seen*, and in the morning you will not be quite sure what your hand looked like before you reached in. No one reaches in anymore. There is a small fine for reaching in. The fine is levied in *time*. The people who are fined in time are sent to work the cabbage. They do not seem to mind. They come back, eventually, and they do not talk about what they did.

The weather is *damp*, the way a stone wall in a basement is damp. The moisture is not a *thing* that happens here so much as a *condition* the place is in. The cliff face weeps, in places. The condensation gathers on the iron and the slag and the mosses and the windows. Sometimes, in the higher switchbacks, there is a real rain, a thin cold needle-rain that comes sideways off the eroded peaks to the east, and when the rain falls, the spine-ferns arch their crowns toward it and the whole upper layer of the city *hums*, a deep subsonic note that you feel in your sternum, and the mosses on the walls brighten, and the vines, even the cold blue ones, flush for an hour, and the city, for an hour, looks the way it looked in the old pictures, in the pictures that nobody has anymore but that everyone remembers.

The temperature is cool, consistently, year-round, the way a cellar is cool. It does not vary by more than a few degrees. The temperature is kept stable by the engines, and by the cliff face, and by the mosses, and by the cabbage. The cabbage, in particular, runs a fraction of a degree cooler than its surroundings, which is why it is possible to sleep against the cabbage in the central plaza in the summer, although it is always summer, and the cool is welcome.

The erosion is *ancient*. The mountain that was here is no longer here. The thing that is here is what the mountain became when it gave up being a mountain: a long, settled, humped, near-flattened spine of grey rock with a deep, layered, vegetated crease down its center, the crease being Lower Verdigre, the crease being about twelve kilometers long and never more than three wide, and the city, such as it is, lives in the bottom half of the crease and climbs the sides. From above, on a clear day, the crease is just visible as a faint dark line in the rock, and the spine-ferns, at their tallest, rise just above the rim, and the bird, when it is flying, is a small dark comma against the sky.

The light is *normal*, the way a late-afternoon light in early autumn is normal, in a place where autumn is the only season. The clouds are persistent but not heavy. The sun, when it is visible, is a pale washed disc. The cabbage, at night, gives off a faint violet glow that is just enough to navigate by, and the engineers will not tell you why, and the glow is the color of the lights in the water, and the people who are fined in time do not talk about it, and the bird, when it returns, will have a small bright insect in its beak, and the insect will be glowing faintly, and the people will lip-read the bird, and the bird will not say what they want it to say.

The scale is *open*, in the sense that you could walk for a day in one direction along the crease and you would not reach the end of it, and you would pass through other small settlements, smaller than Lower Verdigre, with their own cabbages and their own birds and their own mosses and their own small honest windows. These settlements do not, on the whole, like one another. They trade in moss-tea and vine-curtain and engine-parts. They have a shared calendar, based on the return of the bird. The bird returns to each settlement at a slightly different time. This has been the cause of three small wars. The wars were fought politely. The wars are over now. The bird did not return at the agreed-upon time. The bird returned when it wanted to.

The density is *sparse*. The people of Lower Verdigre number in the low thousands, scattered along the crease, with the largest concentration in the central switchbacks around the cabbage. They are not unfriendly. They are not friendly. They are *present*, the way a ruin is present. They have work. They have tea. They have the windows, and the bird, and the cabbage, and the engines, and the mosses, and the small fine for reaching in, and the small hours when the buildings lie, and the small hours when they tell the truth. They get up. They drink the gray tea. They look out the window. They see whether the people across the way are themselves. They go about their day. They come back. They look out the window. They see what the windows see.

The weirdness is the part that nobody talks about, and that everybody, always, is talking about. The city lies. The cabbage eats. The buildings rearrange. The windows tell the truth, and sometimes the truth is that the building is no longer there, that the cliff has opened, that the people across the way are not themselves today, that the bird has come back early, that the engines have stopped, that the engines have *started*, that the cabbage is glowing a different color, that the mosses are warmer, that the vines have re-knitted in a new pattern, that the light in the water has touched someone, that the small fine has been levied, that the bird, when it returned, said something the lip-readers could not agree on, that the engineers are *smiling* today, that the cable-rail is going up, that the cable-rail is going down, that the cable-rail has gone somewhere else, that the underthing is humming a new note, that the underthing is not humming at all, that the underthing is listening.

The weirdness is not a thing that happens here. The weirdness is the *weather*. It is the daily medium. It is the cool damp air you breathe. It is the small fine for reaching in. It is the morning ritual of looking out the window. It is the gray tea, and the warm handshake of the engineer, and the small bright insect in the bird's beak. It is the fact that you have been here for an hour or for a year, and you are not sure which, and the windows are not telling you, and the buildings are.

You stand on the road. The thrumming is everywhere. The spine-ferns arch above you, filtering the light, breathing in the slow motion of the mosses, leaning toward the cliff face in a wind that does not seem to be a wind. The vines part for you, or they do not. The cabbage glows in the center of the city, a soft bruised violet, and someone is attending to it, and someone is attending to the mosses that are attending to it, and somewhere in the deep levels an engine turns over, and somewhere on the upper cliff the bird flies, silent, beak moving, and the lip-readers argue, and the engineers drink their gray tea, and the children sleep on the warm mosses, and the city, in its honest windows, shows you what is actually there.

The buildings, all around you, are lying.

The cabbage is breathing.

The bird, you think, is trying to say something.
