# Inhabitants of the Upper Roost

What lives here is what made the roost the roost, and what made the roost is what keeps living here. The place and its dwellers are the same long sentence, and the sentence has been speaking itself for longer than anyone can say.

---

The bats are the tenants, and the landlords, and the walls.

They live in the dark the way fish live in water — not as occupants but as the medium itself. A person who walks into the deeper grottos and looks up will not see them at first. The eye will register only rock, only the patient polished surface, only the places where a faint dampness marks the breathing of the cave. Then the eye will adjust, and the rock will begin to move, and the person will understand that what they had taken for surface is population. Thousands upon thousands upon uncounted thousands, packed into every crack and seam, hanging head-down from every overhang, their folded wings wrapped close like the cloaks of a congregation at prayer.

They are small. Each one is no bigger than a person's thumb, when a thumb is held at reading distance and the eye is honest. Their fur is the color of the rock — a dark indeterminate brown that drinks the light rather than reflecting it. Their ears are large for the size of them, leaf-shaped, furled, almost ornamental; when one stirs in its sleep the ears move first, and the rest of the animal follows after, as though the ears are the part that dreams. Their faces are small and oddly folded, the way a face folds when it has spent its evolutionary energy on other things. The nose-leaf — that strange fleshy ornament the bats wear above their muzzles — gives each one the look of having dressed for a ceremony no one remembers.

They do not all look alike. There are at least three kinds, perhaps four, and a person who pays attention will begin to tell them apart the way a person begins to tell apart the trees of a familiar wood. The little ones, the ones no bigger than a beetle, live deepest in the cracks and come out last and return first; they are almost weightless, almost rumor. The middle-sized ones — and a person who has spent enough evenings here can think of them as the ordinary citizens, the backbone of the roost — pack the walls in dense folds, shoulder to shoulder in some places, and their murmur is the constant sound of the place. The larger bats, the ones with the broader wings, prefer the high ceiling of the main bowl, where they hang in loose clusters and drop, at dusk, like a slow unraveling, each one falling for a long moment before remembering what wings are for.

The bats make the air. Not metaphorically. The warmth of ten thousand small bodies, breathing and digesting and sleeping and dreaming, raises the temperature of the upper roost several degrees above what it ought to be at this altitude, and the warmth collects in the bowl the way water collects in a cupped hand. The bats make the smell, too — that mineral sweetness, guano and musk and the slow chemistry of a thousand small digestions — and the bats make the sound, the continuous low percussion of ten thousand wings stirring in their sleep, a sound so constant that it has become a kind of silence, the way a heartbeat becomes a kind of silence to the person whose heart it is.

A person cannot stay here long without beginning to feel that the bats are the place. The rock is what they cling to. The air is what they warm. The dark is what they fill. Remove them, and the upper roost would still be a bowl of volcanic stone, but it would not be itself; it would be a place that had been evacuated, a church after the congregation has gone.

---

The pale salamander lives in the wet cracks of the deepest part of the bowl, where the light from the late afternoon never reaches and the condensation gathers in permanent seams. It is not so much seen as suspected. A person who cups water from one of the post-storm puddles may feel a small cool wriggle against the palm, and a quick glimpse of something the color of uncooked dough, and that is usually the whole of the encounter. The salamander is eyeless, or nearly so — the sockets are there, but the eyes themselves have retreated into folds of skin and given up their duties. It does not need to see. It does not need to be seen.

What it is doing in the wet cracks, the salamander knows. It is eating whatever is small enough and slow enough to be eaten in the dark — springtails, perhaps, or the larvae of the pale moths, or the smaller things that live in the biofilm of wet stone. It is breathing through its skin, which is the color and the texture of wet porcelain. It is waiting, in the patient amphibian way, for the next puddle to form and the next small wriggling thing to wander past.

It is, a person suspects, very old as a kind. Not this individual — individuals do not last long in places that have so many efficient small predators — but the lineage of it, the shape of it, the way it has arranged itself to live in this particular wet crack for the many generations that this particular wet crack has existed. The salamander is what salamanders were, in the place where salamanders first began. It has not needed to become anything else.

---

The pale moths are enormous for moths. A person who finds one resting on the damp wall — and they rest openly, plainly, with no attempt at camouflage, as though they have forgotten that anyone might want to eat them — will see a wing-span wider than the palm of a hand. The wings are the color of old paper, almost translucent, veined with darker lines like the lines on a very old map. The body is thick and furred, the way the body of a moth that lives in cold places is furred, as though the animal is wearing a small coat against the perpetual chill.

They hang in the corners. They hang from the undersides of the slight overhangs where the water does not reach but the dampness does. They hang, when the wind comes through in the afternoon, and vibrate — a fine persistent tremor that shakes the wings but does not move the body, as though the moth is trying, in some patient and hopeless way, to warm itself by shaking. A person who watches long enough will see that the moths are arranged in a particular way. They are not random. They are placed. The younger ones are at the edges of the colony, where the air moves more freely. The older ones are deeper in, where the warmth of the bats gathers. The arrangement has the look of a small society, of a long-settled household with its customs and its conventions.

Where the caterpillars feed, no one has ever quite determined. Somewhere on the mountain, perhaps far below, in the wet green places that the upper roost only smells of. The moths come up here to sleep, and to wait, and to mate, and to do whatever moths do in the long cold hours when the wind comes through the bowl and the bats are out hunting the upper air and the roost is, for once, almost quiet.

---

The spider is a question.

It lives — if it is a single spider, and not a succession of spiders maintaining a single web the way a succession of monks maintains a single temple — in the great doorway-web between two columns of volcanic rock near the western edge of the bowl. The web is large. A person could walk through it without ducking, and would, if they were not careful. The web is old. It is not a single sheet but a layered accretion of sheets, each one added on top of the last, each one mended and reinforced and mended again until the whole structure has taken on a kind of permanence that no single web could claim. The silk has yellowed. The strands are thick. In places the web has pulled free of one anchor and been re-tied to another, and the architecture of these repairs has its own logic, its own slow argument with gravity.

A person can press a palm into it. The web gives, and the layers give, and the whole structure shivers and resettles and is, a person discovers, stronger than it looks. Whatever has been caught in the web over the years — the small dry husks of moths, the bright husks of beetles, the occasional feather from a bird that wandered too high and was caught — has been incorporated into the structure, so that the web is, in places, more debris than silk, and the boundary between the spider's architecture and the spider's larder has become unclear.

The spider itself is rarely seen. It waits, presumably, in some deeper fold of the web, in the part that is most layered and most debris-laden and most like the rock around it. A person who sits still long enough may see a slow movement, a shifting of one of the layers, a brief and patient attention. The spider is large, but not enormously so. Its color is the color of the rock. Its patience is the patience of the place.

---

The stunted fir is not an animal and so does not, strictly, belong here. But it lives here in the way that anything which has persisted for longer than memory can be said to live anywhere. It is rooted in the soil that has accumulated in the crack at the eastern end of the bowl — soil that is, in fact, the substance of ten thousand generations of bats, returned to the stone and then, in the slow patient way of mountains, returned to the possibility of soil. The fir is small. It is older than small. It is the remains of smallness, the way a person who has outlived their era becomes the remains of an era. The trunk is twisted. The branches lean eastward, permanently eastward, the way a weather-vane leans in the direction of the prevailing wind. The needles are short and gray and have the look of having given up on being green, the way a person who has lived too long in the dark gives up on certain colors.

It does not grow. This is not quite true; it grows, perhaps, a hand's breadth in a decade. But it does not grow in the way that trees are understood to grow. It persists. It puts out, each year, the smallest possible gesture toward being a tree, and the wind takes most of that gesture and the cold takes most of the rest, and what is left is the fir's contribution to the upper roost: the single vertical fact, the single upright thing, the single reminder that this place is not only horizontal and downward and dark.

Around its roots, in the brief weeks after the rains, a thin fur of moss appears. The moss is the color of memory. It is the only green the upper roost ever sees, and it is here for such a short time that the fir may be forgiven for leaning toward it, the way a person in a long dark room leans toward the window.

---

What lives here, then, is what lives everywhere that has been itself for long enough: small dark flyers in the cracks, pale blind things in the wet places, enormous moths in the corners, an old spider in its old web, a stunted tree that has forgotten how to grow but has not forgotten how to stay. They are not, a person understands after sitting here long enough, the inhabitants of the place. They are the place. The bowl is the shape their living has worn into the stone. The dark is the color of their gathered warmth. The smell is the smell of their slow long digestion of one another and of themselves. The sound — the constant low percussion, the ten thousand small wings folding and unfolding — is the sound of the place breathing in its sleep.

A person who sits with their back against a damp column and listens will, after a time, stop being a visitor. The bats will not know the person is there. The salamander will not care. The spider will wait. The fir will lean. The moths will hang in their corners and tremble in the wind. And the person will begin, very slowly, to feel what it is to be one more small warm thing in a place that has been full for a very long time, and will be full after, and does not, in any of its long indifferent patient arrangements, require the person to be there at all.
