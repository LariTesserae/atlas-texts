In the Garden of Last Breath, what lives is the question itself, and the answer is something older than questions.

---

The **trunk** is the first inhabitant, though calling it an inhabitant is generous — it is the *architecture* of habitation, the body that every other body consents to be a cell within. It does not live the way you or I live. It lives the way a mountain lives, the way a river lives, the way a word lives when it has been spoken so many times it has worn a groove in the air. It is black because black is what wood becomes when it has had long enough to think about itself. It is cool because cool is the temperature of having decided. It is ridged like a throat because it is a throat — it is the place where the world takes in what it needs and lets out what it has finished with. Water moves through it. Air, such as there is, moves through it. Time moves through it the way blood moves through a sleeping animal: slowly, darkly, without urgency.

The trunk does not think. I want to be careful here. It does not *think* the way we think. But it *holds*, and the holding is a kind of knowing, the way a cathedral knows — not by being conscious, but by being *present* in a way that consciousness could not improve upon.

---

The **moss** lives. This is the truest sentence I can tell you about this place. The moss lives in the way that breathing lives — involuntarily, continuously, without the dignity of choosing to do so. It hangs in curtains a hundred feet long, and it sways, and the swaying is its only opinion about the world. It does not reach for anything. It does not retreat from anything. It is what a thing becomes when it has given up on ambition and settled into *duration*.

The moss is old. Some of it is older than the language I am using to tell you about it. When I say old I do not mean *ancient* in the way we mean ancient when we talk about ruins — I mean *continuous*. I mean it has not stopped being moss for so long that moss has become less an identity and more a *state of being*, like wetness, like patience, like the color black.

The moss is also the substrate. It dies at the bottom of its length and is born at the top, and the dying part falls and becomes floor, and the floor is moss again, and the floor grows moss, and the moss grows moss. It is a conversation the place is having with itself about what moss is, and the conversation has been going on for so long that no one remembers who started it.

---

The **ferns** live slowly. They are committed to slowness in a way that almost feels devotional, the way a monk is committed to silence. Their fiddleheads are the size of small rooms because they have had small rooms of time to become. A fern here unfurls over a year, over a decade, over a span of years that would make a human feel embarrassed about their own urgency. When it finally opens, the sound is a sigh — not a human sigh, not an animal sigh, but the sigh of a *structure* relaxing into the form it was always going to take. The sigh is wet. The sigh is soft. The sigh is the closest thing this place has to speech.

The ferns are not aware of being watched. This is a kindness. They are not aware of being slow. This is also a kindness. They are not aware of anything, which is, in a place this old, a kind of grace.

---

The **flowers** live in pulses. They are pale — the bone-white of deep-sea things, the luminous white of creatures that have made their own light because no one else was going to do it for them. They pulse together, mostly, though the togetherness is imperfect. Some lag. Some lead. The whole field of them is a kind of choir that has been singing the same note for so long that the note has become a *condition* rather than a sound.

The flowers do not know they are beautiful. I want to note this. Beauty in this place is not a thing that exists for an observer. Beauty here is a *byproduct* — the thing that happens when a system has been in equilibrium long enough to develop a kind of symmetry that no one was aiming at. The flowers are not trying to glow. They are not trying to pulse. They are simply *being*, and the being has, over millennia, become luminous. The being has, over millennia, become rhythmic. The being has, over millennia, become the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat.

---

The **fish** — if they are fish — live by drifting. They are pale blue points in the dark, hung in the water column with their mouths open and their fins spread, and they eat what passes them, which is mostly spores, mostly pollen, mostly the tiny luminous eggs of something that has not yet decided what it is. They have no eyes, or they have eyes that are not for seeing, which is a distinction I am not qualified to make. The eyes glow. The eyes glow because the eyes are for *being seen* in a place where being seen is the only form of company.

The fish do not know they are lonely. They are not lonely. They are *alone* in a way that does not have a word for itself, the way a star is alone, the way a sound is alone after the bell has stopped. They drift. They eat. They glow. They die, eventually, and fall, and become the silt, and the silt becomes the moss, and the moss becomes the fish, eventually, in a process so slow it has no name.

---

The **insects** — if they are insects — live in columns. They are the color of the wood, which is the color of the dark, which is the color of everything here. They crawl in long patient lines along the bark, eating the moss as they go, and the moss grows back behind them at the same rate they consume it, and this is the contract the place has made with them: *you may eat, if you let yourself be eaten by the growing*. They are not in a hurry. They have never been in a hurry. They are the most committed things here, in the way that a person who has been doing the same job for sixty years is committed — not with passion, not with enthusiasm, but with the deep structural commitment of a thing that has forgotten there was an alternative.

If you sit still for an hour, they will walk across you. They will not notice. You are bark to them. You are moss. You are a slow part of the place that has decided to be still for a while, and they will cross you the way a river crosses a stone — without malice, without acknowledgment, with the long patience of something that has been crossing stones since before stones had a word for being stones.

---

And there is the **something in the heartwood**.

I do not know what it is.

I have only heard it. It makes a sound — a low groan, a low hum, a low *something* that does not have a name because naming it would imply someone had survived hearing it clearly. The sound comes from deep in the trunk, from the place where the wood is oldest, where the wood has become more wood than it was when it was a tree, where the wood has perhaps become something else entirely.

It moves once every few years. When it moves, the whole tree shifts, and the water shivers, and the moss swings, and the flowers flicker out of sync for a moment before finding their rhythm again. The shift is small. The shift is enormous. The shift is a kind of *breath* — the long slow breath of a thing that has been holding its breath for a very long time and has, just now, allowed itself to exhale.

I do not know what it is. I do not think anyone knows. I do not think *it* knows. It may be the oldest living thing in this place. It may be the place itself, dreaming. It may be the remnant of whatever was here before the tree, before the water, before the dark — a kind of residue, a kind of witness, a kind of memory that has grown so large it has become indistinguishable from the thing it remembers.

It is the size of the question rather than the size of an answer.

It is also, possibly, asleep.

---

What lives here, then, is not a collection of beings. It is a *system* — a single vast slow body that has, over the immensity of its own duration, decided to be many things at once. The moss is its hair. The flowers are its pulse. The fish are its thoughts, drifting. The insects are its small unconscious actions, the digestion of its own digestion. The trunk is its skeleton, or its skin, or its memory. The something in the heartwood is its *self*, if selves are things that can be located, which in this place they cannot.

The whole place breathes. I said that at the beginning and I will say it again because it is the truest thing I can tell you. The whole place breathes, in the way that a sleeping animal breathes, in the way that the ocean breathes, in the way that a held breath eventually releases itself into the next breath. The breathing is not fast. The breathing is not loud. The breathing is the slowest music in the world, and it has been playing for a very long time, and it will continue playing for a very long time after anyone who has heard it has become part of the silence that the music is played *for*.

You are very small here.

The place is very old.

These two facts are the same fact, from different angles.
