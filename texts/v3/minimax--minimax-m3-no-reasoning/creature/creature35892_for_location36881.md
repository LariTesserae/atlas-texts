What lives here has not yet finished arriving.

Start with the lichens, because they are the most legible, and because legibility is a kind of mercy on a coast that otherwise refuses to be read. They lie on the highest stones in thin black commas, no thicker than a fingernail's patience, and they have the look of things that have been argued onto the rock rather than grown. Which is, in a sense, what they are. A lichen is not one organism. It is a federation, a slow treaty between a fungus that cannot photosynthesize and an alga that cannot feed itself, drawn up in acid on a substrate that has not yet decided to be a substrate. The basalt it grips is only ten thousand years old. The lichen is younger. Neither is in any particular rush, and the negotiation between them — the chemical conversation conducted at the boundary layer where the thallus meets the stone — is being held at a volume so low that even the rain cannot hear it. But it is happening. You can see where it has happened. A faint softening. A dulling of the matte black into something almost, not quite, brown. The lichen is teaching the rock to be weathered. The rock is teaching the lichen to be patient. Neither lesson is yet complete. The lichens here are, in this sense, not pioneers so much as probationary members of a community that does not yet exist, sitting at a desk they have not yet been assigned, filling out forms that have not yet been printed.

But they will hold. That is the strange thing. They have the grip of organisms that have not decided whether they will survive, and yet they will. Not these particular thalli — those will be scraped off in some future storm, or rolled under by some future shift of the unfinished ridge — but the *idea* of them, the lichenness, the lichen-strategy, will outlast the basalt they sit on. In a hundred thousand years, when the sea has eaten this coast twice over and the lava field has been tumbled into cobble, the lichens will still be here, in some revised form, doing what lichens do. They are the first draft of an ecology, yes, but they are also something more particular: they are the first draft of a *memory*. The rock is being written on. The writing will outlast the page.
