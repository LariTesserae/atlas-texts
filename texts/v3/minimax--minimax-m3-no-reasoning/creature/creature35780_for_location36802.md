Who lives there? Not many, and not for long. But they are there, the way lichen is there.

The ravens are the senior residents, and they behave like it. There is a pair — it might be more, but you rarely see more than two at once — that nests on the upper cross-bracing of the eastern bridge tower, in a saddle of angle iron where the wind eddies and the cables hang their green tassels. The nest is a deep, ragged thing, rebuilt every spring with whatever the pit offers: copper wire stripped from abandoned spools, a length of nylon conveyor belt, dried grass pulled from the haul road shoulders, the odd white plastic cable tie, blue, that catches the sun when the sun bothers to reach the bridge at all. The ravens are heavy birds, oil-black, with a trick of sitting on the cable stays at the exact point where the cable meets the tower, perfectly balanced, watching the pit below with one yellow eye. They are not tame. They are not wild, either. They have a relationship with the operation. They know the blast schedule by the day. They know that when the night shift starts its first round, there will be, twenty minutes later, a detonation somewhere on a lower bench, and that the detonation will flush mice and voles and the occasional grouse from the blast pattern, and that the mice and voles and grouse will be confused and exposed and good to eat. They are already in the air before the siren sounds. The miners have names for them, mostly unprintable. They consider the ravens colleagues.

The fox is a different kind of resident, or rather a different kind of visitor who has become, through repetition, something like a fixture. She — the pit workers say *she*, though no one has checked; this is a guess that has hardened into fact — is a red fox in the third year of her coat, which is to say she has the dark legs and the white-tipped brush of an adult and the still-slightly-clumsy way of moving that an adult red fox has when the country she is moving through is not quite the country she grew up in. She lives, as far as anyone can tell, in a drift of blast rubble on the upper northwest rim, in a cavity between two boulders that were never cleared because no one had a reason to clear them. From there she ranges down into the pit on a circuit that takes her, depending on the day, to the sump, to the base of the primary crusher, to the long grassed-over ramp on the south wall where the haul trucks no longer go. She hunts the mice. She is very good at the mice. The mice of the pit are fat and unafraid, the offspring of generations that have known nothing but the soft abundance of spilled ore concentrate and the warmth of the conveyor galleries, and the fox takes them one at a time, at the edges of dusk, with a quick stiff-legged pounce that ends in a single snap. She eats them whole when she is hungry. She caches them, in the pit's own way, in the cool air space under a haul truck that is parked for the shift. In the morning, when the miner comes out to start his truck, the fox is already gone, and there is a mouse in the wheel well, half-eaten, stiffened by the night. The miner kicks it out and does not think about it. This is a transaction. The pit allows it.

The fox has learned the wind. The wind in the pit falls, as has been noted, and when it falls it brings the cold down in sheets, and the fox hates the cold. She has learned that the east wall, the wall that gets the brief morning stripe of sun, is the warmest wall by a margin that matters, and that the heat is held there, in the rock face, for several hours after the sun moves off. She has learned to den, on the bitter days, in the lee of the coarse ore bin, where a drift of windblown grit has built up against the steel and formed a kind of natural windbreak. She has learned that the sound of the crusher is a constant and means nothing, and that the sound of a truck coming down a ramp means the truck is empty and is not a threat, and that the sound of a truck going up a ramp, under load, means the truck is loud and heavy and she should be elsewhere. She is, by pit standards, a genius.

The trout are the strangest residents, and the most anomalous. They live in the lowest sump, in water that is, by rights, not trout water: it is too warm in summer from the mill discharge, too cold in winter from the air, too high in suspended fines, too low in dissolved oxygen, too laced with the slow bleed of the iron pipeline. And yet there are trout. They are small — the largest, by report, is perhaps nine inches, though no one has caught one in years, because no one fishes the sump, because the sump is a sump, and because the trout are not the point. The trout are a residue. Someone, long ago — the story goes that it was a foreman on the night shift who had a cooler full of fingerlings and a vague plan and too much time between blasts — someone released them. And they lived. They bred. They eat, in the evenings, the insects that have come from nowhere, which is to say the insects that have come from the moss and the willow and the clematis, the small Diptera that hatch from the wet felt of the old haul roads and the mayflies that rise from the sump margins in pale clouds when the air is still. The trout rise. You can see them, if you are down at the sump at dusk in July, in the brief interval when the mill lights come on but the headlamp beams have not yet swung that way. You see the rings. You see the small dark shapes dimpling the black water. The sump is loud at that hour, because the pumps are running and the slurry is being discharged from the secondary crusher a hundred meters up the wall, and a fine white rain of fines falls constantly into the water, and the trout rise through it. They do not seem to mind.

The moths are not one species. They are a community, or rather a succession. The mill building is lit, all night, by banks of high-pressure sodium lamps that throw an orange wash across the crushed-ore apron and out over the lower rim. The moths come to the light from everywhere — from the tailings, where the heat of the decaying pyrite raises the local air temperature by two or three degrees; from the willow stands, where their larvae have fed all summer on the leaves; from the conveyor galleries, where the belt carcasses harbor a particular warmth-loving fauna. They come in numbers that are hard to credit unless you have stood in the apron at midnight and felt them hit your face. They are small, mostly, and they are the color of dust. They die on the concrete. By morning the apron is furred with their bodies, and the morning crew sweeps them into a pile with a push broom, and the pile goes into a contractor bag, and the bag goes onto a pickup truck, and the truck goes out to the tailings, and the moths are spread across the tailings, and the gulls that have followed the truck, because gulls follow trucks, eat them. The gulls are not residents. The gulls are commuters. They come in from the coast, fifty kilometers away, in the morning, and they leave in the evening. They are part of the operation.

The moose was not a resident. The moose was a visitation, and the photograph of it — standing in the sump, water to its belly, head up, looking at the crusher — is taped to the inside of the shift supervisor's pickup truck, where it has been for nine years, and where it has begun to curl at the corners and fade. The moose was, by the account of the haul truck driver who saw it first, a young bull, in hard antler, and it had come down the main ramp in the half-light of an October dawn, walking against the flow of the empty trucks, and the trucks had stopped, and the moose had continued down, and no one had blown the horn because no one was sure what the protocol was. It spent the day in the pit. It stood in the sump. It browsed — there was nothing to browse, but it made the motions, lowering its head into the willow thickets on the sump margin and pulling up nothing. In the evening it walked out the way it had come in. It was never seen again. The photograph is a record of an event, not a tenure. The moose is included here only because the pit remembers it, and because the pit does not have many things to remember.

The mice are not mentioned often, but they are the foundation. They live under the conveyor galleries, in the warm dry dust of the idler housings. They live in the wall rocks, in the cracks that the freeze-thaw has opened, in the pockets of fines that the slurry leaves in the high-angle troughs. They live in the mill building, in the cable trays, in the false ceilings, in the corners where the insulation has come loose. They are *Peromyscus*, mostly, and they are the color of the rock, and they are not afraid of the sound. They are the most numerous large animal on the site. The fox takes them. The ravens take them. The blast takes them in numbers when the blast is in a wall they have colonized, and the mice that survive the blast run out into the open and stand still, stunned, and the ravens are already in the air. The miners see them. The miners do not mind the mice. The miners are glad of the mice, because the mice are evidence that the place is alive, in the way that a place with mice in it is alive, and a place without them is not.

The people are not residents either, though they are the loudest. They work the pit in shifts. They come in on crew buses from the camp fifteen kilometers south, or in their own trucks, or on the company bus that runs from the town. They wear hard hats and coveralls and they are very tired, in the way of people who work under sodium light, and they move in a slow shuffle from the bus to the dry to the equipment they have been assigned. They speak to each other in a register that does not carry. They are not, as a group, given to noticing the ravens, except to curse at them when the ravens sit on the cable stays above the loading bay and drop things, which they do. The fox they have a name for — *clever girl*, someone once said, and it stuck, and now the night shift calls out to her when they see her on the haul ramp, and she ignores them. The trout they have forgotten. The moths they sweep up. The moose is a story they tell to the new hires.

The people do not live in the pit. They go home. They go home to the camp, or to the town, or to the houses on the ridge above the town, and they sleep, and they dream — those who can sleep — of the sound, and the sound is in the dream, and when they wake the sound is not there, and the absence is the loudest thing they have ever heard.

The moss is the resident, if anything is. The moss is everywhere. The moss is in the cracks of the concrete, in the seams of the steel, in the felted dust of the abandoned haul roads. The moss is the *Andreaea* on the high north wall, the *Racomitrium* on the older benches, the *Sphagnum* in the sump margins, the *Polytrichum* on the disturbed fill. The moss does not sleep. The moss does not notice the sound. The moss does the slow work, which is the only work that lasts.

The willow is a resident. The alder is a resident. The rowan, the birch, the feral clematis, the honeysuckle that has run a hundred meters up a conveyor support and burst into white flower at the top — they are residents, in the way that plants are residents, which is to say they are not going anywhere, and they are not in a hurry, and they have, each of them, put down a root system that is more permanent than the structure it is growing through.

The hole remains. The hole is the resident, in the end. The hole is what lives there, in the sense that the hole is the only thing that has been there the whole time, through every blast and every shift and every willow and every moth. The hole was there before the crusher. The hole will be there after the crusher. The hole is not interested in the crusher. The hole is interested, as far as the hole is interested in anything, in the slow, patient work of the water and the cold and the root, and it is content to let the machines run, and it is content to be filled, and it is not in a hurry.
