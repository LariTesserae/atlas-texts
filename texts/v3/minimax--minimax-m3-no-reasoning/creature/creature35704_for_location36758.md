# Who Lives in the Weeping Hall

The first thing to understand is that they do not think of themselves as living in a remarkable place. The Weeping Hall is, for them, simply the place where they live — the way a cliff is the place where seabirds live, without the seabirds needing to remark upon the cliff. They have grown into the damp the way the fig has grown into the brick, and to ask them about it would be a little like asking the fig about the mortar. They have their answers, but the answers are practical and small.

There are perhaps thirty people in the station at any given time, though the number shifts. Some are passing through, and some are the ones who belong to the gallery, the upper wooden walkway that runs the length of the place at second-floor height, and the structures that have been built along it.

## The Old Woman at the Basin

The most present figure, the one you would notice first if you entered, is an old woman named either Maren or something that sounds like Maren, and she is almost always on the gallery. She is sorting. She is always sorting something. Today it is beans, dried, brought up from somewhere below — from a storage room perhaps, or from the broken waiting room where the pigeon droppings are ankle-deep. Tomorrow it might be buttons, or pieces of copper wire stripped from the dead conduits in the walls, or seeds, or screws. She has a copper basin, wide and shallow, and she pours things into it from a tin cup, and the things run across the curved bottom and she reads them the way a fortune-teller reads tea leaves, and she separates them into smaller piles, and the piles go into smaller tins, and the tins go onto shelves behind her in the shack that was once a stationmaster's office.

She is not the leader. There is no leader. But she is the one who knows where things are, and when a child comes up the ladder from below with a handful of something found, she is the one who is consulted. Her face is the brown of the bricks. Her hair is the grey-green of the Spanish moss, or the Spanish-moss-relative, that hangs from the ceiling. She wears a long dress of indeterminate color, patched, the patches themselves patched, and on her feet she has boots that are too large for her and that she has stuffed with something at the toes, and the boots are forever wet, and she does not seem to mind. Her hands are red at the knuckles from the water, the way a dish-washer's hands are red, and they move fast when she is sorting, a practiced rotation that has nothing to do with age.

She speaks rarely. When she does, it is in a voice pitched to carry over the water-sound, and it is usually a single noun. *Brass. Here. Three.* Or *No. Not that one.* Or, very rarely, a longer sentence, and the longer sentences are the ones that mean something, and the children repeat them to each other later, half-understood.

She has been here for a very long time. She was here when the pump last ran, she says, though she may be lying. She has a memory of the place that is not a memory of a dry place. She does not remember the station dry. She knows the station as a wet thing, and so her memory is not a memory of decline but of slow increase, of wetness accumulating, of the fig thickening, of the moss lengthening, of the cat growing older.

## The Children

There are perhaps eight or ten children, ranging from small ones who cannot yet climb the ladders to adolescents who have begun to take on work. They are the most visible, because they are the ones who move. They cross the broken platform below with the ease of animals in their own terrain. They have names that are short, sometimes single syllables, sometimes nicknames that have stuck — Tin, Moss, Fig, the last because she was born under the fig tree and has the pale bark-colored skin of someone whose mother ate a lot of figs during pregnancy, or so the story goes. They are brown, mostly, like the woman. A few are lighter, the descendants of people who came through. They wear clothes that are the wrong size, inherited, and the clothes are patched and damp, and the patches are damp, and the damp is the ordinary condition of cloth here.

The children do not play the way children play in drier places. Their play is quieter, more observational. They watch the water move. They watch the leaves uncurl. They have contests of patience that an outside observer would not recognize as contests — who can sit stillest on the platform edge while a drop forms on the brick above them and falls; who can spot the lizard first as it moves along a joint; who can identify, by smell alone, which of the dangling festoons of moss is oldest. These are not games they invented. These are games that are appropriate to the place. They are learning to read the Weeping Hall the way Maren reads the beans.

A few of them have tasks. The smallest ones fetch water from the catch-basins, which is clean enough, filtered by a cloth, to drink. The middling ones sort with Maren, learning the metal and the vegetable. The oldest ones have begun to take responsibility for the gallery structures — mending a roof, propping a beam, replacing a board that has finally rotted through. They learn carpentry from the older adults, but a strange carpentry, because the wood they work with is always wet, and the joints must accommodate expansion, and the nails are the wrong kind because the right kind has rusted away.

The children are not afraid of the dark interior, the wet brick, the sounds of water. They are not afraid of the fig, even though it is the largest living thing in the place and is slowly eating the building they live in. They understand the fig as an elder, a slow creature, a thing to be respected but not feared. They do not climb it. They know better. The branches are too high, and the trunk is too smooth with the moss that grows on it, and the fig, if you disturb it, drops its fruits, which are not yet ripe, and which hit the platform with a wet heavy sound that the children find ominous without knowing why.

## The Man on the Annex

There is a man who lives in the eastern annex, behind the door half-eaten by the strangler fig. He is the keeper of the pump. He is not its mechanic — there is no one who could be its mechanic, anymore — but he tends it, oils it where the oil will hold, wipes the rust from the flywheel's lowest point, keeps the catch-basin clear of the fern that keeps trying to grow in it. He has a theory about the pump. He believes that if the pump were started again, it would lower the water table and dry the station. He does not want this. He wants the pump to be runnable, in case anyone ever needs it, in case the water rises, in case the balance tips toward flood. He is preparing for a future that may never come. He is the only person in the station who thinks about the future in this way. The others think about it the way the fig thinks about it — incrementally, biologically, in terms of root and branch.

He is a thin man, brown-haired going grey, with the permanent squint of someone who has been looking at dim wet things for a long time. He wears a leather apron over his damp clothes, the leather cracked and mouldy at the edges, and he has tools that he has made himself from pieces of other tools. He does not speak to Maren. They have a disagreement, unspoken but felt, about the pump. She thinks it is dead, and she is content for it to be dead. He thinks it is sleeping, and he is vigilant. They pass each other on the gallery without greeting.

He is unmarried. He is probably not unhappy. He has the pump.

## The Cat

The orange cat is not a person but is, in some ways, the most personable of the inhabitants. It is old. It has been here longer than anyone now living, or so Maren claims, and the claim is plausible because the cat is, by any objective measure, ancient — its fur is matted and faded to the color of old brass, its teeth are mostly gone, its movements are slow and considered. It lives on the dead pump. The iron top of the firebox is warm, faintly, from the decomposition below, and the cat has chosen this as its bed, and no one has objected, and the man from the annex does not chase it off because the cat is, in some way, the proof that the pump is still a pump — that it has a living thing on it, that it is not yet fully a ruin.

The cat does not hunt. It is too old to hunt. It is fed by the children, who bring it scraps, and by the lizard, which has an arrangement with it — or so the children say. The lizard eats the insects. The cat lets the lizard live. The cat eats what it is given. It is content, or it is what passes for content in a cat, which is the same thing.

It is named, possibly, *Pump*, though no one calls it that to its face.

## The Lizard

The lizard is everywhere and nowhere. It is small, the length of a finger, and it lives in the brick joints, and it is the same green-brown as the moss and the wet brick and the clothes of the people. It eats insects. It is eaten, sometimes, by the owl. It is the most numerous vertebrate in the station, more numerous than the people, and it is the one that has been here longest, having arrived before the station was abandoned, or having descended from such an ancestor. It is not tame. It is not wild. It is simply present, in the way that water is present.

## The Owl

There is an owl. It roosts in the upper reaches of the vault, where the brick meets the old iron of the roof structure, and it comes out at dusk and hunts the moss for the small mammals that live in it — mice, perhaps, or the local equivalent. It is pale, droppings-pale, and its call is not the hoot of a familiar owl but something shorter, sharper, a single note that cuts through the water-sound and makes the children look up. It does not interact with the people. It is a different kind of inhabitant — a guest, in the way the owl is a guest in any human structure, briefly, on its way through.

## The Travelers

There are people who come and go. The Weeping Hall is, despite its ruin, on a route. There is a path through the overgrowth that leads out and up to the surface, and from there to other places — a village, perhaps, or other ruins, or a road that still has traffic. The travelers come down through the wet arch and stand blinking in the dimness, and they are loud, by the standards of the place. They have dry clothes. They have boots that are not stuffed. They are uncomfortable, and they show it, and the residents of the gallery watch them with the polite detachment of people who have seen travelers before.

The travelers sometimes stay. A couple of the children are the children of travelers who stayed, or who stayed long enough. A few of the younger adults came as travelers and are now residents. The man from the annex may be such a one — no one is sure. The travelers sometimes bring news. The travelers sometimes bring disease. The travelers sometimes bring nothing.

The travelers do not understand the place. This is fine. The place does not require understanding. It requires only to be lived in, which is what is happening.

## The Dynamic Among Them

There is no government. There is no economy, in the strict sense. There is an exchange — Maren sorts and stores, the man from the annex maintains, the children fetch and mend, the travelers bring things from outside. It works because it is small. Thirty people can be held in a mind, or in a few minds, and the things that need to be done are the things that can be done by thirty people in a wet place with limited materials. The fig grows. The water falls. The pump sleeps. Maren sorts. The man oils. The children learn. The cat sleeps on the warm iron.

There is no ambition here, in the sense that ambition requires a direction. The direction is downward, into the wet, into the green, into the slow biological consumption of the structure. The residents have accepted this. They are not waiting for the place to dry out. They are not waiting for rescue. They are not waiting for the pump to be restarted, except for the man from the annex, who is waiting for a reason that may be obscure even to him. They are waiting, in the way that all living things wait, for the next thing, and the next thing will be wet, and will be green, and will be small.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are, like the place, exactly what they are.
