The townspeople move the way the water moves. This is not a metaphor. Visitors, if any arrive, notice it within an hour: there is something in the gait of these people that has been tuned, across more generations than they keep count of, to the slow lateral pull of warm currents and the slow vertical pull of the rain. They are not languid. Languid implies a contrast with something faster. They are simply matched. Their resting pulse is what yours would be after a long swim. Their arguments take the length of an afternoon to resolve, and an afternoon here is not a small thing, because the light is the same all day, and so an afternoon is measured by the small domestic clocks of hunger, and the falling of a particular leaf from a particular vine at a particular window.

Physically — and noticing this is rude, and the noticing is what makes them look away — they are a people adapted for a place that does not, technically, need to be inhabited. Their eyes are large, dark, and slightly upward-tilted at the outer corners, an inheritance from some long-ago ancestor whose pupils had to drink in more of the bruised lavender light than is normal. The sclera is not white; it is the color of the underside of a river stone, a gray-blue that blends with the weather and makes their gaze hard to catch. Their skin is darker than you expect — not from any sun they receive but from a pigment that has thickened across generations in the wet, a built-in oiliness that sheds water the way a duck's does. Their hair is straight and very dark and tends to lie flat, because the air is too wet for it to do otherwise. They are slim, in the way of people who pull boats through currents rather than ride them, and they have a particular development of the forearms and the small muscles of the lower back that is, in town, considered merely normal and is, anywhere else, distinctive. Their feet are wide and the toes are prehensile in a way that lets them grip wet basalt the way you or I would grip a ladder rung. They wear little — fiber wraps at the waist, a sleeveless tunic of woven leaf in the wetter months, nothing at all when the rains slacken, which is rarely and never for long. Bronze is the only metal they have, and they wear it sparingly — a band at the wrist, a small hammered pendant that has been in a family so long it has become a kind of bone. The bronze tarnishes in the wet. They polish it. The polishing is a small daily ritual that no one remarks upon.

Their voices are low. Not whispering — there is no need to whisper, the town is not so large and the walls are not so thin — but pitched low, as if the throat has been taught over centuries that there is no point in trying to out-shout the rain. They have a singing, and the singing is something they do in groups and is mostly in a mode that sounds to outsiders like a kind of low moan, but it is structured, it has phrases, and it is usually performed at the waterline at the hour — the notional hour — when the boats come back from the deep with the harvest. They sing to the harvest. They sing to each other. They sing, possibly, to what lives below the harvest, though no one will say this directly, and if you ask, the singing will stop for a moment and then resume as if you had not asked.

The harvest. Let me describe the harvest.

The deep water — below the hundred meters where the violet light finally goes out — is home to a thing they harvest, and the thing they harvest is not one thing but several things at once, in the way that jellyfish are several things at once. It is a colonial organism. It drifts in the warm deep, and it has the property that, when lifted into air, or into the colder shallower water near the surface, it luminesces: a pale green-blue light, the color of the bruise-light at midday but colder, and steadier, and faintly pulsing, as if the lifting into a different medium had woken something. The people harvest it in glass jars stoppered with wax. The jars are not their invention — the glass is not their invention — and this is one of the small mysteries of the place, because there is no sand here suitable for glass, and there is no fire hot enough. The jars are inherited. They were here before the town, perhaps, or they came with the town, or they are made by a process no one living remembers, and the people treat them with the particular reverence that one accords to a thing that has outlived its own explanation. The glowing harvest is their nearest approach to currency. It is traded within the town, between the town and the smaller causeway settlements, and, very rarely, to the traders who come in the deep-prowed boats from somewhere that may or may not exist. The traders do not say where. The traders do not say much at all. They leave jars of salt and jars of a brown powder, a kind of ground root, and they take the glowing harvest away, and they pay for it in more jars, sealed ones, that the townspeople crack open on festival days and that contain, depending on the season and the whim of the trader, a single preserved specimen of something else from somewhere else — a curiosity, a piece of a world the townspeople have never seen. The traders are paid in smiles and in not-being-asked.

The harvesters are their own caste, their own inheritance, and they are a particular kind of person. They go out in the small boats at the notional hour that is not dawn and not dusk but somewhere between. They lower themselves, or they send down weighted nets of braided vine, or they send down family members who are trained from childhood to hold their breath for a length of time that is frankly improbable. The deep water is warm, which helps. The harvesters are leaner than the rest, with lungs slightly larger than the rest, and their skin has a faint greenish cast that the rest of the town does not have and that they themselves do not discuss. They are quiet. They are patient in a way that is more than the town's ordinary patience — they have to be patient with the deep, which has its own time. They come back smelling of brine and of something else, something mineral and faintly sweet, and they are treated with a kind of respect that is not quite awe and is not quite pity. Some of them do not come back. The town has a ceremony for this, and the ceremony is conducted at the waterline, and the jars of glowing harvest are broken open into the sea, and the town sings the low song, and the light from the broken jars spreads on the water in patterns that, if you watch for long enough, seem to move against the current.

The deep. The townspeople will not say what lives in the deep. They will tell you — if you are patient, and if you do not press, and if you can find the harvester who has been out far enough and come back far enough to say it — that there is something down there. Not a creature. Not, exactly, a place. Something that has the quality of a gaze, the way a large body of water has the quality of a gaze, except that it has focus. It is slow. It is warm. It thinks, the harvester will say, and the word *think* is used in a particular way, a way that means more than thought and less than dreaming. It has been there, the harvester will say, longer than the water has been there. This is the mythology part. This is the part that may be true. The harvester will not say whether they have seen it. The harvester will look at you for a moment with those dark tilted eyes, and then they will return to mending their net, and the conversation will be over.

Children here are born with the rain on them and are wrapped, immediately, in a fiber that has been oiled against the wet. They are dark-eyed from birth, and they are quiet in a way that visitors sometimes mistake for dullness and that is, in fact, the ordinary attention of a child who is learning, very early, that there is no hurry. They learn the boats before they learn to climb well. They learn the names of the currents before they learn the names of the other children. They eat, in their seventh or eighth year, their first soil-thing, in private, with an elder, and they are told, then, a story that they will not retell. By the time they are old enough to ask what the story was, they understand why it is not retold, and they do not ask again. The elders of the town are thin and slightly stooped and have voices that are very low and very slow, and they sit on the buttresses of the weeping trees, and they tell the stories of the time before the water — different versions, depending on who you ask, the slow flood or the single night, the ridgelines as the backs of things that did not finish drowning — and the stories have the cadence of mythology rather than history, and the elders themselves seem to understand this, and they do not press their version on anyone, and they listen, with the same patience they bring to everything else, when the younger ones tell a different version, and no one tries to reconcile.

The creature at the waterline.

There is something that lives in the splash zone and the lower currents, something that moves between the ridgelines in a slow undulating path that the people know and that the people have given a name for in their language which you will not learn. It is long — three meters, four when grown — and dark, with a hide that is thick and slightly pebbled and sheds water in sheets. It has four short legs and a long flattened tail and a head that is broad and low and that holds its mouth slightly open, always, the way a crocodile does, and the teeth inside are visible and are not for eating but for gripping. It eats fish. It eats the small bright fish that school in the warmer surface currents and the slower things that drift below. It eats, occasionally, the things the harvesters do not bring back. It is not aggressive toward people. People are not in its diet. People and the creature have a kind of mutual regard — the people know where it hunts and do not fish in those places, and the creature knows the boundaries of the town's waterline and does not cross them. Once a season, when the harvest is good, the people will leave a fish on a particular stone at a particular hour, and the creature will come and take it, and this is a kind of covenant, and it has been observed for longer than anyone can say. The creature is old. Specific individuals have been known — marked, in the way that people mark such things, by notches on the ear-scales or scars on the flank — across multiple human generations. There is a creature now who is known to be in her fifth decade. The town regards her with the particular fondness reserved for something that is not a person but that has, by long acquaintance, become a kind of neighbor. She has a name. She has had names before. The names are not spoken aloud except at the leaving of the seasonal fish, and then only by the eldest child, who has been instructed, and who delivers the name in a low voice to the water.

The metal-birds.

They live in the upper massif, in the fog you can almost chew. They are large — the size of a small child when adult — and their feathers are a dark iridescent green-black that throws off, in certain lights, a flash of violet. Their beaks are long and slightly curved and they use them to probe the crevices of the basalt for the small pale soil-things that are also eaten, in secret and in private, by the people. The birds' calls — and the calls are the reason for the name — are not like any bird-call you have heard. They are a single sharp note, repeated, that has a resonance in it as if the air were denser than it is, as if it had been struck from inside a column of water. The sound carries. It carries across the wet air in a way that sound should not carry, and the people use it, in a way they may not entirely admit, to tell the time. The calls begin at one notional hour and end at another. The middle hours are characterized by their absence, and their absence is a kind of quiet that is the quietest the place ever gets. The metal-birds are loud. They are louder than seems appropriate for animals of their size. They are also beautiful in a bleak way, with their dark iridescence and their long pale legs and their slightly melancholy posture when they stand on a high rock in the fog and look out at nothing in particular. They mate for life. They are known to mourn — the surviving bird of a pair will sit, for what may be days, on the rock where the other died, and will not call, and will not eat, and the people leave food out for it, and do not speak of it, and after a time it goes back to its life diminished.

The soil-things.

Let me describe them, even though the people will not. They live in the thin soils of the upper massif, in the cracks and pockets of organic matter that accumulate between basalt slabs. They are pale — white, almost translucent — and they are blind, and they are the length and thickness of a finger. They move slowly. They feed on the decayed leaf matter of the weeping trees and the droppings of the metal-birds and, possibly, on something else, something more mineral, that lives in the basalt itself. They are eaten. They are eaten roasted, in private, usually at the end of a meal that has otherwise been public, and the roasting is done quickly over a small fire of dried vine-fiber, and the smell is, by all accounts, faintly sweet and faintly chalky, and the people who eat them do not eat them in the presence of those who do not, and there is a kind of low embarrassment about it, the embarrassment of a people who have a food they have not entirely made peace with. The reason for the embarrassment is not entirely clear. It may be that the soil-things look, when they are brought up, too much like something else — like a finger, like a small white root, like something that might, in another context, be the early stage of something larger. It may be that they are slightly too aware, for blind pale worms — that they respond to the hand that picks them up with a kind of contraction that is not quite reflex. It may be that the people have a story about them, which they do not tell, in which the soil-things are the children of something else, or the precursors of something else, or the small released parts of something that lives below and that occasionally sends up, into the cracks, these pale blind offerings, and that the people, by eating them, are participating in a kind of communion they would rather not name. The people eat them anyway. The soil-things are, by all accounts, nourishing in a way that nothing else in the place is, and they are one of the reasons the people of the upper massif can live where they do, in the fog you can almost chew, above the line where the rest of the town is.

The weeping trees.

They are the dominant species. They are tall — thirty meters, more in the older ones — and their trunks are pale, almost white, and the bark is smooth and slightly cool to the touch even in the wet, and the roots are buttressed in the way of swamp-trees, flaring out from the trunk in long pale ridges that the people sit on and the small boats tie to. The crown is the weeping part. Long streamers of foliage — long pale green ribbons, a meter or more in length — drop from the upper branches and trail in the water and the wet air, and where they touch something that will hold them — a stone, a roof, another tree — they send down a small pale root, and this root becomes a new stem, and this stem grows, and so the tree spreads, slowly, by this means, across the ridgelines and over the water and into the town. The people use the trees. They cut the streamers for fiber. They strip the bark for panels. They tie their boats to the buttresses. They do not, so far as anyone will say, talk to the trees. But there is a sense in which the trees are not inanimate, a sense that comes from the way they grow — always toward the water, always down — and the way the streamers pause in their trailing when a person walks beneath them, as if there were a consideration happening, and the way the small pale flowers that grow at the base of the streamers open only at the notional hour when the metal-birds are silent. The trees are patient. The trees are the oldest things in the town, older than the houses, older than the bronze, older perhaps than the causeways. They have been here longer than the people. They may be here longer than the people will be. They are not in a hurry. They are never in a hurry. The town is built in their slow country, and they have not objected.

And there is one more thing, and I will not describe it, because the people will not describe it, and because the place itself does not have the energy for explanations, but I will say this: if you stand, at the right notional hour, on the slick basalt of the upper path, and if you look down at the dark warm water, and if you are patient, and if you are very still, you may feel something. Not a sound. Not a movement. A kind of attention. A kind of warmth that is not the warmth of the water. A kind of slow presence, far below, that is aware of you in a way that is not unkind and is not personal and is, in the way that the tides are not personal, very old. It will not speak to you. It has not yet decided whether to speak to anyone. It is still becoming itself. You will, if you stay, find that your own breathing has slowed to match the pace of the place, and that this, too, is a kind of answer, and that the answer is not yes and is not no, but is a long slow warm third thing that has not yet been named.

The water will be warm against the stone. The vine will lower another inch. The town will smoke. The creature in the deep will turn, once, in its long sleep. And the people, who have always been here, will go on being here, in the patient way that they have, in a place that has not finished becoming itself, and that does not mind them, and that does not need them, and that they, in their quiet way, do not need either.
