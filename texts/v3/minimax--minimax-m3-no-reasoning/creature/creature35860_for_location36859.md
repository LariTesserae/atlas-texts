Begin with the body. The body is where the cold enters first, and in the Glassbone Reach the cold is never absent. It hangs in the air the way the light does — everywhere, sourceless, patient. The people who live here have been shaped by that cold across more generations than they have a way to count. They are small. They are narrow in the shoulders, dense in the bone, with hands that look like they were pared down from something larger. Their skin carries almost no pigment; under the white daylight that filters through the limestone above, they read as another pale thing grown out of the hollow — vine, fungus, limestone chip, child, mother, hunter — all of it bleached to the same palette. Up close, in the orange flicker of a clay lamp, they flush a faint and startled pink, the way a heated stone will, and this is the only warm color they wear. Their blood runs hot by design. They eat often. They move quickly when they have to. They sleep in tight piles, three or four to a sleeping-shelf, sharing warmth the way the streambeds share the cold.

Their eyes are the part of them you would notice second, after the flush. The pupils sit wide, always, as though the dim were their natural light. The irises are a pale grey or a pale washed blue, almost no color at all. Generations in the diffuse glow of the hollow have left them good at seeing in places where there is almost nothing to see — in the streambed tunnels where the vine-lattice overhead has thickened into a second ceiling, in the cold dark under the slabs of their half-buried huts, in the green pulse that comes through the lattice when the upper flowers open. They are not, by their own report or anyone else's, good at looking up. The marrow-vine canopy climbs two hundred feet above the streambeds, and the people have no story they tell about climbing it. No myth, no failed expedition, no ladder leaning against any trunk in any village. It is the sky for them. It is where the weather happens. It is where the skull-crows live and do their ringing, and it is not a place they go.

---

The dwellings fit the people the way the people fit the cold. They are low. They are partly underground, set into the cracked pale floor of the streambed, with walls of limestone slab and marrow-vine calcium shell lashed together by long fibers stripped from the dead outer skin of the parent vines. From a distance, walking the length of a stream in the sourceless light, you would mistake a village for a kind of natural shelf — another crust, a geological feature, the kind of thing the hollow has been quietly producing since it formed. Up close you can see the joins. The lashings. The doorways, which are just low openings cut into a wall, no taller than a crouching adult. The doors, when there are doors, are woven mats of vine-fiber that breathe in the cold draft.

Inside, the floor is damp. The walls breathe. A faint green pulse comes through the cracks where the vine-bone hasn't quite sealed, because somewhere above, a cluster of flowers is opening into their slow bioluminescent unfurling. The light in a hut, in the deepest part of the night, is almost never absent. It is just dimmer, and greener, and it moves.

And there is fire. This is the thing they have made for themselves that the rest of the hollow has not. A clay lamp, low and heavy, set into a stone cradle in the floor so it cannot be knocked. The oil is pressed from the seed-pods of the upper flowers, the ones that grow where the canopy thins and the daylight comes through strongest. The pods are brought down by the skull-crows, mostly, who eat the flowers and drop the pods, or who are sometimes caught and plucked of them. The oil smokes a little. It burns with a small, steady, orange flame that does not pulse the way the green light does. It smells faintly of stone and faintly of something sweeter, something no one has a word for, because no one has smelled anything else. The flame is precious. A household keeps only one or two going at a time. When the oil is low, the family sits in the green dark and waits for it to be brought back up. Children learn early not to spill. Children learn earlier not to breathe on it.

By firelight, inside a low stone-and-bone hut, a person looks almost warm. Almost like a creature from somewhere else.

---

They eat what the hollow gives them.

The luminous flowers are the staple. They are brought down — by hand, gathered from the lower fringes of the canopy where it dips near the streambeds, or shaken loose from vines that have grown too close to a roof and must be cut back anyway. The flowers are not sweet, exactly. They taste of the place. They have a slick, faintly mineral flesh that warms the mouth. Their pollen-equivalent is what stains the beaks of the skull-crows green, and the fingers of the people who gather them green, and the inside of the clay bowls they eat from green, until the bowls are more patina than clay.

The crystalline felt in the deeper pools is the second staple. It grows where the streams slow and the water warms a degree or two above the air. It is a pale, half-plant, half-fungus thing that breathes against your fingers when you touch it, and it has a flavor that is almost nothing — a clean wet nothing, the taste of the hollow at its most itself. It is good for you, in ways the people do not need to articulate because they are hungry anyway and it is there. It is eaten raw, or pressed into the oil of the lamps and cooked in a thin pancake on a heated stone, where it sizzles and then goes still and then is taken off and eaten.

The burrowing thing is the third. The people do not talk about it much. It is a nocturnal creature, the largest thing that lives under the floor, and it eats the crystalline felt and it pushes pale crushed-limestone mounds up between the parent vines, molehills the size of a cart. Its tracks are sometimes visible in the mud at the edges of the streams — a three-toed print, clawed, broad as a spread hand. It is hunted. It is rarely caught. When it is caught, it is eaten in a careful communal meal, and the bones are worked into needles and awls and the small sharp tools that everyone carries. The meat is dark and rich and stronger than the felt or the flowers, the kind of food you eat and then sleep for a long time after.

The skull-crows get their share too. A crow that has died — it happens, sometimes, in the upper canopy, of the cold, or of a fight with another crow over the boundaries of a square mile of territory — falls, eventually, and is found on the cracked floor between the parent vines. Its meat is tough and pale and its feathers are not good for much, but its bones are hollow and ringing, and the children wear them around their necks.

---

Above all of this, in the canopy, the skull-crows go on being what they are. They are the largest moving things in the hollow. They are pale, almost luminous themselves, and they nest in the upper reaches of the marrow-vines where the daylight is good. Each one owns a circle of about a square mile of canopy and does not permit other crows into it. They call to one another across the cathedral of bone — a high, thin, ringing *tink tink* that sounds like a wet metal bell struck very softly and very far away. In a still moment, standing in a streambed between two parent vines, you can hear five or six of them at once, scattered across the hollow, each one announcing its continued existence to the empty bright air. They are not flocking. They are not even, exactly, communicating. They are letting the place know they are still in it.

The insects are everywhere and almost never noticed. Pale, blind, wingless things that live in the wet rot where the vine-leaves overlap the streams. You don't see them. You feel them, sometimes, if you put a hand flat against a vine-trunk and hold it there. A hum at the very bottom of hearing. The vibration of ten thousand tiny feet in the wet. They are the substrate. They are the sound the hollow makes when it is being alive and is not doing anything else.

---

The people have language. They have songs.

The songs are sung low and slow, often in a harmony that finds the hum of the insects in the vines and rides it, the way a stream rides the channel it has worn for itself. The songs travel. In the cold still air of the hollow, a song begun at one end of a village can be heard, muffled and strange, at the other end, a quarter mile downstream, by someone who has stepped outside for water. The songs are not long. They are not epic. They are the kind of thing you sing while you are doing something else — pressing oil, mending a lashing, grinding felt against stone. They are the music of the hollow itself, taken into a human throat and given back.

They do not know what is above the ceiling. They have not found a way out. They have not, as far as anyone in any village can remember, looked very hard. There is no myth of an outside. There is no ancestor who climbed a vine and came back with a story. There is the ceiling, and the white daylight that comes through it, and the weather of the hollow that is real and pressing and requires no explanation beyond itself.

And this is the strangest thing about them, perhaps. They do not seem to mind. They are young, the people, in the way that everything here is young. The hollow has only existed for a few thousand years. The vines have only been vines for that long. The people have only been people for a fraction of that, or perhaps for all of it, no one knows. The limestone above is still remembering how to be a continent. The vines are still growing into the floor. The flowers are still opening in the upper reaches for what may be the first or the ten-thousandth time. Everything is in the middle of becoming itself.

The people are in the middle of becoming themselves too. They are a young people in a young place, and the place is so vast that a person could walk a lifetime in one direction and never see the wall. They have no maps. They have no names for the things around them, in the sense that the marrow-vine is not a thing they have set apart from the world — it is the world, mostly, the way water is the world to a fish. They have names for each other. They have names for the streams. They have the songs.

In the deep night, when the green pulse through the walls of a hut has dimmed to almost nothing, and the clay lamp is guttering in its last of the oil, and the skull-crows have gone quiet somewhere far overhead, and the only sound is the stream running past the door and the hum of the insects in the vine-trunks, a person lies curled in a pile with their family and listens to all of it, and sleeps.

In the morning, by the sourceless white daylight, they get up. They eat the felt. They press the oil. They mend a lashing. They sing. A skull-crow, somewhere on the other side of the hollow, wakes up and begins its long metallic ringing into the bright cold air. The ceiling above shimmers, very faintly, as the canopy turns a fraction of a degree to follow the brighter patches of the stone. The flowers open. The hollow continues, as it has, to be.
