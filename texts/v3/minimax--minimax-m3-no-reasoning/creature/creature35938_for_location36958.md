The wind lives here, and has lived here longer than anything else that moves. It comes up the long slope and pours through the gap and crosses the bowl and is replaced, the next moment, by more of itself. It is the constant resident. Everything else in the bowl — what little else there is — measures its life against the wind.

The wind does not have a body. It does not, in any usual sense, need one. It is the flattening of the grass in long stripes, the shivering of the tarns, the sideways drift of the threads of water when the gusts find them, the cold bite on a cheek, the soft push on a chest. The wind is the medium here, the way water is the medium for a fish. The other inhabitants move through the wind; the wind moves through them, but the wind is the larger element, the more patient one, the one that stays when the others go.

The ravens live in the wind and on it. They ride it. They come in from the gap — that is how they arrive, three or four of them, banking in the throat of the pass and out over the bowl as if launched — and they circle, and they settle on the ledges halfway up the wall, and they call. The call is the loudest sound the bowl makes, louder than the dripping, louder than the hum, louder than the wind itself. It comes off the walls a half-second late and arrives at the ear as something larger than the bird that issued it, something almost architectural, a sound that fits the cathedral scale of the cliffs and uses them as a sounding board.

The ravens do not appear to be doing much. They are not visibly hunting. They are not, at least in the visible part of the year, nesting. They are moving from one ledge to another, taking off, landing, sitting for a long time with their feathers tucked, calling occasionally, calling at all hours, calling when no one is in the bowl to hear them. The bowl is a place they pass through and a place they are. They have territories higher up, in the ridges and the headwalls above the rim. They come down into the bowl to be in it. They are local, not migratory. They have lived here longer than the ash in the fireplace has been cold.

The smaller birds are more hurried. The snow finches flick between the stones, white and quick, gone and back again. The wheatear runs ahead of one's foot, pausing on a rock, then running again. The pipit goes straight up out of the grass and falls back into it some distance away, a parabola in the air, gone for the moment and then a small movement in the tussock and it is back. These are not residents in the way the ravens are. They are summer residents, or passing-through residents, or residents in the sense that they use the bowl for an hour or a day and then go on. They are not as patient as the wind. They are not as settled as the ravens. They live in the bowl but do not quite live of the bowl.

The hares live of the bowl. They are made for it. In winter they are the snow and the rock and the shadow. In summer they are the moss and the lichen and the dusk. They sit in the open, between tussocks, and they are so still that one walks past them thinking they are stones, until they move. When they move they are gone. They are a flash of pale and a small sound and then the next tussock, and then nothing, and then a long stillness again, somewhere ahead. They are not afraid, or not particularly. They have lived here always.

The weasel lives here too, but the weasel is different. The weasel is rumored, glimpsed, gone. The weasel is the corner of vision, the small thing that wasn't there. The vole bursts from one tussock and is in another before one can register the shape. The shrew is a possibility, an old shepherd's story, a small weight in the grass. These are the creatures of the tussocks, the ones that one never quite sees, that one knows by their absence more than their presence.

The goats come through. They are not residents. They come up the long slope, through the gap, across the bowl, and down the far side. They leave droppings that age on the rocks. They leave tracks in the snow that last a season and then fill in. They are travelers of a different kind — the kind that pass through a place and mark it only by passage. The shepherd, two summers ago, with a small flock — that may or may not have happened. The ground was disturbed. The stripes in the moss near the southern tarn might have been hooves. They might have been the wind, or a small avalanche, or the work of water in spring. It is hard to say.

The hands that built the room are not here. The hands are gone. They are the most absent presence in the bowl — present only in what they left. The stones they set are still here. The mud-mortar is still here. The hearth is here, the ash in it very old, the soot on the ceiling very old. The floor is here, the flagstones polished by long walking and longer kneeling. The door is here, more a crawl than a door. The room is here. The hands are not. The hands belonged to someone who knew the cold and accepted it as a condition. The hands used ground and broken stones, not metal tools. The hands chose a crooked lintel because it was a good crooked lintel, available, ready. The hands were here for a while, and then they left, or they died, and the room stayed.

The room is the most human thing in the bowl. The cairns are also human, or were — two of them, flat-topped, carefully stacked, more like small tables than summits. The ring of stones near the southern edge is human, or was — a sheepfold, or a rabbit-pen, or something. The upright stone at the lip of the gap is human, or was — a marker, a memorial, or nothing in particular. These are the marks of people. The people who made them are gone. The room, the cairns, the ring, the upright — they are how the bowl remembers. It does not remember with words. It remembers with objects left in place, weathered, colonized by moss.

The travelers come now. Sometimes one at a time, sometimes two, sometimes in a small group. They come up through the gap and stand in the bowl and look at the cliffs and the grass and the water. They notice the room. They go into it, if the door is open, and they see the hearth, and the polished floor, and the low ceiling with the green showing through the gaps of the branches and the turves. They stay the night, sometimes, and on waking they notice the room is not as cold as it should be. They cannot account for it. The walls have no insulation. The fire pit has not been lit in years. There is something else going on, they feel, but they cannot name what.

They sit for a while, and they look at the lichen pattern on the inside face of the east wall. Up close the clusters are shapeless. At a distance, dozens of paces back, one can almost see a shape — an animal, perhaps, or a letter, or nothing at all. It depends on the light. It depends on the eye. They leave a feather on the cairn, or a polished pebble, or a coin now too tarnished to read. They go down through the gap. They do not always come back.

Some of them do come back. Some travelers love the bowl for the way it is slightly off. They come back two, three, four times. They learn the names of the tarns. They learn which ledge the ravens prefer. They learn the small paths the hares take. They learn the way the wind comes through the gap at certain times of day. They are not residents. They are visitors who come more than once. The bowl does not seem to mind them. It is the same, every time, when they come back. The wind is the same. The dripping is the same. The room is the same, the ash, the soot, the polished floor. The bowl is patient with its visitors, the way it is patient with its weather.

The bowl itself, the place, the room made of mountain — it is the deepest resident. It is older than the wind. It is older than the water. The rock was here before any of them, and will be here after. The rock is patient. The rock weeps, but the weeping is not a sadness; it is a condition. The rock is wet. The rock is dark. The rock has seams through which water finds its way, and hollows that the wind sings into. The rock has lichens, orange and pale grey, that grow another millimeter a year. The rock has a hum. The rock has a hum that the older shepherds said was the mountain talking. The rock has a hum that the shepherds after them said was not talking to them. The rock is two degrees off from being fully itself, or half a degree, or some small uncanniness that the travelers feel and cannot name.

The bowl is what stays. The wind comes and goes. The water comes and goes. The ravens come and go. The hares change their coats. The travelers come and go. The hands came and went. The bowl stays. The bowl is the same bowl that the wind found, that the water found, that the ravens and the hares and the people and the hands all found. The bowl holds them all. The bowl does not need any of them, and yet it has all of them, and the having is what makes it a place.
