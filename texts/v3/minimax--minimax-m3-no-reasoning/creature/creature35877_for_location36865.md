# The Inhabitants

There is no one here.

There is also no one *not* here.

---

What lives in the wedge is not a population. It is a condition. The plants do not occupy the place the way animals occupy a forest, as tenants paying rent in passing through. They *are* the place's own body, and the place is their body, and the body is in the middle of becoming something. You have stepped into a single organism that has not finished deciding what it is, and the deciding is the living, and the living is loud.

Stand still long enough and you can feel the seams between them loosen. The stalks you thought were individuals are cooperative limbs of a deeper architecture. The raft to the west, with its parasites and its symbiotes you cannot tell apart, is one lung. The columns to the east, leaning and leaning back into their own wind, are another. The split-topped stalk that opened its seven tongues for you is a mouth — not metaphorically, not in any poetic sense you are softening — a mouth that tasted your heat and your wet and your carbon, and decided, slowly, whether to include you in the next half-hour of its digestion of the air.

There are no names for any of them. You will try. The thing with the tongues you will call the *split-stand* in your head, and the braided columns you will call the *sighing pillars*, and the raft you will call the *woven shelf*, and these names will stick for as long as you are here and mean nothing to the things themselves, who do not need names because they do not separate.

---

## The Mouths

You meet them first.

They are the most articulate. They have to be, because their work is at the interface, where the air meets the green, where the green meets you. A mouth is a stalk, a stem, a hollow tube of braided fiber that has chosen to terminate in a seam. The seam is invisible until it isn't. Then it is the only thing you can see. It parts along a line of paler green, slowly, with a sound like a long wet zipper, and the interior is a deeper green, almost black, and it is wet, and it is warm, and the breath that comes out is the breath of the whole body, expelled, drawn back, expelled, drawn back. The mouths do not eat you. They do not eat anything you recognize as food. They eat the air. They eat the light. They eat the warmth you carry in your clothes and your skin and the bowl of your skull, and they are hungry for it the way a fire is hungry for oxygen, which is to say: not at all hungry, except in the way that is the same thing as being alive.

Some mouths are small, no bigger than your cupped hand, and they open and close in a rhythm that does not match the body's breath. They are beating. They are listening. You notice that they orient toward you when you move and away from you when you stop, and you cannot decide whether this is attention or aversion, and after a while you understand the question is wrong. They are orienting the way a sunflower orients, which is not a question at all, just a turning.

The split-stand you met earlier — the one with the seven pale tongues — is a larger mouth, an old one, possibly a leader, though the body has no leaders. Its tongues are thicker than your fingers and they are still unfolding. They have been unfolding the entire time you have been watching. They will be unfolding after you leave. They are not unfolding for you. They are unfolding, and you are the occasion for noticing.

---

## The Pillars

The columns to the east are the body's bones, or its standing-up, or its way of being vertical in a place where verticality is optional.

Each one is a braid. Anywhere from thirty to a hundred stalks have wound themselves together over — how long. Hours. Days. They are still braiding. If you watch closely you can see a thinner stalk, lime-green, working itself into the twist of a thicker one, finding the spiral, easing in, being absorbed. The pillar leans into the exhaled breath of the body and recovers. It is being pushed and it is pushing back and the negotiation is the pillar. Without the wind of the breathing there would be no pillar. Without the pillar there would be no wind to push. They make each other.

The sound they make is the worst sound in the place, which is to say it is the most articulate. It is a groan, low, structural, the sound a ship makes in a sea that is being decided about. It moves through your sternum. If you press your hand against a pillar you can feel it as a pressure that has nothing to do with the wind. It is the turgor of the living fibers, thousands of them, all pumping their fluid up, all holding their cells rigid, all pressing outward against your palm with the polite insistence of something that has been asked to be elsewhere and is declining.

Some pillars lean further than others. One near the center of the grove has a tilt of perhaps eight degrees, and it leans, and it holds, and it leans back, and it holds, and on the leaning-out breath its surface ripples, a wave of darker green traveling up its length, and where the wave passes the fibers visibly tighten, and where it has passed the fibers visibly slacken, and you understand that the pillar is not leaning because of the wind. The pillar is leaning because it is breathing. The pillar is a single enormous leaf, and it is respiring in the same rhythm as the rest of the body, and the wind is just the body's breath made visible to it.

---

## The Raft

To the west, the floor is not floor. It is the *woven shelf*, the breathing platform, and it is the part of the body that has decided to be horizontal.

It is made of vines. The vines are the thickness of your wrist, of your thumb, of your little finger, of nothing at all, and they cross each other in patterns that are not geometric and are not random and are exactly as necessary as a fingerprint. They are pressed flat under your feet if you walk out onto them — they give, they breathe, they rise an inch and fall an inch in the body's rhythm, and you can feel your balance subtly tested, the way it would be tested on a boat in a mild harbor.

On the raft, the small things grow.

The small things are parasites and the small things are symbiotes and the small things are neither, and the distinction is not useful here. A cup-fungus-looking growth that is not a fungus opens its frill and breathes through it. A trailing vine of oxblood color roots into another trailing vine of lime color and the line where they meet is a third color, neither, both, a thing in the middle of becoming itself. A cluster of tiny pods — the size of chickpeas, the size of grapes — hangs from a thread and each pod is opening at a different rate and each opening is a different color inside. Some are green. Some are the yellow that hurts. Some are a black that is not the absence of light but the presence of a pigment so saturated it eats the light before it can leave.

You look at the small things and you feel that they are the body's experiments. The pillars are settled in their vertical life. The mouths are settled in their interfacing life. The small things on the raft are still trying everything. They are the body's adolescence. The body is adolescent. The body is the age of the place, which is young, which is new, which is shouting.

---

## The Tower

You will see it eventually. You cannot help seeing it. It rises above the columns at the eastern edge of the visible body, taller than the rest by a factor you keep underestimating. Your eye wants to measure it against the pillars and the pillars want to measure it against the sky and the sky is not there.

The tower is braided, like the pillars but tighter, and its color is not the lime and oxblood of the body but something paler. A bone-white. A silk-white. The color of a stalk you saw near the water's edge that you thought was dead and then realized was not dead but full, swollen with something it was carrying upward.

The top of the tower is opening.

You saw this from a distance. You are closer now and you can see that what you thought was a single opening is in fact many openings, layered, a stack of seams each parting in turn, and from the innermost seam something is rising. It is not a flower. It is not a fruit. It is not a spore or a pollen or a seed or any of the words you have for what plants do at the end of their reproductive effort.

It is light. It is the gold light of the morning, which has been ordinary up to now, which has fallen on the extraordinary matter and been accepted by it — but here at the top of the tower the light is being held, and changed, and given back. It comes out green. It comes out wet. It comes out *wrong*, the way the passage described, except now you are close enough to see what wrong means.

Wrong means: oriented. Wrong means: moving with intention. Wrong means the light leaving the top of the tower is no longer the light that fell on the body. It is the body's own light, processed, metabolized, exhaled. It is the light that the body has made out of the morning. It rises like heat. It rises slowly. It carries the smell of overripe mango and metal and the unnamed thing.

The tower is the body's mouth. The body's *highest* mouth. The mouth that does not open for you. The mouth that opens for the sky, except there is no sky, except the tower is making one.

---

## The Tendrils

You have been watched.

Not by anything with eyes. By the body, which has no eyes, which uses tendrils instead. A tendril is a thin green line, the thickness of a thread or a wire, and it descends from somewhere above you, and it sways, and it is feeling for you. You stepped back from one earlier. You will step back from more.

The tendril is not hostile. It is not curious in any way you can recognize as curiosity. It is *assessing*. It is reading the surface you present, the heat, the moisture, the chemical signature of your skin and the bacteria that live on your skin and the oils in your clothes. It is doing what a tongue does when it touches a new food: gathering data, deciding whether to swallow.

It does not swallow you. It sags. It retracts. It goes back up into the body and the body thinks about what you are for a moment and then thinks about something else, because you are not food, you are not threat, you are not mate, you are not useful in any category the body has, and so you are allowed to stay without being included, which is a different thing, and you will come to understand the difference over the hours you remain here.

The tendrils move in their own time. They are slow. Their slowness is not patience. It is the speed of plants, which is the speed at which water moves through cellulose, which is the speed at which decisions can be made by a body that does not have a brain, which turns out to be — once you spend enough time here — perfectly adequate.

---

## The Whole

There is a sound you will hear if you stand very still and stop thinking.

It is not the roar. It is not the pops. It is not the creak of the pillars or the hiss of the pods or the bird-that-is-not-a-bird from the vibrating blade. It is underneath all of those. It is the sound the body makes when it is not making any of the other sounds, and it sounds like — and you will not believe this for a long time — it sounds like *thinking*. A low, continuous murmur, felt more than heard, the way you feel someone thinking near you in a quiet room. The body is thinking about itself. The body is thinking about the morning light and the warm air and the water rising a thumb's width and the visitor standing in its wedge. The body is thinking about whether to open the next mouth. The body is thinking about whether to lean the next pillar. The body is thinking about the tendril it sent down to read your wrist and what the tendril brought back.

You are being thought about.

You are being included.

The water is up another thumb's width. The light at the top of the tower is brighter. The pods are opening faster. The tongues of the split-stand have finally, finally stopped unfolding and are holding, wide, in the still air, and from inside them something is beginning to glow.

You stay.

You were not asked.

---

The inhabitants of the wedge are a single inhabitant, undivided, mid-decision, very young, very loud, very alive, very interested in the strange warm biped who has come to stand in their water and watch them think.
