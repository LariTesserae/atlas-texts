What lives here is several things, layered, none of them certain.

---

The first is the moss, and the moss is not in any sense what you are accustomed to call moss, but it is the closest word available to you, and so you use it. It is the dominant fact of the room. It is alive — this can be said with certainty because it is metabolizing, because it is consuming, because it grows. It grows at a rate you cannot measure, because you have not been here long enough, but you can see where it has grown: up the legs of the presses, into the seams of the brass fitting on the bench, over the leather glove, into the curled fingers of the empty glove, down through the felted mat and into the porous stone beneath. The moss is patient in the way that only very old things are patient. It does not need to hurry because nothing here is competing with it. There is no sun to outrun, no frost to flee, no herbivore to fear. It has only the slow chemistry of stone and copper and iron, and it has all the time there is.

The moss eats metal. You can see this. The legs of the central press have become a delicate cagework of rust and verdigris where the moss has climbed them, and the iron beneath the felt is half the iron it was a century ago, dissolved into the green-black cells of the hypnaceae, transmuted into moss. The brass fitting on the bench has gone soft and green at the seams. The leather glove, when you lift it, comes away in pieces — the inside still faintly the color of leather, the outside a perfect cast of the moss that consumed it. The moss will, given enough time, eat the presses. Given enough time, it will eat the walls. It is working on the stone.

The moss does not know you are here. It is aware only in the loose sense that a forest is aware of the season, that a tide is aware of the moon. It registers your weight as warmth. It registers your exhalation as moisture. If you stood very still for a long time, it would begin to grow up your ankles, the way moss grows up the bases of trees in places where no one walks. It would not be malicious. It would simply be continuing what it has always done.

---

The second thing that lives here lives in the moss. You discover it by kneeling and pressing your face close to the felted surface, into the sourceless dim, until your breath makes a small fog on the green-black velvet. There is movement.

Not much. A stirring. A subsurface shifting, like something turning over in sleep, or like the slow creep of a thing that has nowhere to be. The things in the moss are perhaps a centimeter long, perhaps less, and they are the exact color and texture of the moss itself — so much so that when they stop moving, you cannot find them again. They have no eyes that you can discern. They have no legs you can count. They move through the felt by means you cannot determine, parting nothing, displacing nothing, leaving no tracks. They make no sound — or their sound is below the hum, below the threshold, and so the hum has eaten their voices, and you will never know what they would have said if they had been allowed to speak.

They are not insects. They are not worms. They are not anything you have a name for. They are the things that have evolved, or have been bred, or have simply *occurred*, in the sealed wet dark of the moss layer. They feed on the moss — on its chemistry, on the slow iron-dissolving exudates it produces, on the metallic breath of the verdigris. They live their entire lives in a layer two millimeters thick. They have never seen the ceiling. They have never seen the presses. They know only the felted green-dark and the hum that passes through them, and they reproduce, if they reproduce, in some manner that does not require meeting, because meeting would require eyes, and they have none.

There are many of them, or there are few, and there is no way to know, because the moss does not part for them and they do not disturb the surface and the dim sourceless light does not let you see into the layer where they live. They are simply *there*, a population of almost-invisible inhabitants that the room has made.

You begin to suspect, after you have knelt there long enough that your knees begin to ache, that the hum is *them*. That what you are hearing is the colony — the slow collective metabolism of a thousand small bodies processing iron and verdigris and leather, vibrating at a pitch too low to be heard by anything larger than themselves. That the hum is the sound of them eating the room.

You do not know if this is true. You will never know. But it is the kind of true that fits.

---

The third thing that lives here is the hum itself, or what sustains the hum. This is not the same as the things in the moss, though they may be related. This is older. This is structural. This is the reason the hum continues even when the moss is silent and the things in the moss are still and the presses are not pressing.

The hum is a chord. You can hear this if you press your ear to the stone bench, or to the flank of the nearest press, or to the curving wall. It is not a single note but several pitches layered, some rising, some falling, none of them resolving, all of them kept in suspension like dust in still air. It has been there since before you arrived. It will be there after. It is, you begin to suspect, the actual heartbeat of the room — not metaphorically, but in some way you do not have the language for.

The room is a resonant cavity. Something in it is sustaining the resonance. Whether that something is the machinery still completing a work that has no end, or the moss conducting its slow metabolism, or the things in the moss feeding on the iron, or all of these things together vibrating in sympathy — a single organism made of moss and small bodies and oxidized copper and cold stone — you cannot say.

The hum does not advance. It is the only clock, and it does not advance.

---

And then — the writer.

You cannot find them. You cannot see them. They are not here. But they were here, and the room has not finished with them.

There is a depression in the moss on the bench where someone sat for a long time — a small dark bowl, the shape of a person compressed into the green. There are faint marks on the floor near the central press where someone stood and stood and stood, the moss worn away in two small ovals where feet would have been. There is, on the wall beside the bench, a single charcoal line at about the height of a seated person's hand — a mark made by someone keeping count, or marking time, or simply making a mark because they could not bear the silence.

The folded paper is the most legible trace. You can lift it carefully, because it is fragile as a dead leaf, and the handwriting is too small and too faded to be read, only seen — the even lines, the complete filling of the page, the work of someone who had a great deal to say and a great deal of time to say it. They might have been here for weeks. They might have been here for years. The moss has begun to grow over the paper, but slowly, because paper is not very good food, and so you can still see the ink.

They left, or they did not leave. There is no sign of how. There is no door. There is no exit that you can find. The room is sealed. They were here, and now they are not, and you do not know how a person leaves a place with no exits. Perhaps they did not leave. Perhaps they were eaten by the moss, slowly, and the moss has incorporated them, and what you are standing on is partly them. Perhaps they are the hum. Perhaps they are still here, in some form the room has decided to keep, and what you are hearing, beneath the chord, is a single human voice, sustained, repeating itself, becoming indistinguishable from the other notes.

You do not know. You will never know.

---

The glove still holds the shape of the hand that wore it. The paper still holds the lines that were written on it. The bench still holds the depression of the body that sat on it. The room does not let go. That is what it does. It holds what comes into it, and it converts what it holds into itself — into moss, into hum, into the slow chemistry of sealed time — and it does not give anything back.

You are here now, and the moss is beginning to learn the warmth of you.
