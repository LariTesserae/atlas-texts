# Who Lives in Pennygap

Thirty-one people, by the last count. Then thirty-two, then thirty-one again, the numbers moving like the well itself — rising and falling within a narrow, dependable range.

The baker is a man named Cai, which is not his name but the sound his name has become after years of being called across lanes. He is broad and quiet and his forearms are dusted white with flour that is the colour of the limestone ground down. He rises before the others and kneads the dark bearded grain into loaves that come out of his stone oven with a crust like old leather and a smell that, on a damp morning, will travel three lanes before dispersing into the wet air. He has baked since he was twelve. His teacher was a woman called Maren who died and was carried up the path and laid under a small stone on the southern slope, and the moss has come over her stone, and sometimes when Cai kneads he kneads the way she taught him, which is a way that does not require him to think. His hands remember. He is glad of this.

His daughter is called Iseult, though she is the only Iseult in the book and the book is the only place the name has been written down. She is nine. She sits on the upturned crate by the oven and watches him work, and sometimes he lets her punch the dough down, and the dough takes her small fists easily. She has not yet begun to do anything useful. She will. There is time.

The weaver is a man called Tomasi, and the loom has been in his house for longer than his family. It is a heavy wooden frame, black with age and oiled with his own hands, and the warp threads are the colour of undyed wool, and the cloth that comes off it is the cloth that has come off it for generations, a pale grey-brown that is the colour of the sheep on the hills. Tomasi weaves all day and most of the evening. The shuttle goes back and forth with a sound like a slow breath. He does not sing as he weaves, although an earlier weaver did, and the house has a worn hollow in one of its beams where a heel used to tap the rhythm. Tomasi does not tap. He listens to the cloth. He knows when it is right by the way it receives his hand.

His wife is Delyth, who is small and sharp-faced and keeps the chickens. The chickens live beneath the stairs of the Long House because the Long House has the deepest stair-well in the town and the underside of its treads stays dry even in the wettest weather. The chickens come out in the bright hours, which is most of the hours, and scratch at the moss for whatever it is chickens find in moss. They are not numerous. They have not been numerous for a long time. Delyth knows each of them by sight, which is easier than it sounds because there are only twelve, but also harder, because the twelve change. She does not name them. She considers this.

The spoon-maker is called Bren. He is young, by the standards here — twenty-six, perhaps, though no one is sure, because no one counted years until the book began. He works in alder, which he cuts from the wetter hollows above the town where the alders grow thin and bent and pale. He splits the wood with wedges of bone. He carves with a bronze blade that came down the path in the pack of a traveller ten years ago and that he keeps wrapped in a piece of wool cloth. The spoons he makes are not decorated. They have the shape a hand wants when it holds a spoon. He makes perhaps twenty in a year. He gives most of them away. The travellers take them, when travellers come, and are surprised to find they have no need of them elsewhere, and bring them back, and he makes them again.

The keeper of the book lives in the house with the most books. There are nine books. This is more than any other house. The keeper is an old woman called Essa, who has been the keeper since the previous keeper died and was carried up. She writes in the book in a small careful hand, with ink that she makes herself from oak galls and iron water. She records births. She records deaths. She records the names of travellers who stay, because they stay, and so they become, in some quiet way, residents. She does not record what people do. There is no need. What people do is what people have done.

The shrine is watched over by no one in particular. There is a man who is thought of as the shrine-keeper, but his role is not so much to keep the shrine as to be the person who brings it things. He is called Osprey, for a reason no one remembers, and he is very tall, the tallest person in the town, and he carries water to the shrine stone when the well is low and feathers when they are found and sometimes a sprig of something green when the moss is thin. He does not pray at the stone. He sets the offerings at its base and steps back. He has lived here all his life. He does not know what the stone is. He has no wish to.

The slowworm under the shrine stone has no name. It is orange-brown and the length of a man's forearm, and it has lived beneath the flat stone at the edge of the shrine for as long as anyone has thought to look. It comes out on warm days, which here means most days, and basks in the small pale sun that reaches the lowest part of the bowl. It is not afraid of Osprey. Osprey is not afraid of it. They have an understanding. The slowworm eats the small slugs that come down from the moss, and in return the slowworm does not go into the lanes, where its kind does not live. It has chosen.

The salamanders are many. They slip between the moss and the wall in the lanes near the well, and they are brightest in the early hours when the light is grey and they themselves are bright orange and black and seem, briefly, to be the only colourful thing in the town. They are eaten by no one. They are watched by everyone. A child who is learning to be still learns first by watching a salamander. The salamander does not teach. It is simply the thing that is stillest.

The stoat that has been seen three times is not a resident. It is a visitor, like the travellers, but rarer. It is pale and long and runs along the top of the shrine stone and is gone. The first time it was seen was by Maren, the baker's teacher, when she was a girl, and the second time by Delyth's mother, and the third time by Iseult, who was four. Each time the person who saw it did not speak of it for several days. When they did speak of it, they spoke carefully. The stoat is not understood. It is not feared. It is one of the things the town holds that the town does not claim.

The mice are many and unremarkable. The rats are fewer and glossier and live in the deeper places. They do not trouble the town. They trouble the thatch. The thatch is theirs in some sense that the people have accepted.

The children are four. Iseult is the eldest. There is a boy called Wren, who is six, and a girl called Ailsa, who is three, and a baby called, as of the last writing in the book, simply *the new one*, because Essa is waiting to see what name settles on him. The children play in the lanes. They play with pebbles from the stream-channel, which are smooth and grey. They play a game that involves throwing a pebble at a wall and seeing how close to a joint in the stone it will land. They are not rowdy. They are not silent. They are somewhere in between, which is where children are.

The people do not love each other in any way that would be recognisable elsewhere. They are fond of each other. They know the shape of each other's days. When Cai's bread is particularly good, Tomasi mentions it, and when Tomasi's cloth comes off the loom particularly even, Delyth mentions it, and these are the conversations. There is no more.

When one of them dies, the others carry the body up the path. They carry it on a frame of alder wood that lives against the wall of the house with the most books, and they carry it in the early morning, when the swifts are still in their holes, and they lay it in a small hollow on the southern slope, and they set a stone over it, and they go back down. The moss will come over the stone. The person will be remembered for as long as anyone remembers them, which is a generation, perhaps two, and then they will be a name in the book. That is enough. It has always been enough.

The light fills the bowl. The silence holds. The well rises and falls within its narrow range. The moss grows on the roofs and on the walls and on the southern slope where the small stones are, and it grows very slowly, and it will continue to grow very slowly for as long as the bowl is a bowl, which is to say for as long as the limestone holds, which is to say for a very long time.
