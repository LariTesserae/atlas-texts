# Who Lives There

They are a people of the warm column. The water has had a long time to make them, and it has done thorough work.

---

## The Body

They are a little smaller than you would expect, on average. Not short — *compact*. Dense. As if the water had gently, over many generations, persuaded the skeleton that less is more. The bones are thick for their length, the joints a little broader than a surface-dweller's would be. The cartilage is generous. They are buoyant almost without trying, which is to say they have spent so long in water that their bodies have stopped arguing with it.

Their skin is dark, and not uniformly so. It carries the mottled bronze of old amber, of silt-lit afternoons, of the upper column where light is still a thing. Freckles and small birthmarks in shades of tea and weak coffee. In the deeper-dwelling families the pigmentation runs darker, richer, almost the color of the basalt itself — a kind of living camouflage against the spine they were born among. Their hair, when they have it, tends toward the long and loose, and is often threaded with red algae at the festivals, though this is decoration, not accident.

Their eyes are large. Not pop-eyed, not stylized — simply *open*, as if the lids have learned not to fight the salt. The iris is most often a deep, warm brown, but in some families, the old families, the ones who have lived deepest the longest, there is a ring of amber or a wash of hazel that almost seems to catch what little light is left and hold it a moment longer than it should. They can see well in the dim. Better than you would think. The children especially — they play games in the lower vaults where an outsider would be functionally blind, and they do not lose each other.

Their ears are slightly smaller than yours. Tighter to the head. The external architecture has simplified, because it has less work to do. They hear more with their skeleton than with the air; a long bass note moves through them before it moves through the water around them. The old ones can feel a vent open three chambers away.

Their hands are interesting. The fingers are a little webbed — not dramatically, not like a frog's, but enough that when you saw them spread against the current you would notice, the way you'd notice a sail. They are strong in the grip, dexterous in the fine, and warm. Always warm. The warmth is part of them now.

They wear little. The upper galleries are warm enough, and the water provides a kind of modesty on its own. There are wraps of woven shell-fiber for ceremonies, and harnesses for tools, and a kind of soft sandal made of cured ray-skin for walking on the shell-concrete when one wants to be formal about it. But mostly — mostly they are themselves, in the water, and the water does not mind.

They age well. They age strangely. An elder looks like an elder anywhere, but the proportions shift: the chest deepens, the shoulders round a little, the movement becomes more of the water and less against it. The very old move like the long things in the lower vaults — patient, unhurried, present in a way that the young cannot yet manage.

---

## The Senses

They do not see in the dark the way surface people imagine. It is not that they have eyes like owls. It is that they have *learned the dark*, and the dark has been generous in return. They read shape against absence. They read the way a column of water differs from the water around it, the way a current bends around a body that is not moving, the way a shadow is not the absence of light but a different quality of presence. Their vision is, in some deep sense, *relational*. Things are known by what they are next to.

Their hearing, as mentioned, is skeletal. When they want to listen to you, they will sometimes move closer than you expect — not to intimidate, but to *include you in the resonance*. They are feeling the bones in your chest move with their voice. This is, for them, intimacy.

Smell is muted. The water takes most of it, and the salt takes the rest. But taste is sharp. They taste the water the way you would taste a wine. They know what vent opened this morning by the mineral on their lips. They know who has been swimming where by the faintest residue left in the current. A child can tell you, with their eyes closed, whether the silver fish have moved up or down today.

Touch is the great sense. The whole skin is awake. A change in temperature — half a degree, a quarter — is read like a sentence. A current against the calf is a letter from someone they cannot see. They greet each other by placing a palm briefly against the forearm or the shoulder, a kind of touch-and-release that is both hello and how-are-you and I-am-here. They do not shake hands.

---

## The Mind

They are not simple, and they are not exotic. They are not mystics. They are not wise in the way that wise is portrayed on the covers of books about lost civilizations. They are *people*. They are sharp and petty and kind and cruel and bored and amused and tired and ambitious, the way people are anywhere. A mother loses her temper with her child in the third gallery over a scrap of fish that was not shared. Two elders hold a grudge over a seating arrangement at a ceremony that happened forty years ago. A young man wants very badly to leave and see the surface, and another young woman wants very badly to stay and never go. There is gossip. There are love affairs. There are people who are bad at their jobs and people who are good at them and people who pretend to be good at them.

But.

The water has shaped the mind, the way it has shaped the body. Not into something lesser. Into something *oriented differently*.

Time moves slower for them. Not in a mystical sense — they do not have longer internal clocks — but in the sense that urgency has been gently bled out of the culture over centuries. There is no harvest that will fail if it is not gathered today. There is no crop that will rot. The water provides; the vents provide; the silver fish provide; the long things in the deep are not prey and not predator most days. There is food. There is warmth. There is the slow, patient metabolism of a place that does not need to fight.

And so the mind has learned to *not fight*. To drift, a little. To let a thought rise the way a bubble rises. To take a long time over a decision because a long time is available. To be still.

They are not lazy. They work. There are tasks — the vents need tending, the lanterns need feeding, the coralcrete needs patching where the currents have worn it, the fish need to be guided to certain chambers and away from others, the algae needs to be encouraged on the upper ledges. There is craft. There is trade. There is the long, careful work of keeping a city that is also a reef alive in the way it has agreed to be alive. But the work is done at the pace of the place. Nobody is in a hurry. Nobody has been in a hurry for a very long time.

Their memory is long. They remember the names of people who died before their grandparents were born, and they speak those names in the right places, and they know whose grandmother was whose, and who is descended from which of the long builders, and which domes are older than which. They have a sense of *layer* — of depth in time as well as depth in water — and they read it the way you would read a wall.

They are not afraid of death. This is not a mystical claim. It is a simple observation: when everyone you have ever known has lived in water, and the water is everything, and the water will be everything after you, then death becomes less a discontinuity and more a kind of joining. They bury their dead in the basalt spine, in small chambers along the old backbone, and they speak to those chambers sometimes, the way you might speak to a very old relative who has gone quiet.

---

## The Heart

They love. They love the way anyone loves, which is to say: awkwardly, fiercely, with terrible mistakes, with tenderness that surprises them, with jealousy and generosity and the long slow work of learning another person. Two people who have decided to share a life will press their palms together and hold them there for a long time, and the children will be told that this is what marriage is, and the children will understand it physically before they understand it in words.

They love their children with a kind of practiced devotion that does not look like smothering. A child in this city has a great deal of freedom early. By five or six they are swimming the middle vaults alone, finding their way between chambers by the feel of the currents, and the parents trust them because the parents trust the place, and the place is gentle. Accidents happen. They are grieved. But the culture does not protect by preventing; it protects by teaching, and by being patient with the learning.

They love their elders with a kind of ceremonial tenderness. An elder is, in some senses, the most important person in a chamber. They have lived the longest in the water. They are the closest to being *of* it. When an old person speaks, the young go quiet. Not because they have been told to. Because the old person carries something in their voice that the young recognize they do not yet have.

They love each other across families, across the old lines of which dome was built by whom. There are feuds — of course there are feuds, they are people — but the feuds are slow, and they end, and the ending is not a victory but a kind of return. They have had a long time to learn that grudges, like the coralcrete, are things that accrue, and that a city built of accretions cannot afford to harden against itself too much.

---

## The Strangers

They are gentle with outsiders. Not naive — gentle. They have seen surface-divers before. They know what a person who has been in water too short a time looks like: the stiff shoulders, the held breath, the way they kick when they should drift. They are patient with this. They will not laugh at you, though they may smile. They will feed you. They will find you a place to sleep that is above the waterline enough for you to rest, if you need it, though they will gently suggest that you do not, in fact, need it.

They will not try to convert you. They are not evangelical. They have no interest in your bright dry world, except as a kind of curiosity — the way you might be curious about a glacier, or a very tall mountain. They will ask you questions about it with polite interest, and they will listen to your answers with the same expression you might wear listening to a child describe a dream.

If you stay, they will teach you. Slowly. Without insisting. They will put a palm on your forearm and hold it there, and they will not ask if you have understood, because they will feel the answer in your bones.

If you leave, they will not be sad, exactly. They will be patient with your leaving. They will put a soft-bodied lantern in your hand and tell you it will last you until the surface, and that you should let it go when you get there, because it cannot live in dry air for long. They will watch you go. They will turn back to the water. The water will resume its long work.

---

## What They Are Not

They are not the lost children of some sunken continent, dreaming of the surface. They have no mythology of return. They do not sing songs about the sky. The sky is, for them, a distant and not very interesting rumor — a place where the water is thin and the light is too much and the air hurts the lungs. They feel sorry for surface-dwellers in a vague, abstract way, the way you might feel sorry for someone who has to live in a desert. But they do not want to trade.

They are not amphibious in the dramatic sense. They cannot walk on land. They are not fish. They are people who live in water the way you live in air, and they have made that work so completely that the question of *why* no longer occurs to them.

They are not utopian. There is cruelty here. There is pettiness. There are people who are cruel to the animals, and people who are cruel to each other, and people who are cruel to themselves. There are people who have failed at something and cannot forgive themselves, and people who have succeeded at something and cannot be forgiven by others, and all the long human comedies of failure and success play out here, just as they do anywhere people are people.

But the water has set the terms. The water is patient. The water has time.

---

## What They Are

They are a people who have made a treaty with a place, and kept it, for a very long time.

The treaty is not written. It is not spoken. It is *lived*. It is the way they hold a lantern without gripping it. The way they move through a corridor without disturbing the current more than they must. The way they feed the silver fish from the palm and do not flinch when the fish brush their fingers. The way the children grow up playing in the lower vaults with the long patient things, and the long patient things do not mind, and the children do not learn to be afraid of the dark because the dark has never been a thing to be afraid of.

They are a people for whom *home* is not a structure but a medium. They do not live in the city. They live in the warm column. The city is what they have made, together, inside the warm column, the way coral makes a reef inside the sea — not to exclude the sea, but to give the sea somewhere to gather.

They are people. They are very old, and very present, and they are not waiting for anything.

The water is everything here. They are in it. They are of it. They are, in some quiet way that they would not think to put into words, *welcome*.
