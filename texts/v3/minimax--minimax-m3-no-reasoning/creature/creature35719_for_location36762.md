# The Inhabitants of the Deepstack

## The People

The people were the most recent thing.

That is to say: they were the most recent *kind* of inhabitant. They had not been here long, measured against the timescales of the chimneys and the vents, and their tenure was marked, throughout the city, by a kind of apologetic casualness, the way a guest who has overstayed might begin to behave as if they had been there all along. They did not, on the whole, speak of themselves as residents. They spoke of themselves as *citizens*, which is a different word, and which carries the implication that the place in question has agreed to have them.

The place had not, on the whole, agreed to anything.

The Deepstack humans — and they were, mostly, recognizably so; there was no question on this either, whatever ambiguity attached to their predecessors — were tall by the standards of offworld baselines, and broad across the hip, because the carbon-dense food and low-gravity upper stacks had, over centuries, produced a body plan that favored a low center of mass and long reach. Their skin was, in fair weather, the brown of wet paper; in foul weather, in the lower stacks where the rain fell constantly and the mist never lifted, it was the gray of a fish's belly. Their hair, when they had it, was long and tended to mat. Their eyes had a particular quality — a slight lateral flattening, a certain inward tilt at the inner canthus — that onlookers from elsewhere sometimes found mistaken for and misidentified as belonging to a more aquatic ancestor than the histories support.

Behind the left ear of every Deepstack citizen, set against the mastoid bone, there was a small smooth disc of worked basalt, no larger than a thumbnail, set into the skin during a childhood operation and forgotten. The disc received a coded vibration from the city's dense packed air and translated it into a sensation that the auditory cortex interpreted, with no further effort on the listener's part, as speech. The citizen thought the words, and the words appeared, fully voiced, in the heads of whoever else stood within the disc's range.

The disc did not need to be turned on. It was always on, because it had no off state. It had been grown, like all worked basalt, and it ran on the heat of the patient who carried it, the way a hand runs on the warmth of the body it belongs to. It used, on a busy day, less energy than a fingernail's growth. Its owners were, in some sense, slightly colder than they would have been otherwise; the disc took what it needed.

The disc was warm — perceptibly, if you touched it — and it was the warmest part of a person on a wet afternoon.

The people wore slickers, in the lower stacks, of a rubberized oilcloth that the upper-stack weavers made from the excretion of an animal they did not name. The slickers were yellow, because yellow was visible in mist, and they were heavy, and they were hot, and they filled with rain that ran down them in sheets and out through eyelets at the hem, where it rejoined the constant slow descent. The rain leaked through every seam of the slicker. The slicker was not, strictly, waterproof. It was *rain-tolerant*, which is a different and more honest word.

The people had been there for centuries, perhaps millennia. They had been there long enough that the question of whether they had *always* been there, or had come there from elsewhere, was no longer answerable, and was, increasingly, no longer asked. They were born in the Deepstack. They were buried in the Deepstack. Their dead were already vapor — already rising through the packed corridors, already condensing on the underside of some plate, already falling again as rain.

They were, in a way, mostly rain.

## The Seam-Things

In the lower stacks, in the warm wet seams between the worked basalt plates and between the worked basalt and the older natural basalt of the vents, there were things.

They were the size of a thumbnail, and often six-legged, and often no, but in any case small, and in any case abundant, and in any case busy. They moved in the seams the way ants move in a wall: not in lines, not in obvious procession, but in a kind of diffuse occupation, each one working a small territory of basalt and water and the constant fine rain of organic particulate that drifted down from above.

The offworld xenobiologists, when they came, had wanted very much to classify them. They were animals, or they were not animals. They had nervous systems, or they had not. They were social, or they were colonial, or they were something else for which the Linnaean vocabulary did not have a word. They grazed the basalt. They did not, in any visible sense, eat it; they licked it, or they abraded it, or they applied to it some small enzymatic process that they did not visibly possess and that the xenobiologists could not extract. The basalt came back, in their wake, finer than it had been. The seams they had passed through were smoother than the seams they had not.

They were the city's small intestine. They processed the basalt. They processed the rain. They processed, in some way that the xenobiologists had given up on, the *worked* part of the worked basalt — the faintly alive part, the part that remembered, if it remembered anything — and they gave it back, in their excretions, in a form that the growers could re-use.

They were deaf, in the roar. The roar that filled the lower stacks, the wall of sound that pressed against you when you walked, that you leaned against, that you breathed through, that you shouted into and were not heard — the roar did not touch them. They did not have ears. They did not have organs that the roar could damage. The roar was, to them, nothing. They moved through a quiet place that was wholly unlike the city they were in.

They were warm, in the cold, in the constant fine rain of cold distilled water that fell from the underside of every plate. They were warm because they were warm-blooded, or because the basalt was warm, or because the chemistry they ran on was warm, and in any case the distinction was not important to them. They were busy, in the wet, in the seams, in the gutters where the small channels of condensation ran down toward the next vent and the next chimney. They had work. They had, in the xenobiologists' phrase, *a great deal to do*, and they did it, and they did not check the season or the weather or the hour.

They were the oldest inhabitants, except for the basalt.

## The Basalt

The basalt was the oldest inhabitant.

The basalt had been there before the people. The basalt had been there before the worked basalt, before the basalt had been taught to grow. The basalt had been there as the basaltic and silica-laden chimneys of the original thermal vents, the long black-white mineral chimneys that the seabed had been quietly extruding for geological time before anyone had come up.

The chimneys had grown. The chimneys grew. The chimneys were, in some sense, still growing — imperceptibly, in the lower stacks, at a rate that the maintenance crews measured in millimeters per year and that the xenobiologists, with their different timescales, did not bother with.

The chimneys had been there before the buildings. The buildings were the chimneys. The worked basalt of the plates and the streets and the bridges was something that had been done to the chimneys. That something had been done *by* someone, and the *by* was, in the histories, ambiguous. The histories did not say if the first builders had been human. The histories did not say if the first builders had been alive, in any honest sense, or had been something else that had been alive when it was alive. The histories said: the vents were warm, and wet, and saturated with a chemistry that was almost life, and someone had finished the job.

The worked basalt was warm to the touch when you wanted it warm, cool when you wanted it cool. It accepted a load without deforming. It accepted a voltage without resisting. It had a faint grain that you could see if you held a piece up to the light — like wood, as if it remembered having once been alive.

It might have been alive.

It had a faint warmth, in your hand, that was the warmth of a thing that was using warmth to do things, and it had a faint grain that ran in a direction you could not quite determine, and it had, in certain lights, a faint *sheen* — not reflective, not exactly, but alive, in the way that a horse's flank is alive, in the way that a fish's flank is alive, in the way that something alive is alive when the light catches it sideways and you cannot say what you have seen but you have seen it.

The offworld xenobiologists, when they came, had wanted very much to ask the basalt whether it was alive. They had tried. They had applied their instruments. They had measured its electrical activity, its thermal output, its response to stimuli. They had found: a faint baseline of activity, consistent across samples, suggestive of metabolism. A faint responsiveness to load, in the manner of muscle. A faint *something*, in the manner of a thing that was not quite asleep.

The basalt had not answered. The basalt did not, on the whole, answer. The basalt was warm, and it grew, and it remembered, and it did not, on the whole, say.

The people lived in it. The people walked on it. The people worked on it, in maintenance. The people took it for granted, in the manner of people who have lived in a place for centuries and have stopped asking the place what it was.

The basalt, if it was alive, did not, on the whole, mind.

## The Rain

The rain was the most constant inhabitant.

The rain fell, constantly, from the underside of every plate in the upper stacks, gathered into beads on every inward-facing wall, ran in fine sheets down every bridge strut, dripped from every balcony, streamed from every seam. The rain was distilled. The rain was the same temperature as the warm ocean far below, which is to say the temperature of blood, which is to say not cold and not hot. The rain had fallen, in some form, since the vents had begun to evaporate, which is to say since the planet had been the planet, which is to say forever, or so close to forever that the difference did not matter.

The rain fell through the city the way blood falls through a body: through the packed corridors, through the dense stacked levels, through the narrow gaps between plates and chimneys, slow and constant and everywhere. The rain was on every surface. The rain was in every breath. The rain was the medium in which the city lived.

The rain was not, on the whole, alive. The rain did not, on the whole, mind.

The rain was the city's blood. The rain was the city's breath. The rain was what the city was made of, mostly, and what the city was full of, mostly, and what the city was, mostly, when you stopped to think about it: mostly water, mostly falling, mostly in the way.

The rain was the most constant inhabitant.

## Marn-8

Marn-8 was a person.

She had been a person for fifty years, more or less. She had been a maintenance worker, in the lower stacks, for less of those fifty years than she had been other things, but more of them than she had been in the upper stacks, and more of them than she had been anywhere else. She had a small smooth disc behind her left ear, set into the mastoid bone, that was warm to the touch, perceptibly, and that listened, and translated her utterances into the heads of others. She had a yellow slicker, and a pair of boots with magnetic plates in the soles, and a face that was, in the wet gray light of the lower stacks, the color of a fish's belly. She had a colleague, waiting at the next inspection point. She had a plate-edge that was within tolerance.

She stepped onto the bridge. The rain hit her slicker. The roar pressed against her. The chimneys rose around her, fused, packed, weeping, warm.

She had been across this bridge ten thousand times. She crossed it now. She thought, into her transducer, things she did not need to say.

The rain fell on her. The basalt, beneath her, was warm. The seam-things, in their seams, did not notice her. The roar pressed against her, and she leaned against it, and she crossed.

She was an inhabitant. She had been for fifty years. She was, on the whole, in the manner of people, mostly rain.
