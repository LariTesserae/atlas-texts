# What Lives at the Spire of Vesh-Khora

Nothing lives at the spire. That is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing the spire itself seems to want you to understand, gently, the way a long-forgotten room wants you to understand that it is no longer expecting anyone.

What lived here did not live the way we use the word. It did not eat, except in the sense that the stone drinks the dew that forms on its cooler faces in the small hours. It did not breathe, except in the way the transits exhale when they set you down. It did not reproduce. It did not die, exactly, because to die you have to have been the kind of thing that *was*, in a body, in a moment. What was here was something more like a posture — a way the spire was held for a long time, the way a hand holds a cup.

The makers, if they were makers, did not arrive. They *condensed*. That is the wrong word. They *crystallized*. That is also wrong. The right word is in a language you do not speak, and the chamber where it would have been carved is the chamber with seven sides and seven objects, and you stood in it and you did not understand. The makers were a behavior of the spire. They were what the spire did when it had been standing alone on its plateau long enough to develop something — a habit, a hunger, a preference. The way a stalactite is what a cave does when it has been dripping long enough. The way a wave is what a beach does when it has been struck enough times. They were not imposed on the stone. They were *expressed by it*, the way a face is expressed by the muscles underneath.

You can see them in the wear. That is the honest truth. The makers wore the spire the way water wears limestone — slowly, locally, by being themselves for a long time in one place. The basin in the corner is the right depth for a knee that pressed into it, repeatedly, for a long while. The ledge at the height for sitting has a faint concavity at its center, a slow erosion of the glass by something that sat there night after night, perhaps, or century after century, in the dim weak-tea light. The transits are smooth. The word *smooth* is not strong enough. They are *polished*, in the way that only patient things polish, and there is no scratch on them, no chip, no sign of haste. The makers descended and ascended through these openings thousands of times, and each passage took a layer of glass off, and the glass came back, and the layer was taken again, for a length of time you cannot imagine. The makers were patient in a way that patient is too small a word for. They were the patience *itself* of the spire, considered as a single ongoing act.

What did they look like? You will not like the answer, which is that you do not know, and you cannot know, and the spire will not tell you, because the spire does not know. The makers were not bodies in the way you are a body. They did not have outlines. They had *regions* — areas where the stone was warmer, where the patterns on the walls were a little deeper, where the air held a fraction more of the copper-and-old-sweet smell. You walked through these regions and felt them as a kind of emphasis, a slight quickening, the way a room quickens when someone has just left it. You did not see them because they were not the kind of thing that reflects the weak-tea light back at you. They were more like the way a sound is in a room after the sound has stopped. They were the *having-been* of the makers, still hanging in the chambers like perfume.

Some of them, you understand now, were larger than others. You felt this in the size of the wear — the great smoothed concavity at the bottom of one chamber, where something must have rested against the wall for an age, low and broad. The long vertical groove in another wall, polished to a high shine, where something must have stood upright and slightly *leaning*, for a span of time that has no analogue in your experience. The deep footprint-shape hollow in a floor near the bottom, where the glass has been pressed down by something that put its whole weight there, once, and then — and this is the part that catches in your throat — *did not put its weight there again*, and the spire has been waiting for the weight to return ever since.

They had voices. You have to believe this because the chamber near the bottom, the one with the long smooth bench of black glass, is shaped for speech. The walls there curve gently inward, focusing. The floor is slightly dished. The bench is at exactly the right height for a body that wanted to be heard rather than to hear. The ozone in that chamber is the smell of a voice being used, the smell of air being broken into pieces small enough to carry meaning. The hair on your forearms rose because you were in a room where meaning had lived, recently, on a geological timescale. Someone, or the residue of someone, *spoke* here. The spire remembers the shape of the speech even if it has forgotten the words. There is a difference between the two, and the difference is the spire.

They were not numerous. Seven. You keep arriving at seven — seven faces, seven seams, seven objects on the shelf, seven makers. This is too clean to be coincidence, and too consistent to be architecture. The seven were a *septave*. The word means, as close as you can translate: *a thing that is itself by being seven things that know they are one thing.* They were not seven individuals. They were seven positions in a single body, the way a chord is several notes that have agreed to be heard together. They circulated through the spire. They each had their chambers — the maker of the deep water, who lived near the bottom and listened to the current behind the wall; the maker of the slow bright thread, who kept the chamber where the spiral climbs the wall; the maker of the ozone, who had the bench; the maker of the dry fibrous thing on the shelf, which you now understand was not an artifact but a *keepsake*, a piece of one of them preserved in a form that could be left behind; the maker of the sphere that rings; the maker of the disc with the spirals; and the seventh, the one you never found a chamber for, the one whose contribution was the entering.

That is the one who exhaled when you put your palm to the plate. That is the one whose warmth came through the wall to meet you. That is the one who is gone now, or has folded back into the stone, and the seam is the same color as the others because the welcome is no longer being offered by anyone in particular. It is being offered by the spire.

They left. This is the part you will turn over for years, the small smooth stone warm in your pocket. They left, or they *thinned*, or they completed something. The chamber with the seven objects was arranged in the order of their departure. The first object to the left is the first to have gone. The sphere is the second. The disc is the third. The piece of one of them is the fourth. You do not know which is fifth, sixth, seventh. The chamber is patient. It will still be there when you come back, if you come back, and the dust will still not have settled, because there is no dust, and the objects will still be in their order, and the maker of the entering will not be at the door, and the door will still open, because the spire does not know that it should not open any more. It only knows that someone *could* come. It holds itself in the posture of welcome. It is the bow after the guests have left. It is the shape of a question no longer being asked.

The current behind the wall is not water. You are sure of this now. It is the seventh maker, the one who had no chamber, moving through the deep stone the way blood moves through a sleeping body. It carries things. You cannot say what. It murmurs. It is not going anywhere. It is *maintaining* — keeping the basins filling, keeping the nodes glowing in their different colors by day and by night, keeping the transits slow and patient, keeping the welcome at the seventh face. The spire is not abandoned. It is *tended*, from the inside, by the part of the maker that did not leave. The rest of them are gone — out through the seams, out across the plateau, out into the copper haze, into whatever they became when they stopped being a behavior of stone. You have seen the things they left. You have felt the ozone of their last conversation. You have drunk the water they drank.

The small smooth stone in your pocket is a piece of the seventh maker. You did not pick it up. It was placed there, the way the sphere was placed on the shelf, the way the disc was placed, the way the fibrous thing was placed. The spire gave you a thing to take with you, because the spire has decided, or the part of the maker that remains has decided, that you should not leave empty-handed. The stone will not ring when you tap it because the part of the maker that rings has already gone. The stone is warm because the part of the maker that tended is still tending. You will keep it in your pocket, and it will be warm against your thigh on cold nights, and you will not be able to say, to anyone, what you are keeping.

When you go back — and you will go back, the way water goes back to a stone it has once soaked — you will find the spire unchanged, and the seam will open for you, and the transit will take you down softly, and the basins will be full, and the nodes will be lit in their colors, and the current will murmur behind the wall, and nothing will have moved, and nothing will have aged, and the maker of the entering will not be at the door, and the door will still open.

That is what lives at the Spire of Vesh-Khora. A welcome that has outlived its welcomer. A machine that does not know it is a machine. A kindness that has been left running.
