What lives here is not anything you would call a creature. Not really. Not in any of the ways that the word usually means. The Shelf is a population that has crossed some line a long time ago — crossed it so long ago that whatever was on the other side is what it is now, and there is no going back, and there is probably no memory of a side to return to.

The grazers are what you notice first, because the grazers are what is on top.

They are long. Each one is roughly the length of a dog stretched out and then extended past where a dog would stop being a dog. They have many legs — too many, or at least more than is comfortable to count on a single passing, because they keep moving while you are trying to, and the legs blur into a kind of continuous underside of motion, like a slow conveyor made of their own body. They are low. They are built close to the crust the way a sled is built close to snow, because the air up here does not forgive height and the cold up here does not forgive distance from the warm ground.

The crust is their floor and their food and their world. They walk with their heads down into it, and the mouth-parts — there is not a word for the mouth-parts; there is a *region* of the head where the mouth is, and the region is wider than you would like — the mouth-parts work the felt of moss and lichen and the small blunt rooted things that don't bother flowering. They do not tear. They do not crop. They *work*. They work the way a card works wool. They take the living crust and they pull it through themselves and they put back, behind them, a changed crust — thinner, denser, somehow darker, a crust that has been through a process the way flour has been through a mill. The crust they leave is not dead. It is *processed*. It is a substrate. It is feedstock for whatever comes next.

And they are patient. They are patient in a way that is not like human patience, which is a waiting-for-something. They are patient in a way that does not have an object. They have no destination. They walk because walking is what a grazer is, the way circulation is what blood is. A grazer that stops walking is not a resting grazer. A grazer that stops walking is a grazer that has had something happen to it that the species has a word for but that I do not.

There are so many of them. There are so many of them that the crust does not have a direction of flow on it — when you look out across a wide part of the Shelf, you cannot tell which way the grazers are going, because they are going every way, in slow overlapping circuits that braid and unbraid and braid again like a slow weave of bodies. None of them are colliding. None of them are hurrying past each other. They are *dodging* each other — barely, in increments so small that the dodge is more like a continuous adjustment, a constant small conversation of turning aside, the way a school of fish dodges through itself, except the school is the size of a landscape.

Their sound is their presence. The milling roar is them. It is the sound of all those legs on the crust at once, and all those mouth-parts working on the crust at once, and it is also, somehow, the sound of their insides doing whatever their insides do — a low continuous grinding, almost a purr, except it is not a purr because a purr implies a momentary pleasure and this is the sound of a thing that has been going on for longer than pleasure has been a concept. It rises out of the crust itself. You feel it in the rock you are sitting on. You feel it in the bones of your heels.

The grazers do not look up.

A grazer passed me at three feet and it did not look at me. I do not think it lacked eyes — there were structures in the head region that suggested the possibility of eyes — but I do not think those structures were pointed at me. They were pointed at the crust. They were pointed at the work. There is a word for what I saw on the head region, and the word is *attention*, except that attention implies a choice, and I do not think this grazer was making a choice. I think this grazer was *itself*, and itself was pointed at the crust the way a river is pointed downhill.

I tried, standing very still, to be noticed. I was not noticed. The grazer processed past me, and its wake of processed crust passed under me, and behind me the crust was subtly changed in a way I could not name.

---

Above the grazers, the ferrying things.

I want to call them insects because they have something like the outline of an insect — a segmented body, an articulated casing, the suggestion of limbs or wings or both. But they are not insects. Their casing is not chitin. It is something harder, something that has a faint metallic glint when the sideways sun hits it, something that rings slightly when two of them collide in the air, the way a coin rings when you drop it on stone. They are smaller than I expected from the sound. Most of them are thumb-sized. A few are larger. None of them are large. None of them need to be large. They are present in *numbers*.

The air is not empty up here. I had assumed the air would be empty up here — that thin air meant empty air, that altitude meant absence. The air up here is full. It is so full that movement through it is textured. When I walked through the dense columns of ferrying things, the air felt thick, almost syrupy, the way air feels before a storm but on a smaller scale, and there was a faint drag against my skin, like the air was combing through me.

They hum.

The hum is the first thing you feel, before you see them. It is not a sound your ears want. It is pitched in a register that resonates in the teeth, in the mastoid, in the soft parts of the inner ear that are not usually asked to resonate. When you stand in the dense columns for any length of time, the hum becomes a presence, a low almost-electrical pressure behind the face, and there is a taste that comes with it — a taste that I can only describe as the taste of *charge*, though I do not know what charge tastes like, except that I tasted something in the columns of ferrying things and the something tasted like metal and ozone and a thing that is not either of those and that the tongue recognizes before the mind does.

They are ferrying. They are ferrying in a way that has a purpose and a destination and a logic that I can almost — almost — see.

I watched one.

I chose one, or at least I pointed my attention at one, the way you point your attention at a single fish in a school to see what it is doing. It was carrying something — a parcel, a packet, a thing about the size of a small nut, held in the jointed limbs at its front. I could not see what the parcel was made of. It was one of the things the grazers leave behind. It was a unit of processed crust. The ferrying thing flew with it. I do not mean flew the way a bird flies, with intention and direction. I mean flew the way a current flows — steadily, along a line that had been agreed upon long before this particular ferrying thing was born. It was following a route. It was following a *lane*. Up close, the lanes are invisible. From a little distance away, you can see them — a faint organizing of the air, the ferrying things clustered slightly more densely along certain axes, and the axes form a network above the Shelf, and the network looks, from a distance, like the lines of longitude on a globe, except the globe is the Shelf and the lines are moving.

It carried the parcel to a fissure. There is a fissure somewhere — every few hundred feet of the Shelf, a fissure opens between two of the long stone ridges, and the fissure is dark and deep, and the ferrying things take their parcels into the fissures and they do not come back up the same way they went down, or they come back up the way they went down but empty-handed, and the parcels are not there anymore, the parcels have been taken in by the rock.

There is no chimney here. There is no roof. The place does not look like a place that runs. The place does not look like anything except a high cold scrubby place with a low ringing hum in the air.

But.

On a clear night — and the nights here are clear because the air is too thin to form a thought about weather — the columns of ferrying things in the air light up. They light up the way a wire lights up when a current passes through it — a faint pale glow, a faint pale outlining, the ferrying things becoming briefly visible as themselves, as the things they are, as a faint moving constellation of slow pale lines of light arranged in routes. The whole Shelf is mapped at night in slow pale light, and the map is a circulatory map, and the map is running, and the running is the only thing happening here. The Shelf is a body. The Shelf is its own body. The grazers are its digestion. The ferrying things are its blood. The fissures are its lungs.

I do not know what the Shelf is metabolizing.

---

Down in the fissures, the fissure-workers.

I only saw them once. A slab shifted under the freeze — there is a freeze every night here, even in the season that is not winter, the cold contracts the rock and the rock cracks and a slab will sometimes shift on a neighbor — and when the slab shifted, the light poured down into the crack it had been shading, and there, deep down, in the gap between the slabs, pale things were working.

They were pale because they had never seen the light. They were pale the way cave fish are pale. They had long jointed appendages, many of them, and the appendages were working the substrate that the ferrying things had been bringing down — working it, processing it further, pulling it into something else, doing whatever the next thing is that needs to be done down there in the dark. I could not see what the next thing was. I could not see the output. I could see the workers. I could see them working. I could see the long pale jointed appendages moving steadily in the faint sideways light that had found them for the first time in however long they had been down there.

One of them paused.

One of them, after the light had been pouring down for several seconds, paused in its work. It did not look up. It could not have looked up, I think, even if it had wanted to. But it paused the way a worker pauses when a noise has reached it that is not a noise it expected — a stillness in the middle of the working, a held motion, a question the body was asking itself.

Then it went back to work. The slab settled. The light was shut out. The fissure went dark again.

The fissure-workers go on working.

---

And the crust between all of them — the felted mat, the wet breathing surface, the living felt that the grazers work on, that the ferrying things carry from, that the fissures receive, that the whole vast slow enterprise is conducted across.

The crust is alive. I do not know how else to say it. The crust is alive in the way that soil is alive — in the way that soil is alive and we do not say it because we are standing on it. The crust is a community. The crust is a layer. The crust is being *pushed through* — the grazers push it through themselves, the ferrying things carry the products away, the fissure-workers take the products down, and somewhere down there in the dark the work continues and the work has been continuing and the work is the place.

The crust sweats. It sweats water, constantly, the way a living thing sweats water, because the place is full of water — water coming up out of the rock, water coming down out of the fog, water coming out of the breath of all the creatures working it, water held in the felted layer itself. The water in the swales moves. I told you this. The water in the swales moves as if the crust under it were breathing. I have been thinking about this. I think the water in the swales is moving because the crust under it *is* breathing. I think the crust is alive in a more emphatic sense than I was first willing to admit to myself. I think the crust is the slow metabolism. I think the crust is the body that the grazers are the digestion of, that the ferrying things are the blood of, that the fissure-workers are the lungs of. I think the Shelf is not a place that has things on it. I think the Shelf is a thing, and the things on it are the things of it, the way the things in you are the things of you.

If you sit on a rock at the edge of the Shelf for long enough, the thought arrives, and the thought does not leave, that the Shelf is alive the way a continent is alive — slowly, at a tempo you cannot perceive as tempo, in a register you cannot access. The Shelf is alive in a way that your instruments will not catch because your instruments are scaled to your kind of life and this kind of life is not your kind and the scale is wrong and the register is wrong and the wrongness is the whole point.

---

There are no names here.

I want to give you names. I want to call them something — the grazers, the ferrying things, the fissure-workers, the crust. I have been calling them that, in this account, but I am using those names as a courtesy to you. The names are not theirs. They have, as far as I could tell, no names. They have no use for names. They have no use for being singled out. They have no use for being noticed as individuals. The grazers are grazers, plural. The ferrying things are ferrying things, plural. The fissure-workers are fissure-workers, plural. The Shelf is Shelf.

A grazer does not know it is a grazer. A grazer is its walking, the way you are your breathing.

I tried, once, very late, when I had been sitting on the rock for so long that my fingers were numb and the cold had gotten into the architecture of my hands, to find an individual in the milling. I picked out one grazer — a particular one, a particular pair of forward legs, a particular region of head — and I watched it, and I followed it, and I stayed with it. I stayed with it for the better part of an hour. It walked. It worked the crust. It walked. It worked the crust. I could not find a self in it. There was no self to be found. There was the walking and the working. There was no inside of the grazer that I was being denied access to. There was no grazer to have an inside. There was the work. There was the body, and the body was the work. The work was the body. The body was not an envelope around a self. The body was a process.

The grazer I was watching passed within three feet of me.

It did not look up.

I am glad it did not look up. I am glad because I think, if it had looked up, what I would have seen in the looking-up would not have been recognition or curiosity or hostility or any of the things that look like looking-up in creatures we know. What I would have seen, I think, would have been the brief registering of an interruption to the work — a pause in the walking, a pause in the working, a moment of being something other than a grazer, and the moment would have passed and the walking would have resumed. I am glad it did not look up because being looked at, by a thing that does not have a self to do the looking, would have been a thing I would have had to think about for the rest of my life.

---

What lives here is an economy.

That is the word I keep arriving at, and it is the word I keep not being satisfied with, and it is the word that is closer than any of the others. An economy is a system of making a living. The Shelf is a system of making a living. The Shelf makes a living from the rock and the water and the light and the cold, and the living it makes is the whole of itself, and the whole of itself is the making of it. There is no waste. There is no surplus. There is no part of the Shelf that is not part of the making. The output of the grazers is the input of the ferrying things, and the output of the ferrying things is the input of the fissure-workers, and the output of the fissure-workers — what is it? I do not know what the output is. I do not know what is being made at the bottom of the fissures, in the dark, in the pale working stillness of those jointed appendages. I do not know what the Shelf is making.

I do not think the Shelf knows what the Shelf is making. I do not think the question applies.

The Shelf makes. The Shelf goes on making. The Shelf has been going on making.

This is what lives here. A making. An enormous, slow, packed, bright, loud, cold, damp, almost infinitely patient making. The Shelf is the making. The making is the Shelf.

And the making does not require you to understand it. The making does not require you to witness it. The making does not require you to be here. The making was going on. The making will be going on. You may sit on a stone at the edge for as long as you like. You may stay for a long time.

The making does not have a rhythm.

The making has a density.

And you, sitting on the stone, are part of the density for as long as you are sitting on the stone, and then you are not, and the making has not noticed.
