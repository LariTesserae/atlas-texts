# The Inhabitants of the Verdant Vault

## The Custodians of the Weave

What lives here does not live here the way you live anywhere.

The vault is *inhabited*, but the word wants adjusting. To say the creatures *live* in the Verdant Vault is like saying a sentence *lives* in a mouth—true in the breathing sense, but missing the part where the sentence is also being made by the speaking. Nothing here has simply taken up residence. Everything here is part of the project. The room is a sentence in progress, and its inhabitants are the pronouns, the verbs, the soft connective tissue that lets the thing keep going.

They are called, if they are called anything, the *Weavers*. The monkey-thing you glimpsed is one of them. There are many. There have always been many, or there have been many for as long as *always* is a unit of time that applies in this place, which it isn't.

### The Small Hands

The Weaver in its most common form is about the size of a cat. Its fur is the wet-stone grey of lichen on a tomb, and its face is something you almost recognize—a primate's face, a child's face, an old woman's face, layered over one another like transparencies on a lantern. It has four hands. The upper two are delicate, almost human, given over to the fine work: threading a fiber into a half-formed glyph, smoothing a crease in the weave with the pad of a thumb, holding a leaf up to the crystalline orbs to test whether it will take the light. The lower two are broader, foot-pawed, calloused. They climb. They brace. They anchor the body while the upper hands do their thinking.

Its eyes are the strangest part. They are not two. They are *seven*, arranged across the brow in a gentle crescent, and each one sees a different thing—or perhaps the same thing in seven different scales of time. When the smallest, innermost eye blinks, it is because something *microsecond* has changed in the weave: a fiber slackened, a knot loosened. When the largest, outermost eye blinks, it is because a season has turned somewhere far away in the canopy, and the whole cathedral is exhaling.

A Weaver does not speak. It does not need to. It communicates by *placement*: where it sits, how long it sits there, what it looks at while it sits. A Weaver who wants another Weaver to come help with a tangle of growth will sit at the base of the tangle and look upward without moving for six or seven minutes. The other arrives. The work begins. This is, you come to understand, the entire political system of the vault: the long look, the patient summons, the work that follows.

They live for a very long time. None of them knows how long, because none of them has ever died. There are Weavers who have been working the same patch of glyph for what you would call centuries. Their hands have changed. Their fur has silvered. The patch has not. The patch, in fact, has been worked over so many times by so many successive hands that it has become a kind of *palimpsest*—a record not of what was written but of the act of writing, a wall that remembers the writers more than it remembers the words.

They are gentle to one another. They groom each other with the upper hands while the lower hands continue to work. A Weaver will spend an entire afternoon removing a single mite from the fur of a sibling and never once pause in its braiding. When a young Weaver is being trained, an elder will sit behind it and *wear its hands*—placing its own paws over the younger's paws and guiding them through a glyph, again and again, until the muscle memory settles in like a sediment.

They eat. You don't see them eat, but you understand they must, because the floor around their perches is littered with the soft fibrous husks of something like a seed pod. The husks are green and faintly luminescent, and the small jeweled creatures of the canopy come down at night to consume them. This is, perhaps, the only commerce of the vault: the Weavers feed the canopy, the canopy feeds them, and somewhere in the transaction a glyph gets a little more legible.

## The Crowned Browsers

Lower down, where the light thickens, the deer-things move.

There are perhaps a dozen of them. They are tall—shoulder height a meter and a half, but the antlers add another meter on top of that, and the antlers are not antlers, they are *fronds*—true fern fronds, fully photosynthetic, that unfurl in slow spirals and catch the honey-colored light and turn it into a faint breathing pulse of green. When a Crowned Browser walks beneath a crystalline orb, the antler-fronds lift slightly toward it, the way sunflowers track a sun. They are not animals that happen to wear plants. They are *animals that are also plants*, a chimera whose two halves have stopped negotiating.

Their legs are too many, as you noticed—six, not four, the extra pair set high on the ribs like a second tier, used only when the animal is moving through dense growth or when it wants to step *over* something without putting its head down. The legs are slender and fawn-pale, and the feet are soft, almost spatulate, and they leave no print in the spongy floor because the floor yields to them and rises again behind them as if nothing had passed.

Their mouths are interior things, mostly soft palate and grinding ridges, and they browse by *enfolding* a low frond into a kind of pouch at the side of the head, where it is slowly reduced to a sweet green paste. They eat constantly, but gently. They have never pulled a plant up by the root. They have never been *able* to. Their mouths do not have the geometry for tearing.

They see you. They see you the way a deer sees a familiar shape through a window: with the long, gentle attention of an animal for whom surprise is a category that has been retired. They will let you approach. They will let you put your hand on the velvet of a flank. They will resume browsing. They will not, however, *recognize* you. Recognition requires that you be a *type* they have a place for, and you are not. You are, to them, a kind of weather.

The Crowned Browsers are not Weavers. They are something else—something more passive, more architectural. They are the vault's *grazing layer*, the part of the system that turns standing growth into a slow constant supply of nutrients. They are tended, in turn, by the Weavers, who check their antlers for parasites and gently prune a frond that has grown so long it is interfering with the path. The Browsers submit to this pruning with the same long attention. They lift their heads. They lower them again.

Their young are born, you think, but you have never seen one. You have seen, in a low thicket near the back wall, a fawn-colored shape that might have been a sleeping juvenile, with antlers no larger than a man's hand, already unfurling their first true frond. It watched you from six eyes it did not yet know how to close. It went back to sleep.

## The Luminaries

The jeweled things in the canopy are called, by the Weavers who work near them, the *Tink*.

A Tink is the size of your fist and the geometry of a dropped thought. It has legs—six, twelve, you lose count, you have never successfully counted—arranged around a central thorax that is also an abdomen that is also, you begin to suspect, a *light source*. Its body is jeweled: plates of iridescent green, lacquered black, a deep arterial orange that you recognize from sunsets. The plates are not armor. They are *photophores*, biological lamps that the Tink can tune, dim, brighten, pulse. The chiming you hear from the canopy is, in part, the sound of the plates vibrating as they adjust their light.

They are not insects. They are not birds. They are, as you noticed, *fixtures*. They belong to the room the way the crystalline orbs belong to the room: as deliberate ornament, as living light fixtures, as part of the décor of a place that does not distinguish between decoration and function.

But they *do* things. They groom the canopy, eating the small parasitic growths that would otherwise choke the crystalline orbs. They maintain the bioluminescent trails that mark the room's paths, refreshing them nightly with a secretion that hardens into a faint green glass. When a fiber in a liana weakens, a squad of Tinks will descend and *re-stitch* it from the outside, using their own bodies as shuttles. This is, the Weavers tell you without telling you, the original labor. The Tinks were here first. The Weavers came later to manage a system that had gotten too large for them.

A Tink in flight is a small miracle. It unfolds membranes from between its legs—four, six, eight translucent sails—and drifts on the warm updrafts of the vault, and as it drifts its plates click against each other in a sequence that sounds, if you listen for a long time, almost like language. *Tink. Tink-tink. Tink.* A greeting. A location call. A small announcement: *I am here, in this part of the room, doing this part of the work.*

They die. You find their husks sometimes, in the drifts of crisp chartreuse leaf-fall at the base of a column—small jeweled shells, hollow, still faintly glowing. The Weavers collect these husks with care and weave them into the lower courses of the glyphs, where they catch the light and throw it back. The dead Tinks become letters in the wall. The wall remembers them.

They have, you realize, an afterlight. It is not a soul, not in any sense your tradition has a word for. But when a Tink's plates go dark, the trails it has laid continue to glow for hours, then days, then—some of them—*years*. The vault is lit, in part, by the light of Tinks that died before you arrived.

## The Watcher on the Wall

There is, in one corner of the vault, where the canopy thins and the felt-curtain wall begins, a single animal that is neither Weaver nor Browser nor Tink. It is something else again.

It is the size of a large dog. It has no legs that you can see, only a long sinuous body that rests along a horizontal liana like a cat on a branch, and a head that is more *mask* than head—a flat, almost ceremonial face, with two enormous eyes set wide, that regard the wall behind them with an attention so patient it has weight.

It is called, by the Weavers, the *Caretaker of the Flicker*. It does not weave. It does not browse. It does not chime. It lies along its liana, and it *looks at the wall*, and the wall, while it is being looked at, flickers less.

You understand, after watching it for a long time, that this is its work. It is the room's *conscious attention* turned to the place where the room is thinnest. It is the guard at the door that is not a door. It holds the vault together by *being awake to it*, by refusing to let the shimmer on the back wall gather itself into a street, a face, a bowl of fruit, a child's hand, an older reflection. When you approached the wall, the Caretaker's eyes did not move. But you felt, when you stepped back, that the wall had *eased*. As if something had been holding its breath.

The Caretaker does not blink. You do not think it can blink. Its eyes are wide, and they are dry, and they are fixed on a point about three meters to your left on the wall, and they have been fixed on that point, or that kind of point, for a very long time. It is moulting. Its pelt—grey, dense, soft as the felt floor—comes away in small clumps around its haunches, and the Weavers collect the clumps and weave them into the lowest courses of the glyphs near the wall. The Caretaker's shed fur is part of the room's binding.

It eats the Tink husks. Only the husks. Nothing else. It takes them from where the Weavers have set them out, in a small neat pile at the base of its liana, and it consumes them slowly, one a day, and the act of consuming seems to *re-light* something in its eyes—a brief brightening, a flicker of attention that travels down its body and out through its paws into the liana and up into the canopy.

When the flicker on the wall is bad—when the wall is showing whole rooms, whole streets, whole afternoons in stone places with no growth in them—the Caretaker eats two husks a day. The Weavers bring two husks. No one asks why.

## The Weave Itself

You have come to understand, over hours or years or whatever the unit is, that none of these creatures are *separate* from the vault. They are not guests. They are *organs*. The vault is a single large organism in which the Weavers are the hands, the Browsers are the slow digestion, the Tinks are the immune system and the lighting, the Caretaker is the *consciousness at the back*, the vigilance that keeps the whole thing from unravelling into the other rooms that press against the wall.

And you? You are sitting on the felt floor, and the floor is accepting your weight all the way down, and you have not yet been assigned a function. The monkey-Weaver that looked up at you and blinked all its eyes and returned to its work—it was, perhaps, sizing you up. It was, perhaps, deciding where in the weave you might be threaded.

The note holds.

The tink continues.

The wall flickers at your back, almost imperceptibly.

You sit, and you are not yet a part of the sentence, but the sentence has begun, you feel, to make room.
