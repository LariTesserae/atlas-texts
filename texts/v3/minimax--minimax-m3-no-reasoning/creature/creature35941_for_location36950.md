The basin is full of watchers, and most of the watching is mutual, and most of it is patient. There is no hurry here, and there are few surprises, and the creatures that live in this bowl have arranged themselves — or been arranged — into a kind of quiet consensus about how to be in the same place at the same time. The consensus is not a truce. A truce implies there was once a war. What holds the basin together is older than war and quieter than peace. It is more like grammar. It is more like the way water finds the shape of whatever it sits in.

The watcher — there is one — has been here three days. She sits on the white floor with her notebook in her lap, and her clothes have darkened at the small of her back and at the creases of her elbows, and a pale thing that may be a root has begun to curl around her left boot with the slow deliberateness of something that has decided she is going to stay. She is being taken in. She is being taken in the way the rain is taken in, the way the column of light is taken in, the way the rim was taken in: gently, completely, without comment. In another week the ferns will lean against her shins. In a month the moss will come up over her soles. Her handwriting is already changing — not because she is changing, but because the wet air has got into the paper and made it accept the ink differently. She is already, in her small way, an inhabitant.

The smallest inhabitants are too small to have names, and the ones that do have names do not keep them. The wet ticking she hears in the leaf litter is the sound of beetles the size of her smallest fingernail working through the mulch, and they are legion, and they are not working together, but they are working in such dense parallel that the sound acquires a coherence it does not deserve. The aggregate she feels through her soles when she stands still is not, she has decided, a single creature. It is a population that has forgotten it is a population. When she shifts her weight, the aggregate shifts with her — not in fear, not in attention, but in the way a swarm shifts when a stone is dropped into it, only slower, and with more politeness. The small thing with too many legs that walked across her notebook was not curious about her. It was simply using the most level surface available. The track it left in the wet page is the same color as the page, slightly raised, still legible an hour later, and she has decided not to brush it off. It came back. It crossed her page again. She did not brush it off again.

Above her, in the canopy, something keens. It is not a sound with a source. It is more like the canopy itself has been tuned, and a particular arrangement of leaves and air and moisture produces a high, thin note that drifts down in pieces. She has listened for hours and cannot find the singer. She has begun to suspect the singer is not a single thing but a consensus of small motions — the uncurling of a frond, the unfolding of the flat bright thing, the passage of a creature so light the leaves do not register its weight. The note is the sound the basin makes when it is being itself. She has come to rely on it. When it stops, briefly, the basin feels as if it has stopped breathing, and then it resumes, and she breathes again.

The dragonfly-things are wrong in ways she has catalogued with care. Four wings, like dragonflies, but the wings beat at a frequency that produces no hum, only a faint displacement of air she can feel against the skin of her forearms when they pass close. Their abdomens are too long — as long as their bodies, sometimes longer, and they taper to a thread, and the thread trails behind them and writes a slow figure-eight in the wet air. They cross the pools in lanes, and the lanes are not territorial. Two dragonfly-things will cross the same lane at the same time, an inch apart, and neither will deviate. They eat something. She has not seen what. She has seen them pause above the convex pool near the listing pillar, hover, descend until their thread-trails touched the meniscus, and then rise again without drinking. They were tasting it. Or they were being tasted. She could not tell. They were doing something she had no word for, and she watched them for a long time.

The pale quadruped comes to the western pool at a regular hour. She does not know what hour. She only knows that if she sits still for long enough, the pale quadruped will come. It has the body of a small deer and the legs of something that has been edited over many generations. The joints are wrong. There are too many of them — she has counted four in each foreleg, five in each hind — and the extra joints do not appear to make it faster or slower, only more deliberate. It walks as if each step is a sentence it is composing. It drinks from the pool with the oval surface, and its tongue, when it extends, is the exact pale gold of the column of light at the basin's center. She has not worked out what this means. She has worked out that the quadruped is not afraid of her. When she shifts her weight, the quadruped does not startle. It lifts its head and regards her with eyes that are darker than its coat, and then it drinks again. It is the most patient thing she has ever seen, and she has begun to think of it as the basin's other observer, and she is not sure she is wrong.

The bright flat thing that unfolds from a low branch is harder. She has seen it three times and has not been able to confirm whether it is one creature or many. It unfolds like a fan, like a sail, like a flag that has decided to be a creature. It is the color of new birch bark. It is not symmetric. It has edges that ripple slightly when the air moves, and the rippling produces no sound. The first time she saw it, she thought it was a wing — a single wing, unattached, opening and closing on a branch. The second time, she noticed the branch was different. The third time, she noticed there was no branch. The flat thing was simply in the air, two meters off the ground, holding its shape against nothing. It folded. It disappeared. She has decided it is a creature that is also a moment, and the moment is also a creature, and the basin is in no hurry to tell her which.

The pools are inhabited, and the inhabiting is different at each pool. The oval pool at the western rim is the quadruped's. The dragonfly-things use the shallow pools near the ferns. The convex pool near the listing pillar is the pool she has not tasted. She has watched three different creatures drink from it without disturbing the meniscus, and she does not understand how. The meniscus holds. The meniscus is not water, or is water that has decided to be something else. She has put her hand above it and felt the air above the pool thicken as if the pool were breathing back. She did not put her hand in it. She will not. The pool is patient too. The pool is waiting for her to decide.

The pillar at the basin's edge is warm in the cool morning, and the ferns climb it without rooting into it. They simply lean. She has leaned against it, briefly, and felt the warmth through her jacket, and the warmth was not heat. It was more like the pillar had been thinking about her and had reached a conclusion. The pillar is not an inhabitant. The pillar is what is left of an inhabitant, or what is left of an act of inhabiting. The ferns that lean on it are the current inhabitants. The current inhabitants do not know there was a previous act. The current inhabitants simply lean.

The column of light is not an inhabitant. She has thought about this for three days. The column of light is what the basin is for. The creatures are not indifferent to it. They orient around it. The beetles spiral gently toward it as they work through the leaf litter, then away. The dragonfly-things cross its vertical line on their slow lanes and emerge unchanged. The pale quadruped's tongue is its color. The frog she saw leap through it was a small green frog, ordinary in every way, and it emerged from the other side of the column mid-leap and continued its arc as if nothing had happened. The frog may have noticed nothing. The frog may have noticed everything and decided it was not worth reporting. The column is too patient to be a beacon and too quiet to be a signal. It is more like a question the basin is asking itself, and the inhabitants are the answer that has been accumulating for a very long time.

Time here is a thing the basin manages. The brief afternoon rain is not weather. It is a punctuation the basin inserts into its own sentence. After the rain, everything is wetter, and everything glistens, and the column of light at the center brightens — or appears to brighten — and the air above the white floor fills with the thin mist she has been trying to describe. The mist rises. The mist thins. The mist returns. The fauna adjusts. The beetles surface. The dragonfly-things return to their lanes. The pale quadruped comes to drink. The flat bright thing unfolds once, briefly, and folds again. She writes in her notebook, and the small thing with too many legs comes back and crosses her page again. The root around her boot has begun to tighten. She has not moved her boot.

She is being kept.

This is not a thing she would have said a week ago. She would have said she was observing. She would have said the basin was a subject. She would have said the fauna was the fauna and she was she. But the basin has not made this distinction, and the fauna has not made this distinction, and the small thing with too many legs walked across her notebook without acknowledging the boundary, and the pale quadruped drinks beside her without acknowledging the boundary, and the root around her boot has not acknowledged the boundary. She is in the basin. The basin is in her. The watcher and the watched have been gently, slowly, without violence, edited into the same sentence.

The basin is full of watchers, and most of the watching is mutual, and most of it is patient, and she is one of them now, and the column of light at the center of the place is patient too, and what it is doing has no scale she can recognize, and she does not need to recognize it, because the recognition has been taken from her the way everything is taken from her here: gently, and without comment, and without end.
