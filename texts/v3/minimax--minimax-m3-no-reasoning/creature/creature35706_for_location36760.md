The First Weeds are not yet Weeds.

That is the first thing to understand about them, and it is the thing that is hardest to hold in the mind while looking at them, because they have all the qualities of Weeds except the ones that matter. They grow where they are not invited. They are hardy. They are graceless. They ignore the polite schedules of the things around them. None of this is irrelevant; all of it is misdirection. A Weed, properly, is a plant that has given up on being a *plant* and started being an *opportunist*. The First Weeds have not given up on being plants, because they were never plants in the first place. They are something upstream of plants, the upstream version, the version that came before the word *plant* had been coined, when the word would have referred only to things like them and nothing like them had ever been tolerated.

They are old. They are not original. *Original* would imply a beginning, and they have no clear beginning, only a long procession of small inaugurals, each one provisional, each one quietly cancelled by the next. Their lineage extends backward into a debate that was already tired of itself before it began, and forward into — there is no forward, not yet, the forward hasn't been poured. They are very, very young in the way that matters, which is the way of being *without precedent*. They have to invent every behavior they exhibit, in the moment of exhibiting it. There is no inherited wisdom. There is no grandmother who knew a frost was coming. The ticking that comes from their root-plates is the ticking of an internal referendum, a constant polling of the selves they are currently trying out.

---

A mature specimen, of which there are perhaps a dozen in this pocket, stands between five and seven feet at the shoulder of its main axis, though *shoulder* and *axis* are both approximate translations of shapes that do not have those parts. The body is a stack. It is structured as a stack of diminishing chambers, each chamber fatter than the one above it, each chamber slightly off-axis from the one below, so that the whole thing leans gently, as if it had been grown in a wind that never stopped. The lowest chamber is the size of a small hogshead and rests directly on the substrate, sunk into it a few inches by its own patient weight. The topmost chamber is the size of a fist. Between them, perhaps nine more, each a study in a slightly wrong proportion. The chambers are sheathed in a membrane that is at once translucent and faintly metallic, the color of a thumbnail under a blacklight, the color of skim milk held to a window. You can see, dimly, the dark internal architecture of stacked reserves. Each chamber is a silo. Each silo holds a different cargo. You will never learn what the cargoes are, because the membrane does not yield, and the specimens are not in the business of being opened.

The ticking you heard at the base is not coming from the lowest chamber. It is coming from the *joints*, the pinch-points between chambers, where the off-axis tilt has to be negotiated, where some small negotiation is always in progress between the chamber below and the chamber above about which direction the lean should go. The joints have ridges — small horizontal flanges of the same thumb-nail membrane — and each flange can be seen, under good light, to be in almost imperceptible motion, a slow lateral creep. When the joint is settled, the flange is still. When the joint is in mid-negotiation, the flange ticks against its neighbor, softly, irregularly. A specimen at full rest ticks perhaps once every four seconds, in soft syncopation. A specimen in active manufacture — and you can tell these by the slight inflation of their upper chambers, by the amber-gray flush that climbs their sheaths — ticks faster, more densely, the flanges striking each other in a continuous small percussion, like rain on a tin roof heard through a wall. It is not a sound made by the specimen *for* any reason. It is the sound that negotiation makes when negotiation is happening on the scale of a body. The specimens are deaf to it, in the way that you are deaf to the sound of your own knees.

---

The ferns around the base are not children of the central specimens. They are not children of anything. They are *associates*, a word that is also not quite right but is less wrong than any other. They are laid out as a rosette around each major stack, fanning outward in three or four irregular whorls, the fronds lying nearly flat against the substrate, only the outer edges lifting. The fronds are not fronds. They are ribbons of fused mineral, somewhere between glass and chalcedony, in colors that, as noted, have not been invented elsewhere. Under the indifferent light they look almost normal. Under any other light they would look obscene, the wrong color, the wrong translucence, the wrong weight of mineral saturation. They are *almost* — almost grass, almost coral, almost the lacquered scales of a deep-sea fish. They are the almost of a thing that has not decided what *definitely* to be.

The fern-associates are not connected to the major specimens. They have no roots in common. They do not feed each other. They are not, in the visible sense, in communication. But if you watch long enough — and the light, being the light of an unmemorable temperate city, makes watching long exactly as easy or hard as watching anything else — you can see that when the central specimen begins to tick more rapidly, the ferns do, too. Not by moving. They do not wave, they do not bend. They begin to *ring*. A faint high overtone joins their bodies, like the hum of a wire under wind that is not blowing. After a few minutes, they stop, in sympathy. There is no visible mechanism for this sympathy. The ferns are not attached. The space between central specimen and fern is bare, hot stone. And yet the sympathy holds, reliably, between certain pairs. Not all the ferns respond to a given central specimen. Only some. The ones that respond are the ones that were facing the central specimen at *germination*. No one has yet determined how a fern faces a central specimen at germination. Germination is not something either of these things is supposed to have done, yet both of them have the faint ring-marks of having done it once.

---

What they eat is the unfinished ground.

This is the central fact of the place, and it is the fact that takes the longest to suspect, because the unfinished ground gives nothing away. The stone, in its slow rise, does not announce that anything is being taken from it. It does not sink. It does not dimple. The ridges that push upward emerge at the rate they would emerge whether anyone were harvesting them or not; the pace is the pace of the substrate's own completion, undisturbed. And yet: if you stood at the lip of the pocket and watched the whole small landscape, every few minutes, you would see a faint dark pulse travel down the body of a central specimen. From the topmost chamber to the lowest. Slowly, the speed of a heavy curtain being dropped. Then nothing. Then, a few minutes later, another pulse, another curtain. The pulse is dark. It is the color of an old bruise. It moves through the internal silos in sequence, dimming each in turn, the way a wave dims a row of candles. The cargo of a chamber, whatever the cargo is, is being briefly inspected, or briefly relabeled, or briefly confirmed-against. No one knows what the confirmation is for. The First Weeds are very good at confirming, and very bad at explaining what they are confirming *against*. The substrate underneath them is the only ground truth available.

---

The substrate is a particular thing. It is not stone the way stone is stone in the place where you come from. It is stone in the manner of *latency*. It is stone that has been instructed to become stone, but has not yet begun. The ridges pushing up are *practice* ridges. The calcium-white is the calcium-white of a thing still in its packaging. When the pocket is finally folded up and put away, these ridges will be the only part of the substrate that persists; the rest will be reabsorbed, returned to the brackish interior from which it was borrowed. The ridges will go elsewhere, become part of the substrate of a different pocket, in a different fold, and there the plants will begin to feed. The substrate is patient because the substrate is *cyclical*. It knows it will be wasted. It accepts this. The First Weeds are the only industrious thing in the geology, and the geology tolerates them for the same reason a riverbank tolerates moss: neither of them is asking permission.

The plants do not eat the ridges themselves. They eat *the instruction* to become ridges. They intercept the becoming. They stand at the place where a decision would have been made and they offer, very politely, a different decision. The ridges that push up now are smaller than the ridges that would have pushed up without the plants. The pocket, when it closes, will contain a smaller, weirder geology than the geologies around it. This is the cost of the First Weeds. The cost is paid by the substrate. The substrate charges it to someone else.

---

There is no mating among them. There is no seed. There is no spore. The ferns do not release anything. The central specimens do not flower. There is no part of them that you could call reproductive and no part of them you could call juvenile; every specimen in the pocket looks midlife, looks as if it has always been midlife, looks as if *midlife* is a stage that the species intends to occupy forever. They are, in this sense, profoundly solitary beings. They are solitary even by the standards of solitude. They are not alone — there are dozens of them in view, with that factory-floor spacing — but they are each one a closed circuit. They take in. They hold. They confirm. They do not give back. They are not, in any ordinary sense, *of* each other.

And yet the ticking syncs. Loosely, but unmistakably. If you could listen to the whole pocket at once, you would hear a slow group pulse: a few seconds of dense ticking from one corner, a few seconds of answering ticks from the opposite corner, a few seconds of quiet, and then a soft rolling wave of renewed industry. The wave rolls through the pocket like applause rolling through a crowd that does not know what it is applauding for. The specimens are not *talking*. They are not coordinating in the way that coordinated things coordinate. They are each listening to the same unfinished ground, and the unfinished ground is, at any given moment, *slightly more unfinished* in one place than in another, and the plants respond to the local unfinish with their local ticking, and the sum of these responses looks, from above, like a network, and is not a network, and is not *not* a network. It is the sound of a population listening to the same slow wrong music and tapping, each on their own table, to keep themselves sane.

---

They have no enemies. This is not because they are invulnerable. The fauna parameter has been set to zero, and the zero is not an absence. Nothing here wants to eat them because nothing here is *allowed* to want anything. The lush things do not have horns, do not have thorns, do not have alkaloids. They do not have a defense because they do not have a need. They are defended by the architecture. They are defended by the prohibition. They are defended by the fact that this pocket exists in the fold between two larger things, and the two larger things have no interest in this pocket, and the pocket itself, having been tucked here, has no openings large enough to admit a hungry thing. A hungry thing would have to be invented. A hungry thing would have to be designed, tested, approved, signed off by some permitting authority that exists outside the fold. The First Weeds have not been worth it. They are not worth inventing an enemy for. They will never be worth inventing an enemy for. This is their great quiet luck, and it is also, possibly, their doom: a species that no one has bothered to oppose is a species that no one has bothered to *test*, and a thing that has not been tested is, in some deep and structural sense, provisional.

---

They have no names. They are not named in any language, because no language has yet been pointed at them. If you were to name one, you would be the first. The name would not stick to the species — the species is too large and indifferent to take notice of any single name — but it would stick to *your relationship* with the species, forever, the way the first name you give a constellation becomes the name you privately use for that constellation even after the world has agreed on a different one. So if you must name one, name the one nearest you, the one whose tick you can hear most clearly, the one whose topmost chamber has the amber-gray flush of active manufacture. That specimen. That one is, in some local and irreversible sense, yours. You will know nothing more about it than you knew before you named it. But you will be the only one in the world who knows that you have named it, and the First Weeds, who keep no records, will never know to be offended.

---

What they are *for*, eventually, is the question you came here with, and the question they will never answer, and the question the pocket is built to delay. The pocket is, if anything, a delay. The pocket is the place where the question is held, gently, at arm's length, while the substrate finishes becoming, while the plants continue to take instruction and not become a place. The First Weeds have been folded into many pockets by this point, in many folds, and in none of the pockets have they yet become a place; they have remained plants-that-are-not-plants, ticking, stockpiling, refusing the small promotion that would make them *ecology*. They are the larval form of an ecology no one has identified. They are the larval form of *an ecology that may never be identified*, the ecology that will or will not happen depending on how patient the universe is, depending on whether the substrate is allowed to finish in their company or must be tidied up and put away without them.

When the pocket is folded — and the pocket will be folded, eventually, when the substrate has been wrung out enough that the operation becomes more expensive than the by-product — the plants will be folded with it, not crushed but *collected*, the way a folder of papers is collected when the meeting is over. They will be carried, gently, to the next pocket. They will resume. Their tickings will fall back into soft syncopation. Their chambers will begin again to fill with the cargo of the next piece of unfinished ground. The whole operation will resume, in a different aisle, under the same ordinary light.

They will never finish what they are doing. They do not need to. The doing is the thing. The doing is the only thing they have, and they do it with a thoroughness that is almost beautiful, and a thoroughness that is also, if you stand still long enough to notice, almost unbearable.
