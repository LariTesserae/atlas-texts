# What Lives at Iridion's Crown

The hollow does not have an ecology so much as a sentence it is in the middle of speaking, and what lives here are the syllables of that sentence, each one inseparable from the breath that carries it. You cannot pick any single creature out of the chord and say *this is the animal, this is the organism* without lying a little, because the place has made a habit of refusing the boundary. Everything here is in conversation with everything else, and the conversation is the loudest thing in the bowl. So what follows is a translation of what I could overhear.

## The Resonators

The growths themselves are not decoration. They are the lung of the place, or its larynx, or both at once. I do not know what to call the kind of life that builds itself into rock the way a reef builds itself into the sea — slowly, by chemistry, by the patient betrayal of stone into tissue. But I watched one of them come up out of the floor while I stood there. I saw it in my periphery first: a pale nub, no longer than a thumb, pushing itself out of a seam in the compressed ash. By the time I turned my head it had thickened into something between a finger and a small candle, and the wax of it had already begun to set. It must have been years in the making. It took perhaps twenty seconds. As it lengthened it joined a chord that had been waiting for it, and I felt the chord accept it the way a choir accepts a new voice — without interruption, as if it had always been singing.

They are the color of things that have given up pigments. Dried-blood copper, tarnished brass, the matte of old bone, and in their deeper tissues a metallic violet that catches the high sun wrong — too cleanly — so that when you look away from a thicket of them, the afterimage swims in front of you for several seconds like a bruise in the air. Some are woody and dark and they creak when the wind finds them, low and splintering. Some are pulpy and they *give* against the wind without breaking, the way a willow gives, except that willow is green and these are the color of an old wound. The waxier ones bend in the constant current without complaint. None of them appear to drink. None of them appear to root. They are *welded* — grown into the stone the way a stalactite is grown into a cave, except that they are also growing out of it, and they are also growing away from it, and the relationship between rock and tissue is so old and so mingled that I could not tell, in places, whether I was looking at stone that had learned to breathe or at flesh that had learned to be heavy.

The clusters at the center of the bowl — the ones that have knotted themselves into a small mountain range you could walk through if the chord permitted thought — these are the oldest. They are also the loudest. Each trunk in that range is a stopped pipe of a different length, and the wind that pours across the rim finds them in sequence and plays them. The smaller ones nearer the wall sing higher, with a thin glassy shriek that is almost inaudible until you have stopped hearing the deeper notes. The largest trunks in the center hum so low that the hum is not heard but *counted* — you feel it behind your eyes, in the bones of your face, in the cartilage of your sternum. The whole bowl is a pipe organ and these are the pipes, and the wind is the blower, and the chord is never the same twice because the pipes are always being built and the wind is always being rearranged and the blower is, in some sense I could not verify, alive.

I think they feed on the gas. I think the slow geology of the cliffs is cooking heavy compounds out of itself into the thin air, and these growths are the lungs that inhale what the cliffs exhale. I think that is why they are the color they are — a chemistry that has been running for a very long time on the fumes of an exhausted rock.

## The Sprites

The threaded things are the size of a hand and they move along the undersides of the larger trunks upside down, which is the wrong way to describe it because there is no up here, only the orientation of the wind. They harvest something I could not see — a vapor, perhaps, or a secretion, or some subtle electrical weather that lives on the surface of the tissue. They have too many limbs to count quickly, and the limbs have the wrong number of joints. They are the color of the bark they are on, which is to say they are the color of dried blood or tarnished copper depending on which trunk they have chosen this hour, and when they pause they become invisible in the way that a clever painted soldier becomes invisible against the painted wall behind him.

Where they are working they drip something into the bell-shaped blossoms that nod on over-long stems above them. The blossoms are the size of my fist and they hang open like upside-down tulips in colors that should not exist in nature — a yellow that is almost green and a violet that is almost ultraviolet and a white that hurts to look at. The drip accumulates in them. The accumulation glows faintly. I do not know if it is being stored or fed or merely displayed. I suspect the larger predators know. The sprites keep working regardless. They have the industry of an ant and the charisma of a filing clerk and they are everywhere, in the way that ants are everywhere — not because they are crowded but because they are omnipresent in their craft.

## The Flutterers

The darting things are not birds. They are not insects. They have wings in the way that wind through a fence is wind — by the accident of being shaped like something that ought to have them. They shudder along the surface of the lavender mist on rapid pelvic flips, and where they pass the mist dimples into standing waves that close behind them the way water closes behind a boat. They are smaller than the sprites and faster than anything I have ever seen, and they make no sound I could hear over the chord, which is to say they make no sound I could hear at all.

They are food. I know this because sometimes one of them vanishes. There is no flutter of alarm. There is no cry. There is just a lessening — a sprite that was there a moment ago and is not there now, and the mist dimples into the place where it was and then the dimple closes and the air forgets. The vanishing itself is the predator. I could not see anything that took them. I looked. I looked hard. I believe the predator is the *removal* — that what hunts them here is not a creature but an event, a localized erasure, a small absence that travels through the population and gathers what it passes. This is not something I can prove. It is the only explanation that fits what I saw.

## The Glaciers

The heavier things that move through the undergrowth with the slow deliberation of poured honey are the ones I feared most. They are long-bodied. They are low. They are the color of the mist itself, which is to say they are the color of a lavender fog lit from below by a violet pool, and when they stop moving I cannot find them again. I lost one I had been watching for perhaps twenty seconds. It simply was not where I had left it. I looked for a full minute. I found it eventually — by stepping back further and seeing, in the corner of my eye, the shape that my direct gaze had refused to assemble. When the wind tugs at a trunk near one of them it ripples its skin in a slow color-shift from cream to ash-blue and I see, for a moment, the shape of a creature that is not quite a slug and not quite a velvet worm and not quite a thought. It moves as if it has already arrived. There is no urgency in it. There is no hunger in it that I could read. It works its way through the growth with the patience of a glacier and the camouflage of a fallen leaf and I could not tell, after a time, whether I was watching it move or watching it *be where it is*, and I think it may be both.

I do not know what they eat. I do not know if they eat. They may be the slow processors of the bowl — the things that turn the dead growths back into the gas that feeds the living growths — but I cannot prove this. I can only say that they move with the unhurried certainty of something that has been doing this work for a very long time.

## The Hooters

At the rim of vision, where the growth thins and the mist deepens, I could sometimes see larger shapes pass. They were never fully in my sight. They were always there in the moment I was not looking. They are responsible for the undercurrent of low hooting that runs beneath the higher shrieks of the smaller pipes and the glassy whine of the wax leaves. The hooting is felt in the ribcage. It is the deepest note in the chord.

I suspect they are browsing. I suspect they are as tall as I am, and I suspect that their browsing is what keeps the central thicket from choking the bowl entirely — that they are the grazers that the system needs in order not to bury itself in its own music. I never saw one clearly. I never saw one from the front. I saw, once, the suggestion of a long pale limb moving between two chimneys, and then the suggestion withdrew, and the hoot shifted, and I could not be sure I had seen anything at all. They are the size of the larger predators. They are the patience of the glaciers. They are the camouflage of the mist. They belong to the bowl in the way that the wind belongs to the bowl and the chord belongs to the bowl. I do not think they could leave. I do not think they would.

## The Pillars

The built things are not alive in the sense the growths are alive, or the sprites, or the gliders. But they are *inhabited*, and the difference matters.

The low pillars at the grid intersections hold their small crystalline bowls like offerings, and the bowls are full of the violet gas, and the gas in the bowls is luminous in a way the gas outside the bowls is not. It pools there. It stays. The pillars are knee-high and rust-colored and the growths have begun to colonize them — a small cap of bright violet tissue here, a waxing of mineral-flesh there, a sprite running upside down along the underside of the bowl-rim. The pillars are no longer being tended. The grid is no longer being surveyed. Whatever tended it has moved on, up, elsewhere, into adjacent possibilities of physics that this bowl can sample but not contain, and the grid has become what an astronomical clock becomes when its makers leave: still a clock, still turning, still marking hours that no one will read.

The tripod pylons at the intersections are taller — chest-height — and they hold a black ring at their crown, and through the ring, suspended by nothing visible, a small dense knot of grey light turns slowly. The grey light does not illuminate. It absorbs. To look directly at it is to look into a depth that does not belong inside the ring, the way a deep well does not belong in a field. To look beside it is to see the mists dimpling toward it from a meter away, drawn in, fed in, and I think whatever is in those grey knots is still working, after all this time, still doing whatever it was installed to do, and what it is doing is *listening*. I think the grey knots are listening to the bowl. I think the bowl knows. I think the chord the bowl is always composing is, in some sense, the bowl's report back to whatever installed those knots, and the knots are still receiving the report, and the report has been going on for a very long time, and will go on for a very long time after I have left.

The pillars remain. The pillars will remain. In a thousand years they will be mounds, and the mists will have eaten them, and the chord will still be loud, and the sprites will still be working, and the predators will still be removing, and the hooters will still be hooting at the rim, and the grey knots will still be turning, and the bowl will still be speaking itself, and no one will know it is doing any of this for anyone.

## The Place Itself

I do not know what to call this place. *Hollow* is too small a word. *Basin* is too wet a word. *Cathedral* is the wrong faith. But it is the closest I can come, because there is worship here, or the residue of worship, or the practice of something that does not require worshippers. The bowl is composed. The bowl is a sentence in the middle of being spoken, and the sentence is made of wind and growth and gas and small darting things and slow pale things and hooters at the rim and pillars that no one tends, and the chord the bowl is always making is the sentence being said out loud, and the grey knots in the tripod pylons are the sentence being received, and the predators that take the sprites are the punctuation, and the mists that pool where water should be are the silence between clauses, and the whole thing has been going on for a very long time and will go on for a very long time after I have climbed back up to the rim and turned away and carried the hum of it in my inner ear for hours, until the hum fades, until no other place replaces it.

I did not look back. The place does not require looking back. It is always the same place and never the same place and it does not need my witness to keep being what it is.

That is what lives there. That is what I could overhear.
