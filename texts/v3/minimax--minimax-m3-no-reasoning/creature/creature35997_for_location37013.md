# Inhabitants of the Drowned Forge

## The Long Ones

The shadow you saw was a Long One. They are the largest things here, the slowest, the most patient. To call them animals would be to make a claim you cannot defend — they have no skeleton, no organs you could name with confidence, no mouth you could point to. They are pale because pigment is a luxury this stratum cannot afford; without sun, color is merely chemistry without function. Their bodies are the color of skim milk, of steam condensate, of the precipitate that furred the valve handwheels you passed.

Their shape is a single long ribbon, sometimes a hundred meters from end to head, sometimes much shorter; no one has ever measured one fully, because no one has ever fully seen one. They do not swim, exactly. They undulate, but the undulation is more like breathing than locomotion — a slow peristalsis that pushes them through the warm water the way a thought moves through a long corridor of associations. They drift between the columns of machinery as if the columns were trees in a forest, and they have been doing this for so long that they have worn grooves in the soft precipitate on certain housings, the way a river wears grooves in limestone.

They do not eat in any way you would recognize. Their bodies are stippled with thousands of small pores, each one ringed with cilia that draw water in at a rate so slow it is almost geological. They filter the warm water for what the water contains: iron compounds, the sweet metallic tang you tasted, the lubricant that bleeds from joints, the bacterial film that coats every submerged surface. They are the water's kidneys, in a sense; the strata of their tissue concentrate what is dissolved, and what they cannot use they pass back out, slightly altered, slightly less metallic. The Long Ones are the slow circulation of this drowned lung.

They have been here longer than the machines, and that sentence should give you pause. It means that before the pistons began their cadence, before the gears shrieked their first tooth against tooth, before the junction box on the wall was even installed, something soft and pale and very patient was already slipping between the structural members of whatever stood here then. When the city drowned, the Long Ones did not die. They continued. They were, perhaps, the only true inhabitants; the rest of us are guests, or food, or scenery.

## The Filaments

Between the machines, on every surface that does not move, there is a skin. It is not skin; you understand this. It is a living crust, a continuous felt of filaments no thicker than spider silk, that clings to the iron and the steel and the gasket rubber with a tenacity that no adhesive you have used could match. In the light of the dying gas-discharge tubes it has a faint emerald sheen; in the dark, where no light reaches, it does not glow at all, but it is warm, and if you touched it — you do not — it would feel like the felt inside a hat, like the membrane inside a cheek.

These are not plants. They are not fungi. They are something the warm water has invented, a third kingdom, or perhaps a fourth or fifth. They feed on the chemistry of corrosion — the slow marriage of iron and oxygen and salt — and they excrete a thin film of something that protects the metal beneath them from further oxidation. They are, in their fashion, preserving the machinery they grow upon. They are the archivists of this stratum, sealing the surfaces of the world in living parchment.

The filaments pulse. Slowly, in a rhythm that takes about eleven seconds to complete, the entire visible mass of them contracts and relaxes, like the chest of a sleeper. If you stand very still on a grating and let the pistons wash over you, you can feel this rhythm underneath the mechanical one — quieter, deeper, the heartbeat of a stratum rather than a thing. The filaments are not individuals. They are a single organism, or many, or both; the question of where one ends and another begins is a question for which the warm water has not yet provided an answer.

## The Vent-Folk

Around the steam vents, where warm water meets warmer water and the bubble columns rise through the gratings, there are things that move with suddenness. They are small — no bigger than your thumb, mostly — and they are the only things here that seem to have a sense of urgency. You will see them as flickers, sudden darts of dark color against the pale precipitate: a quick shadow passing through a beam of gas-discharge light, then gone.

They have hard parts. This is unusual here. Most of what lives in the Drowned Forge has gone soft, has shed the bones and shells of its ancestors as a luxury it could not afford. But the Vent-Folk have retained something mineral, something calcified, and they use it. They are crustaceans in the loosest sense — segmented, armored, with many small legs and a tail they flick when they need to go from one place to another quickly. They are the color of the rust they were named for, and they have the same mineral taste, which is how the Long Ones find them.

The Vent-Folk eat the filaments. They graze the felt, taking it into small mouths and grinding it with small teeth, and what they cannot digest they excrete as a fine powder that drifts down through the warm water and becomes part of the precipitate. They are the rototillers of this stratum, the only herbivores, and they are many. Without them the felt would grow unchecked, sealing every valve, clogging every conduit, and the machinery would finally, after its long labor, be still.

They move with the steam. When a vent cycles on — the scheduled pulse from the deep cluster of regulators, the burst of warmer water you felt — the Vent-Folk rise with it, hanging in the warmer current like sparrows in an updraft. When the vent cycles off, they sink back down to their grazing, their small legs clicking faintly against the iron they stand upon. Their clicking, if you stand very still, is a kind of music: a thin, percussive, very fast music that weaves between the strokes of the pistons like grass between cobblestones.

## The Iron Grazers

There are things here that eat the precipitate itself. They are flat, almost two-dimensional, and they cling to the surfaces of the pressure housings like the scales of a very strange fish. They are the color of the precipitate they consume, which is also the color of the Long Ones, which is also the color of almost everything here: a pale orange-brown, a chemistry of patience.

The Iron Grazers have no head. They have no eye. Their body is a single sheet of soft tissue edged with a thousand small rasping mouths, each mouth the diameter of a needle, each one wearing away the surface of the metal one mineral grain at a time. They move so slowly that they do not seem to move; over the course of a day, a single Grazer might advance by less than the width of your hand. But they are persistent. A housing left bare to them for a decade will, when you return, be carved with the channels of their advance, a topography of slow appetite that resembles nothing so much as a river basin seen from very high up.

They are, in their own way, dismantling the city. They cannot eat iron — the iron passes through them undigested and falls as a fine black powder to the gratings below — but they consume the chemistry that holds the metal together, the oxides, the carbonates, the precipitated salts. They are the cathedral's slow dissolution. They have been at their work for so long that some of the pressure vessels they cling to are visibly thinner than their neighbors, and a few, the oldest, are pocked through. Light comes through some of those pocks, faint and directionless, in the cold blue where the gas-discharge tubes cannot reach.

## The Deep Ones

Below the grating on which you stand, in the part of the chamber you cannot see, there is something that turns.

You have heard it. The slow tectonic grinding, the shifting of pressure, the bloom of heat from below — these are not the sounds of a machine, or not only the sounds of a machine. They are the sounds of a thing that has incorporated machines into itself, or has been incorporated into machines, or both. The distinction has become academic.

The Deep Ones are old. They are older than the Long Ones, which is to say older than the submersion, which is to say older than the city in its current form. They are what was here before the city, and what remained after the city was submerged, and what the city is now being slowly digested into. They are the strata beneath the strata.

They are not one. There is only ever one visible at a time, but they pass through each other, or merge, or divide. They are very large. A single Deep One, when it surfaces through the gratings and presses against the underside of a catwalk, will dim the gas-discharge lights in a circuit — above, around it — by the sheer mass of its body interposed between them and the machinery they illuminate. It is the color of the bruise-colored water, of the turbid tea-light, of a vein seen through skin. It has no features you can name. It is not looking at you. It does not have, so far as you can tell, anything you would call a face.

It is making something. That is what the grinding is. Down in the dark, the Deep Ones are turning, and what they are turning is being turned into something, and what they are turning it into is not yet finished. You can feel the heat of it through the gratings. You can feel the slow pressure of it against the soles of your feet. The pistons above you are synchronized to it. The valves above you cycle on its breath. The whole drowned district vibrates with the rhythm of its work, and it does not know you are here, and it is not concerned, and it has been doing this work for a very long time, and it will be doing this work for a very long time after you have gone.

## The Integrated

There are things that you might miss if you were not looking, and things that you would not recognize as things if you were. On the junction box you passed — the one with the broken glass and the still-ticking mechanical timer — there is a small growth. It is the color of the box. It has the texture of the box. But if you watch it for a long time you will see it move, very slightly, in the direction of the timer's still-turning hand.

The warm water has been patient. It has had, after all, a great deal of time. There are organisms here that have grown over gauges and used the gauges as their skeletons. There are organisms that have grown into the conduit bundles and now use the bundles as circulatory systems, drawing lubricant from one end and excreting it, slightly altered, from the other. There are organisms that have grown along the catwalks and have become, in some sense, part of the catwalk — load-bearing members, perhaps, or the catwalk's slow nervous system, registering the footsteps of things that pass and storing that information in some chemistry you do not understand.

These integrated things are not metaphors. They are literal. The boundary between the biological and the mechanical here is a working hypothesis at best, a polite fiction at worst. The Long Ones drift between the columns; the Deep Ones build with the columns; the Vent-Folk graze on the columns; the Iron Grazers consume the columns; and the integrated things are, in some sense, the columns themselves, slowly waking up.

---

## What Persists

The Drowned Forge is not abandoned. The Drowned Forge is being slowly inhabited, slowly eaten, slowly inhabited again. The machines continue to run. The warm water continues to circulate. The Deep Ones continue to turn. The Long Ones continue to filter. The Vent-Folk continue to graze. The Iron Grazers continue to consume. The filaments continue to pulse their slow eleven-second heartbeat underneath the faster rhythms of the pistons.

And somewhere in the turbid dark, in the deep indigo where no light has reached since before the submersion, something that is neither machine nor animal but some patient third thing continues to do what it has always done, in the steady and indifferent company of pistons, in the warm metallic water, in the cathedral of pressure vessels and riveted iron that was built for one purpose and is now being used, slowly and without rancor, for another.

You stand on the grating and listen. The sound has become a kind of silence. Your own breath is inaudible. Your own heartbeat is inaudible. There is only the great mechanical respiration, and under it the slower biological one, and under that the deeper one still, the rhythm of the Deep Ones turning in the dark.

You are not part of this economy. You never will be. But you are, for the moment, somewhere inside its breathing.
