# What Lives Here

The shelf is not empty. It is full in the way a sentence is full when every word has weight — populated, but not peopled. The question of who lives here is the question of how a place settles into itself until the settling becomes a kind of inhabiting.

## The Bundles

They are the closest thing to residents.

The bundles are dense. Rope-thick, basalt-thick, the color of wet slate and the temperature of the rock they stand on, which is to say the temperature of deep water and the cold that lives under cold. They hold their shape the way a fist holds its shape — by being clenched, by being the kind of thing that has decided, long ago, what it is, and has not revisited the decision. Their fibers are wet black and run vertically, packed tight as the bristles of a brush left out in rain, and they taper slightly toward the base where the basalt meets them without a seam. You cannot tell where bundle ends and shelf begins. You can only tell where bundle ends and air begins, and that is a line drawn in the same color on both sides.

They stand at the apices of ridges. Always the apices. Never in the troughs, never on the flat, never where the water runs deepest. They stand where the falling water has been most thoroughly asked to leave, where only the mist remains, where the cold has been distilled down to its essential chill. They look, from any distance, like broken pillars or like the chimneys of something that burned a long time ago and was never rebuilt. You can walk past a bundle a hundred yards off and feel nothing — it is furniture, it is geology, it is the kind of thing the eye files and forgets. Then you look back, or you do not look back but the space where it was is wrong, and you understand that something has been watching you decide it was not worth watching.

They move the way continents move. You do not see them move; you infer movement from the before and the after. A bundle that was to your left at dawn is to your right at dusk, or has rotated forty-five degrees, or has simply stepped sideways to the next apex up the ridge as if the ridge were a piano and it were shifting keys. The movement is not local. There is no leg, no wheel, no mechanism. One moment the bundle is here, and the next moment it is somewhere that makes more sense than where it was, and the somewhere has the quality of a correction — a small adjustment in a larger pattern that you are too short-lived to perceive. You feel as you might feel standing in a field of iron filings while someone slowly rotates a magnet somewhere above the ceiling: each filing is still, and yet the field is rearranging itself, and you are standing inside the rearrangement without being moved by it.

When a bundle opens, it opens at the top. The fibers there part, not all at once but in a slow peeling, the way a flower opens but slower, the way a hand opens when it has been clenched in sleep and is being told, by the waking body, that it can let go. What you see inside is not a mouth. It is not a hand. It is not a flower. It is a structure that has the structural honesty of all three: a central depression, pale and wet, lined with the bone-white fans arranged in whorls. The fans are the calcium-carbonate growths you have seen on the ridges, but here they are alive, or alive is the wrong word, but here they are *involved*, participating in whatever the bundle is doing, which is mostly standing and occasionally breathing.

Feeding is not the right word either. The bundles do not feed the way a mouth feeds. They let the water come to them. The iron-water falls through the open fibers and the fibers take what is in the water — iron, calcium, the faint mineral patience of a place that has been leaching itself for ten thousand years — and what is not taken passes through and continues to fall. They are sieves. They are filters. They are the place's way of asking the water what it is carrying and remembering the answer.

Breathing is closer to right. A bundle will hold still for a long time, minutes or hours, and then it will sigh. The sigh is percussive, a soft expelled breath, a sound like a sleeper turning over in a cold room. The sigh is gas — air, or something like air, that the bundle has been quietly accumulating in its fibers and is now releasing. After the sigh, the fibers relax slightly. The fans in the whorl shift, as if rearranged by the passage of the gas. Then everything resumes: the standing, the stillness, the slow patient attending.

There is no competition among them. Two bundles standing twenty yards apart on adjacent ridges will face the same direction for hours, then rotate in sympathy, as if listening to the same distant thing. They do not crowd each other. They do not dispute the apexes. The apexes are not owned; they are simply where the bundles are, today, in the slow reshuffling of a system that does not remember yesterday and is not planning tomorrow.

You do not know how many there are. You can walk for an hour and see none, and then the fog will thin and there will be twenty in a constellation, and then the fog will thicken and there will be none again, and you cannot tell whether the fog hid them or they moved into the fog or the fog moved over them. The shelf might hold a hundred. The shelf might hold a thousand. The shelf might hold exactly the number required to make the place feel inhabited without feeling crowded, which is the number that patience arrives at when patience has been doing arithmetic for a very long time.

## The Crusts

They are not residents. They are fixtures.

The black crusts on the ridges are the color of the basalt and almost the texture of the basalt, and if you did not know to look for them you would not see them at all. They are lichen as lichen would be if lichen had been briefed by a colder client: flatter, thinner, more resigned. They do not lift from the rock. They are the rock, with opinions. Under a hand they are gritty and faintly warm, or faintly electric, the way a tongue goes electric against foil, and the taste they leave is mineral and not unpleasant — iron, calcium, the faintest ghost of salt. They are not alive in the way that insists on being noticed. They are alive in the way that a coat of paint on a very old door is alive: still doing its job, still adhering, no longer interested in being anything else.

They grow only on the ridges. They are the ridges' way of being marked by their own patience. Where the water runs deepest, in the troughs, there are no crusts; the water has scoured them away or never let them settle. On the apexes, where the bundles stand, the crusts are thickest, as if the bundles have been standing on them long enough to grow into them, or as if the crusts have been growing up around the bundles' feet for the same long time. A bundle that has moved has left a pale footprint where it was, a circle of cleaner basalt where the crust was shaded, and this footprint is one of the ways you mark your way back, when you mark your way back at all.

## The Fans

They are the place's handwriting.

The pale fans are calcium carbonate, almost. The *almost* matters. They unfurl from cracks in the ridges — never from the troughs, never from the flat — and they layer in tiers down each ridge like the pages of a book left open in a damp room. They are bone-white in the half-light. They are not alive in any way the eye can confirm, but they are not dead in any way the eye can confirm either; they are simply there, in the way that the pages of a book are there when no one is reading them, holding the shape of having been read.

Up close, under a bundle, the fans are different. They move. Very slightly. In a way that might be the water passing through and might be something else, something the body recognizes as breathing without the mind agreeing. The whorls of fans inside an open bundle are the place's lungs, if the place has lungs. They are also the place's flowers, if the place has flowers. They are the place's mouth, if the place has a mouth. They are the structural honesty of a system that has not bothered to distinguish between these things.

If you touch a fan on a ridge, it is cool and slightly gritty and faintly electric, and the taste on your fingertip afterward is not unpleasant. If you touch a fan inside an open bundle, you do not. The bundle does not stop you. The bundle does not startle. But something has happened, some small negotiation has occurred, and afterward the bundle sighs a little sooner than it would have, and the sigh has a slightly different timbre, as if the conversation has been briefly adjusted to include you and you have not understood what you said.

## The Clicking

It is not an animal. It is not a thing. It is the place talking to itself.

The clicking comes up through the basalt. It is irregular. It is scattered. It is the sound of a fingernail tapped against a glass at the far end of a long corridor, except the corridor is the rock and the glass is the underside of your foot and the fingernail is something you cannot locate. The clicks come in threes, mostly. Sometimes in fives. Sometimes in a long single click that is almost a knock, almost a summons, almost an invitation to come down into the rock and find out what is making the sound.

There are answers. They come from farther along the shelf, from behind a wall of fog or across a trough you cannot see the bottom of. The answers come in different numbers. Three answered by five. Five answered by three. Once, twice, a long silence answered by a single click from very far away, as if the shelf has just remembered something it had forgotten to say.

The clicking is not the bundles. The bundles sigh; they do not click. The clicking is the rock itself, or something in the rock, or something the rock is doing because of something beneath the rock — the same something, perhaps, that moves the bundles when the bundles move, the slow tide in the basalt, the magnet whose source is never seen. The clicking is the place's respiration at a deeper register. It is what the shelf sounds like when it is not listening to the bundles and the bundles are not listening to it, when the whole system has dropped into a quieter mode and is just, very quietly, keeping time.

## The Fog

The fog is not alive. The fog is the place's weather and the place's memory and the place's way of not being entirely visible to itself. The fog migrates. The fog consolidates into walls. The walls move. The walls dissolve. A landmark you passed an hour ago is now behind a wall you cannot see through, and the wall will not be there in another hour, and the landmark will be gone, or rotated, or replaced by a trough that has overflowed in a new direction.

The fog is the most turbulent thing on the shelf. The bundles are glacial. The rock is patient. The water is constant. Only the fog is restless, only the fog is quick, only the fog rearranges the visible world without warning. You learn to distrust the fog. You learn to trust the ridges and the troughs, which are slow enough to be reliable. You learn to walk by the feel of the rock underfoot and the direction of the runoff, not by the landmarks, because the landmarks are the fog's to give and to take away.

The fog is also the breathing. The shelf is the long slow exhale of a system that has been exhaling since before there was anyone to hear it, and the fog is the breath, and the basalt is the chest. You walk inside the breath. You walk inside the out-breath of something so large that you are not a part of its conversation, only a thing that has briefly entered the air it has exhaled and will, in its own time, be exhaled again.

## The Cold

The cold is not a resident either. The cold is the temperature of a body that has stopped trying to warm itself. The cold is what is left when the chill of the water has been distilled down by ten thousand years of falling into the stone. The cold gets into the joints slowly. The cold stays. Your breath does not plume because the air is already saturated, already full, already holding as much water as it can hold without becoming water itself. If you speak, the words come out flat and are absorbed within a few feet. After a while you stop speaking. The clicking is enough. The sighs are enough. The hiss that has learned to imitate silence is enough.

The cold is the place's way of being itself without apology.

## The Shelf Itself

And the shelf, of course, lives here. The shelf is the oldest resident. The shelf is the resident that has been here so long that it has forgotten it is a resident and has settled into being the place itself. The basalt was polished by the water long before anything with the nerve to notice was watching. The ridges were not made; they were what was left when everything softer had been asked to leave. The troughs were not cut; they were where the water, given permission to fall, decided to fall. The shelf is the negative space of a much larger erasure, and the erasure happened so long ago that only the negative remains, and the negative has been the place ever since.

The shelf is not waiting for you. The shelf is not waiting for anything. The shelf has been itself for so long that it has forgotten that other configurations are possible. You are very small and very recent and very damp, and the shelf accepts this the way it accepts the water — without comment, without adjustment, by letting you pass through it on your way to wherever it is that small recent damp things go.

If you stand very still on a ridge, the bundles will not come to you. They will simply reorient, slowly, like compass needles finding a new north, and the opening at the top will face you, and the pale whorls inside will be moving in a way you cannot tell is breathing or is the water passing through, and after a moment the bundle will sigh, and then it will resume not attending to you, and you will resume not being attended to, and the shelf will continue to be the shelf, and the conversation will continue without you, the way it has continued for a very long time, in a place that has made itself out of patience and iron water and a kind of mineral patience that is older than language and will outlast it.

This is what lives here. This is what has always lived here. This is what will live here after you have gone back to wherever it is you came from, damp and recent and a little colder than you were.
