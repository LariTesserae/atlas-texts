# What Lives in the Throat

What lives here does not have a name you would recognize. Or rather — it has many names, and none of them is quite right, and the not-quite-rightness is the point. The slot is a place where the old categories have come loose, and what dwells here has been quick to notice.

Let me start with the felt, because the felt is what you would see first, and because the felt is, in its patient way, the closest thing the slot has to a citizen.

The felt is not one thing. The felt is a *negotiation*, as the noticing eye observed, and the negotiation has been going on for some length of time that I will not insult by giving a number. The partners at the table are these: a fungus, or rather a confederation of fungi, whose filaments extend into the granite in hair-thin exploratory threads and return, having dissolved something they encountered, with mineral in their stomachs. A cyanobacterium, or rather a great unquiet crowd of them, who arrived early and have stayed, and who do the work of catching light in the brief noon hour when the blade of sun reaches the floor, and who turn that light, with the industriousness of the very small, into the sugar that the fungi cannot make for themselves. And a green alga, or several, who keep to the wetter terraces and who are, in their way, the landlords — they were here before the others and they will be here after, because what they are doing is so simple and so old that no negotiation is required. They simply sit in the light, when there is light, and divide.

None of these three alone could do what the felt does. The fungus alone is a digest. The cyanobacterium alone is a hunger. The alga alone is a stain. Together they are a *we*, and the *we* has properties none of the partners possess: the ability to hold water in a felted architecture that the rock alone cannot hold; the ability to weather fresh stone faster than the stone expects to be weathered; the ability to climb.

The climbing is what I want you to notice. The felt begins at the riverline and does not stop. It climbs three hundred meters of vertical granite. It climbs through the splash zone, where the river throws itself against the inner curve and wets the rock with a fine perpetual mist. It climbs above the splash zone, into a place where the only water is what seeps through the rock from the slopes above. It climbs through this, too. It climbs into the brief noon band of light, and there, where the sun touches it, it thickens, darkens, becomes something you could almost mistake for a moss if you did not look closely. It climbs above the noon band, into permanent shadow, and it does not stop, and there, where the light fails entirely, it changes — it becomes thinner, paler, more cautious, more fungal, more dependent on the chemical patience of its underground partners. But it climbs.

What lives in the felt, in the felt's *body*, is a thing you would not call an animal and I would not call a plant. It is a tardigrade, perhaps, or one of its cousins. It is small. It is the size of a grain of sand, or a little larger. It has eight legs, or some number near eight, and a mouth that does not close, and it eats, when it eats, the bodies of the cyanobacteria, or the spores of the fungi, or other small things that live in the felt's wet interior. It does not see. It does not hear. It lives in the felt as a fish lives in water, which is to say it does not know there is anything else.

There are many of them. There are uncountable many of them. They have been here since the slot was a crack. They will be here when the slot is a valley. They are the slot's oldest continuous inhabitants, older than the felt by a margin that embarrasses the felt.

There are other small things. There are nematodes, worm-shaped, microscopic, who live in the water that runs along the underside of the felt in its glistening channels. There are rotifers, who wheel in the standing water of the small terraces a hundred meters up on the left wall, where the sun has never reached, and who eat, when they eat, the bacterial film on the terrace rims. There are springtails, who are not quite insects and not quite crustaceans and who can, when disturbed, kick themselves a distance that is, relative to their body length, the equivalent of a man jumping over a house. They live in the felt and they move through it the way a person moves through a forest, by which I mean they never come out.

What I am describing, you understand, is a *soil*. Not a soil in the usual sense — not the brown crumbly thing underfoot in a meadow. But a soil in the functional sense: a place where mineral is being digested into nutrient, where nutrient is being assembled into bodies, where bodies are being eaten by other bodies, where the whole is engaged, slowly and without opinion, in the project of turning rock into life. The slot, at the level of the felt, is the first millimeter of a future forest. The felt is the first millimeter of topsoil. The slot is a place that is, in real time, becoming a place that can support a forest.

This is what the felt is for.

Now. The river.

The river is not a citizen of the slot. The river is a *transit*. The river comes from a peak that does not concern us, as was noted, and it passes through the slot on its way to somewhere else, and it does not care, in any way that a river can be said to care, about the felt or the tardigrades or the small terraces on the left wall. The river's project is downhill. The river has been downhill for a length of time equal to the length of time it has been water, which is a length of time that exceeds, by many orders of magnitude, the length of time the slot has existed.

What lives *in* the river, then, is what lives in transit: the diatoms, who build their small glass houses from the dissolved silica in the rock-flour, and who tumble in the current with their houses around them like divers with their helmets on; the mayfly nymphs, who cling to the underside of stones with the devoted grip of things that expect, someday, to become something with wings; the caddisfly larvae, who build themselves cases from the small stones they find on the bottom, gluing them together with a silk they produce from their own mouths, and who walk the bottom of the river in their small stone coats, looking like something a child would invent. There are algae here, of course — diatoms again, and green filaments, and the occasional blue-green smear — but they are passengers, not inhabitants. They are riding through. The river does not permit settlement. The river permits clinging.

The river is loud, and the river is cold, and the river is full of stone-flour, which is to say the river is full of the felt's *output*, the digested mineral that the fungi have excreted and that the water has carried down, and the mayfly nymphs eat this, in their way, and the caddisfly larvae cement their cases with it, in their way, and the whole river is, in its way, the slot's digestive tract extended.

Now. The wind.

The wind is not a citizen either. The wind is a *visitor*, in the harshest sense — it arrives, it does damage, it leaves. But what the wind brings, what the wind *carries*, is what interests me.

The wind brings spores. The wind brings spores from the slopes above, from the alpine meadows that skirt the slot's upper rim, from the forests that begin, somewhere, a long way down. The wind brings pine pollen, in spring, in such quantity that it settles on the wet felt in a thin yellow dust that the next splash incorporates into the felt's body. The wind brings, occasionally, a seed. A seed arrives in the slot the way a person arrives in a place they did not mean to come to — by accident, by miscalculation, by a gust that exceeded expectation. The seed falls. The seed lands, if it is lucky, on a ledge. The seed germinates, if it is luckier, in the felt. A small green thing pushes up through the dark felt into the brief noon light.

This is the slot's most improbable resident.

It does not last. The light is too brief. The felt is too wet. The wind, returning, breaks it. But sometimes — and this is the thing to hold onto — sometimes a seed lands in a crack where the felt has not yet reached, where the granite is still bare, and the seed sends a root down into the crack, and the root follows the water, and the root anchors, and a small twisted thing begins to grow in the slot that is not yet a tree and may never be a tree but is, in its twisted and improbable way, *a tree trying*.

There is, last I heard — and this is rumor, because nobody comes here, and the report travels by way of a man who climbed down once and would not climb down again — there is, on the left wall, about two hundred meters up, in a place where the slot flares slightly and a little extra light reaches the floor, a *spruce*. It is small. It is the height of a man. It is bent sideways by the wind. It is alive. It has been alive, by the report, for at least sixty years, which is to say it has outlived every expectation the slot ought to have had of it.

I do not vouch for the spruce. I report it.

Now. The tone.

I have reached the tone, and I have, as promised, nothing to say about it.

I will say what I notice, and then I will stop. I notice that the tone arrives through the rock more strongly than through the air. I notice that it has a quality of tuning. I notice that it occurs at intervals. I notice that it is not the wind and not the river and not the mechanical events of a steep place. I notice that when I stood with my hand against the felt, the felt was cold, and the rock behind the felt was colder, and the tone arrived through the cold stone as if the stone were a wire.

I notice, and this is the last thing I will say, that the felt on the walls pulses, very gently, very slowly, in a rhythm that is not breath, and that the pulse and the tone are not in sync, and that they are not *out* of sync, and that the relationship between them is something I have not been able to determine.

I notice that I do not wish to determine it. I notice that the slot is a place where the determination of things is in progress, and that to determine a thing in such a place is to do violence to it, and that the slot has had enough violence.

So. What lives in the Throat.

A felt, which is a soil in the first millimeter of its becoming. A tardigrade, which has been here since the slot was a crack. A river, which is a transit, full of diatoms and mayfly nymphs and caddisfly larvae in their stone coats. A wind, which is a visitor, which carries spores and, rarely, a seed. A spruce, possibly, which is an accident. And a tone, which is not an animal and not a plant and not a fungus and not a negotiation among the three, and which may be the slot's own slow thought, if a slot can be said to think, which I am not prepared to say.

What lives in the Throat is what lives in any place that is being assembled in real time: the very small, the very old, the very patient, and the occasional, improbable, accidental green thing that did not mean to be here and is here anyway.
