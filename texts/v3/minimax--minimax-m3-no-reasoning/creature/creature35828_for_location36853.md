Who Lives on the Tabletop

The slow people live there. That is what the traders used to call them, long ago, when there were still traders who came up the saddle-path and went back down again with something to show for the climb. The slow people. They did not mind the name. They had other names for themselves, but those names were not for outsiders, and the outsiders were not many, and are not any now.

They are not slow in the way the word suggests. They move when they need to move. A woman can cross the top of the Tabletop before the midday meal if she has somewhere to be. A man can take a goat by the horns and drag it down a lane when the goat has decided to die in the wrong place, and he can do this without particular hurry and still arrive in time. They are quick enough. They are slow in the other sense — the sense that a thing has when it has been going on for a long time and has worn itself into the shape of what it is doing. A river is slow. A lichen is slow. The slow people are like that.

There are perhaps three hundred of them now. There have been perhaps three hundred of them for as long as the oldest grandmother can remember, and the oldest grandmother is the oldest grandmother, and her mother before her was the oldest grandmother, and so on back into the fog. They do not grow in number. They do not shrink. A child is born in the lower lanes and a child is carried out across the saddle-path and the number holds. Three hundred is the number the place will hold, the way a bowl holds only the water that fits in it. They know this without being told. They do not speak of it. When a child is born they are glad. When someone dies they grieve, the proper grief, the long grief, the kind that goes on for a season. But somewhere underneath there is the bowl, and the bowl is the same size, and they do not pour harder than the bowl can take.

Their skin is the color of the stone they live on — a pale gray-brown, darker in the low sun and lighter in the fog, with the kind of undertone that comes from generations of a particular diet and a particular sky. Their hair is black and is worn long by everyone and is dressed with the fat of the small mountain bees, which makes it shine in the lamplight and lie flat in the rain. Their eyes are large. Everyone remarks on this, or would, if there were anyone to remark. The eyes are large and the color of old amber, and they move less than the eyes of lowland people. They have learned not to move their eyes much. There is nothing on the Tabletop that moves fast, and the eye that flicks after every mote in the air is an eye that tires. They look. They look for a long time at one thing. They see what is there.

The children are the loudest of them and the quickest. They run in the lanes the way water runs in the lanes — finding the path of least resistance, which is the only kind of path there is. They are small and dark and barefoot, and their feet are wide and tough, and they know every raised stone in every lane by the feel of it. They climb the walls. They climb the trees. They sit on the eaves and watch the hawks, and they throw things at the hawks, and the hawks ignore them, and the children take this as a kind of equality. They have games, the children. They have a game with a stone and a mark on a wall that is older than the wall. They have a game with a length of knotted string that no one has explained to them and no one can explain to them, because no one remembers what the knots meant. They play it anyway. They make up the rules as they go. The rules accumulate. The game is older than any of them and will be older than any of them.

The women are the center of the place, the way the hearth is the center of a house, though there are many hearths and no one is the center in the way the lowlanders mean. They keep the lanes. This is not a metaphor. They sweep the lanes, in the mornings, with brooms made of the twisted branches of the small waxy trees, and the sweeping is not to clean the lanes but to maintain them, the way one maintains a path through a garden by bending back the plants that would otherwise take it over. They tend the moss-beds, which are the gardens. They milk the shaggy ruminants, which stand in the courtyards and on the rooftops and are willing to be milked at any hour and are milked twice. They work the bees. The bees are theirs in a way the goats are not the goats of anyone. The women go out in the fog-mornings with a small clay pipe and a hide bucket and they take the honey, and the bees permit this, and the honey is thick and almost black and tastes of the lichen and the fog and the mineral dust. They put it up in jars of fired clay, in a glaze that is more accident than design, and the jars sit on shelves in the cool rooms and are used a little at a time. They make the bread. The bread is flat and is baked on a stone that has been heated by a lamp, because there is no oven. There has never been an oven. The bread is good. The bread is what the children eat first when they are weaned, and what the old are given when their teeth are gone, and what is set out on the small stone ledges for the hawks when a hawk has died, which is not often.

The men do the heavy things. They haul the water. The water is the work of the place, more than any other task, because there is no standing water and there has never been standing water, and what there is must be gathered, stone by stone, surface by surface, in the mornings and the evenings when the fog condenses. They go out with hide buckets and shallow stone bowls and they move along the edges of the Tabletop where the wet is thickest, and they gather, and they carry it back. The buckets are heavy. The men are broad in the shoulders and narrow in the hips, the build of a person who has been carrying things up and down narrow stairs for his whole life. They also do the stone-work. The stone is split with wedges of a harder stone and with patient blows from a hammer that is a rounded river-rock lashed to a handle with strips of hide. The blows are rhythmic. The blows have a particular rhythm that is not the rhythm of any lowland work, a kind of two-three, two-three, with a long breath between, and from somewhere on the Tabletop, at any time of day, you can hear this rhythm going on, like the heartbeat of the place. They do the carpentry. There is a timber, a slow-grown timber, that comes up the saddle-path on the backs of the shaggy ruminants, one log at a time, and the men work it with bone tools and stone axes, and the beams are spliced and patched and spliced again, and a beam in a house on the Tabletop may have been set by a man whose grandfather set its grandfather. The men are quiet, more quiet than the women. They are the ones who go out on the edges of the cliff, on the ledges where the hawks nest, to take the young hawks for the pigeon-roosts, and this work is done without speech. They climb. They do not look down.

The old are the most particular of them. There are not many. A person who has lived sixty years on the Tabletop has done something that the place itself respects, and the respect is shown in small ways — a better place by the fire, a share of the honey, a word asked of them before a word is asked of anyone else. Their faces are deeply lined, not in the way of lowland faces where the lines come from grief or weather, but in the way of a leaf that has been folded and unfolded many times. The lines on a grandmother's face on the Tabletop are the lines of the lanes she has walked, the courtyards she has swept, the stairs she has climbed. They are a map. Their hands are large and still. They sit in the doorways in the afternoons, when the light is at its dimmest and the fog is at its thickest, and they do small work — they split a wisp of moss for a wick, they turn a stone in a mortar, they card a little of the wool from the morning's milking. The young come to them. The young always come to them, not for instruction but for the thing that is not instruction — the sense of a person who has been in the lanes so long that they are part of the lanes. A grandmother on the Tabletop is the lanes. She knows which stone is loose before she steps on it. She knows which roof will leak in the next rain. She knows whose child is ready to be trusted with the bees and whose child is not, and she says this not as advice but as observation, the way she would say it is going to rain.

The dogs are theirs and not theirs. The dogs came up the saddle-path long ago, or were always here, no one knows which. They are the color of the stone. They are the size of the lanes. They sleep in the passageways and come out when there is food and go back when there is not, and they are barked at by no one and are fed by everyone, and they are the same dogs that have always been here. The cats are theirs in a different way. The cats are smaller, finer, the color of the lichen, and they hunt the rock-rats in the granaries and are valued for this and are otherwise left alone. The cats sit on the window-ledges and look down the long fall to the haze of the lower mountains, and they have the look of something that is considering leaving and has decided, for the moment, to stay.

The pigeons are theirs. The pigeons are not wild and are not tame. They live in the rooftop coops and they fly out in the mornings, in a wheel of dark wings that goes up over the edge of the cliff and comes back again, and the hawks take one now and then, and this is accepted, and the hawks are not hated for it. The pigeons are eaten at the festivals. The festivals are not many. There is a festival when the honey is taken in the late summer, and a festival when the first kid of the year is born, and a festival for the dead, which is in the fog-season and is the longest. At the festivals there is singing, the measured exhalation, the long phrase with the small ornament at the end, and there is dancing, which is not dancing in the lowland sense but a kind of walking in a circle, very slowly, with the arms held out and the eyes closed. The singing goes on for a long time. The singing goes on until it becomes part of the weather, and then it stops, and the silence afterward is the same silence that was there before, and is also different.

The bees are theirs. The bees are small and dark and they work the fog-flowers, the small white ones that open only in the fog, and they make a thick almost-black honey that is the only sweetener anyone knows. The honey is kept in the cool rooms. The honey is used for the bread and for the small cakes that are made on the rare occasions when there is flour to spare, and for the tea, which is not tea but an infusion of a lichen that grows on the north faces of the buildings, and which is drunk hot in the mornings and cold in the evenings. The bees sting. The stings are accepted. A woman whose hands are swollen from the bees is a woman whose work is going well.

The shaggy ruminants are theirs. They are kept for milk and wool and for hauling, the one log at a time, up the saddle-path. They are larger than goats and smaller than the lowland cattle, and they have a look of patient long-suffering that is not unkind. They stand in the courtyards. They stand on the rooftops, which are flat and can bear them, and which they warm with their bodies in the cool nights. They are milked twice. Their wool is combed in the spring, with bone combs, and it is spun on a spindle that is weighted with a small flat stone, and it is woven on a frame of the slow-grown timber, and the cloth is rough and warm and is the cloth of every garment on the Tabletop except the beeswax in the hair.

The hawks are not theirs. The hawks are their own. The hawks nest on the ledges of the cliff and they feed on the pigeons and on the small foxes and on anything that comes near the edge, and they are admired for this, the way the wind is admired, and the way the fog is admired. A hawk on the Tabletop is a thing that is doing what it was made to do, and this is respected. The hawks are large and dark and they fly in the long pale sky with a kind of ease that the people, who have no sky except this one, find enviable. The hawks do not fear the people. The people do not fear the hawks. There is an understanding. A hawk will take a pigeon. A man will take a hawk's egg, once in a generation, for a purpose that is not explained. The understanding holds.

The crows are not theirs. The crows nest in the rooftop trees and they are smaller than the lowland crows and they are bolder, and they feed on everything, and they are disliked, mildly, the way a relative is disliked. The children throw things at the crows. The crows take the things. The children and the crows have an understanding of their own.

The lichens are theirs. The lichens are the oldest living thing on the Tabletop, older than the people, older than the settlement, and they are tended in a way that is not tending. They are not cut. They are not pulled. They are eaten back, a little, by the shaggy ruminants when they are let out onto the stone in the fog-mornings, and they grow back, and this is the right amount. The particular green that is not found anywhere else is the color of the place, and the people have been looking at it for so long that it has become part of how they see. A child who is taken down the saddle-path, into the lowlands, will remark, on seeing the grasses, that they are the wrong color.

The fog is theirs. The fog comes up from the lowlands in the evenings and pours itself over the edges of the Tabletop like milk poured off a table, and it fills the lanes and the courtyards and it condenses on every cool surface, and in the morning it lies in the stone basins that the men have set out for it, and it is gathered and used. The fog is not weather. The fog is the place. A day without fog on the Tabletop is a day without breathing, and this happens rarely, and when it does the people wait, and the hawks wait, and the dogs lie closer to the people than usual, and the fog comes back, and the breathing resumes.

What they believe is hard to say, because what they believe is not separate from what they do. They believe the stone is alive. They believe this in the way one believes a thing that one is standing on. They believe the lichen is older than the people and will be older after. They believe the fog is the breath of the place and they are careful with it. They believe the dead go down the slope and into the lower air and are not lost. They believe the hawks are watching. They believe the singing keeps the weather the way it is. They do not speak of these beliefs at length. They live them. A woman who sweeps a lane in the morning is living her belief in the stone. A man who splits a stone with patient blows is living his belief in the stone. A grandmother who sets out a little bread on the ledge for a dead hawk is living her belief in the hawks. The beliefs are not held. They are worn, the way the lanes are worn, into the shape of the place.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are suited. The word is not quite right. There is a word in their speech, a word that is not translated, that means the fit between a person and the place the person is in, and it is a word that is used of the lichen on the stone and of the stone on the mountain and of the mountain under the sky. They use it of themselves. They are suited. The groove they have worn into the Tabletop is the right groove. The wind will wear the mountain down, eventually, but not yet, not for a long time, and when it does, the people will go with it, down the slope, into the lower air, into a place that is not theirs, and they will not fit there, and they will not be suited there, and what will become of them is not spoken of, because it is not yet.

For now, they are there. They are on the top of the last high thing. They are sweeping the lanes and splitting the stones and milking the ruminants and taking the honey in the fog-mornings, and the children are throwing things at the crows, and the dogs are sleeping in the passageways, and the hawks are circling, and the fog is coming up, and the singing is going on somewhere, the measured exhalation, the long phrase with the small ornament at the end. They are the slow people. They are finished. They have been finished for a long time. They are not waiting for anything. They are not waiting for anyone. They are the bowl and the water in the bowl and the fit between them, and this is enough.
