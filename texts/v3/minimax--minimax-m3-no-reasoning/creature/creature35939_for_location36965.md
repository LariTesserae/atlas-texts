The herders of the eastern flats are the freest of the Hevelkari, and the loneliest, and the only ones among them whose lives the city does not much govern. A man who has taken the goats out for the season will not see a paved street, or a baker's smoke, or a woman other than his own, until the first snow drives him home. He is out before the sun, and the goats are out before him, and the small bells they wear make a sound like a far-off chapel in the thin air. He wears a coat of goat leather that has been re-sewn so many times it is more thread than hide. He carries a long staff, a short knife, and two water-skins, one of them full and one of them not, and the second one is the question of his life.

He knows the flats the way a man knows his own body. He can feel, under the stone, where the water is. He has stood on a piece of ground that looks like every other piece and known — by a faint darkening of the lichen, by a smell of iron in the air, by nothing he could explain to you — that if he dug here until sunset he would have two skins of water and a story for the men at the well. He has dug there, and he has had the water. He has dug elsewhere and got nothing but a sore shoulder and a chip in his blade. He does not explain how he knows. He does not say. The wet places are sacred in a religion he does not much think about, and they are also the thing that keeps him alive, and a man does not usually speak about the thing that keeps him alive.

He knows his goats by their faces. He has named perhaps a dozen of them, the ones with character — the one that leads, the one that is mean, the one whose kid died and who stood over the body for two days and would not be moved. He has not named the others because there are too many. He milks them in the cool of the morning, when the milk is thick, and he drinks it thin or thick depending on what kind of day he is having. He eats the dried apples he brought with him, and the hard bread, and the meat of a goat that has stopped being useful. He is not unhappy. He is the kind of person Hevelkar makes when it is allowed to take its time.

In the city, the masons work the white stone. They are the other kind of person Hevelkar makes — the kind that stays put, that lives in the noise of the streets, that has neighbors above and below and a ladder that is not his own. A mason is recognisable by his hands. The calcite gets into the skin and dries there, and a mason's hands at forty are pale and cracked and slightly luminous, as if he has been handling chalk or bone. He follows the bone-vein — the pale thread of calcite that runs through the basalt — because the bone-vein tells him where the stone will split clean and where it will take a carved edge. He is not an artist. The wide-eyed figures he turns out, which the pilgrims buy and carry home wrapped in linen, are crude by any city with a hundred years of artistic tradition behind it. The eyes are too large. The limbs are too short. The figure that is supposed to be the sleeping god looks, if you have not been told, like a man with a headache. But the stone is the thing the pilgrims have come for, and the stone is holy — or may be — and the masons are the men who shape it, and they have been shaping it for four generations now, and the wide-eyed figures are what they have agreed upon.

The quarrymen are the masons' cousins and their opposites. A quarryman goes deaf young, because the basalt rings when you strike it, and a quarryman is striking it all day, and the ringing does not stop when the striking stops. By fifty he is reading lips at the well. By sixty he is leaning into the wind on the parapet because the wind is a sound he can feel and does not need to hear. He does not carve. He splits. He knows where to set the wedge and where to leave the stone alone. He is paid by the cartload. He is a substantial man in his own way — not on the Council, but the kind of man the Council buys drinks for, because without the quarrymen the white stone does not leave the plateau.

The women of Hevelkar are the keepers of water. This is not a figure of speech. In a country where the cisterns brim in a good year and breathe in a bad one, a woman who tends a cistern well is a woman whose family survives the dust. She knows how much was used yesterday. She knows how much is left. She knows which of the children dipped the cup without asking, and which of them has been sick and needs more, and which of the lodgers sleeps with his mouth open and wastes what he should not waste. She has a religion of her own, which is not the priests' religion, and which is older in some ways, and her saints are the women who came before her and who taught her how to read the level of the water by the dark stain on the inside of the stone.

The water-sellers are loud. They have to be — the city is dense and the wells are six and a man who has climbed down to draw may not come back up for an hour. The water-seller calls in the morning, when the air is thin and the city is waking, and his voice is the first loud thing a Hevelkari hears each day. He knows the wells by their mood. In a wet year they brim and he has to fight for his trade. In a dry year they have dropped a man's height, and his trade is good, and the children are sent down to clean them, and the city smells of wet limestone for a week — which is the smell Hevelkari loves best, and which, for that week, makes the place feel almost like a place that has been loved.

The Council meets in a low room at the back of the market. It is not a grand room. The chairs are stone and they are uncomfortable and they have been uncomfortable since the founders sat in them, and no one has ever suggested cushions, because cushions are timber and timber is imported and the Council will not import anything for itself. A man who owns four hundred goats sits on the Council. A man who owns the largest of the quarries sits on it. A man who is the senior priest sits on it. The rest of the city is not on it, and this is not resented, because Hevelkar is a place where the visible weight of a man is his weight, and a man with four hundred goats is a heavy man.

The Council speaks slowly. It speaks in the same register as the place — patient, almost geological. It argues about the price of white stone for three days and then agrees to what it was always going to agree to. It argues about which of the wells to deepen, and in what year, and which of the walls needs the leaning patched. It does not argue about the wet places, because the wet places are sacred and the herders do not speak of them anyway and the Council has never asked. It does not argue about the eastern wastes, because no one on the Council has been to the eastern wastes and no one on the Council intends to go.

The senior priest is a quiet man. He has been quiet for so long that people have stopped expecting him to be anything else. He knows the wet places better than the herders do — he has walked to some of them that the herders have never been to, the ones in the deeper folds of the plateau, where the moss is almost black and the seep fills a skin in an afternoon. He has blessed them. He has not dug at them. He has stood over them in the thin air and felt, he says, the heat of the god's dreaming. Whether he does or not is not a question the city asks, because he is a careful man and his care is the city's care, and the water is the city's water, and the god is asleep, and no one wakes him.

The priests under him are younger and less quiet. They tend the small chapels cut into the basalt at the city's edge, where the calcite veins come close to the surface and the bone-white stone is visible. They sell the carved figures to the pilgrims and take a cut. They bless the caravans when they leave. They marry the children, and they bury the old ones in the stone floor of the family house, because the stone is holy and the god will know his own.

A family in Hevelkar occupies what it can hold. A man and a woman marry and take the ground floor of a cousin's house, or build out a room onto a relative's roof, and the new family is added to the city the way a new stone is added to a wall — fitted in where there is space, mortared with what can be found. Twenty years later the son of that family marries, and he builds a floor onto his parents' roof, and the only way up is a ladder that lives in his parents' house and is brought out for him and his wife when they come down in the morning and put away when they go up at night. Privacy in such a house is a small terrace where the laundry dries in the moments when the sun finds the city's angles. The cooking is done in the lower kitchen, where the heat goes up through the floor and warms the upper room in winter, which is when warmth matters.

A Hevelkari family does not have much. They have a bowl each, and a knife, and a sleeping mat, and the one cup that has traveled far and is shown on feast days. They have a tallow dip for light. They have a store of dried apples in a basket that hangs from the ceiling, where the mice cannot easily reach. They have, if they are lucky, a small carved figure of the sleeping god on a shelf, and a piece of bone-vein calcite that is supposed to bring water. They have the stew of goat and root and dried apple, eaten late, eaten by all, eaten in the company of family and lodger and whoever has climbed up the ladder tonight. The clatter of bowls is the evening sound of Hevelkar, and the smell of the stew is the smell of home, and a Hevelkari who has been away for long will smell that smell in his sleep and wake with it in his throat.

The children of Hevelkar are born on stone floors and grow up on stone stairs and learn to walk on the basalt flats where the footing is treacherous and a fall means a long slide. They learn to read by tallow dip, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the book on a flat stone, and the letters are pale against the dark, and they will not forget them. They learn to count by the goats their family owns, and to multiply by the loads of white stone a cart can carry, and to argue by the time it takes to walk from the market to the well and back. They cough in winter, the way all Hevelkari cough in winter, but they do not die of it the way their grandparents did, because their mothers have taught them to wrap the cloth and not to breathe deep when the dust is up. They play in the one square that catches the sun, and their laughter is the only loud and unplanned sound in the city, and the old folk stop to listen to it. The boys go out onto the flats with the goats when they are old enough, and the girls stay in the city and learn the cisterns. Both are real work. Both are honored.

The old of Hevelkar have seen a great deal of dust. They have seen the winters when the dust did not stop and the old died in their beds and the bowls were not cleared for a week. They have seen the winters when the snow came down clean and the children made figures of the white hawks and the city was beautiful for a month. They have seen the wells drop a man's height, and rise again, and drop again. They have seen the caravans come and the caravans go. They have the patience of the place, because patience is what the place teaches, and they have the cough, because the place teaches that too.

A Hevelkari who reaches seventy is considered to have been favored by the god. He is given a seat by the well in summer, in the sun, and the children bring him water in the cup that he keeps for himself. He tells the children about the winters, and the children listen, and they do not believe him entirely, because they have not seen what he has seen, but they will. The god is asleep. The wells breathe. The wind blows. The dust comes and goes. The old man sits and waits for the next winter, and the next, and he is not afraid.

The pilgrims come up from the lowlands with their wallets and their reverence and their eyes already wide from the altitude. They have heard that the stone of the plateau is holy. They have heard that the figures carved from the bone-vein will bring water to a house and peace to a marriage. They arrive at the city's edge and stop to catch their breath and look out at the Cold Reach, where the mist lives, and they are afraid for a moment, because the country is so open and so bare and so unlike anywhere they have been. Then they come into the city, and the city closes around them, and they feel safer. They buy the wide-eyed figures. They buy a piece of calcite. They have the priests bless them. They go home and tell their friends that the stone is holy, and the stone may be.

The caravan drivers come in from the west, six days on the road, with the timber and the salt and the dried fish and the small tough apples of the river valleys. They are wary of the altitude. They are wary of the dry country, which is so unlike their own wet valleys. They are wary of the dust, which gets into everything and which a man from the west does not know how to spit out properly. They come anyway, because the white stone is worth the trip and the Hevelkari pay fairly. They sleep in the lower town, among the pigeons and the rats and the dogs. They eat the stew and find it strange and good. They load their carts with the pale cubes of stone and they go home, and the stone they carry will, in the lowlands, become the lintels of temples and the floors of counting houses, and it will weather clean, as the masons have always promised, and the people who walk on it will not know the basalt flats above them and will not think of the herders.

The *carreiros* — the burden lizards — are not quite reptiles. They have a fine fur, the color of the basalt in shadow, and they bear the cold better than any beast of burden ought to. They pull the carts of white stone down to the river for loading onto the barges, and they come back with the caravans, and the journey is slow, and they do not seem to mind. They can be eaten in a famine, and they have been. They are not loved, exactly, but they are respected, the way a tool is respected, and a man who treats his carreiros badly is a man the other carters will not drink with.

The dogs of the lower town are mongrel and hard to kill. They are friendly in the decided way of dogs that have been beaten and have decided you will not beat them. They sleep in the sun when there is sun. They sleep in the cold when there is not. They eat what they can find. They bark at the carreiros, which are too large and too slow to be bothered by the barking. They follow the caravans out of the city and as far as the edge of the flats, and then they come back, because beyond the flats there is nothing for a dog.

Hevelkar is a city of patient people. They have learned the patience from the place, and the place has learned it from itself — from the wind that does not hurry, from the stone that splits when it is ready and not before, from the god who sleeps and does not turn. They do not love the place, exactly. They have a word for it that is stronger than love, and it means something like *being held*, and it is the word they use for the stone floor of the house where they were born. They expect to die in the place where they were born. They expect their children to be born on the same floor. They expect the wind to blow and the dust to come and the wells to breathe and the white hawks to wheel and the goats to crop the thin grass and the masons to carve the wide-eyed figures and the caravans to come and the caravans to go.

The city turns. It turns slowly. That is the nature of the place.
