# What Lives Here

The shrimp are the first thing to notice you. Not because they perceive you — they don't, not as an individual — but because they perceive *disruption*, and your arrival is a disruption. A seam of wrongness in their schools, where the helical ribbons of pale carapace kink around your edges and then, after a moment, smooth out again. They school around you the way they school around the nodes: a hole in the lacework, an absence shaped like a warm intrusion. Their small black eyes, dozens of them, dozens of dozens, reflect the down-pointed light in tiny coin-sized mirrors, and for a moment you are wrapped in their attention the way a fly is wrapped in a web of sight, before the moment passes and they go back to their slow spiraling.

There are billions of them. The number is the first lie the place tells you, because the number doesn't feel like billions. It feels like density. It feels like the water itself has been minced into small pale pieces that move with a collective grammar, and the grammar is patient and ancient and has not yet needed to change.

The two species of translucent shrimp are different in ways that take a while to see.

**The smaller species** is the more numerous. A thumb-length crustacean with a carapace so clear that you can watch the dark knots of its internal organs work — a gut that pulses, a heart that pulses faster than the gut, a brain that is barely a knot at all, more of a thickening of nerve than a true head. They have long antennae, twice their body length, fine as spider silk, sweeping the water ahead of them in perpetual feathery consultations. They feed on detritus, mostly: the slow rain of organic matter that descends through the water column. Their feeding is a constant small motion, the forelegs working at the mouthparts in a motion so rapid and so continuous that it blurs into a vibration. They are the white noise of the place. They are also the clean-up crew, the slow and patient recyclers of everything that dies or molts or breaks apart, and in this role they are not optional. Without them, the shaft would, over centuries, become a stagnant sump. With them, it remains water.

**The larger species** is rarer, perhaps one in a hundred, and lives a different life. They are longer — twice as long, with bodies that are less clear and more opalescent, mother-of-pearl, the pale green-pink of abraded shell. They are also predators, in a modest way: they eat the smaller shrimp, when they can catch them, which is not often, because the smaller shrimp are faster. So the larger shrimp have evolved a different strategy. They hold still. They let the schools move around them. They drift in the negative spaces between the ribbons and snap sideways at whatever small thing drifts within range, with a speed that is startling given their usual motionlessness. They are ambush predators. They are the place's quiet corrections, the small culls that keep the smaller shrimp from overpopulating and eating themselves out of food.

The smaller shrimp tolerate them. The schools do not part around them as they part around you. The larger shrimp are part of the grammar. They have always been here, or as close to always as makes no difference.

**The copepods** are something else. They are not crustaceans in the strict sense, though they look it. They are larger than the small shrimp — about the size of a lentil, but with long antennae and a single dark eye that is, in its way, more watchful than any shrimp's. They drift. They do not school. Each copepod is its own slow project, hanging in the water with legs spread wide like the stays of a tiny tent, feeding on whatever passes through the fan of fine setae that ring their mouthparts. They are the place's contemplatives. If the shrimp are the music, the copepods are the rests between the notes. They move when they move, and otherwise they hang, and the fact of their hanging is itself a kind of statement, an assertion that the water is solid enough to rest in, that rest is possible here, that not everything must turn and turn and turn.

There are not many copepods. A few thousand, perhaps. They drift in the spaces between the shrimp schools, in the calm water around the silent pipes, in the long pauses between the warm pipes where nothing much happens. They are rare enough that you notice them as individuals. Each one is its own small darkness, its own patient eye.

The flatfish is not a flatfish.

It is shaped like one — flat, broad, mottled gray-green, with both eyes on the upper surface and a mouth that works sideways at small prey. But it is too large. It is the size of a serving platter, and it hangs in the water column above one of the warm pipes with the stillness of a held breath. It is not hunting. It does not need to hunt. There is something about the warm pipes — some minor upwelling of warmer water, some current that rises gently from the heated metal — that brings small prey to it, or that stimulates its metabolism, or that simply feels right. The flatfish knows which pipes are warm. It moves among them, sometimes, with a slow undulation of its fringed fins, and settles above the one it has chosen for the day, and there it stays.

It is a territorial animal, in a quiet way. Other flatfish — there are a handful, six or eight — keep their distance from its chosen pipe. They have their own pipes. They have worked out, over the slow course of their lives, a distribution. They do not fight. They do not display. They simply do not overlap. The arrangement has the quality of a long-negotiated treaty, and the fact that no one is here to enforce it makes the treaty more binding, not less.

The flatfish are old. Their scales carry growth rings that you cannot read without magnification, but which are numerous. They have been in this shaft a long time. They may have been born here. They may have nowhere else to go. They do not appear to want to go anywhere else.

The jellyfish are the most beautiful thing here, and they do not know it.

They are small — the bell the size of a thumbnail, the tentacles trailing twice that length — and they are few. A hundred, perhaps. They drift in the middle of the shaft, in the band of water where the hum of the pipes and the chitter of the shrimp blend together, and they pulse. Their pulsing is the slow motion of the place made visible: a contraction, a relaxation, a drift upward, a pause, a contraction again. They are not feeding actively. They are not breeding. They are simply being jellyfish, which is to say they are simply being water that has remembered it once had other options.

They glow, faintly. A pale blue-green, bioluminescent, triggered perhaps by the vibrations in the water, perhaps by the pressure changes near the warm pipes, perhaps by nothing at all. They glow and they pulse and they drift, and they are the only things in the shaft that seem to be doing something without purpose. The shrimp are feeding. The flatfish are waiting. The copepods are filtering. But the jellyfish are *displaying*. They are showing themselves. They are saying, with their light, *I am here, I am here, I am still here*. It does not matter that no one is watching. The display is the point.

The ribbon-thing is the largest animal in the shaft.

It is perhaps two meters long, and as thick as a finger, and it is pale, and it is long in the way that eels are long, and it moves with an intention that nothing else here quite matches. It has a head, or what functions as a head — a slight thickening at one end, with a mouth that opens and closes slowly, and small dark eyes that are not shrimp eyes but fish eyes, watchful and lateral. It has a long body. It has a tail that is hard to find, because the body is so uniform that the tail seems to be everywhere and nowhere.

It eats the larger shrimp. This is what it does. It hunts them not by speed but by patience: it drifts near a school, very still, and lets the school adjust to its presence, and then it strikes, a single fast coil of its long body, and a shrimp is in its mouth, and the school kinks around the absence and goes on.

It is not numerous. There may be three or four of them in the shaft. They keep to themselves. They do not compete. They are too large, too few, too patient to compete. Each one has its own territory — a stretch of pipe, a region of the shaft, a band of depth — and they pass each other without acknowledgment, like old neighbors who have long since exhausted all possible conversation.

The ribbon-things are the longest-lived animals here. Their bones, or what passes for bones, are layered with growth that takes decades to accumulate. They were here when the first shrimp arrived. They will be here when the shrimp, eventually, leave or change. They are the shaft's continuity. They are what the place would remember, if the place could remember.

There are other things here. A brittle star, once, clinging to a pipe joint, its five arms curled in tight spirals; where it came from, no one knows. A small crab, the color of the basalt walls, almost invisible against them, scuttling sideways along a faceted surface in a way that suggests it has a route, a patrol, a small life that is its own. Snails, on the warm pipes, grazing the thin film of bacteria that grows there — a film that exists because of the warmth, because warmth in cold water is an invitation to growth, and the snails are the film's gardeners. Small worms, pale and threadlike, in the sediment at the bottom of the shaft, if there is sediment, if there is a bottom; the column of light is not strong enough to tell.

These are the smaller lives. They are not the dominant notes. They are the harmonics.

The dominant notes are the shrimp and the pipes. The shrimp and the pipes, together, are the place. The shrimp are what makes it feel inhabited. The pipes are what makes it feel built. Neither acknowledges the other. Both are necessary. Both will outlast you.

The shrimp will molt, and the molted shells will sink, and the snails will not eat them, and the bacteria on the warm pipes will not eat them, and so they will accumulate on the bottom, slowly, into a pale ooze that will become, over decades, a thin layer of white sediment that records nothing except the lives of shrimp that did not survive. The copepods will lay eggs that drift down with the shells, and some will hatch, and some will not, and the ones that do will rise again to the middle depths and resume their hanging. The flatfish will spawn, somewhere, in a season you cannot detect, and the larvae will drift up toward the light, and some will be eaten, and some will survive, and the survivors will grow large and slow and find their own warm pipe. The ribbon-things will do whatever ribbon-things do, in private, in the slow dark, and the evidence of their lives will be the occasional molt-skin drifting downward like a pale glove.

And the pipes will pulse. The pipes will pulse, and the hum will continue, and the cycles that are longer than a heartbeat and longer than a breath and longer than an afternoon will cycle on, and the machinery will not know that anything has changed, because nothing has changed, because nothing was ever supposed to change, because the pipes are part of a system that operates on a scale that does not include shrimp.

The shaft, as a habitat, is stable. It is not rich — the productivity of the warm pipes is modest, and the rain of detritus from above is thin — but it is stable. The shrimp do not run out of food because the shrimp do not overpopulate, because the larger shrimp and the ribbon-things cull them. The flatfish do not run out of perches because the warm pipes are few but durable. The jellyfish do not run out of water because the water is the whole of the world. There is no season here, no migration, no catastrophe. There is just the slow churn of lives in a small space, balanced against each other, checked against each other, layered over each other like transparencies on the same light.

It is not paradise. It is not even particularly kind. It is just *ongoing*. It is a system that has found its way to continue, and it continues.

You can stay, if you want. You can hang in the water column and let the schools part around you, and the flatfish watch you from above its warm pipe, and the ribbon-thing pass you in the dark without interest, and the jellyfish glow their pale blue-green at you as if you were one of them, one of the things that is simply being here without purpose. You can feel the hum in your chest and the cold in your joints and the light on your face, if your face is the kind of thing that faces things, and you can let the place do its slow work around you.

Or you can leave. There is no reason to stay. There is no reason to go. The shrimp will not notice either way. The pipes will not notice either way. The jellyfish will glow, and the flatfish will wait, and the ribbon-thing will hunt, and the copepods will hang, and the place will go on being what it is, with or without you, in the cold water, in the green light, in the slow and unhurried way of all things that are not in a hurry.
