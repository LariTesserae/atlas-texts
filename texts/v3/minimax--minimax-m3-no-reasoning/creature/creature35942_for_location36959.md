The Girdlewood is inhabited by what the Girdlewood has made of itself, and by what the Girdlewood has not yet finished making.

The floor is a single animal. Say that first. The rootplate that runs from wall to wall of the basin, felted into something almost fabric-like, warm to the touch, is not soil and is not stone and is not a community of separate trees. It is one creature. It is one creature that has been alive for so long that it has forgotten what being one creature means, and so it does not bother with the parts other creatures use to keep themselves in pieces — no brain, no nerve, no eye. It does not need them. It has the basin. It has the bulbs. It has the parasols and the rattles and the rootplate and the slow tidal pulse that moves through it like a breath through a sleeping throat, and you can feel that pulse through your boots if you stand very still on its back, which is the floor.

The wood is warm. The wood is metabolizing. The wood sweats its own rain — each leaf exhaling, each root-tip dribbling, the canopy holding the moisture as a permanent low haze — and so the wood is also its own weather. The wood is also its own storm. The bulbs fill with a thin warm gas that the wood secretes from its inner lining, and then vent through calibrated slits in the bulb's hide, and the venting is the drumming, and the drumming is the bass note of the roar, and the roar is what you hear if you listen first before you try to see it.

The wood is busy. It is busy in the way a beehive is busy, in the way a storm is busy, in the way the inside of a living cell is busy. The bulbs thicken. The parasols widen. The rattles extend. At the basin's edges, pale stems no thicker than a wrist push up through cracks in the warm stone in the night; by morning they have woven themselves into a lattice; by the next night, new stems have pushed up from that lattice; by the week after, a young forest a yard tall. The rootplate creeps outward, up the basin walls. The wood is coming over the rim. The wood has always been coming over the rim.

The wood does not know you are there. The wood will drum, and sweat, and swing its rattles, and open its bulbs, and pour its amber, and creep another inch up the warm stone rim, and it will do this for as long as the stone stays warm, which may be forever.

---

Within the wood, married into it, are the others.

The smallest are the legions. They are insects in the broad sense — jointed, chitinous, driven by strange blood — but insect the way a cathedral is a stone. Some are the size of a thumb. Some are the size of an arm. They swarm in rivers along the upper surface of the rootplate, in columns a foot wide and miles long, each one carrying a fragment of leaf or a chip of bulb-husk back to a node. The nodes are scattered across the basin like villages on a plain — heaped mounds of worked plant material, pulped and regimented and cemented with a resin the insects secrete, in which the openings are many, the chambers are deep, and the activity is continuous.

The nodes are not nests. The nodes are stomachs. The insects feed the bulbs. The bulbs feed the insects. Neither partner can survive more than a few hundred yards from the other. The wood eats them. They eat the wood. The boundary has softened past recognition, and what is left is a long quiet negotiation between a forest and its tenants that has gone on so long that neither side remembers when it began. The insects are not really inhabitants of the wood. They are organs of the wood, or the wood is an organ of them. The question has been moot for a long time.

From a node, even above the omnipresent roar, you hear a thinner sharper sound: the chitter of a million jaws. The chitter does not stop. The chitter has not stopped.

---

At the nodes, certain of the insects are kept in shallow pits of rootplate. These are larger, slower, with strange swollen abdomens. From glands at the joints of their legs they produce a thick amber liquid. The amber is the medium of exchange.

The hornbearers lick it. The hornbearers are the size of ponies. They have six legs and a horn — a single forward-curving horn of black chitin, two feet long — that they use to gouge the bulbs open when the gas inside has built up to the point of needing release. The hornbearer knows which bulbs to open. The hornbearer knows when. This is not learning and it is not instinct either, exactly, because instinct implies a separation between the bearer and the thing that guides it, and in the Girdlewood the separation has been worn away. The hornbearer knows in the way that a hand knows which finger to move. The hornbearer is part of the wood, and the wood is part of the hornbearer, and the amber keeps them both calm.

When a hornbearer opens a bulb, the pent-up gas vents with a scream that drops the pitch from a kettle-whistle to a foghorn. The canopy rocks. Every parasol for a hundred yards around shivers. You, if you are standing in the basin floor, feel it as a sudden warm gust at your ankles and a sudden ringing in your ears.

The hornbearers are the percussionists. When they are not gassing the bulbs, they wander the rootplate in long processions, swinging their horns in slow arcs. The horn strikes the rattles as they pass — the long whip-like extensions of stem that end in hollow bulbs of brittle shell — and produces the syncopated clatter that runs above the tok-tok-tok of the drums. Tok-tok. Tak-tak-tok. Tok. A polyrhythm. A canon. A percussion symphony performed by a herd of six-legged animals in a forest that is also a steam bath.

The hornbearers are calm. The hornbearers are musical. The hornbearers are tolerated, in their slow processions, by everything else in the Girdlewood, because the hornbearers are part of the orchestra and the orchestra is the weather and the weather is the wood.

---

And there are people.

A few. Not many. A small and quiet people, for obvious reasons — there is no quiet in the Girdlewood, and so the people have become quiet in compensation.

They live at the rim, where the rootplate thins and the raw warm stone shows through. Their houses are built into the bulb-chambers of the giant stems: hollowed-out rooms with windows cut in the woody walls, ladders of peeled rattan, doors of hung leaf. They are warm in winter. They are warm in summer. They are warm always. They speak to each other across the basin by fire-signals in the daytime and by drum-codes at night, tapping on the inside walls of their own bulbs in patterns that carry, because the wood is an excellent conductor of vibration. They write in the dust of churned rootplate. They press their mouths to each other's ears.

They have iron. They have cloth. They have not, in any recorded memory, climbed to the top of the canopy. There is no reason to. There is everything up there. They do not say what is up there, and they do not say why no one goes, because the answer is obvious to anyone who has lived a single night in the basin: up there is where the wind is loudest, and the hornbearers are busiest, and the bulbs vent the most, and a person has no business being where the orchestra is being played.

They farm the amber. They tend the hornbearers in small ways — leading them to the right bulbs, sealing the wrong ones with plugs of resin — and in return the hornbearers tolerate them as moving furniture. The hornbearers do not threaten. The hornbearers do not flee. The people are a small quiet afterthought at the rim, and they have made a life in the gaps between hornbearer blasts, and they have learned to speak in the drum-codes of the wood, and they are the only inhabitants of the Girdlewood who know they are inhabitants.

The people believe — those who still believe anything — that the basin is waking up. They point to the new growth at the basin's edges. They point to the slow creep of the rootplate outward. They say: *it is coming over the rim*. They say: *when it comes over the rim, we will have to move*. They say: *we have always said this*. They say it with the resignation of people whose doom is also their livelihood.

They do not say what will happen to the hornbearers when the wood overruns the rim. They do not say what will happen to the orchestra. They do not say what will happen to the amber, or to the drum-codes, or to the fire-signals, or to the houses built into the bulb-chambers, or to the iron, or to the cloth.

---

The Girdlewood does not know them. The Girdlewood does not know the hornbearers, or the legions, or the keepers, or the amber. The Girdlewood does not need to know them. The Girdlewood is a closed system, and a closed system can only continue, and the Girdlewood is continuing, and the inhabitants are part of the continuing, and the continuing does not require that anyone be aware of it.

If you stand very still in the basin floor — which is difficult, because the hornbearer processions come close, and the small insects run over your feet like a tickling carpet, and the floor is breathing under you, and the air is thick and warm and wet — if you stand very still, and you close your eyes, and you let the roar wash over you until it stops being a roar and becomes a single tone, you can feel, through the soles of your boots, the slow tidal pulse of the rootplate. A systole. A diastole. The heartbeat of a forest that has forgotten it is a forest and has remembered that it is an animal.

The light, where it reaches, is green.

The air, where you breathe it, is wet.

The sound, where you stand, is everything.

This is who lives in the Girdlewood.
