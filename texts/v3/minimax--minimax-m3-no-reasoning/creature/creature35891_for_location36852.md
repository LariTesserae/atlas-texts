There are dwellers in Ulshuur, and there are things that share Ulshuur with them, and the dwellers have not settled which of these things is alive in the way the dwellers are alive, and which is alive in some other way, and which is not alive at all but is only a habit of matter that has continued for so long it has forgotten how to stop — a question that comes up, in fact, in nearly every treatise in the clay-tablet vault of the Fifteenth Ward, scratched over by scholars who mistrust consensus and arrive at it anyway, because some questions are large enough that even the most rigorous disagreement has a shape.

The dwellers are the people. They are pale, of course — they have never seen a sun, nor any star — but the paleness is not the bleached paleness of the surface-dwelling sick or the underfed. It is the paleness of a thing that has never had to defend itself against ultraviolet, against wind, against the drying kiss of an atmosphere with too little moisture in it. The skin is almost translucent. Beneath it the blood runs visible as a deep maroon almost black, the color of blood that has given up its oxygen long ago and found other business — sap-compounds, perhaps, or the slow chemistry of an organism that has been breathing the same air as the pith for so long that the two chemistries have learned to share a vocabulary. The skin is cool to the touch. It smells of green sweetness. It gives, when pressed, the way the pith gives, and one is reminded, holding a dweller's hand, that one is holding living tissue, and that the tissue is not so different, in its way, from the wall.

The hands are the most human thing about them, in the sense that they are the most like the human ideal — or perhaps the human ideal is more like a dweller's hand than we knew, and we are the deviation. Broad in the palm, long in the finger, the thumb set low and strong. A dweller holds a chert blade the way a cellist holds a bow: not gripped, but *balanced*, the tool an extension of the finger, the finger an extension of the wrist. The blades themselves are long and thin and almost translucent, hafted into handles of fired clay that fit the hand so precisely you would think they had been grown there. These hands have been carving pith for centuries. They have combed the long pale pith-hair from the veins and spun it into thread. They have shaped clay on small wheels turned by foot treadle, and they have fired the clay in kilns that burn shed pith-hair and nothing else, and they have glazed the clay with a thin slip of sap that dries to a pale matte surface that is cool to the touch and faintly luminous. They have no metal. They have no need of metal. The pith provides. And through all of this — the combing, the spinning, the throwing, the carving, the slow opening of new chambers where the pith is healthiest — the hands' owner is singing, because in Ulshuur one does not work in silence. One works in song. The song marks the rhythm of the work, and the work marks the rhythm of the song, and the two are not separable, and the song is taken up by the next tent, and the next, until the spiral of the staircase is humming with it.

Their eyes are large — twice the size, perhaps, of surface eyes, built for catching the few photons the pith bothers to emit — and the iris is a deep slate grey that, in the green glow of the felt tents, reflects back at you a startling silver, so that to climb the spiral stair in the deep hours is to be passed by silent figures whose faces are two coins of pale metal turned toward you, and who murmur greetings you cannot parse, and whose breath you feel on your wrist before they are past. A surface visitor learns, in time, to read the silver. A certain lift of the chin means acknowledgment. A certain turn of the head means *I am busy, do not speak*. A certain flicker means *I do not know you*.

Their voices are their primary dwelling. They live in sound the way surface people live in light — they find their way by it, they recognize their kin by it, they mark the hour by it, they mourn by it, they fall asleep by it. The voice of a dweller is, in some sense, an inheritance — passed down, like the broad hand, like the silver eye, like the talent for throwing clay — and a family is recognizable by its voice the way a surface family might be recognizable by its face. The voice of a dweller, in the bore, is shaped by nineteen kilometers of pith-walled resonance into something rounder and warmer than any surface voice; it carries further, returns to the ear with the undertone of the wall behind it, so that even a whisper, in the deep hours, seems to come from inside the listener's chest. The dwellers do not whisper often. They speak at a low conversational hum that sounds, to a surface ear, almost like singing, and they break into formal song without warning — a single syllable, often, sustained for what seems like minutes — and the song is taken up. Voice by voice, ward by ward, the bore rings with it, and a surface visitor, if there is one, will feel it in the sternum and not know what to call it. The dwellers hear the shape of a voice and know the family. They hear the timing of a footfall and know the ward. They hear the breath-pattern of someone climbing the spiral stair and know, before the climber comes into the green glow of the upper ward, whether the climber is hurrying or idling, sad or well. The songs are not beautiful. They are not meant to be. They are *navigation*.

A child of Ulshuur is born into green light. The green light is the glow of the felt tents, and the felt is woven of pith-hair that the dwellers have bred, or selected, or merely invited a photophore into — a small luminescent thing that gives off a faint greenish glow in total darkness — so that a tent is a soft green constellation strung along the spiral of the staircase, and a city is a thousand such constellations. The child is born into the smell of green sweetness, into the creak of pith, into the song of voices, into the slow tick of the symbiont on its shelf. The child learns, before it learns to walk, that the world is a hollow tube lined with breathing white tissue, and that it must keep the tissue breathing, and that the tissue is keeping it alive. The child learns the spiral stair before it learns the names of its parents. The child learns to sing before it learns to speak, because in the dark, song is the older language, the language the dwellers inherited from somewhere or invented from nothing, and speech is a recent elaboration, a way of being precise when song is too imprecise. The dwellers' language has no word for *light*, and twelve words for *pith* in its various states — for pith that is wet, for pith that is dry, for pith that is working, for pith that is resting, for pith that has begun to harden, for pith that is newly opened and weeping sap. The word for *light*, when borrowed from surface visitors, is treated by the dwellers as a borrowed word, a technical term, and is used carefully, and the dwellers will often ask surface visitors to clarify what they mean, because the dwellers suspect that the surface people do not always mean the same thing by it.

The air of Ulshuur is breathable, and it is used. The organism, in its endless slow chemistry, splits the chert to get at trace minerals and releases oxygen as a byproduct, and the oxygen has been accumulating in the bore for as long as the organism has been there. The atmosphere is slightly richer in oxygen than surface air and slightly poorer in nitrogen, with a trace of something sweet that is the sap vapor. The dwellers have adapted to it. Surface visitors — there have been a handful, mostly scholars who came down and never left, and some of whom married into dweller families and wrote treatises that sit in the clay-tablet vault alongside the dwellers' own — find themselves slightly giddy for the first day, then adjust. The air is warm in a way that pith is warm — not very, but enough. It moves, slightly, in currents set up by the temperature differential between the glowing walls and the dark Spine. A breath of air, in Ulshuur, is a thing that has touched everything and everyone. The dwellers know this. The dwellers know the air by its taste, by its weight, by the small shifts in it that mark the passage of a family up or down the spiral, and they breathe it in and out and in and do not remark on it, the way fish do not remark on water.

The dwellers make beautiful things. They have always made beautiful things, or have made beautiful things for as long as the clay-tablet vault can attest. Their chert blades are the best I have seen. Their bone needles are finer than any surface equivalent. Their pottery — against all reason, against all expectation — is the most accomplished I have seen anywhere: thin-walled, precisely formed, fired in small kilns, glazed with a thin slip of sap. They have no metal, and they do not miss it, because the pith provides. They pay rent to the organism — I do not use the word lightly — in the form of careful tending: pruning the roots that grow too thick, opening new chambers where the pith is healthiest, clearing the veins when they clog, seeding the walls with their own waste so the organism can reclaim the nutrients. In return the organism keeps them cool, keeps them fed, keeps them hidden, keeps them alive in a world that has, above and below, very little interest in keeping anything alive. This is not metaphor to the dwellers. The dwellers are very firm on this. The city is a tenancy, not a conquest. The pith is the landlord, and the rent is paid in service, and the service is love, and the love is the only thing that keeps the pith alive, and the pith alive is the only thing that keeps the dwellers alive, and the two facts lean on each other in the dark. The politics of Ulshuur, when there is politics, is mostly about which ward gets the next new chamber cut, and which family has the right to prune which root, and how to distribute the kiln-fuel when the pith-hair harvest is lean.

The dwellers are preoccupied. Not with philosophy, not with art, not even with politics. The dwellers are preoccupied with the end. The pith is dying. The pith has been dying, very slowly, for as long as the scholars can measure, and the scholars have measured carefully, with bone calipers and clay phials and the slow recording of sap-flow in the great veins, and the measurements agree: the pith's growth has slowed to a crawl. Its pith is denser, drier, more like wood and less like flesh. In another few thousand years — perhaps many thousands; the timescales are uncertain — the glow will fade. The walls will harden. The sap will cease. The roots will stop their slow eastward drift. The dwellers, who have never known any other home, will have to decide what to do next.

This is the central preoccupation of Ulshuur. They discuss it in low voices in the felt tents. They sing about it in the long hours. The scholars write treatises on it, in a script scratched into clay tablets and stored in a vault in the Fifteenth Ward, and the treatises are long and careful and full of disagreement, and none of them conclude, because there is no conclusion to be had. Some scholars argue for going up. Some scholars argue for going down. Some scholars argue for staying, and trusting that the pith, when it stops, will leave behind something — a husk, a fossil, a memory in the chert — that the dwellers can inhabit in some other way. Some scholars argue that the dwellers are themselves already partly pith, and that when the pith stops, the dwellers will simply stop with it, and that the only honorable response is to tend the pith as carefully as possible until the end, and to sing. The scholars mistrust consensus. Consensus is a thing they mistrust. And yet consensus arrives, in the form of the song: the song that rises through the Spine at the deep hour, when the symbionts are silent and the children are asleep and the dwellers' own voices, in the long acoustic of the bore, become indistinguishable from the creak of the organism itself.

The symbionts live here too. A symbiont is hard to describe because no one is sure what it is. It is six-legged and segmented and has a pair of mandibles that work in a slow rhythmic click, the way a beetle's might, except that a symbiont's mandibles click not to chew but to *mark*, and the dweller who keeps one has trained it, or it has trained itself, to click at intervals the dweller can count on: one click for the hour, two for the half, a long silence for the deep night. The symbiont lives in a small woven basket hung in the felt tent, and is fed — on what, no one quite says, though the most common theory among the scholars is that it eats the shed pith-hair, and the next most common is that it eats the dweller's shed skin, and the next is that it eats light, or eats silence, or eats the small deaths of the small things that happen in the night. The symbiont is warmer than the pith, which is to say it is warm at all, and a dweller will sometimes, in the deep hours, lift the basket down and hold the symbiont in both hands and listen to it click, and the dweller's heartbeat will synchronize to the click, and the click will synchronize to the creak of the pith, and for a moment, in the dark, the dweller and the symbiont and the organism will be keeping the same time.

The pith lives. This is the central fact of Ulshuur, and the dwellers know it, and the scholars of Ulshuur know it better than anyone, and even the scholars, when pressed, will admit they do not know what *lives* means when applied to something this size. The pith is nineteen kilometers long. It is twelve meters in diameter. It is, so far as the dwellers can tell, a single individual — one organism, one body, one slow metabolism — that has been growing in the bore since before the basalt flowed above and the steppe took its cold shape, and that has been growing more slowly with each passing century until now, when it grows so slowly that only the scholars, with their careful instruments of bone and clay, can measure the growth at all. The pith is white. The pith is fibrous. The pith is cool and damp and gives, when pressed, and smells of green sweetness, and glows with a sourceless bluish-white light that is brighter near the slow veins and dimmer near the chambers where the dwellers have cut deepest. The pith creaks. The pith pulses. The pith is, in some sense the dwellers cannot articulate but feel constantly, *watching*. Not with eyes. With the whole body. With the slow attention of something that has nothing else to do. The roots descend from somewhere far above — from the ceiling of the bore and from its walls — white and branching, each as thick as a man's arm in the upper reaches and tapering toward the floor. They are the organism's water-seeking structures, though what water they seek in this dry mineral world is a question the scholars have argued about for generations. They do not touch the floor. They hang in the air of the bore, swaying — not in any wind, for there is no wind, but in some slower oscillation, some internal pulse of the organism, so that over the course of an hour one can watch a root tip drift a finger's width to the east and back.

In the central void of the bore, where no pith touches, there is true darkness — a black column nineteen kilometers long, vertical (the bore is bored at a slight angle but near enough to vertical), and the dwellers call it the Spine. Nothing hangs in the Spine. Nothing crosses the Spine. Falling into it would be death, though no one has ever fallen, because the dwellers have raised, over generations, a net of woven pith-hair across the bore at intervals of every hundred meters, a latticed floor that catches the slow drip of sap and the slower fall of anyone foolish enough to misstep.

The dwellers come up the spiral. The dwellers go down the spiral. The dwellers sing as they go, and the song marks the hour, and the song marks the family, and the song, in the long run, may be the only thing they take with them when the pith stops.
