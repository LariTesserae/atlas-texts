# The Inhabitants of the Verdant Hollow

The small god on the branch sees you first. It is called, in the only language the canopy permits, a *throat-of-many-seasons*, and it is ten years old and will live to be four hundred, and it has never touched the ground, and it has never wanted to. It was the first thing to notice you, because its eyes number eleven and they are set into its body at angles that permit no single direction to be called *behind*. It is not alarmed by you. It has no category for alarm. You are, in its long memory, a shape that has appeared perhaps eleven times, and the eleventh time is always the same as the first time, because nothing here has learned to expect a thirteenth.

The throat-of-many-seasons is the size of a small cat but it is not the shape of a small cat. Its body is a long articulated thing, soft-boned, a little like a salamander with the manners of a sloth and the habits of something older than either. It walks along the underside of branches as often as the top, hooked by a curling tail-tip into bark that has been worn to polish by generations of similar tails. Its many legs — twelve, in three sets of four, the middle set longer than the others — move in paired waves, a gait that produces no perceptible sound against the wood, and this is because the pads of its feet exude a thin resin that grips and quiets. The wings folded along its back are not for flight. They are fans. They spread, in the heat of the mating season, into wide oval sails of translucent membrane veined with the faintest blue, and the throat-of-many-seasons hangs from a high branch and beats them slowly, and the sound this makes is the high thin whine you have been hearing, the eight-second swell, the note that the chamber uses for timekeeping.

Its throat pouch is the color of a ripe persimmon and it inflates with each call, an orange lantern pulsing against the green. The call itself is not a song. The throat-of-many-seasons does not sing. What it does is count. Each pouch-inflation is a number. It is the cathedral's clock. Somewhere in the canopy, a dozen of them are ringing off the hours in staggered shifts, and the chamber is never without a number being spoken aloud, and the deep green pulse of the light, the slow migration of the columns across the floor, is synchronized to their counting. They are older than the cathedral. They are, in some private sense that no naturalist has ever confirmed, the reason the cathedral is a cathedral.

---

Below the throat-of-many-seasons, in the tangle of aerial roots, the pale shape breathes.

It is asleep. It has been asleep for what you would call a season, though the seasons here are not the ones you grew up counting. It is a *stone-keeper*, and its name, in the language of the damp places, is a sound that involves a long hum and a small click at the back of the tongue. It is the length of a man laid down. Its skin is the color of a mushroom's underside, a kind of luminous grey-pink that catches the greenish light and seems to give it back warmer. It is smooth. It is not slimy; it is moist in the way a stone is moist in a forest, in the way a mouth is moist, in the way a thing that is alive in a wet place is moist.

The stone-keeper is dreaming, and the dream is audible.

The low sustained vibration you have been feeling in your chest is the stone-keeper's dream made manifest. It does not snore. It hums. Somewhere in its sleeping body, a set of structures you have no name for vibrates at a frequency tuned to the resonant frequency of the root-mat beneath you, and the root-mat picks the hum up and propagates it through the cathedral, and the cathedral is in this way, at all times, a part of the stone-keeper's dream. The stone-keeper does not know you are here. The stone-keeper has not, in its slow life, particularly wanted to know that you are here. It wants only to sleep and to eat and to sleep again, and the eating is rare — a single large meal every few months, a slow thing caught in the black-water basins, a thing dragged under — and the sleeping is the rest. It is, by the standards of this place, a simple creature. It has no architecture to its days. It is one of the cathedral's basement dwellers, one of the things that lives below the lights and the wars and the chittering, one of the things that only comes up into the greenish columns when the hunger is finally, after a long time, larger than the patience.

It is patient. It is the most patient thing here.

---

The luminous ants are not ants. They are called *leaf-mothers*, and what they are doing is not foraging. They are burying their children.

Each leaf-mother is the size of your thumb joint, segmented, soft-bodied, glowing a steady pale green from glands along her thorax. The light is not for her benefit. The light is a kind of medicine, a way of saying to the leaf-blade in her jaws: *I am alive, I am a relative, please do not rot around me too quickly.* She carries the fragment of leaf to a low place in the root-mat, where a small chamber has been dug out by previous generations. Inside the chamber, invisible to you, are her eggs, perhaps two hundred of them, the size of pinheads, already beginning to divide. She is burying them in leaf. The leaf will compost around the eggs, and the heat of the composting will keep them warm, and the leaf's slow decay will feed them when they hatch. The leaf-mothers have been doing this in this place for, by the throat-of-many-seasons' count, eleven million years. They have, in that time, become the most numerous thing in the cathedral. Their bodies, when they die, are carried by their sisters to the same chambers, and the same composting heat warms the next generation, and so the leaf-mothers and their dead are, between them, a kind of slow fire. The percussion you have been hearing — wood on wood, the distant drumming — is not wood on wood. It is a million small soft feet in a million small soft rhythm, and the rhythm is the rhythm of a thing that has never stopped building, never stopped burying, never stopped tending the small warm dark rooms of the next.

The leaf-mothers do not have a leader. The leaf-mothers do not have a plan. The leaf-mothers are, taken together, a single ongoing project, the way a coral reef is a single ongoing project, the way a forest is a single ongoing project, and the project is: *continue*.

---

The wet rhythmic sound from behind the buttress-root is the work of a *scale-loom*, and the scale-loom is doing what scale-looms do, which is weave.

The scale-loom is the length of your forearm. It is a snake, if a snake were plated in overlapping keratin scales the color of wet bark, if a snake had a flattened head and a mouth like a heddle, if a snake had four small clawed feet at the back end that it used not for walking but for anchoring itself to a branch. It does not weave cloth. It weaves *air*.

What the scale-loom produces is a kind of membrane, a film of crystallized saliva and shed skin and a secretion unique to its species, stretched between two points and beaten, over hours, into a thin shimmering sheet. The sheet is not for shelter. The sheet is a song. The scale-loom runs the edge of its flattened head along the taut sheet, producing a note, and the note is then reflected back from the other side and the scale-loom adjusts, slightly, and runs the edge again, and the two notes interfere and produce a third note, and the third note is the one that the scale-loom was after. The scale-loom is tuning. It is tuning the air in its small region of the cathedral. It has been doing this for what the leaf-mothers would call three days. It will be doing it for what the throat-of-many-seasons would call a week. It will, at the end, have tuned a region of perhaps four cubic meters of air into a single resonant chamber, and the resonant chamber will attract a mate, who will come — drawn by the sound, drawn by the particular timbre of the sound, which is the scale-loom's way of saying *I am here and I am of this size and I have this many scales and my mother ate this kind of leaf* — and the two scale-looms will weave together for a season and then one of them will die, because that is how scale-looms are, and the other will eat the dead one, because that is also how scale-looms are, and the eggs will be laid in the tuned air and the tuned air will hold the eggs warm and the small scale-looms will hatch and begin, in their turn, the long tuning.

There are perhaps two hundred scale-looms in the cathedral at any time, each tuning its own small region, and the aggregate of their tunings is a kind of weather, a kind of aural climate, and the cathedral is never the same cathedral twice, because the tuned regions are always shifting, always being tuned and re-tuned, and the interference patterns of the tuned regions produce the cathedral's other ambient sound, the layered almost-musical hum that lives beneath the chittering of the leaf-mothers and the counting of the throat-of-many-seasons and the dream of the stone-keeper. The cathedral is, in this sense, always being composed.

---

And there are the small ones.

There are the small ones in the moss. There are the small ones in the bark. There are the small ones in the chambers of the leaf-mothers, riding the composting heat. There are the small ones in the black-water basins, pale and blind, breathing through their skins, eating the falling spores. There are the small ones in the canopy that you cannot see and have not seen and will not see, the ones the size of pinheads and the ones the size of pinpoints, the ones whose entire life cycle is the time it takes a single spore to fall from a leaf to the floor, and who are born, and eat, and breed, and die, in that falling. There are the small ones whose job it is to eat the dead of the larger ones, and the small ones whose job it is to eat the small ones that eat the dead of the larger ones, and the small ones whose job it is to clean the small ones that eat the small ones, and the cathedral is, when you get down to the scale where the small ones live, a single layer of living film, a kind of biological paint applied over every surface, and the paint is wet, and the paint moves, and the paint has been moving for longer than the cathedral has been a cathedral.

The small ones do not have names. The small ones do not have names in the language of the damp places, because the language of the damp places is the language of the throat-of-many-seasons, which is the language of *number*, and the small ones are too many to number. The small ones are the cathedral's noise floor. The small ones are what is left when everything else has been accounted for.

---

None of them know your name.

None of them have a category for *you*. You are, to the throat-of-many-seasons, a shape that has appeared for the eleventh time. You are, to the stone-keeper, a slight coolness in the warm air. You are, to the leaf-mothers, an inconvenient height of thing that has not yet understood the right of way. You are, to the scale-loom, a region of un-tuned air, a small pocket of acoustic wilderness, a thing to be gently tuned around. You are, to the small ones, irrelevant. You are too large to be a habitat. You do not, in the cathedral's reckoning, *do* anything.

And yet.

The throat-of-many-seasons has, on its eleventh seeing of you, done something it has not done in the previous ten. It has descended a branch. It is now on a branch that is roughly at your eye level, and it is watching you, and its eleven eyes are open, and its throat pouch is not yet inflated, and the eight-second cycle has been suspended. The count has stopped. The chamber is, for the first time in the long memory of the throat-of-many-seasons, off by one.

The leaf-mothers have paused, in a wave that traveled across the floor at the speed of a slow walk, all the workers in the immediate region standing still with their leaf-fragments half-raised, their small green lights dimming slightly. The scale-loom behind the buttress-root has stopped running its head along the tuned sheet, and the sheet is now making its own faint note, untended, the note of a thing that has been left alone too long. The stone-keeper has not woken. But the dream has changed. The hum in the root-mat is a half-tone lower, and the half-tone is the color of something you might call *attention*, if *attention* were a color.

The small ones in the moss have, all at once, begun to climb. They are climbing your boots. They are climbing the cuffs of your trousers. They are climbing each other. They are producing, in the aggregate, a small moving carpet of themselves, and the carpet is ascending you, and the carpet is, in the cathedral's oldest and least expressible language, saying a word that means *taste*.

The cathedral is considering you. The cathedral has, in its slow and distributed way, a kind of opinion, and the opinion is forming now, and the opinion will take a long time to reach, and the opinion will, when it reaches, be delivered as a sound, and the sound will be the sound of a place deciding whether you are, in the cathedral's terms, a thing that lives here now.

You should not move. You should, in particular, not move quickly. The throat-of-many-seasons, eleven eyes open, throat pouch beginning — slowly, slowly — to inflate, is about to speak the next number, and the next number will be the eleventh number, and the eleventh number has never been spoken before, and the speaking of it will change the count, and the count is the time, and the time is the light, and the light is the cathedral, and the cathedral is, in this moment, watching you with every one of its million small wet eyes, and the watching is not hostile and the watching is not friendly and the watching is, simply, the watching of a thing that is alive, and that is, in the end, the only kind of attention that the cathedral knows how to pay.

The dew gathers on your eyelashes. The greenish light migrates, a quarter-inch, across the root-mat at your feet. The small ones taste you. The throat-of-many-seasons prepares to speak.
