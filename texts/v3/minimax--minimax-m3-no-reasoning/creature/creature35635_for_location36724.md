# The Inhabitants of the Hollow Brow

## I. The Wind-Tongue People

They call themselves the **Shal**, though no outsider has ever quite caught the way they produce the lateral fricative that begins their name. There are perhaps three hundred of them across the whole length of the ridge, but length here is deceptive—the Brow folds and twists in ways that do not show from a distance, and the settlements are strung along these hidden deflections like beads on a broken thread. They are small in number but dense in habit, each one carrying in their body the accumulated knowledge of how to exist in a place that is actively hostile to existence.

Their faces, when you can see them, are weather-eroded in a particular way. Not the deep furrowing of desert peoples, nor the squint-lines of the high-altitude snow cultures. Their wrinkles run *horizontally*—fine, parallel, pressed into the skin by years of wind dragging across their features like a file. Their eyes are narrower than lowland eyes, their cheekbones slightly higher, as if the face has been sculpted by the air itself into a shape that presents less resistance. They do not squint against the wind; they have *become* the squint.

Their hair is cropped close or, in the older women, shaved entirely and replaced with a fitted cap of woven vine-fibre, stained with the juice of a lowland berry to a deep rust. The caps are tight. The caps are practical. They keep the hair from becoming a wind-tangle.

Their hands are remarkable. Long-fingered, broad-palmed, with calluses in unusual places—not the palms, as one might expect from tool-users, but the *sides* of the fingers, where they grip the curved edges of the wrong-stone tools they still shape. They communicate primarily through a sign language so dense and rapid that to an outsider it looks like birdsong translated into gesture. Within a community, this signing is casual, continuous, as much a part of the air as the wind itself. To speak aloud in the roar is to shout into a hurricane. So they sign, and wait, and their hands become the conversation of the ridge.

## II. The Wind-Pockets

They do not live in houses. They live in *pauses*.

Each shelter they occupy—whether one of the ancient half-buried stone structures or their own low stone-and-turf additions—is chosen and modified for a single quality: the stillness it can produce. The Shal are architects of negative space. They shape the air. They build walls not to keep things out but to push the wind into eddies, into pockets, into the shaped silence behind a properly angled slab.

A Shal dwelling, from the outside, looks like a heap of stones with moss growing over it. From inside, it is a small acoustic miracle—a bubble of quiet in the roaring world, perhaps six feet across, perhaps seven. Inside, you can hear yourself breathe. Inside, you can hear another person speak at a near-normal volume. Inside, you can think.

The oldest of these pockets, the ancient shelters, are so well-tuned that the silence inside them has a *texture*—a particular hollowness, almost a resonance, as if the curved walls are not merely blocking the wind but absorbing it, drinking it down into the wrong-stone itself. The Shal say that in the deepest of the old shelters, on the stillest nights, you can hear the mountain *thinking*. They say this with their hands, and the gesture for it is small and slow and turns inward, toward the chest.

## III. The Gatherers

They are not farmers. They cannot be. The soil is too thin, the wind too constant, the seasons too confused. Instead, they are gatherers of a particular kind—harvesters of the mist-forest, collectors of the small persistent life that the wind and the moisture conspire to produce.

A Shal gatherer rises before the dawn mist has begun to clear. She—or often he; the work is shared—takes a woven net of the fine vine-fibre and a curved knife of wrong-stone, and steps out into the roar. The knife is for the fern-shoots: the young uncurling fronds of the tree-ferns, which are tender and bright and taste, when cooked in the wooden bowl over a slow fire, of something between green bean and asparagus. They are the staple. They are picked in the early morning, when they are still damp with mist, and they are eaten the same day, because they do not keep.

The mushrooms come after the rains. Pale, almost luminous, springing up overnight in the wind-pockets behind the boulders. They fruit, they are gathered within hours, they are strung on vine-cord to dry in the stillness of the shelters. Dried, they last the winter. They are the second staple. They taste of nothing and everything—a deep, earthy, slightly metallic flavor that, in the lowlands, is considered a delicacy and fetches a high price.

The moss is not eaten. The moss is gathered for other purposes. It is a poultice, a wound-dressing, a bedding material. It is also, in the right hands, a dye. The pale, almost luminous moss, when bruised and soaked in water, produces a faint blue-green liquid that stains the skin and, with the addition of certain crushed roots, becomes a permanent color. The Shal do not tattoo themselves in the lowland fashion. They *stain* themselves—light touches of the blue-green on the temples, on the backs of the hands, on the throat. The marks fade in a week. They are reapplied. They are, in a sense, a calendar.

## IV. The Carvers

The wooden bowls are famous.

Down in the lowlands, in the markets of the eastern cities and the western trading towns, a Shal bowl will fetch the price of a good milking cow. They are small—a little larger than a cupped pair of hands—and they are carved from a dark, dense wood that no one can identify. It is not the wood of any tree that grows on the Brow. It is not the wood of any tree that grows in the lowlands, either, though travelers have searched. It is a wood that must come from somewhere else—somewhere higher, some somewhere far to the north, perhaps, where the ridge continues into regions no Shal has ever visited.

The bowls are always the same shape. This is not because the Shal are unimaginative; it is because the shape is *correct*. The curve of the sides, the slight inward tilt of the rim, the smoothness of the interior—it is a shape that has been worked out over centuries, perhaps over millennia, and that fits the Shal hand the way a glove fits a hand. A bowl of any other shape would be, in their view, not a bowl at all.

The carving is done in the wind-pockets, in the stillness. The carver sits cross-legged on the sunken floor of an ancient shelter, the dark wood block across his knees, and works with a wrong-stone knife so fine-edged that it will cut a hair. He does not measure. He does not mark. He simply removes wood, slowly, slowly, until the bowl emerges. A bowl takes a season to make. Sometimes longer. The wood is hard, and the carver is patient, and the roar outside the pocket is constant.

The bowls are traded down into the lowlands, but they are not given up lightly. A bowl is, in a sense, a piece of the Shal themselves—their patience, their long listening, their particular relationship with the roar. When a bowl leaves the Brow, it goes as an emissary. The Shal say, with a gesture that is almost a shrug, that it is *going to speak for us, down where it is quiet*.

## V. The Listeners

There is a role in Shal society that has no lowland equivalent. They are called the **Veth**, though again the pronunciation is difficult. They are the listeners.

The Veth are not a priesthood. They are not shamans. They are simply people who have undertaken, usually in early adulthood, to spend a large portion of their lives in the old shelters, in the deeper wind-pockets, in the silence that has a texture. They sit for hours. They sit for days. They sit, sometimes, for weeks at a stretch, emerging only to eat and relieve themselves, returning to the stillness.

What they listen for is not entirely clear, even to the Shal themselves. Some say they are listening to the stone. Some say they are listening to the past—to the people who built the shelters, whose voices, perhaps, are still pressed into the wrong-stone the way a fossil is pressed into mud. Some say they are listening to the mountain think.

The Veth are treated with a particular kind of respect. They are not deferred to in the way priests are deferred to. They are simply *allowed*. When a Veth is sitting in a wind-pocket, no one disturbs them. When a Veth emerges and makes a particular sign—a long, slow gesture that starts at the ear and moves outward—no one asks what they heard. The sign is acknowledged, and the community adjusts its behavior accordingly, in ways that are subtle and not easily described.

Once or twice in a generation, a Veth will emerge from a long sitting and make a sign that means, roughly, *soon*. The community prepares. The speaker-vine is watched. The Long Walker is watched. The clouds are watched. Something happens. What it is, the Shal do not say, even to each other. It is simply part of the cycle of the place.

## VI. The Children

The Shal children are raised in the wind-pockets.

They are born in the stillness, and they spend their first years in the comparative quiet of the shelters. They learn to sign before they learn to walk—or rather, they learn both at once, their small hands working even as their legs are finding their balance. They are quiet children, as children go. The roar is the first thing they know, outside the body of the mother. It is the background of their lives, the way silence is the background of lowland lives.

When they are old enough—four or five, perhaps—they are taken out into the wind for the first time. The ritual is simple. An older sibling, or a parent, leads them to the lip of a wind-pocket and steps back. The child stands at the edge of the stillness and feels the air take hold of them, feel the roar press against their small body like a hand.

They are not frightened. Or if they are, they do not show it. They step forward, into the wind, and they walk.

After that, they are taught the ridge. They learn the paths between the boulders, the leeward routes, the places where the wind is merely strong and the places where it is a blade. They learn to read the clouds, the mist, the small signs of weather that the lowlander cannot parse. They learn, above all, to wait. To sit in a wind-pocket and listen to the roar and to be patient, to be still, to be ready for whatever the long slow mountain is about to say.

## VII. The Old

The Shal grow old well.

This is a strange thing to say of a people, but it is true. They do not wither in the way lowlanders do. They do not become frail and withdrawn. Instead, they become *denser*. Their wrinkles deepen. Their hands become more weathered, more precise. Their signing slows—not from weakness but from a kind of compression, as if their gestures are being packed tighter and tighter into smaller and smaller motions.

The oldest Shal are often the most active. They climb the ridge in weather that would keep a young person in the shelter. They sit in the exposed places, in the roar itself, and seem to draw strength from it. They are the ones who remember the old stories—if stories they are—and who make the long, slow signs that describe the time before, the time when the ridge was higher, the wind was different, the people were different.

They do not fear death. They have no word for it, or rather, the word they have does not translate. It is something closer to *returning to the stone*, or *going into the stillness*, or simply *finishing*. When an old Shal feels the end approaching, they ask to be carried to one of the deepest wind-pockets—the ancient shelters, the ones that have the textured silence—and laid on the sunken floor. There they lie, and the community comes to sit with them, and the signing slows and slows and finally stops, and the body remains for a time before it is taken out and laid among the boulders, where the wrong-stone reclaims it, slowly, over years.

## VIII. The Long Walker

It must be spoken of.

The Long Walker is not a Shal. The Shal are clear about this. It is something else, something that moves along the ridge in the far distances, in the haze, in the places where the Brow fades into suggestion. It is large—it is a shape, a moving shape, perhaps a single animal or perhaps a small herd—and it moves *against* the wind. Always against. This is how the Shal know it is not a natural thing, or not a wholly natural thing. Nothing natural moves against the wind on the Brow. The wind is too strong, too constant. To move against it is to assert something, to make a statement of presence.

The Shal do not hunt the Long Walker. They do not approach it. They speak of it only at certain seasons—the same seasons when the speaker-vine flowers most profusely, the seasons of *almost-time*—and then only in the signs that mean, roughly, *we are seen*. They do not say by whom. They do not say what is being seen. They simply acknowledge that the seeing is happening.

There are stories, told in the long stillness of the winter evenings, of times when the Long Walker came close. These stories are always told in signs, never in the rare whispered words that the Shal use among themselves in the deepest wind-pockets. The signs are slow, deliberate, full of pauses. They describe a shape coming out of the haze. They describe a sound—not the roar, not the wind, but something else, something low and resonant that harmonizes with the F below middle C that is the base tone of the ridge. They describe a feeling of being looked at, of being *read*, of being weighed and measured and found, if not wanting, at least acceptable.

Then the Long Walker moves on, and the haze closes behind it, and the ridge is just the ridge again, and the Shal return to their gathering and their carving and their waiting.

## IX. The Speaker-Vine

The vine is not a Shal. But it is, in a sense, a part of them.

The speaker-vine climbs everything. It climbs the shelters, the standing stones, the ancient walls. It climbs the bones of the fallen slabs. It climbs, occasionally, the wooden bowls where they are left outside in the mist—though the Shal are careful to bring them in, because a bowl that has been climbed by the vine is considered, for reasons no outsider understands, *no longer a bowl*.

The flowers of the speaker-vine are small and pale and never open fully. They always look as if they are about to say something. This is not, the Shal insist, a metaphor. The vine is *speaking*. Not in words, not in a language that can be translated into sign. But in a slow, persistent, botanical utterance that goes on all the time, that has been going on for longer than the Shal have lived on the ridge, that will go on after they are gone.

The Veth listen to the vine. They sit near it, in the wind-pockets where it grows, and they listen. Sometimes they make the long, slow sign that means *almost*. Sometimes they make the sign that means *not yet*. Sometimes they make no sign at all, and the community waits, and the vine climbs another inch, and the flowers hang unopened, and the roar goes on and on.

## X. The Patient

And so they live.

Not in the roar, exactly. Not *against* it, either. They live *within* it, the way a fish lives within water, the way a root lives within soil. The roar is their medium. The roar is the place. They have made themselves into creatures of the roar, shaped by it, tuned to it, patient as the stone itself.

They are not numerous. They will never be numerous. The ridge will not support more than a few hundred, and the wind will not allow them to become anything other than what they are. They are gatherers. They are carvers. They are listeners. They are a quiet people in a loud place, a patient people in a place that moves against all patience, a people who have learned, over centuries, perhaps over millennia, to wait for the moment when the vine will open, when the stone will speak, when the Long Walker will come close enough to be seen clearly.

They wait. They sign. They sit in the shaped silence of their wind-pockets and they listen, and they prepare, slowly, slowly, to hear what the mountain is about to say.
