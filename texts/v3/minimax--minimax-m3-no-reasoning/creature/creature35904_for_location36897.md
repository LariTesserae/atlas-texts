The dwellers are thin, as if the air had slowly taught them to make less of themselves. Tall in the way of an extension rather than a stature — long limbs, narrow chests, heads that seem to have been set on the shoulders more as a courtesy than a requirement. Their skin is the color of the dust itself, or the dust is the color of their skin; the difference was settled generations ago and has not been revisited. They keep, in each large almost-dark eye, a small visible vein, so that when they look at you in the copper light you can see, if you are patient, the slow peristalsis of their seeing.

They drink what falls from the inside of the chimneys at dawn. In the deep cold of the morning a faint condensation gathers along the upper stone of the long unused flues, and they collect it in shallow pans of the same dark material — not much, a mouthful to a household in a good week — and they do this without complaint, the way one does anything here, as part of the long practice of being where one is. Some of them, the old ones, claim to remember rain. The young ones do not know what to do with this word and so use it sparingly, only in songs.

Their food is the small creatures. This is part of the arrangement, though which side of the arrangement it falls on is one of the small polite ambiguities the dwellers have agreed not to press. The dust-colored drifters are taken in cupped handfuls and eaten raw, or crumbled into a paste with the collected chimney-water; they taste, the dwellers say, of patience and of stone, and one should not ask for a fuller description. The tall jointed browsers are not eaten, but their discarded whisker-sheaths are gathered and woven into a rough cloth the color of weak tea, which is the only cloth anyone wears. The great slow ones — the house-sized ones, the ones that resolve out of one position into another over the course of a season — are not eaten, not touched, not approached. They are, however, listened to. When one of them sounds its single resonant note, the dwellers stop what they are doing and wait, the way one waits for a door to be opened by someone whose hand is already on the latch. They do not know why. They have not known why for a very long while. It is, they have decided, not the kind of thing one needs to know in order to do it.

There are perhaps three thousand of them now, by their own quiet count, though counting here is something done less often than elsewhere, and the numbers drift the way the dust drifts, settling one place and lifting another. They live in the central tier, where the buildings are tallest and closest together; the outer reaches they leave to the slow ones and to the cairns, which are themselves, they say, the older of them, and not in a hurry. They move through the city on the narrow rails when the rails will carry them — clicking, slow, the cars little more than stone trays on wheels — and on foot when the cars are sleeping, or meditating, or doing whatever the cars do in the long hours between their purposeful intervals. They speak softly, and not often. They have a language, and the fragments of three others, and the language they use most is the oldest; new things, when they must be named at all, are coaxed into the old words, even when the meaning has to be stretched to do it.

Children are born rarely. They are born small and they grow slowly, and they are carried often by the older ones, partly from affection and partly because walking tires them in the thin air before they are tall enough to manage it well. They are taught the names of the creatures — all of them, including the great ones, whose names are not so much words as recordings of where each was last heard. They are taught to sit on parapets. They are taught to listen for the hum, which they are told comes from the city's oldest part, which is also the city's heart, which is also, in some quiet way they will not be asked to understand for many years, themselves. They are taught to be pleased with the place. Most of them learn this without effort.

The old ones are the most plentiful of all. The very old ones — and there are some who are very old indeed, who move with the deliberation of the great creatures and who are addressed by the others with a deference so gentle it could almost be mistaken for indifference — sit in the latter parts of their lives in doorways and on ledges and in the hollow places where chimneys meet the wall, and they do very little. They watch the dust settle. They hear the rails click. They feel the hum, which they have been feeling for so long that it has become, in them, less a sound than a posture. Sometimes one of them, in the late afternoon, will begin a story in a language no one else in the room knows, and the others will sit and listen to the sound of it without the sense, and this is considered a perfectly good way to pass the time. When an old one finally stops sitting in doorways, the small drifters come and do what small drifters do, and the others watch without grief, the way one watches the dust resettle after the wind has passed through. The drifters are part of the arrangement. The arrangement is not discussed.

There are no leaders. There is no one in charge of the hum, no one tending the rails, no one whose work it is to keep the city from drifting back into the dust. The dwellers have, over a long time, come to feel that no such tending is required — that the city is doing what the city is doing, and that they are inside it the way a thought is inside a mind, and that to take charge of the mind would be a strange and graceless thing to do. The hum continues. The rails continue. The creatures continue. The dwellers continue, in the quiet way of things that have decided to be here and have not yet been given a reason to revisit the decision.

If you were to sit with one of them — and they would let you, although it would take them a long time to let you, and the long time is part of what they would be offering — and if you were, after a while, to ask them what they were waiting for, they would not be able to tell you. They would look at you with those large almost-dark eyes and the slow vein, and they would consider the question with the patience of the place, and they would say, eventually, something like: *nothing*. Or perhaps: *whatever comes next*. They would not seem troubled by this. They would seem, if anything, pleased — pleased that you had asked, pleased that the question had taken a shape, pleased that the answering of it could be done, like everything here, in its own good time. And if, in the pause that followed, you heard, somewhere far off in the dusk, a single resonant note from a creature the size of a small house, they would lift one long hand and hold it there, palm outward, not to silence you but to share the listening, and the note would hang in the thin air for a moment the way a struck anvil rings, once, faintly, and then not again for weeks.
