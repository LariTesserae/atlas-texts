# The Inhabitants of the Tracheophyte Reach

There is no clean boundary between person and substrate here, and the inhabitants would be the first to tell you that this is not a metaphor. They have a word for it—*lignification*, though it does not mean what your botanists mean by it. To lignify, to a Parenchyman, is to become more truly oneself by becoming more truly part of the wood. They speak of it the way coastal peoples speak of going to sea. It is a way of being in the world. It is not, they insist, a diminishment.

To describe them, you must begin by setting down your expectation of the individual as a sealed envelope of skin. The Parenchymans are persons, yes. They walk, they speak, they have names. They fall in love and they grieve and they hold grudges with the slow, settled patience of people who have nowhere else to go. But each of them is also, continuously, in negotiation with a larger system that they tend and that tends them in return. The small capillaries of their skin are more than capillaries; they are conduits. The dark amber fluid of the substrate, in trace amounts, moves through them. When a Parenchyman sleeps on the wood, as they often do, they are not merely resting on a surface. They are, for a few hours, a peripheral organ of a nine-million-year-old body that is, technically, a continent.

Their skin has the color and the faint grain of aged boxwood. It is not a coincidence. Generations of life lived among trees that exhale terpenes and shed a fine reactive dust have, over evolutionary time, encouraged the development of a dermal chemistry that is more vegetable than animal. A Parenchyman in good health has a faint smell of green wood and warm resin, and in the cool air of the Reach, this smell gathers around them like a low personal atmosphere. Their hair, when they have it, tends to be fine and pale, the color of young xylem, and it grows in patterns that are not quite the patterns your anthropologists would expect. Some of them cultivate it into long trailing vines, deliberately encouraged to twine with small living ornaments that they wear in it—sprigs of phosphorescent mycelium, dried seed-pods, the occasional shed feather of a laminator.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Every adult Parenchyman has hands that are slightly asymmetric, the fingers longer on one side than the other, and the palms marked with deep creases that are not the random creases of your species but are instead specific, named, hereditary. A Parenchyman can read another Parenchyman's palm the way you might read a face: the long crease that runs from the base of the little finger to the heel of the palm is the *flow-line*, and it is believed to indicate the character of a person's relationship to the substrate's fluid dynamics; the shorter crease across the root of the thumb is the *pith-crease*, and it is read for temperament. A palm-reader here is not a fortuneteller. She is a kind of structural diagnostician, and her observations are taken seriously by everyone, including the engineers, who will sometimes consult her before making a major pruning to one of the great trees.

They live a long time. Not immortal—death is common, accident is common, the stilters are a hazard and the blooms are occasionally worse—but the natural lifespan of an uninjured Parenchyman is around two hundred and twenty local years, and there are a handful of elders in Parenchyma who are older than the town's written records. These elders are not venerated as saints. They are simply present, the way a very old tree is present—unhurried, listening, slightly more connected to the substrate than younger people, and considered useful in deliberations because of this. They are the town's memory, but they are not its only memory; the wood remembers, too, in its own way, and the elders are the people who have learned to ask it the right questions.

They eat wood. This is the fact that visitors find most difficult. Their diet is primarily the soft inner cambium of certain cultivated trees, fermented for weeks in sealed vessels until it develops a complex, faintly sweet, faintly sour flavor that they compare, when pressed, to a kind of cheese. They eat the clock-beetles, salted, as a snack. They eat the spore-paste that the trees exude in season, which is bland and filling and has, they claim, mild consciousness-altering properties if eaten in large quantities, though they do not recommend this. They drink the dark amber fluid in small doses, diluted with water that they condense from the air in cool hours using broad leaf-fans placed on the windward sides of the great trunks. Their water is precious. Their resin-wine is precious. Their food is mostly wood, and they are not ashamed of this. They are, in fact, quietly proud of it. To eat wood is to take part in the cycle. To refuse would be, in their view, a kind of violence.

They speak a language that has no word for *alone*.

They have words for *solitude*, certainly, and for *privacy*, and for the particular melancholy of being in a crowd of people you do not know. But the word *alone*, in the sense of *fundamentally without connection to anything else*, has no direct translation. The closest phrase, when pressed, is something like *wood-far*, which is what you say of a seed that has not yet germinated. It is not a positive term. A Parenchyman who is *wood-far* is, in the deepest sense, unfinished. They will grow out of it. They will, one way or another, be joined to the larger system of kinship and obligation that is the true substance of their lives. This is not a spiritual claim. It is, to them, simply the way things are. You are kin to the wood, or you are not yet kin, and if you are not yet kin you are a kind of potential, a seed, and you will be tended.

Their politics are incomprehensible to outsiders and not very interesting to them. There is no state, exactly. There are engineers, who tend the substrate and the great trees and who make the slow collective decisions about when to prune and where to plant and how to maintain the doorway. There are tenders, who care for the cultivated trees that the townspeople live in and on and whose walls they sleep within. There are readers, who can listen to the wood and interpret it, and who serve as a kind of distributed memory for the town. There are what they call *the older ones*, the elders, who are consulted when a decision has effects that will outlive any single person. And there is a loose assembly of all adult townspeople, held in the market hall on certain evenings, where anyone may speak and where the business is conducted at a pace that would make your legislatures weep with envy or frustration.

They have no war. They have had no war for as long as their oral history extends, which is a statement that contains within it the implication that there may once have been war, but the records are not clear and they do not press the question. They have disputes. They have feuds. They have the occasional slow-burning resentment that is nursed across decades and that everyone knows about and that no one will speak of directly. They have a custom, when a dispute cannot be resolved, of sending the disputants together up into the callus on the Index tree, where they sit in the amber light and the cool air and listen to the substrate think, and where, more often than not, one of them will begin to weep, and the other will put an arm around them, and they will come down reconciled. They say that the tree does this. They say that the tree *helps*. Whether this is literally true or whether it is a story they tell about a kind of cognitive shift that occurs at altitude and in the presence of the amber light is not something they are interested in disentangling.

Their art is made of wood and grown into being. They do not carve. They do not paint. They grow. A Parenchyman poet will spend a decade training a single panel of living cambium to grow in a pattern that, when read by a contact probe, will yield a sequence of tones that the listener experiences as a kind of slow song. A Parenchyman painter will grow a wall in such a way that its grain, when the light from the mycelium-lamps strikes it at a particular hour, appears to depict a face, or a tree, or the surface of the substrate seen from above. These works are not permanent. They continue to grow after the artist is dead. Some of them become more beautiful with age. Some of them become unrecognizable. The artists, it is said, do not mind. The art is not the image. The art is the act of tending, and the image is a kind of gift the living wood gives back to the tender, and the gift is not required to last.

Their funerals are a kind of grafting. When a Parenchyman dies, if they have lived a full life and have been tended well and have loved and have been loved in return, their body is not buried or burned. It is carefully, lovingly, pressed into a fissure in the substrate, in a place where the dark amber fluid runs close to the surface, and it is left there. The wood absorbs it. The wood incorporates the body's organic compounds into its own structure, and over the course of a season or a year the place where the body was laid becomes a slight rise in the bark, a small rounded dome, warmer than the surrounding plates, and in the center of the dome, if the person's kin have been attentive and have done the small rituals correctly, a new growth appears. A shoot. A sapling. The person's name is added to the long list of the tended-dead, the ones who have become, in the truest sense, part of the wood. The kin tend the sapling as it grows. They prune it. They speak to it. They do not, as a rule, expect it to answer, though there is a story—everyone knows the story, no one claims to have witnessed it—of a sapling that grew, over the course of a single night, into the exact shape of the dead person's face, and then grew further, and became a new limb of a great tree, and was still, they say, recognizably the person, in some way that no one could quite describe.

They do not worship the wood. They tend it. The distinction matters to them, though they will not press it on a visitor. They do not believe the wood is a god. They do not believe the wood is conscious, exactly, though they are not certain what consciousness is, and they are open to the possibility that the substrate's behavior, in aggregate, across nine million years of slow distributed processing, may constitute something that is at least analogous to thought. They are, in their quiet and settled way, *agnostic* about the wood, in the literal sense of the word: they do not know, and they have made a practice of not needing to know. They tend the wood. The wood tends them. The arrangement works. They do not require it to be more than it is, and they do not require it to be less.

They love the town of Parenchyma. They love it the way you might love a body, or a parent, or a self. They know its flaws—the street outside the market hall is uneven, and has been uneven for as long as anyone can remember, because the underlying root has grown in a way that the engineers cannot, or will not, redirect; the eastern quarter is warmer than the western quarter in the cool hours, and this is a minor grievance that flares up in the assemblies every few years and is never quite resolved; the mycelium-lamps in the upper rooms sometimes fail to bioluminesce on schedule, and no one knows why. They love the town despite these flaws. They love it through them. They love it the way you love a thing that is alive and that is, like all living things, imperfect in ways that are specific to it and that no other place shares.

They are, on the whole, kind. Not in the way that you are kind, with your sudden bursts of charity and your long periods of inattention. They are kind the way the wood is kind, which is to say: slowly, continuously, and without the expectation of acknowledgment. They are kind to the clock-beetles, which they eat. They are kind to the great trees, which they prune. They are kind to the stilters, which occasionally kill a child, and which they do not hunt to extinction because the stilters are part of the cycle, and the cycle, they believe, is more important than any single life, including their own. This is not a comfortable belief. It is not meant to be. It is the kind of belief that can only be held by people who have lived with the consequences of it for a very long time, and who have decided, again and again, that the alternative is worse.

They are afraid of the doorway. They maintain it. They are polite to its visitors. They do not go through it. They do not know what is on the other side, and they are not certain they want to know. They have, over the centuries, developed a small literature of speculation—quiet, late-night conversations in the upper rooms, gentle debates in the market hall—but no consensus has ever emerged, and no one has ever been appointed to find out. The doorway is a fact. The visitors are a fact. The rest is, for now, a question they have chosen to leave open.

They do not sleep the way you sleep. They sleep in cycles that are tied to the substrate's hum, and a Parenchyman who is out of phase with the hum—a person who has been ill, or grieving, or newly arrived from elsewhere—will sleep poorly, fitfully, with bad dreams. The town's rhythm is keyed to the hum. In the late hours, when the hum is at its lowest pitch, the town is quiet and the amber light from the Index tree is at its softest and the mycelium-lamps have dimmed to a faint green glow. This is when the Parenchymans sleep, and they sleep deeply, and they dream, and in the morning—when the morning comes, which is more a decision than an event—they wake rested and ready to begin again.

They do not have a word for *week*. They have a word for *cycle*, which is the time it takes for the substrate's hum to return to its starting pitch, which is approximately eighteen of your hours. They organize their lives in cycles rather than days, and the town's assemblies, the schools, the work of the tenders and the engineers, all run on a cycle-based schedule. Visitors from elsewhere find this disorienting at first, and then, often, oddly comfortable. There is something restful about a schedule that is not tied to the arbitrary alternation of light and dark but to the slow breathing of a thing that has been breathing for nine million years.

They are not, in any meaningful sense, *your* kind of people. They are not even each other's kind of people, in the way that you mean; each of them is a slightly different negotiation between an individual body and a much larger system, and no two of them are quite alike. But they are *a* people. They have names for each other. They have stories. They have music, and art, and food, and funerals, and feuds, and reconciliations, and the slow patient work of tending a thing that is larger than any of them and that none of them would, on the whole, want to be without.

They are, in the end, the way the wood is: alive, and old, and quiet, and still becoming.
