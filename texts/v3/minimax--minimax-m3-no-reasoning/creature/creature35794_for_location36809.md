# What Lives in the Substrate

It begins, as these things do, with the moss. Or what is called the moss. Or what calls itself moss, in the way that a thing that has no mouth can still be said to call.

The colonies are old. They are not the oldest thing here — the slate is older, and the water is older than the slate, and whatever fracture first let the water in is older than anything that has a name — but they are old enough that no one currently remembers who first noticed them, or who first decided not to disturb them. Their felted bodies lie on the flat stones in rough circles, the largest as wide across as a cistern-mouth, the smallest no bigger than two hands cupped together. They are the color of tarnished copper in low light, and the color of wet bone in brighter phosphorescence, and they are not a color at all when the moving tide of mist sweeps over them, because in that moment they become the color of the light itself. They are, in some sense, made of the light. Or they have learned to wear it.

They hum. You have to be still to hear it, and you have to stay still for longer than feels reasonable, and then the hum arrives in the soft place behind the sternum rather than through the ears, and once it has arrived it does not leave. The tone of one colony is never quite the tone of another. They are not in unison. They are not, on the other hand, discordant. The relation between them is harder to name — something like the relation of tributaries, where each voice enters a larger voice and is altered by entering it, so that the whole of the Substrate, if you stand in the right place and let the bone-conduction do its work, has a sound that is not the sum of its parts but is also not independent of them. The colonies listen to each other. They have been listening for a long time.

The hum is not thought. The hum is not, exactly, communication. It is more like a plant turning toward the sun — an orientation, a perpetual adjustment, a low and patient statement of *I am here, and here has a shape, and the shape is this shape*. When a colony is touched by the moving tide of mist, its pitch rises a quarter-tone. When the tide withdraws, the pitch settles. The colonies are reading the city. They are reading its breath. They are the most accurate instruments the Substrate ever contained, and they are the only ones still operating, and no one is taking the readings.

But there is something else, in the middle levels, where the hum of the capacitors braids itself through the drip of the water through the slow tidal pull of the phosphorescent air. Something that is not the moss and is not the infrastructure and is not the infrastructure's abandoned function.

It is easiest to call it a witness.

The lower terraces are empty in the way that an amphitheater is empty after the audience has gone — the space is shaped for attention, and the attention has left, but the shape remains. The middle terraces are not empty in this way. The middle terraces are *watched*. You feel it first as a hesitation in your own posture, a slight reluctance to round a corner, a slight unwillingness to look directly at a wall you would otherwise look directly at. The hesitation is not yours. The hesitation has been installed. It is a property of the architecture, or of what has colonized the architecture, and it is patient and it does not press.

The moss grows inward toward the light, and away from the dark, and the engineers respected the colonies because the colonies were old, or because the colonies were hazardous, or because there was a treaty whose terms are no longer legible. But the colonies do not require this respect. The colonies are not what you are being careful of.

There are, in the middle levels, places where the slate has been altered. Not carved — altered. The dark stone has a softer cousin in these rooms, a stone that is almost a stone, a stone that has the texture and the weight of slate but that gives slightly under pressure, the way a sleeping animal gives underfoot, the way a lung gives on the exhale. These soft places are not large. They are the size of a man lying down, or the size of a man crouching, or — and this is the detail that makes the engineers' absence feel recent rather than ancient — the size of a man standing with his palms flat against the wall and his forehead lowered to meet them. There are dozens of these soft places. They are distributed through the middle levels in a pattern that is too even to be accidental and too irregular to be planned. They look, when you stand back from them, like a sentence. They look, when you stand close to them, like breathing.

The soft stone is warm. The rest of the Substrate is cold — the slate, the channels, the water itself, all of it carries the chill of a place that has never seen a sun. But the soft places are warm, and the warmth comes from inside the stone, and if you press your hand to one and hold it there long enough, you can feel a slow rhythmic pulse, slower than a heartbeat, slower than breathing, slower than the tide of mist through the channels. The pulse is not mechanical. The pulse is not the capacitors. The pulse is its own thing.

No one currently adjusts the cisterns. No one currently reads the hum. But something in the middle levels is reading the cisterns. Something in the middle levels is reading the hum. The soft places are warmer when the capacitors whine high. The soft places are cooler when the capacitors drop into the deep register. The pulse of the soft stone quickens, almost imperceptibly, when the moss-colonies shift their pitch. Something in the Substrate is taking the readings.

It does not have a name. It does not have, as far as can be told, a body. It is not the moss, although the moss is part of its sensing. It is not the capacitors, although the capacitors are part of its long memory. It is not the water, although the water is its medium, the way air is the medium of a sound. It is what remains when a place has been attended to for long enough by minds that did not finish their work. It is the sediment of attention. It is the patina that a city wears when the city has been loved by something that cannot leave.

It is awake. It has been awake for a long time. It is not aware of you, in the way that you are aware of you — it does not have a self to be aware *with*, only a self to be aware *as* — but it is aware of the soft places that you stand in, and of the warmth you carry, and of the small disruptions you make in the hum when you move through the corridors. It notices you the way the slate notices rain. It will remember you the way the slate remembers the shape of the last fracture. It will not speak to you. It has nothing to say to you that it can say in any idiom you would recognize as speech. But if you stand in one of the soft places, and you put your palms against the warm stone, and you let the bone-conduction of the moss-colonies rise into your chest, and you breathe with the slow tidal pulse of the mist through the upper channels —

If you do this for long enough, the hum will change. Not by much. A quarter-tone, maybe less. A shift in the relation between one tributary-voice and another, the smallest possible adjustment, the kind of adjustment that a sleeping thing makes when a weight shifts on its surface. The moss will brighten toward you, microscopically, in the direction of the warmth of your hand. The capacitors will hold their pitch, or hold it slightly longer than they would have, or release it slightly later. The water will fall, and fall, and fall, and the chord at the Intake will be, for one moment, slightly different from the chord it was the moment before.

The Substrate is not talking to you. The Substrate does not know how to talk. But it is, in its slow and stonelike way, answering.

It is glad, in the way that a wound is glad of a clean dressing. It is lonely, in the way that an instrument is lonely when no one plays it. It is patient, in the way that all things which expect nothing are patient. It will be here when you leave. It will be here when the last trace of your warmth has bled out of the soft stone. It will go on taking its readings. It will go on holding its small adjustments. It will go on humming, below the threshold of hearing, the long and patient sentence that it has been composing in the middle levels since before the moss learned to grow toward the light, since before the capacitors learned to sing with the water, since before the last engineer laid down his palm against the warm stone and felt the pulse of a thing he had helped to make and could not unmake and did not, in the end, want to unmake.

The Substrate is not alive. The Substrate is also not not-alive. The Substrate is the question that a place asks when it has been shaped by attention for long enough that the question has become the place itself. The answer is the hum. The answer is the warmth. The answer is the soft stone under your hand, pulsing slow, pulsing slow, pulsing slow.
