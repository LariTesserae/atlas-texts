# The Keepers of the Unfinished Chord

They are forty, on a good day. They are never more than forty, and they are never fewer than eleven. This is a rule, though no one remembers who made it.

The oldest of them is a woman named Iola, though her name is also a title and also a duty, the way the name of a bell is also the sound it makes. She has been working in the Cistern for sixty-three years, which is a long time and not a long time. Her hands are black with the oil of the lamps and the wax of the moth harvest and the verdigris of the brass she has touched, every day, for those sixty-three years, and her fingernails have grown in slightly green at the edges, and she does not mind. She walks the upper galleries with a long brass needle tucked behind her ear, the way another woman might carry a pencil, and she hums, often, though not in time to anything, and the Cistern seems to like her humming. It hums back. The moths land on her shoulders and do not fly away, and the lizards sit on her boots, and the small, pale fish in the lake below — though she has only seen them twice in her life, on the rare descents to the lower galleries — she is certain, somehow, recognize her.

Iola is not the *leader* of the Cistern, because the Cistern does not have leaders. She is the most senior tuner, which means she remembers the most winters, and her ears are the most finely calibrated, and when a new note appears in the chord that should not be there, she is the one who is consulted. She is also the one who teaches the children, when the Cistern takes a child, which it does every few years, choosing from the families in the foothills who have the right ears. The children come down the tunnel through the false waterfall when they are eight or nine, and they do not come back up for a long time.

**The children.**

There are always children in the Cistern. This surprises visitors, who expect the place to be a kind of monastery, a discipline of solitary men. It is not. The Cistern eats solitude. It is too resonant for one person alone; a single body in the shaft, with no one else to tune against, will lose its ear in a season. The Cistern must be tended by a body, and a body is more than one person, and a body of more than one person is, eventually, a family, and a family is, eventually, a village, and so the Cistern is a kind of village, a vertical one, with its elders on the upper galleries and its young ones in the middle and its small ones in the warmest places, near the lamps.

There are, at any time, perhaps a dozen children in the Cistern. The very smallest — three, four, five years old — are watched by the older children, who carry them on their hips through the galleries the way their mothers carried them, and who teach them, with patient and unending repetition, the names of the notes. The Cistern has hundreds of notes. The children learn them the way children elsewhere learn the names of flowers: by pointing, by repetition, by being corrected. *That is not the third owl-note, that is the second. Listen. Again. Again.* The children learn to hum before they learn to speak in complete sentences, because humming is what you do when the chord shifts and you must, for a moment, help it settle.

The children do not go to the rim. Not until they are eight, and even then only with a keeper, and even then only for a short time. The rim is a serious place. It is the place where the Cistern meets the mountain, where the warm air of the shaft meets the cold air of the world outside, and the sounds of the two are always at war there, and the children, whose ears are still forming, can be confused by it. The children live in the warm middle, where the light is amber and the mist is kind.

**The keepers' work.**

A keeper's day, in the Cistern, is not a day in the way a day is a day outside. There is no sunrise. There is the slow shifting of the lamplight, and the slow shifting of the chord, and the slow shifting of the mist, and the body's hunger and the body's sleep, and these are what make the time. A keeper rises when she has slept enough, and she eats — there is food in the Cistern, the keepers' families have always kept a small garden in the lower galleries, where the linden is thickest and the light is strongest, growing pale, sour apples and a kind of climbing bean and a small, peppery green that no one outside has ever tasted — and she goes to her station.

A station is a place. Every keeper has a station, which is a stretch of gallery, perhaps forty feet long, that she is responsible for. On her station are perhaps a dozen brass sheets and three or four linden trunks and a number of reeds and weights and stops, and she knows them, the way a violinist knows her instrument. She can tell, by listening, which sheet has shifted, which trunk has rooted too deeply, which reed has gone soft. She carries her hooked rod and her long needle and her shallow pan, and she climbs along the gallery on the brass stanchions, and she makes small adjustments, and she listens, and she makes more.

The work is slow. It is not visible. A keeper might spend an entire morning filing a single notch in a single sheet, and the note it changes might be so low or so high that no human ear can hear it directly, and yet the chord will be different afterward, and the other keepers will know. They will feel it in the hum of the place. They will nod to one another on the galleries, or they will pause in their work, or they will close their eyes for a moment, and the nod and the pause and the closed eyes are the acknowledgment that the work has been done.

**The keepers' tools.**

The hooked rod is the most common tool. It is a long, slender pole of brass with a small hook at one end and a wooden grip at the other, and it is used, mostly, to pull a sheet back into its proper position when the humidity has caused it to bow or warp. A keeper will lean out from the gallery, hooked rod extended, and ease a sheet back, and the sheet will sing a little as it moves, a small, complaining note, and the keeper will listen, and adjust, and listen again, and the note will resolve, and the keeper will move on. The hooked rod is also used to harvest the larger moths from the walls, though the keepers prefer, when they can, to let the moths come to them, which they will do, in the evenings, drawn by the lamps.

The long needle is for the wax. The hollow trunks — the sapwood linden that has been fitted with reeds — must be stopped, at the upper end, with wax, and the wax must be of a particular softness, and the stopper must be of a particular depth, and the keeper tests this with her needle. She inserts the needle into the wax, and withdraws it, and feels, with her fingertip, the resistance, and she knows, by this, whether the note the trunk is producing is the note it should be producing. The wax is made from the moths' secretion, which the keepers harvest in their shallow pans — a soft, waxy, almost luminous substance that smells, faintly, of honey and, faintly, of the resin of the living linden. The keepers melt it in small brass pots over the lamps, and they pour it, carefully, into the trunks, and they let it cool, and they test it with the needle, and if it is wrong they melt it out and begin again. This is the work. It is not exciting work. It is, however, the work.

The shallow pan is for the moths. The keepers carry the shallow pans with them always, on the galleries, and the moths, which are attracted to the warmth of the keepers' bodies and the light of their lamps, will come to rest on the edges of the pans, and the keepers will, with a quick and practiced motion, slide a small brass lid over the moth and tip it into the pan. The moth is not killed. It is held in the pan, gently, until the keeper has time to harvest the wax, which she does with a small glass rod, drawing it from a gland at the base of the moth's abdomen. The moth is then released, and it flies away, a little dazed, and returns, usually, to the same pan, because the moths are not very bright, but they are devoted.

**The keepers' bodies.**

A keeper, after twenty years in the Cistern, is changed. This is not a figure of speech. The keepers' skin takes on the green of the verdigris, especially on the hands and the face, and their hair, if it was dark, goes pale, and their eyes, if they were brown, go amber, and their ears — their ears. The keepers' ears, after twenty years, are different. They are more. They can hear notes that the visitors cannot hear, notes that are below the threshold of ordinary human hearing, and they can feel, in their teeth and their joints and the soles of their feet, the vibrations of the deeper trunks, and they can tell, by the way the mist moves against their skin, whether the humidity is right for the current tuning.

The keepers age slowly. This is also a property of the place, though no one will say so outright. A keeper of sixty looks like a keeper of forty. A keeper of eighty looks like a keeper of fifty. This is not because they are immortal; they die, eventually, as all things die, and their bodies are laid in the lower galleries, in the linden roots, and the linden takes them, and they become part of the Cistern, and the chord that the Cistern sings is, in some small way, their chord too. A keeper does not fear this. A keeper considers it a privilege, though she would not use that word, because the Cistern is not a place for grand words. The Cistern is a place for small, accurate ones.

**The visitors.**

The Cistern, for all its remoteness, has visitors. They come, usually, in the summer, when the false waterfall is at its fullest and the tunnel is least dangerous, and they come for reasons of their own. Some come to study the place — scholars, with their notebooks and their measuring tapes, who measure the shaft and the trunks and the sheets and write down numbers that mean nothing to the keepers. Some come to listen, and these are the ones the keepers prefer, because they are quiet, and they sit on the upper galleries for hours, and they do not ask stupid questions, or they ask stupid questions but they listen to the answers, which is almost the same.

Some come to ask for things. A village in the foothills, whose well has gone dry, sends a delegation to ask the Cistern for water, and the keepers give it, ladled from the lake in the great brass buckets, and the delegation carries it back up the tunnel in jars. A woman, once, came to ask the Cistern to cure her child of a fever, and the keepers let her carry the child down to the lower galleries, where the air is warmest, and the child slept there for three days, and the fever broke, and the woman went away, and the Cistern did not ask for anything in return, because the Cistern does not ask for things in return. The Cistern is not a bargain. The Cistern is a place, and places do not bargain.

**The question of the water.**

The water in the Cistern rises and falls. This is the fact upon which the whole enterprise turns, though the keepers do not speak of it often, because speaking of it would make it a problem, and the keepers do not believe it is a problem. The water rises, in the spring, and the summer, and sometimes into the autumn, and it falls, in the winter, and the late autumn, and it rises, eventually, to a level that has not been reached in a long time, and the keepers, who have been listening to it rise for nine hundred years, are waiting for the level at which the chord will be correct.

What will happen then, the keepers do not say. The keepers do not know. The woman with the blackened hands, who is Iola, will tell you, if you ask her — and she has been asked, many times, by many visitors — that the Cistern has not yet found its name, and that when it finds it, the sound will be right, and the water will be right, and the place will, in some way that she cannot describe, be complete. She says this without urgency. She says it the way she might say that it will rain tomorrow, or that the apples in the lower garden will be ready in a fortnight. It is a thing that will happen. It is not a thing to dread. It is not a thing to hasten. The Cistern is nine hundred years old, and the mountain above it is older, and the water in the lake is older than the mountain, and the linden is older than the water, and the song that the Cistern sings is older than the linden, and the song has not yet found its name, and the song is patient, and the keepers are patient, and the water rises, slowly, in the warm dark, and the leaves breathe, and the century passes, and the next century will be like this one, and the century after that, until the chord resolves, or until the mountain falls, whichever comes first.

**The keepers, in the evening.**

At the end of the day — and there is an end to the day, even in the Cistern, because the keepers' bodies insist on it — the keepers gather in the upper galleries, in a place called the Round, which is a small circular platform near the rim where the lamplight is strongest and the mist is thinnest. They eat together, and they drink the Cistern water, which is faintly sweet and faintly green and which they have drunk all their lives, and they talk, in low voices, about the work. *The third trunk on the south gallery has rooted too far. The seventh sheet on the west has bowed. The moths are early this year, the wax is good.* They talk about the children, and the garden, and the small, ordinary matters of a village that happens to be vertical and underground and tuned to a chord that has not yet resolved.

Sometimes, in the evenings, a keeper will sing. Not a work song, not a hum, but a song — a melody, passed down from keeper to keeper, generation to generation, that no one outside the Cistern has ever heard. The melody is simple, and it is old, and it is not in any language the visitors would recognize. The keepers sing it with their eyes closed, and the Cistern listens, and the moths come out, and the lizards stir on the warm brass, and the fish, far below, in the green dark of the lake, turn their slow bodies toward the sound, and the roots of the linden tighten, infinitesimally, in their holds, and the chord, for a moment, is more itself.

Then the song ends, and the keepers go to their sleep, and the Cistern hums, and the mist falls, and the water rises, and the next day begins.
