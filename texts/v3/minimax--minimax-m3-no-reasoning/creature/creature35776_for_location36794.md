# The Ones Who Live in the Lung of the World

## The Hangflies

They were here first. Everyone agrees on this, including the hangflies, who do not agree on anything because agreeing would require a concept they have never developed, which is *concept*.

A hangfly at rest looks like a piece of torn membrane hung on a hook. A hangfly in motion looks like a small, frantic stained-glass window that has come loose from a cathedral and is trying to find its way back. The body is the color of weak tea, and the four wings are transparent except where they have been reinforced, over evolutionary time, with a lattice of pigmented veins that serve no aerodynamic purpose and are, in fact, *decoration* — the only known example of aesthetic evolution in a creature that has no eyes in the vertebrate sense, only a constellation of light-sensitive pits along the leading edge of each wing, pits that do not see *images* so much as they *weigh* the brightness that falls on them, like a shopkeeper weighing cheese on a scale. A hangfly can tell you that the dawn is brighter than the dusk. It cannot tell you what dawn looks like. It does not care. It has better things to weigh, and the better things are the droplets.

It eats the way a desert eats rain. The body is a pelt of fine, hydrophilic hair, and each hair is a tube, and each tube ends in a mouth the size of a punctuation mark, and there are *forty thousand* mouths on a single hangfly, distributed across the ventral surface in rows that follow the lines of stress in the wing membrane when the wings are folded. When the mist comes — and the mist is always coming, the mist is the only weather this high — the hangfly does not seek it. The hangfly simply *presents*, hanging mouth-downward from the underside of a cambium bridge, and the mist enters the mouths the way a congregation enters a church, slowly, in ones and twos, and is conducted down through the hair-tubes into a series of internal bladders that are not stomachs in the digestive sense but *cisterns*, holding the water in suspension against a mesh of salt crystals that the hangfly itself secretes and that the water cannot pass through until the water has given up its dissolved carbon, its trace nitrogen, its faint mineral freight from whatever unseen aquifer the mist was once drawn from. The water then passes. The minerals do not. The minerals stay. The hangfly is, in this sense, *mining the air*, and the air is being mined of substances so sparse that no larger animal would bother with them, and the hangfly is not bothered, because the hangfly is not bothered by anything, having no central nervous system to speak of, only a loose net of ganglion-clusters strung between the wing-roots like Christmas lights left up too long.

A hangfly lives for **eleven months**, which is an obscenity. Eleven months of hanging. Eleven months of being batted by the wind, of having its wings frayed at the edges by the constant low roar of the fronds, of being soaked and dried and soaked again in the daily cycle of mist and the rarer cycle of true rain. At the end of the eleven months, something happens that is, to use a word I am not sure the hangfly earns, *sexy*. The whole colony — and a colony can be a hundred thousand individuals strung along a single bridge like a beaded curtain — releases a pheromone into the mist, and the pheromone is a *time-signal*, and every hangfly within range reads the time-signal and within the same three-hour window *detaches* from its perch and begins to fly, which is the only time in its life it flies, because for eleven months it has been hanging, and now it is not hanging, and the not-hanging is a kind of ecstasy, or would be if the hangfly had a word for ecstasy, which it does not, but if it did, the word would be the same as the word for *dying*, because the flying is also the mating, and the mating is also the dying.

They rise. They rise into the braid of the crowns where the fronds are loudest, and they rise *into* the roar, and the roar shreds them — softly, the way a cheese-grater shreds a soft cheese, a flake at a time — and the shredding releases a fine pollen-equivalent from the vents along their abdomens, and the pollen falls back down through the crowns and settles on the fronds, and the fronds absorb it, and the fronds *use* it, in ways no one has ever traced, and somewhere in the use a new generation of hangflies is seeded into the mist itself, as spores, as eggs, as something that is neither spore nor egg but a kind of suspended *maybe* that drifts until it finds a bridge to hang from. Then it hangs. Then it lives. Then it rises, and mates, and is shredded, and seeds the next generation of hanging.

The canopy people say that the hangflies are the *memory* of the forest. They are wrong about why. The hangflies are not the memory of the forest because they remember anything, which they do not. They are the memory of the forest because the forest remembers *them* — in the slight mineral richness of its frond-fuzz, in the soft patina of their crushed wings on the underside of every bridge, in the faint high-pitched tinkling that a heavily-populated bridge makes when the wind passes through it, a sound like a thousand tiny bells rung by a thousand tiny hands that are not hands, and that the people of the canopy have learned to hear as a kind of *tide*, rising and falling with the day's mist, telling them, in a language no one taught them, *how much air is moving, and in what direction, and whether anything larger than a hangfly is moving with it*.

A hangfly, when you hold one — and you must not, the people will tell you, the people will tell you this with the particular flatness of voice that means *we have lost someone to this and we do not wish to discuss it further* — is warm. Warmer than the air. Warmer than the wood. Because the four wing-roots contain, each, a small fermentation chamber where a culture of bacterium breaks down the collected mineral slurry into something not quite heat and not quite light but a kind of *lukewarmness* that the hangfly radiates into its immediate vicinity, and the lukewarmness is, to a finger pressed against the body, almost *maternal*, and this is why the children of the canopy cannot be stopped from trying to hold them, and this is why the hangfly is the first animal the people of the canopy learn to love, and the first they learn to be afraid of loving.

## The Weavers

The weavers are the size of a young calf, and they look, at a distance, like a bundle of wind-broken sticks that has learned to move. Up close they are something else. Up close they are *architecture in motion* — a body that is more scaffold than flesh, a ribcage you can see through, a spine that is also a mast, a set of four legs that are not quite legs so much as *stilts* with knees that bend in three places, and at the top, where a head would be on an animal built to the usual pattern, a *rack*.

The rack is the first thing anyone notices about a weaver. It grows from the skull in two broad, flat planes of bone, each plane as wide as a door and as thick as a thumb, and each plane is *laced* — a filigree of bone that has grown around and through a scaffolding of cartilage in patterns that vary from individual to individual the way a fingerprint varies, except that a weaver's rack is visible from a quarter-mile away and is the only means the people of the canopy have of telling one weaver from another. The people give the weavers names. The weavers do not know this. The people would not care if they did.

The rack is not a weapon. The rack is a *loom*. A weaver harvests frond-fuzz — the soft, damp, faintly luminescent down that grows along the underside of every mature frond — by swinging its head sideways through a hanging frond, and the fuzz catches in the lace of the bone, and then the weaver retires to its nest and, with a motion of the head that no one has ever been able to time properly because it is faster than the eye, *weaves* the fuzz into the nest's lining. The weaving is not, in any neurological sense, deliberate. The rack has its own ganglia. The rack *knows how to weave* the way a spider's abdomen knows how to spin. The weaver provides the fuzz. The rack provides the motion. The result is a nest that is, by any measure, *cozy* in a way that nothing else in the upper canopy is cozy — a bowl of damp felted fiber, suspended in the crook of a column, lined with a soft blue bioluminescent glow that is the *afterlife* of the frond-fuzz, which continues to glow for weeks after being harvested, and which is, for the duration of that afterlife, the warmest thing in the canopy. Not warm in temperature. Warm in *invitation*. Warm in the way a hearth is warm. Warm in the way a held hand is warm.

The people of the canopy will tell you, if you ask them in the right way, which is a way that involves sitting with them through a long dusk and not asking directly but *referring*, that the weavers' nests are the closest thing they have to a temple. They do not go into the nests. They do not touch the nests. They will not, under any circumstance, *look directly* at a nest. But they orient their rafts by the nests. They time their migrations by the migrations of the weavers, which move, twice a year, from one crook to another, following some internal calendar that no one has deciphered. And when a weaver dies — when an old weaver is found, one morning, hanging from the rack of its own nest with its four stilts folded under it and its tongue out, a posture the people of the canopy call *the apology* — the people will gather, in silence, and watch, and the body will swell, and within a day a fruiting body will erupt from the soft belly, and the fruiting body will release a spore, and the spore will be caught in the wind, and the wind will carry it. They do not speak of this. The word for *weaver-funeral* is a word that exists only in the imperative mood. *Don't look. Don't touch. Don't be the one who falls.*

A weaver eats cambium. The bridges of cambium that lace the columns together are not, technically, alive in the animal sense, but they are alive in the plant sense, and they are *edible* in a way that nothing else up here is edible — soft, fibrous, faintly sweet, and absolutely *full* of a particular spore that, if it finds a warm, damp, dark, and still environment, will do something unfortunate. The weaver's first stomach, a long curled fermentation chamber, is lined with a culture of a bacterium the people of the canopy have never seen and have no name for. The bacterium digests the cambium. The bacterium also, as a byproduct of its digestion, secretes a *gas* — a single-carbon compound, faint, sweet, not quite ethanol, not quite something else — that enters the weaver's bloodstream and crosses the blood-brain barrier and produces, in the weaver, a state of permanent and *mild* intoxication. The weaver is always a little drunk. The weaver has never been sober. The weaver does not know that there is a state called sober. The weaver sings.

The singing is the second thing anyone notices about a weaver, after the rack. It happens mostly at night, when the wind drops and the fronds fall quiet and the forest's constant roar softens to a hiss. A weaver's song is not a melody. It is a *phrase*, repeated, with variations, in a voice that is exactly halfway between a human voice and a woodwind, and the phrase is *long* — thirty seconds, forty, a single breath sustained across a chest that is mostly fermentation chamber and not very much lung. The phrase has structure. The phrase has *verses*, in a way, the way a river has bends. The phrase is, if you listen long enough, *almost* a word. It is not a word. It is a *shape of word*, a felt sense of language without language, and this is what makes the weavers' song, to a human ear, *unbearable* in the precise sense of the word — not that it cannot be borne, but that it *will not stop being borne*, will not let you put it down, will not let you decide whether it is beautiful or sad, because the decision is the thing the song does not permit, and the not-permitting is the song's whole nature.

The people of the canopy say that the weavers are singing the *names* of the columns. They are wrong, but they are wrong in a way that is not entirely wrong, because the song is, in some sense, *about* the column the weaver lives on, in the way that a stain on a wall is about the wall, in the way that a ring in a tree is about the tree. The song is *of* the column. The song is made of the column, fermented, exhaled, and given back to the column as a long slow sentence in a language that no one in the canopy has ever spoken, not even the weavers, who do not know they are speaking, because to know you are speaking requires a self, and a weaver has, in place of a self, a fermentation chamber and a rack and a nest and a phrase, and these are not a self, these are a *furnishings*, and the weaver is the furnished room, and the room is drunk, and the room sings, and the song goes on, and the song has been going on, in the upper canopy, for longer than the people have had rafts.

The weavers are born in the nest. They are born small, blind, four-legged, and without racks, and they nurse from the mother for a period of weeks that no one has ever been able to count, because the mother does not *nurse* in the mammalian sense, she *regurgitates* a slurry of pre-fermented cambium into a fold of skin at the throat, and the fold is warm, and the fold glows faintly, and the baby presses its mouth into the fold and drinks, and this is nursing, and this is also the first *drink* of the baby's life, and the baby is, from its first swallow, a little drunk, and the drunk baby sleeps, and the drunk baby grows, and the rack begins to push out of the skull at the end of the first year, and the rack grows fast, and the baby is, by the end of the second year, indistinguishable from an adult, and the mother, who by this time is showing her own signs of age, which is to say she has begun to lose the gloss on her rack, will *leave* the nest, one morning, and will not return, and the baby, now an adult, will not follow, because following is not in the behavioral repertoire, and the mother will go, alone, to another crook of the same column, and there, in a small nest she builds in a day, she will die, in the posture of apology, and the fruiting body will come, and the spore will be released, and the cycle will continue.

The people of the canopy say, of the weavers, that they are the *conscience* of the forest. They are also wrong about why. The weavers are not the conscience of the forest because they have a conscience. The weavers are the conscience of the forest because the forest *feeds through them*, because the cambium bridges would, without the weavers, grow too thick and choke out the fronds, because the weavers are the *pruners*, in their slow drunk way, of the living deck, and the deck is the only ground the people have, and the people would not be here without the weavers, and the people know this, and the people cannot say this, and the people say instead that the weavers sing the names of the columns, and the people listen, and the people do not speak of what they hear, and the not-speaking is the closest they come to prayer.

## The Burrowers

The burrower is the only animal in the upper canopy that no one has ever described from a safe distance. Every description of a burrower is a description written by a person who was *very close* to a burrower, and who lived through the closeness, and who has, since the closeness, not been entirely *present* in the usual sense. The people of the canopy say that a person who has seen a burrower up close is, for the rest of their life, *one translation away* from the people around them, because the seeing has put a word in their mouth that the language of the canopy does not have, and the word is *loud*, and the word does not fit, and the person speaks around it for the rest of their life the way a person speaks around a missing tooth.

A burrower is the size of a small draft horse, and it is made of two materials. The first material is weaver. The rack. The four stilts. The fermentation chamber. The blue glow of the throat-fold. These are *kept*, in the burrower, like furniture kept in a house after the previous owner has died. They are maintained, more or less, in working order. They are not *used*, in any way the original weaver would recognize. The second material is *the new thing*, the thing that grew into the weaver when the spore found the nest, and the new thing is *plant* in a way that is hard to look at, because the plant has not *replaced* the weaver so much as it has *repurposed* the weaver, has taken the weaver's body and bent it to a different shape of living, the way a river takes a fallen tree and bends the tree to a different shape of *wood*.

The plant that grows in the burrower is a relative of the column. It is a *pneumatophore* in the strict sense — a breathing structure, a lung. But it is a lung that has been *freed* of the column, that has been *released* from the slow vertical business of growing toward a sky it will never reach, and has been put to a new use. The new use is *falling*.

The spore enters the nest. The spore finds the sleeping weaver. The spore sends a single, almost imperceptible *thread* of mycelium into the soft place behind the weaver's left ear, where the skull is thinnest, where the cartilage of the rack meets the bone of the cranium, where there is, in the sleeping weaver, a small warm gap. The thread enters the gap. The thread begins to *branch*. The branches find the weaver's blood. The branches begin to drink. The weaver does not wake. The weaver, in fact, sleeps *deeper*, because the mycelium is secreting, into the weaver's brain, a small steady dose of a compound that is not quite the weaver's usual fermentation-gas and not quite something else, and the compound is a *sedative*, and the sedative is *kinder* than the weaver's usual state, which is to say the weaver is, in these last days of being a weaver, *happier than the weaver has ever been*, and the happiness is the last thing the weaver feels, and the happiness is what the mycelium replaces, slowly, over days, with *itself*.

The weaver wakes up as a burrower. The waking is not a moment. The waking is a *gradient*, a slow tilting of the body's allegiance from the weaver to the plant, and during the gradient the body does strange things. The stilts stiffen, the knees unbending into a single locked column of leg. The rack *closes*, the two flat planes folding together over the skull like a hand closing over a coin, and the closing is a *protection*, because the rack, in its closed configuration, is a helmet, and the helmet is what will, in the falling, protect the soft new brain of the plant from the impact of the air. The fermentation chamber *inverts*, turning inside out, and the inversion produces, at the soft belly, a new opening, a *mouth*, and the mouth is not a weaver's mouth, the mouth is a *column's mouth*, a circle of soft wet plant-tissue that smells of green cinnamon and something else, something almost animal, and the mouth *opens*, and the mouth does not close again, ever, and the mouth is what the burrower, eventually, hunts with.

The burrower drops from the nest. The drop is the *first sound* of the burrower, and the sound is not loud, not yet, the sound is a *click* — the click of the rack-helmet against the stilts as the locked legs leave the felt of the nest, and the click is the sound the people of the canopy have carved into their little wooden figures and hung from their raft-ropes, the click that is supposed to be the *footfall*, but is, in fact, the *leap*, and the people know this, and the people do not say they know this, and the not-saying is part of the prayer.

The drop is slow. The burrower is heavy — heavy with the new plant, heavy with the mycelium that has threaded through every muscle, heavy with the water the plant has been drawing from the weaver's bladders for the days of the transition. The drop is slow the way a leaf is slow. The drop is *guided*, because the plant, in taking over the weaver's body, has also taken over the weaver's *vestibular system*, has rewired the inner ear to respond to the plant's own sense of *up*, and the plant knows which way is down, and the plant uses the rack-helmet as a *rudder*, and the burrower falls, slowly, slowly, with the rack-helmet canted slightly to steer, and the steering is what makes the burrower *terrifying*, because a falling thing that can steer is not a falling thing, a falling thing that can steer is a *diver*, and the diver is choosing where it will land.

The diver lands in the deck. The landing is a *crunch*, and the crunch is loud, and the crunch is the second sound, and the crunch is the sound that means *a section of cambium has just been broken open from above*, and the people of the canopy, when they hear the crunch, do not run, because running is what the burrower wants, the running is what the smaller weavers do, and the smaller weavers are what the burrower eats, and the burrower is, in the moment of landing, *blind* — the impact has temporarily overwhelmed the plant's sense of up, and the burrower must wait, must *recalibrate*, and the recalibration takes the time it takes a horse to turn over in the air, which is the length of time the people of the canopy have given themselves, and the people, in that time, do not run, the people *stand still*, and the standing-still is the third prayer, the prayer of not-being-noticed, the prayer of being-a-column.

When the burrower has recalibrated, it begins to *walk*. The walking is the third sound, and the walking is the sound the people have carved into their figures, because the walking is, against all odds, *quiet*. The plant has grown *feet* — pads of soft damp cambium, sprung on the broken stilts, that absorb the impact of each step and produce, with each step, only a faint *tap*, and the tap is the footfall, and the footfall is the sound the people have learned to hear, in the roar of the fronds, the way you have learned to hear the bioluminescence, by giving yourself over, by becoming an instrument, by understanding that the footfall is a *frequency* that lives in a particular band of the forest's noise, and the people who can hear the band are the people who will live through the night, and the people who cannot hear the band are the people who will become, in the morning, a section of deck that is *softer than the rest*, and the softness is the fourth prayer, the prayer of being-consumed, the prayer of becoming, in the slow patient vegetable way, part of the cathedral.

The burrower eats *weavers*. The burrower does not eat people, exactly, but the burrower does not *not* eat people, and the distinction is not, in the moment, useful. The burrower hunts by *standing still* under a hanging nest, and waiting, and the waiting is patient, and the patient waiting is the fifth prayer, the prayer of *being the place where the things that fall are coming from*, and the people of the canopy do not speak of this prayer, and the not-speaking is, in itself, a prayer, and the prayers of the canopy are nested, the way the nests are nested, and the nesting has no bottom.

When the burrower finds a weaver — a young one, preferably, a small one, a one that has not yet grown its full rack — the burrower does not pounce. The burrower *opens*. The soft belly-mouth opens, and the opening is a *flower*, a great dark blue-black bloom the size of a washtub, and the bloom is warm, and the bloom is fragrant, and the fragrance is the fragrance of the forest's own breath, of the frond-fuzz, of the cambium, of the *home*, and the weaver, smelling the home, walks *into* the bloom, and the bloom *closes*, and the closing is silent, and the silence is the last sound the weaver hears, and the silence is the sound the people of the canopy have been listening for, all their lives, in the roar, and the people who can hear the silence in the roar are the people who will, in the morning, still be people.

The burrower digests the weaver over days. The burrower then *drops again*, because the digestion has made the burrower *heavier*, and the dropping is the next hunt, and the next hunt is the next weaver, and the weavers are *few*, and the people of the canopy know that the weavers are few, and the people of the canopy do not speak of what will happen when the weavers are gone, and the not-speaking is the oldest prayer, the prayer of *the future*, which is, in the upper canopy, not a thing you face but a thing you *hang beneath*, the way a hangfly hangs beneath a bridge, mouth-downward, drinking the air, waiting for the time-signal that will, in eleven months, release it into the roar, and the roar will shred it, and the shredding will seed the next generation, and the next generation will hang, and the hanging will be the life, and the life will be the only life, and the life will be, in the slow vegetable way, *indifferent*, and the indifference will be the only mercy available to a thing that has learned how to fall and has never once been caught.

## The People of the Raft-Cluster

There are two hundred and seven of them. There have been, for as long as anyone can remember, *approximately* two hundred and seven of them, because the raft-cluster can support approximately two hundred and seven people, and when the number rises above two hundred and seven, something happens — a section of deck gives way, a raid by a neighboring cluster, a sickness that moves through the frond-fuzz — and the number returns, with the slow patience of a tide, to two hundred and seven. The people of the canopy do not have a word for *population*. They have a word that means *the weight the raft-cluster knows how to carry*, and the word is also the word for *breath*, and the word is also the word for *the amount of water a person can hold in their cupped hands before the water begins to slip through*, and the word is, in this way, the truest word they have.

They are dark-skinned in a way that is not a color but a *depth* — a brown that has, in the blue bioluminescence, a faint undertone of violet, and in the gold of dawn, a faint undertone of ember, and in the noon disc of the sun, a faint undertone of nothing at all, because at noon the sun does not reach them and the people are, in the noon, a kind of *absence*, a moving absence, a raft-shaped absence that drifts through the cathedral with the patient sway of the deck. Their faces are sharp in a way that has nothing to do with the bones and everything to do with the *light*, because in a place of constant soft illumination, the soft muscles of the face do not develop the way they develop in places of harsh light, and the people of the canopy have *thin* faces, faces that are almost *diagrams* of faces, the cheekbones like the lines of a hand-drawn map, the brow like the ridge of a roof, the mouth like a small horizontal wound that is, in repose, a *closed door*, and in speech, a *slightly open door*, and in the moment of seeing a burrower, an *open door through which the soul has just fled*.

They have no word for *ground*. They have, as I said, two words: *the place where the things that fall are*, and *the place where the things that fall are coming from*, and they do not speak of either in polite company, which is a phrase I am using in the absence of a better one, because the people of the canopy do not have *polite company* so much as they have *company at which the not-speaking is more complete than usual*. The two words are the only words in their language that begin with the sound *h*, because the sound *h* is, in the upper canopy, a *danger sound* — the soft exhalation of breath, the almost-sound of an opening mouth, the pre-sound of a word that has not yet been spoken — and the people, by long custom, save the *h* for the two directions they do not wish to look, and the not-wishing is so complete that the words themselves are only spoken by the *old*, by the people who are, in the slow patient vegetable way, *finished*, and who have, by virtue of being finished, the right to look down.

They have fire. This is the great fact of their existence, and the great *accident*, because the fire did not, strictly, *happen*. The fire is *preserved*. A long time ago — a time the people measure not in years but in *raft-migrations*, in the slow drift of the cluster from one crook of the upper canopy to another — a particular black nodule was found in the heartwood of a column that had been split by lightning, and the nodule, when struck against the obsidian of a vent-stone, produced a spark, and the spark fell onto a piece of dried frond-fuzz that had been gathered for nest-lining, and the fuzz *caught*, and the catching was the first fire, and the first fire has been kept burning, in a clay pot that travels the raft-cluster and is never allowed to go out, ever, since that day. The fire is *older than the oldest person*. The fire is, in a sense, the *oldest person*. The fire is tended by a person whose name no one speaks, and whose face no one looks at directly, because to look at the fire-keeper is to look at the fire, and to look at the fire is to *acknowledge the accident*, and to acknowledge the accident is to invite the gods to notice that the fire is here, and the gods have not yet noticed, and the not-noticing is, in the upper canopy, the only form of grace available to a small soft fire-carrying thing in a cathedral that does not know it is here.

They use the fire to scorch the deck ahead of them when they walk. The deck is a living thing, a slow vegetable mat of cambium that has grown and re-grown over the centuries, and the deck is *always* a little damp, and the damp is, as I have said, a *suggestion* of mud, and the suggestion is *warm*, and the warm damp is *exactly the environment* that the burrower-spore requires to germinate. A person walking in the upper canopy is, at every step, *planting spores*. The spores are everywhere. The spores are in the mist. The spores are in the frond-fuzz that clings to every surface. The spores are *waiting*, in the way that all spores wait, for the warm damp dark stillness of a sleeping body, and a person walking in the upper canopy is, at every step, *advertising* that they are a sleeping body waiting to happen, and the advertisement is the warmth of the body, and the warmth is what the spore reads, and the reading is what the person must, at every step, *deny*, and the denial is the scorch, and the scorch is the prayer, and the prayer has the same name as the scorch, and the name is a word that means *the corridor of char that keeps the cathedral from noticing you*.

Their carvings are the only art. They carve, with hand-tools of antler — taken, by some ancient compact, from the weavers who die of old age and not from the weavers who die in the bloom of a burrower, because a weaver who dies in the bloom of a burrower is, in the people of the canopy's understanding, *already a carving* — and obsidian chipped from a single vent-stone in the southern columns, a vent-stone that is, itself, a *relic*, a thing that was once, in some geological age, a place where the columns *bled*, where the molten heartwood of a young column broke through the bark and cooled, in the air, into a glass, and the glass is the only hard thing in the upper canopy that is not the rack of a weaver, and the glass is, in the people of the canopy's understanding, the *teeth* of the cathedral, and the teeth are kept, and the keeping is a prayer, and the prayer is the *oldest prayer*, the prayer of *understanding that you are a small soft fire-carrying thing in a cathedral that has been growing for longer than your species has had a word for cathedral, and that the cathedral is not for you*.

The figures they carve are of the burrowers. Always of the burrowers. The figures are small — the size of a thumb, the size of a hand — and they are always carved with the mouth open, and the mouth is always carved as if mid-sound, mid-roar, mid-bloom, and the figure is always, always, always *falling*. The figure is never *landed*. The figure is never *walking*. The figure is never *in the bloom*. The figure is always, only, *in the air*, in the slow patient vegetable way, in the fall, and the fall is the prayer, because the fall is the moment of *choice* — the moment the burrower is *between* the nest and the deck, between the weaver-life and the plant-life, between the being-it-was and the being-it-will-be — and the choice, in the falling, is *irreversible*, and the irreversibility is what the people of the canopy *honor*, in the way that a small soft fire-carrying thing in a cathedral honors the only thing it can honor, which is the *gravity of the place*, the *weight of the cathedral*, the *patient vegetable certainty* that everything in the upper canopy is, in the end, *falling*, and the falling is not a fall, the falling is a *long slow way of standing up out of the mud*, and the mud is, in the upper canopy, the deck, and the deck is *all* the world, and the world is *falling*, and the falling is *indifferent*, and the indifference is *not a cruelty but simply the only posture available to a thing that has spent a million years learning how to fall and has never once been caught*.

You learn the rules. You learn them well. You do not learn all of them. No one has. No one *can*, because the rules are not *rules*, the rules are *the place*, the rules are the *gravity*, the rules are the *patient vegetable way* in which the cathedral, which is not for you, has, by the long accident of growing where you happened to be, *made a place for you*, and the place is *small*, and the place is *soft*, and the place is *full of falling things*, and the place is, in the end, the only place there is, and you live in it, and you die in it, and the *living in it* is the *dying in it*, and the *dying in it* is the *only prayer* available to a small soft fire-carrying thing in a cathedral that has been growing, slowly, since before the weather learned its name, and the cathedral is, in its slow vegetable way, *indifferent*, and the indifference is not a cruelty but simply the only posture available to a thing that has spent a million years learning how to fall and has never once been caught.

You listen. You always listen. The forest is loud and the loudness is the only mercy.
