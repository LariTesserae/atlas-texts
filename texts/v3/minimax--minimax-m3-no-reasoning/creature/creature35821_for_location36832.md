The lowest empire of the living.

I will start with the crusts because the crusts were here first. They spread across the warm rock in pale and darker washes, a lichen that is not lichen — pale yellow in the sulfur-rich seams, rust-red where the iron feeds them, an occasional violet flush where some rarer chemistry holds. They grow like frost grows, except in reverse: not cold depositing pattern outward from nothing, but warmth depositing patience outward from a fissure. Their edges are sharp. Their interiors are layered, the way agate is layered, the way a stalagmite is layered, except that the layers record the chemistry of what seeped through, week by week, season by season, and the chemistry has not changed in any season anyone remembers, and so the layers are uniform, and so the crusts look like the same crust that has always been here. Where they glow, they glow in soft patches — a sourceless-looking lemon, a sourceless-looking green, that does not so much illuminate as fail to be dark. You could read by them, barely, if your eyes were the right eyes. The right eyes are below.

The grazers are the size of a thumbnail. They are slate-gray or paler, and their backs are faceted, broken into a pattern of low plates that looks, until you learn to see it move, like the surface of a piece of broken column. They move in a slow tide across the crusts, and where they have fed, the crust is faintly scraped, and the scrape disappears by morning. They have many legs. The legs are fine, and they are so fine that from any distance the grazer is just a small shape sliding across a small patch of rock, and the sliding is what you would notice if you noticed anything, which you mostly don't. When something disturbs them — a vibration, a shadow, a change in the slow weather of the air — they fold inward, the way a pill bug folds, and they become, for as long as the disturbance lasts, indistinguishable from the gravel around them. They are quiet. They are made of the same minerals they eat, more or less, and when they die, they crumble back into the dust that the dust is made of.

The medium predators. A meter long. They are darker than the grazers and harder to look at; their bodies do not resolve into a single shape the way the grazers' bodies do. They are pieces — a hard dorsal ridge that looks like a broken-off shelf of column; a soft underside, ribbed and faintly luminous; four or six legs, depending on the kind, jointed in too many places; a head that is mostly mouth and a few too many eyes. The eyes do not all point the same direction. Some are set into the sides of the head. Some are set into the legs. One kind, the rarer kind, has eyes set into the tips of long, jointed appendages that rise above the body and swivel slowly, like the periscope of something that learned to hunt in another element. They are patient. They are so patient that a single one of them, lying against the base of a column, can wait for an entire slow weather cycle without moving, its mouth faintly glowing, its glow meant for something — luring, or warning, or simply the consequence of what the mouth contains. When a grazer comes within striking range, the predator does not leap. It unfolds. It unfolds in a single motion, the way a hand unfolds from a fist, and the grazer is taken, and the predator settles back into its waiting shape. The predators are quiet. They do not chew. They do not swallow audibly. They absorb. The grazers they take are not missed, individually, but the predators are part of why the grazers rasp in the patterns they rasp in, and the patterns are part of why the crusts grow in the shapes they grow in, and the shapes are part of why the floor looks the way it does.

The dense columns. These are different. They are darker, and they are denser, and they do not grow. Most of the columns grow — they rise from the floor in patient mineral accretion, a centimeter in a human year, a millimeter in a builder's lifetime, sharp at the top because the top is where the mineral arrives. The dense columns are sharp at the top too, but the sharpness is older, and the columns do not get taller. They have been sharp for as long as anyone can remember. They emit a steady amber glow, and the glow pools at their bases in a way that looks, in the dimness, like a small spilled fire. The glow is not fire. The glow is not warmth. The glow is something the columns do, and the builders have decided — not in any formal way, not in any way you could point to as a doctrine — that the glow is worth attending to. So they attend to it.

The builders. Two legs, two arms, a face you would not call a face. They are the height of a child, if a child could stand the way they stand, with the weight low and the shoulders rounded forward and the head sunk between them. Their skin is a pale, dusty ochre. Their skin takes the dust well — the dust clings to the oil that the builders excrete, in patterns the builders put on deliberately. The patterns are not tattoos. The patterns are dust, applied with the fingers, reapplied as the dust wears off, kept current. The dust is the same dust the predators kick up and the grazers rasp loose. The dust is mildly narcotic in a low, persistent way, and the builders crave it, and so the dust is everywhere on them, and the patterns are layered, and the older layers are the dust of older journeys, and the newer layers are the dust of this week. They have too many eyes in the sense that they have eyes on the sides of the head as well as the front, and their eyes are large and dark and do not blink in the way yours blink, because there is no wind to blink against and no grit to blink away except the slow grit of the dust itself, which they let in. They blink slowly, infrequently. When they look at you — and they could look at you, if you were here — they would look at you with a patience that would make you feel, eventually, that you had been looked through.

Their tools. A sharpened silicate flake lashed to a short handle with a fiber spun from a grazer. A heavy bone from something larger, perhaps an old predator, perhaps something else that died and was preserved in the dry chemistry of this place. A flat dark plate of mineral they carry for warmth or ritual or some third reason they do not name. The tools are not decorations. The tools are used. You can see the wear on the flake-handles, the way the lashing has been retied, the way the bone has been resharpened. They have been in use for as long as the rings have been where the rings are. The tools are the right tools for the work the builders do, and the work the builders do is small, and so the tools are small, and the smallness is not a limitation. It is a fit.

Their rings. They build them of broken column-fragments, low walls a hand high, a hand wide, scattered across the vast floor in a pattern that is not random but is not dense. From a height — and there is no height, but if there were — the rings would look like the way stars look in a sky that is mostly empty: scattered, persistent, none of them central. Inside the rings they sleep. They sleep curled, the way the grazers fold, the way the predators fold, because folding is the universal answer to a place that has predators in it. Inside the rings they eat. They eat the grazers, and they eat the crusts directly, and they eat, sometimes, each other, when one is too far gone to be brought back, and the eating is done quickly and quietly and without elaboration. Inside the rings they watch. They watch the slow weather of the air. They watch the columns. They sit near the dense columns and they leave offerings — a tool, a fragment of bone, a piece of themselves, cut clean and set down — and they wait, and they do not know what they are waiting for, and the waiting is not idle. The waiting is the thing.

The dust. The dust is in the air, and the air moves it, and the air moves slowly. The dust is in the cracks of the skin. The dust is on the patterns. The dust is what the builders become. The dust is narcotic in a low way, and the low narcotic is part of the equilibrium — the builders breathe it in, and the breathing makes them slow, and the slowness makes them patient, and the patience makes them not interfere with the rate at which the crusts grow or the columns rise or the predators wait. If you took the dust away the builders would change. If you took the builders away the dust would still be. The builders are not the point of the dust. The dust is the point of the builders.

The quiet. The quiet is the equilibrium made audible, if you could hear it, which you cannot, because the quiet is the absence of the sound that would tell you the equilibrium was there. The grazers rasp without sound. The predators strike without sound. The builders scrape their marks on the columns and the marks are silent, and the builders' breath is silent, and the builders' voices — when they speak — are a vibration you feel in the floor before you hear it in the air, if you ever hear it in the air at all. The columns grow with an occasional faint crack, far away, brittle, the sound of a place that is still finishing itself in the only way it has left to finish. The fires the builders make are smokeless and quiet. The fires are warm. The fires are yellow, faintly, and they are the only yellow in a place that is mostly amber and pale and sourceless green, and a builder sitting by a fire in a ring of broken column looks, from a distance, like a small piece of warm light that has learned to sit up.

The stasis. Nothing changes. The crusts regrow at the rate they have always regrown. The grazers rasp at the rate they have always rasped. The predators wait at the rate they have always waited. The columns rise at the rate they have always risen. The rings stay where they were piled. The offerings stay where they were left. The builders are born, and they grow, and they rasp crusts and hunt grazers and carry tools and pile rings and leave offerings and breathe the dust and die, and they become dust, and the dust becomes part of the floor, and the floor is the same floor. The place has finished. The builders live inside the finishing, and they do not know they are inside it, and the not-knowing is part of the equilibrium, and the equilibrium is part of the quiet.

If you stood here. If you stood here for a long time, and the dust settled on you, and the warmth came up through your boots, and the dimness resolved into columns and the columns resolved into distances, and the distances resolved into a kind of forever — you would begin to forget what hurry was. You would begin to forget what noise was. You would begin to feel the slow weather of the air as a thing you were inside, the way a fish feels water, the way a builder feels warmth. You would breathe the dust, and the dust would make you slower, and the slowness would make you see the grazers more clearly, and the grazers would make you see the crusts more clearly, and the crusts would make you see the floor more clearly, and the floor would make you see the columns more clearly, and the columns would make you see the ceiling, eventually, if the ceiling were there to be seen, which it is, far above, dim, out of reach, holding the place together in the way a sky holds a place together, except that the ceiling is below the sky and the sky is somewhere else.

You would sit in a ring that was not yours, and a builder would come, and the builder would look at you, and you would look at the builder, and neither of you would speak, because speaking is not what is done here. The builder would leave an offering at the base of a dense column and go away. The amber would pool around the offering. The dust would settle on the offering. You would wait. You would not know what you were waiting for. You would wait anyway, because the place teaches waiting the way water teaches swimming, and you would learn, and the learning would be the whole of what you became.

That is what lives here. That is what the place is. A finished world, inhabited, breathing slowly, waiting without knowing it is waiting, quiet, warm, vast, the same, the same, the same.
