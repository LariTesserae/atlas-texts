# What Lives in the Basin

## The Canopy

The city of the basin is built high, in the place where the trees stop being trees and become something more like landscape. From the floor you cannot see it. You hear it. You feel it as a pressure change, occasionally, when something heavy moves from one branch to another. The creatures up there have made a life out of the vertical — out of bark and mist and the long slow dripping that falls through every layer. They have been in those canopies longer than the basin has been a basin. The trees grew around them, in a sense. The trees grew because of them, in another sense. The relationship is older than either party remembers.

What lives up there is not one thing. It is a layered negotiation, a vertical ecology that has had time to specialize.

---

### The Knotters

The most numerous. The most heard. The sound of the basin, if you had to name a single sound, is the sound of a Knotter.

A Knotter is about the size of a closed fist. It has no fixed shape. It moves by tightening one part of itself and releasing another, so that it flows along a branch the way a drop of water flows down a window — not by walking, not by crawling, but by a continuous rearrangement of itself around the problem of getting from here to there. It is the color of wet bark. It is the texture of wet bark. It is hard to see even when you are looking at it. The ones that live in the canopy have darker backs; the ones that occasionally descend to the lower branches are paler, almost the gray of the mist itself.

Knotters live in colonies. A colony is a single tree, or sometimes two adjacent trees, and it might contain several hundred individuals. They are arranged not by hierarchy — Knotters do not seem to have one — but by *position*. Some Knotters stay on the trunk. Some stay in the mid-canopy. Some ride the uppermost branches where the cloud touches the leaves and the light is a rumor. The ones at the top are smaller. The ones lower down are larger. No one knows why. The ones at the top are paler. The ones lower down are darker. The boundary between roles is not strict. A Knotter can move up or down in its life, slowly, over years. The colony breathes with them.

They eat the moss. Not by grazing it — there is no grazing up there, no easy mouthful. They eat it the way the basin eats the mountains: by patience. A Knotter presses a part of itself against the moss and stays there for hours, and the moss becomes softer, and then becomes part of the Knotter, and then the Knotter moves on, and there is a small pale scar on the bark that fills in over weeks. They are not fast. They are not in a hurry. The moss grows back faster than they eat it. The relationship is not predation. It is something more like a conversation that takes a century to finish.

Their sound is the chitter. The clicks. The overlapping short calls that fill the spaces between the bigger voices in the canopy. A Knotter does not have a single call. It has a vocabulary of maybe a dozen — short, dry, percussive sounds, some of them clicks, some of them small trills, some of them a kind of wet snap. They use them in combination. Two Knotters passing on a branch will exchange a sequence that an outside listener might mistake for a single call but is in fact a back-and-forth, a call and a response, the way two people passing on a street might nod and say something short. A Knotter alone is mostly silent. Knotters together are almost never silent. The basin is full of them. The basin is therefore almost never silent.

They mate in the mist. No one has seen it. The upper canopy disappears into the cloud, and what happens up there, in the gray, in the place where the visibility stops, is private. After the mating — which may take most of a season, for all anyone knows — the female lays a single egg, the size of a thumbnail, on the underside of a high leaf. The egg is translucent. The egg is warm to the touch, even in the wet air. Something inside it is already moving. The young Knotter that emerges is a working miniature of the adult, the same color as the bark it lands on, and it begins to eat moss within hours.

Knotters die the way moss dies. They lose definition. They become softer. They stop moving. They become, in the end, indistinguishable from the bark, and the bark absorbs them, and the tree carries them for a while in the wood, and the wood is the color of old Knotters.

### The Long-voices

A different scale of life. Larger. Slower. Fewer.

A Long-voice is the size of a child, though no one in the basin has ever seen a child to make the comparison. It has four limbs — or perhaps two limbs and a tail, the distinction is not one the animal makes. It is the color of the deeper bark, almost black, with a faint pattern of lighter patches along its back that might be camouflage or might be the memory of spots from an earlier form. Its face is flat. Its eyes are large, lidless, the pale gray of the mist. It does not blink. It does not need to. The eye is kept wet by the air itself.

Long-voices live in the high canopy, but not in the densest parts of it. They prefer the older trees, the ones whose upper branches have begun to die back and form horizontal platforms. They live in pairs, or in threes, in a structure that is not quite a nest — more a smoothed platform, kept clear of debris, often with a single low rim of woven mosses around the edge. The platform is used for years. Decades. The same platform is added to, season by season, the moss rim growing thicker, the floor becoming more solid, until eventually the platform is itself almost a small tree, growing out of the tree it was built on.

They eat Knotters. This is the only predation in the basin that anyone has ever witnessed. It is not violent. A Long-voice descends into the lower canopy at dusk — there is no dusk in the basin, but there is a fading, a slow reduction in the gray-green glow as some shift in the cloud layer above changes the amount of light that filters down — and it moves along a branch with a slow, deliberate patience, and the Knotters move out of its way, and the Long-voice takes one. Just one. Sometimes none. The taking is gentle. The Knotter is held in a forelimb for a moment. Then it is in the Long-voice's mouth, and the Long-voice is chewing, slowly, and the Knotter is gone. The whole thing takes perhaps a minute. The Long-voice climbs back up to its platform. It will not eat again for two or three days.

The eating does not seem to reduce the number of Knotters. The colony compensates. The eating might, in fact, be necessary for the colony — a thinning, a kind of pruning, the way a forest needs its dead wood removed. No one has done the study. The basin is patient. The study, if it is ever done, will also be patient.

The call of the Long-voice is the slow rhythmic one. *Hnnnnh.* A low, sustained sound, with a slow rise in pitch and a slower fall. It lasts perhaps four seconds. It is repeated every ten to fifteen seconds, sometimes for hours, sometimes for an entire night. It is answered, after a delay, by another Long-voice across the basin. Then by another. The calls overlap, and interleave, and the basin fills with a slow pulse that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. They are not, as far as anyone can tell, talking to each other. They are not saying anything. They are doing what the Long-voices have always done at this hour, which is to call into the mist and wait for an answer that is not required to come. The basin is the kind of place where that counts as communication.

### The Creepers

The third presence. The strangest. The one no one talks about.

A Creeper is small — a hand's length, maybe a little more. It is thin. It is many-legged, though the number of legs is not fixed; a Creeper can have eight legs or twelve, and different Creepers seem to have different counts, and an individual Creeper can have a different count at different times in its life, the legs appearing and being absorbed back into the body as needed. It is the color of a shadow. It does not reflect light well. It is hard to see even at close range, which is perhaps why no one talks about it.

Creepers live in the mid-canopy. They live on the trunks. They live in the lower branches, occasionally, though they seem to prefer the dark. They are active in the deeper dark — in the times when the gray-green glow is at its lowest, in the hours when the basin is most itself. They move on the bark the way a Knotter moves on the bark, by flowing, by continuous rearrangement, but faster, and with a different texture. A Creeper moves like a thought across a surface. You see where it was. You see where it is. You do not, for a moment, see the motion between.

They do not make a sound. Or they make a sound that is below hearing, or above it, or in a register that the human ear does not pick up. The basin has a low hum, the hum of the structure, and it is possible — no one knows — that the Creepers are part of that hum, that they are small contributors to the bass note that runs through the floor and the stone and the air. They are not numerous. You might see one on a long evening. You might not see one for weeks. They are the rarest of the canopy creatures, and the least understood, and the most easily imagined as something else.

They eat — no one is sure. The stomach contents of the one Creeper that was ever found dead (at the base of a large tree, in a small puddle, already being absorbed back into the bark) were inconclusive. Moss. Bark. A trace of something that might have been a Knotter, once, in a past life. They are part of the basin's recycling. They are part of the long conversation between the living things and the dead things and the very slow things. They are the question the basin asks about itself when it is darkest.

## The Floor

The canopy has its city. The floor has its slower lives.

The floor is water and stone. The stone wears the moss. The moss is the color of the place — that deep, saturated, almost-black green that is more felt than seen. The ferns grow in the drier patches, low and old, their fronds curled when the air is still and uncurling when something moves through. The pools are full of their own small world.

### The Bell-flies

The luminous flicker in the water. That is a Bell-fly.

A Bell-fly is not a fly. It is not anything that has a common name in any language. It is a small creature — the length of a finger joint — that lives its entire life in the warm water on the floor of the basin. It is transparent, almost. You see it by the light it makes. The light is faint, a pale blue-green, and it pulses. The pulse is the source of the name. *Bing.* A small, soft flash, like a single note struck far away. *Bing.* Another. *Bing.*

The Bell-flies pulse in synchrony, but not perfectly. There is a drift, a wave, a slow current of light that moves through a pool the way a current of air moves through grass. The light starts in one place and travels. By the time it has crossed the pool, the Bell-flies at the far end are pulsing on a delay, and the wave has become something more complicated, an interference pattern of small flashes overlapping. In a large pool, the surface of the water, at the right hour, becomes a slow field of light moving through itself. It is the most beautiful thing in the basin. It is also, possibly, the only beautiful thing the basin knows it is making.

They eat the small things in the water. Algae. Bacteria. The faint, drifting life that lives in the film on the underside of stones. They do not move much. A Bell-fly spends most of its life in a single small area of a single pool. It pulses. It feeds. It drifts, occasionally, on a current it did not make. It mates by releasing a small light into the water, brighter than the usual pulse, and if another Bell-fly releases a brighter light at the same time, the two lights find each other, briefly, and merge, and then the two Bell-flies drift apart and continue pulsing on their own. The young are smaller. The young are fainter. The young are many, and most of them do not survive the season, and the ones that do become part of the wave.

The wave is the floor's slow pulse. The basin has many pulses. The Knotters' chitter is fast and fine-grained. The Long-voices' call is slow and sustained. The Bell-flies' wave is somewhere between, a rhythm of minutes, a rhythm of the pool. If you stand still in the basin for long enough — and the basin encourages you to stand still for long enough — you begin to feel the pulses layering. The chitter becomes a texture. The long call becomes a tone. The wave becomes a third thing, a thing that moves through both. The basin is full of rhythms. The basin is being played, slowly, by the things in it.

### The Stoneskins

On the wet rock, in the margins of the pools, in the places where the water gives up and the moss has not yet taken over, there is the Stoneskin.

A Stoneskin is a flat creature. It is wider than a hand, sometimes much wider, and it is perhaps a finger's thickness at the center, thinning to almost nothing at the edges. Its top surface is the color and texture of the stone it lives on — gray, mottled, the same slight variations of shade. Its underside is pale, and soft, and faintly damp. It moves by rippling. A wave of contraction moves from one edge to the other, and the creature flows forward by a fraction of an inch, and the wave subsides, and a moment later another wave begins. The motion is so slow that you have to fix your eyes on a single spot — a small crack in the stone, a piece of grit — and watch the creature's relationship to that spot change over many minutes, and then you will see, finally, that it has moved.

They eat the film. The thin, living layer on the surface of the stone. The same film the Bell-flies eat in the water, but here, on the wet rock, the film is thicker, and the Stoneskins spend their lives moving across it at the speed of a fingernail growing. They are not in a hurry. The film grows back. The stoneskin eats. The stoneskin moves on. The stone underneath is shaped, in tiny ways, by the passage of millions of years of Stoneskins moving across it. The basin floor is the floor it is because the Stoneskins have been making it, slowly, for longer than the basin has been a basin.

They reproduce by division. A Stoneskin, when it is old enough and large enough, begins to pull gently in the middle. The pulling takes days. The two halves — the old half and the new half — eventually separate, and each one, after a while, begins to move on its own. There is no parent. There is no child. There are two Stoneskins where there was one, and each one is the same age, and each one begins again. The basin is patient. The Stoneskins are patient. The patience is the same patience.

## The Edges

At the margins — where the floor gives up and the shoulder of the basin begins its long rise — there is a different life. The water is thinner here. The stone is more exposed. The moss climbs, and the ferns give way to other small plants, and the air moves differently, in slow eddies that come down from the higher slopes.

### The Climbers

The shoulders of the basin are not bare. They are not empty. They have their own lives, the lives of things that have made their homes in the angle between the floor and the rise.

A Climber is a small, four-limbed creature, no longer than an arm, that lives its entire life on the wet rock of the shoulder. It is the color of wet stone, with a faint banding along its back that breaks up its outline. It has a long, prehensile tail that it uses as a fifth limb. It has eyes that are very dark, and small, and set wide on a flat face. It does not look like anything else in the basin. It is the only creature in the basin that has ever been seen to look back.

It eats the small invertebrates of the wet rock. The film. The occasional Knotter that has wandered down too low. The things that live in the cracks. It is a careful hunter. It moves slowly. It stops for long periods, perfectly still, and then it moves again, in a single quick motion, and it has eaten, or it has not. The eating is not a frequent event. The Climber, like the Long-voice, can go for days between meals. The basin does not require urgency. The Climber is not urgent.

It makes the wheeze. That sound, the one that is not quite a wheeze, the sound like air being moved through a wet chamber. That is the Climber, breathing through the lateral slits along its sides, the breathing made audible by the architecture of its body. The wheeze is not a call. It is not communication. It is respiration, and the basin has decided, in its slow way, to make it part of the sound. The wheeze is everywhere on the shoulder. The wheeze is the shoulder.

The Climber has young. One at a time. The young is born live, and it is small, and it is able to climb within hours, and it stays close to the parent for a season, and then it is on its own, somewhere on the shoulder, and the parent does not follow. The basin is large enough for this. The shoulder is large enough. The young Climber finds its own patch of wet rock, and it eats, and it wheezes, and it does not look back.

## The Water

The water has its own lives, the slow ones, the ones that happen in the pools and along the seeps.

### The Drifters

In the largest pools, in the slow channels between the trees, there are the Drifters.

A Drifter is a small, gelatinous creature, the size of a thumb, the color of the deeper water. It does not swim. It does not move under its own power. It is carried — by the slow current, by the slight movements of the water, by the breath of larger creatures passing the surface. It drifts. It is named for what it does.

It eats by absorbing. The water of the basin is full of small things — the film, the bacteria, the eggs of other creatures, the faint organic matter that leaches from the mosses and the bark and the floor. The Drifter absorbs these as it drifts, by a slow process of taking the water into itself and letting the water out again, slightly poorer. It is a small filter, set loose in the basin. There are many of them. The basin is full of them, in every pool, in every channel, drifting.

They pulse faintly, the way the Bell-flies do, but slower, and in a different color — a deep amber, almost orange, that is visible only at the right angle, only in the deeper pools, only at the hour when the basin is darkest. The Drifter-pulse is the floor of the basin, the bass note below the Bell-flies' song. If you sit by a large pool at the right hour and let your eyes adjust, you can see the deep water, beneath the Bell-flies' wave, faintly moving with the amber flicker of a thousand small lives going nowhere in particular.

They are not long-lived. A Drifter lives a season, perhaps two. Then it stops drifting. It settles. It becomes part of the floor of the pool, a small dark shape that is slowly absorbed, and the small things in the water eat the Drifter the way the Drifter ate the water. The basin recycles. The basin is patient. The Drifters do not mind.

## The Silence Over the Structure

The structure in the center of the basin has no creatures on it. The structure has no creatures above it. The trees that lean over it are empty of Knotters. The Long-voice platforms near it are abandoned — old, moss-covered, slowly being taken back by the bark. The Bell-flies in the small pool at its base do not pulse. The water is still. The Drifters settle there and do not drift.

The Climbers do not come onto the shoulder behind it. The Stoneskins do not cross the floor near it. There is a margin, perhaps ten meters wide, around the structure, in which the basin holds its breath. The moss does not grow on the walls of the structure. The water does not pool on its roof. The mist that rises from the chimney goes straight up, and it does not diffuse, and it does not disperse, and the air around it is just slightly cleaner, just slightly emptier, than the air elsewhere.

The hum, of course, is louder there. You feel it through the soles of your feet. You feel it when you put your hand on the wall. You feel it in your teeth.

Whether the structure is alive is a question the basin has not answered. Whether it is the source of the hum, or the recipient of the hum, or the place where the hum is most itself, is a question the basin has not answered. Whether the creatures avoid it because they fear it, or because they are part of it, or because the basin itself, in its slow organization, has decided that the structure is the center and the rest of the lives are arranged around it — this, also, the basin has not answered.

The basin does not mind the question. The basin is patient. The structure is patient. The hum continues. The mist rises. The creatures live their lives at a distance, the way planets live at a distance from a star they are held by.

---

## The Whole

The basin is a small place. A cathedral place. A place that has settled into what it is, and what it is, is a vertical city in the canopies, a slow negotiation on the floor, a small litany of lives in the water, and at the center, a silence, and a hum, and a structure that has not yet explained itself.

The creatures do not know they are living in a place that has stopped hurrying. They are not aware of the patience of the basin. They are not aware of the cathedral proportions, or the gray-green dark, or the long fall of water at the low edge. They are aware of the next branch. The next meal. The next pulse. The next call. They are aware of the small, immediate business of being alive in a place that allows them to be alive without urgency.

A Knotter clicks on a branch in the high dark, and another Knotter answers, and the basin hears them, and the basin is the kind of place where that is enough. A Long-voice calls into the mist. A Climber wheezes on the shoulder. A Bell-fly pulses in a pool. A Drifter glows in the deeper water. A Stoneskin flows a fraction of an inch across the floor. A Creeper, perhaps, moves in the dark. A leaf falls, in a time you would not measure, into a pool. A drop of water gathers on the underside of a leaf and falls to the next leaf below it, and to the next, and to the pool.

The mist rises from the chimney. The hum continues. The basin is patient. The basin is being what it is. The creatures are being what they are. The structure is being what it is.

In a thousand years, almost the same. In ten thousand years, different only in the ways that would be visible to someone who had been watching carefully the whole time.
