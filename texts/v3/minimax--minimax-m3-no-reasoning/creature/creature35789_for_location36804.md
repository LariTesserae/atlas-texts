What lives here is what the place is. The things here are not quite separate from each other, the way organs are not quite separate from a body, though whether they are organs of one larger thing or simply neighbors that have learned to share a warm and dim and humming darkness is not something the place has decided, and not something any of them have asked.

The stems are the first thing one meets, and they are the most present. They are as tall as three men, and slender, and they grow in clusters of three or four, the way figures gather around something they are not quite discussing. They are a deep wine color, almost black where the dimness gathers at the top, and a bruised violet where the light pools at the base. Their surfaces are smooth and faintly glossy, the way the inside of a shell is glossy, or the way skin is glossy under a thin sweat. They have no leaves. They have no roots one can see. They rise out of the crust and stop at the cup.

A stem moves, but slowly, and from the inside. There is a faint traveling constriction that goes up the length of it at intervals, a peristalsis, the same motion a throat makes when it swallows. It is as if the stem is drawing something up out of the paste under the floor and passing that something through itself, and what is being passed is warm, because the stem is warm — warmer than the air, and warmer than the floor, and the warmth has a pulse. If one puts a hand against a stem, one feels the pulse before one understands what one is feeling, and one pulls the hand back, because the warmth is too close to the warmth of something that knows one is there.

At the top of each stem, the cup. The cup is like a bell with the clapper taken out, or like a mouth held open and never closed. Inside the cup there sits a small, dense growth, yellow, almost luminous, the color of the inside of a honeycomb on a cold morning. The yellow growth is the brightest thing in the place. It pulses in a rhythm close to the rhythm of the stem but not quite the same, the way two clocks in the same room will drift in and out of phase. When it pulses, it brightens, and at that same instant there is a small, high sound — almost a chirp, almost a strike — that rises into the hum and dissolves into it, and the hum is the louder for a moment and then the same as before.

The yellow growths are what the stems are for, or one of the things the stems are for. From time to time, a yellow growth will detach from the cup. It will not fall. It will lift. It will rise a little way into the still air and turn slowly, once, twice, with the lazy authority of a leaf let go from a great height, and then it will drift, on no current, toward the curved ceiling. The ceiling is hung with other growths, pale and root-thick and wet-looking, slick with a thin clear liquid thicker than water that does not run but that gathers, and these ceiling growths are the place's tongue. They taste the dim, mineral air. They make the dim light. They are waiting, in their slow way, for what the stems send up.

When a yellow growth touches a ceiling growth, there is a soft, wet sound. The yellow growth is absorbed, and the ceiling growth pulses once, in its own slower rhythm, and the pulse travels down the hanging stem and into the ceiling and is gone. The place is lighter by one small sun. The place is heavier by one sweet thing taken in.

What falls short of the ceiling is taken by what lives on the floor.

The largest creatures are the size of a large dog, and they are the color of the floor, and they have no visible eyes. They have a front, and the front is oriented toward whatever they are attending to. They move with a gait that is boneless, as if the paste under the crust had come up to have a look around and was not in any hurry about getting back. They wait beneath the clusters of stems, and they wait the way a stone waits, or the way a low cloud waits above a hill. When a yellow growth lifts, they do not chase it. They follow it, and they follow it the way a shadow follows a thing, with the same slope and the same slowness, and if the growth lands on the crust — which it does sometimes, missing the ceiling, missing everything — they fold around it. They do not chew. They do not seem to have mouths. They fold, and the thing they have folded around is gone into them, and the place where the thing was inside them is a faint warmth for a little while, and then nothing again.

They are not aggressive. They are not afraid. They seem to regard any presence that is not themselves as a kind of weather — something that will pass, something that does not need to be understood. Once in a long while, one of them will stop, and settle, and press itself down into the crust, and the crust will give, and the creature will sink, slowly, the way a coin sinks into soft butter, and the floor will close over it without breaking, and there will be a long, low thud felt in the chest before it is heard, and then a small silence, and then the creature will be somewhere else, a little way off, and it will be moving again, as if it had always been there. They are the source of the deeper thuds. They are the deep weather of the place, under the hum.

On the stems, there are smaller creatures. They are the color of the stems and almost invisible against them, which is part of how they live. They have many small, dry feet, and they cling to the glossy surfaces the way a beetle clings to bark, except that bark is not warm and has no pulse, and these creatures, when they cling, are clinging to something that is alive the way a heart is alive. They move quickly. They make the clicking — the soft, busy clicking of beads shaken in a leather pouch — which is the sound of their many feet and also the sound of their small, busy jaws. They feed at the rims of the cups, chewing at the tissue where the yellow growths sit, and the chewed rim weeps a thin dark sap. The sap is almost black and slow, and it hardens on contact with the air into a brittle red lacquer, and they eat the lacquer. They eat the place's own small wastes. They are the place's least conspicuous inhabitants and among its most constant, and the place does not seem to mind their grazing the way a large animal does not mind the small things that live on its hide.

In the air, though the air is still, there are things that drift. They are paler than the stems, and translucent, and they glow with a light that has no source, the way a deep-sea thing glows, the way the inside of a shell glows when one holds it at a certain angle to the last of the daylight. They drift slowly. They make the papery rustle — the sound of a great many small wings being folded and unfolded in slow rotation, though they have no wings and the air is not moving. They do not seem to eat. They do not seem to do anything except be part of the air and part of the sound. They are the place's small, glowing weather. They are the moving light the way the floor creatures are the moving dark.

And in the crust, in the open spaces between the clusters, there are marks.

The marks are shallow grooves in the floor, not scratched but shaped, the way a tile is shaped before it is fired. They form patterns — concentric rings, long looping script, spirals that come inward toward a center that is always just out of sight, because each pattern ends just before its heart and the next pattern begins. There are also small arrangements: a circle of flat pale stones, a cube of the brittle red lacquer, and a tool — a single long stem, carefully split and hollowed, with a sharp point of a harder mineral bound into the hollow at one end and bound there with more lacquer. The tool is old, or very well made, or both. It is the only tool in the place. Whoever made these marks worked with the floor rather than against it.

What lived there then — what lives there now, in the slow, embedded way a tree lives in the rings of its trunk — was a maker, or was patient, or was a kind of breath the place drew once and has not yet exhaled. The marks remain. The tool lies where it lies. The hum does not stop. The dim light does not change, except that the yellow growths brighten a little when they pulse, and the ceiling growths brighten a little when they take a yellow one in, and then the brightness settles back to its usual dim, shell-colored, almost-not-there.

What lives here, then, is a community, or it is a single creature with many kinds of limb. The stems drink. The yellow growths lift. The ceiling growths absorb and breathe out the dim light. The floor creatures wait and follow and sink. The stem creatures climb and click and eat the place's own red weeping. The drifting creatures glow and rustle. The marks remain. The hum remains. The warm, mineral, iron-tasting air remains. None of them have a name for any of the others, and none of them need one, and the place is alive in the way a river is alive, or a weather, and it does not know it is a place, and it does not mind, and it is very beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are not trying to be.
