What lives in the basin is mostly small, mostly patient, and is in some cases alive in a sense that only barely deserves the word.

The deepest inhabitant is below the floor of the basin, somewhere in the rock that produces the heat and the water. The description does not say what is down there, and the description is correct not to say: the place's origins are not the place's business. What is down there is running — turning, bleeding, or simply persisting at a temperature high enough to keep a body of water hot from below, year after year, on a clock that no visitor can read. Or it is not, and the pulse is just what this dense saturated air does when it finds every metal surface and resonates against it. Either way the basin behaves as though something is running. This is its engine. It does not answer to anyone, and it does not need to. Its pulse is what you feel in the sternum and the teeth, beneath the hiss of the column and the small endless sound of the spill. Everything above it, if there is an it, is exhalation. Everything that lives in the basin is living on that exhalation.

Above the engine, the water. The water is the most constant resident of the place. It enters heated, pressured, mineral-laden, through whatever vent or fracture feeds the basin, spends time in the pool, leaves over the lip as a sheet no thicker than paper. Some fraction of it is older than the shed. Some fraction has cycled through the deep rock and come back. The dissolved solids in it — silica, calcium, whatever the local chemistry is willing to contribute — are part of the basin's character. They are why the rocks at the fluctuating waterline wear the pale stain they wear, why the spillway is white, why metal left in the basin slowly goes gray instead of orange. The water is the medium. The medium is an inhabitant.

In the water, along the fluctuating waterline, in the pale stain the rocks wear, lives the basin's proper biology. A community of thermophiles, or something like thermophiles. Single cells — bacteria, archaea — selected by the simple condition of being able to function in water that would cook anything else into something done. They live in films so thin they read as mineral stain. They are pale because green is wasted here, and because they do not harvest sunlight; they harvest chemistry, taking their energy from sulfur or iron or hydrogen sulfide or whatever the deep source has been quietly metabolizing for them. They do not green the surface. Greens would be wasted. Some of them migrate down out of the heated band as the waterline fluctuates and dry onto the white crust at the lip, where their bodies are incorporated and become a small part of what the crust is. They will be the last inhabitants when the basin finally has its last inhabitant. They are the oldest living residents of the basin in the strict sense — the kind of organism that has been colonizing hot water since hot water first had living colonizers. They are barely visible. They are populous the way cells are populous. They are the layer that fits least in the description's claim that nothing lives here, and the layer that fits most in the description's claim that what does live here does not read as alive.

On the upper walls, in cracks that hold both heat and the moisture the air sweats out of itself — ferns. Pale ferns. Steam-loving, sparse. Probably Adiantum or some local spleenwort; the description is right to leave them unnamed, because the truth of them is not which species they are but that they have, against the long conservatism of ferns, agreed to live here. Their fronds are open, never fully curled, even when the air is hottest; curl is for conserving water, and there is more water in the basin than the ferns can use. Their roots are in cracks that do not see the sun. They lengthen their fronds by a thumb's width across a season. They photosynthesize in the diffuse light that the humidity scatters down into the bowl. They are the most plant-like residents. They are plants.

In the shallows at the far edge, the reeds. Three or four of them. Yellow-green. Slightly bent in a direction that suggests a permanent wind, although there is no wind. Probably a Schoenoplectus, a small Typha, a Cyperus — some ordinary marsh-edge kind drawn into the basin by the warmth and the shallows, tolerating, the way plants tolerate, more than they love. They will grow, slowly. They will seed. They will not prosper. They will be the only residents visibly making a living from the saturated light. They have no answer for why they lean. There is no wind to remember.

On the white mineral crust at the spillway, where the hot sheet of water dries — the fuzz. The description calls it a thing that requires patience to name, and the patience is right. It is a microbial crust, or part-microbial: cyanobacteria, algae, liverwort-like bodies working together in a thin, continuous, pressed skin. It is alive the way a felt is alive — a community of very small things held together by being a community. It builds itself slowly out of mineral, water, and the dim light that filters down. Given enough years it will climb back up the spillway. Given enough centuries it might close over the basin's lip, in the slow pale way that such crusts eventually do. It is on a longer clock than the reeds and a shorter clock than the basalt.

In the shed, and on the pad, are another kind of inhabitant, and these are not alive in any standard sense, but they live here in a way that requires a verb. The steel frame that once bolted down something larger. The coil of cable stiff as rope. The switch panel with its dark toggles, its labels bled into illegibility. The pump, on its pad, holding its lines. The pressure gauge behind its fogged glass, the needle a suggestion. The wheel-valve still mostly green. The concrete of the pad, sharp-cornered. The rebar that has not rusted but has gone gray, into the look of wet slate.

These were once functional inhabitants of the place — they were the basin's original reason. They are now retired inhabitants. They occupy. They persist. They hold their lines. Their chemistry has not yet started its slow argument with time, but it will. The pump will go quiet in its own way. The cable will eventually fray. The labels will become less legible than they are. The concrete will eventually soften at its corners. The rebar will eventually come back to iron. For now they are in the first stage of being ruins, which is being a thing that no longer does anything and does not yet look as if it has stopped. They are patient in the manner of objects. They have the "patient, blank regard" the description gives them — the look of things that are not yet aware they have been forgotten.

The air itself is an inhabitant. The basin holds its own weather — a body of warm, saturated, mineral-heavy air that has weight, and temperature, and content. It condenses on every surface. It beads and re-beads. Its skin is everything you can see in the basin that is wet. It does not move much, but it is not still. It is the medium through which everything else in the basin communicates — heat, smell, humidity, the small shocks of the spill and the column.

And the geometry watches. The round of the basin. The rectangle of the pad. The straight runs of the dead pipes. The plumb of the shed's doorframe. These are not alive. They are patient. They hold the shape of the place against the slow erosion of use, and they look back at any visitor with the regard of objects that do not yet know they have been forgotten. They watch the way a still pool watches — out of its own walls.

A heat source, maybe. A body of water. A community of thermophiles in a film so thin it reads as stain. A few ferns in the upper cracks. Three or four reeds leaning in a direction they will not name. A microbial crust on the spillway. Retired machinery on the pad. A held body of air. Holding geometry. None of them chose. None of them is in a hurry. They are not in competition. They are not cooperating. They are coexistent in the manner of things that have nowhere else to be, and will be this way tomorrow, and the day after, and for some time yet — not forever, but in the way that the patient are forever to the hurried.
