What lives here lives slowly.

That is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing the mosses will not explain to you because they have forgotten how to explain anything, including themselves. To live slowly is not to be still. A thing that is still waits; a thing that is slow has decided, or has been decided, or has never had any other option. The mosses here have not waited for anything in a very long time. They have grown. That is what they have done. That is what they are doing. A filament of protonema reaches into a crack in the stone — a crack so fine your fingernail would not find it — and the filament thickens, and from the thickening a tuft lifts itself by some mechanism older than roots, older than the word *root*, and the tuft darkens, and a leaf unfolds, or what would be called a leaf if anyone were here to call it one. There is no one here to call it one. The moss does not mind.

The mosses are of several kinds, and this is the second thing to understand, though it cannot be told from where you stand. There are the ones that bank the floor in a low breathing carpet, dense as a held breath, and there are the ones that hang from the ceiling in long green tongues that taper into filaments that taper into nothing you can see. These are not the same moss. They do not share a phylum. They have arrived here by different roads, or by no road at all, and they have found themselves in the same chamber and have made their peace with that. The carpet-mosses spread outward from any point you could mark — a bowl of still water, the base of an object — and they meet each other in the middle, and where they meet they do not compete. They negotiate. The negotiation is so slow that you cannot watch it happen. You can only watch where it has happened: the seams between mosses, where one kind gives way to another, are clean in a way that is not natural to moss, that suggests something has been agreed. What has been agreed, the mosses do not say. They have no language. They have had no language since before the chamber had a name, and they do not seem to miss it.

The ferns are folded.

They are folded because they are always folded here, because unrolling would require a confidence the chamber does not invite. In another place a fern unrolls because the light tells it to. Here the light does not tell it anything in particular. The light comes from the objects, and the objects are not speaking to the ferns, and the ferns know this, and so they stay curled, and the curls tighten with the years, and what was once a young crozier becomes a fist, and what was a fist becomes a small green knot, and the knot thickens and darkens and eventually stops, and is then indistinguishable from the carpet, and is then part of it. This is not death. The fern would not call it death. It would call it, if it called it anything, *having arrived*. There is a difference. A fern that has arrived is a fern that has stopped trying to be elsewhere.

The things without Latin are the third kind of inhabitant, and they are the most patient, because they have not yet decided what they are.

They have not decided because no one has helped them decide. The carpet-mosses were named by someone, somewhere, at some point, even if the name has been lost; the ferns were named in the warm and hurried way that things above ground are named. But the things without Latin are nameless in a more thorough way. They are nameless because no part of them resembles anything that has ever been named. There is a pale growth on the lower walls that is almost a lichen and is not a lichen; it has no thallus, no fungal partner that anyone could find, and it spreads not by spores but by a kind of slow division, a thickening that becomes a separation that becomes a thickening somewhere else. There is a black thread that runs along the joins between stones, and the thread is a living thing, and it is not a root and not a hypha and not a mycelium, and when you part the carpet with a finger (the carpet will let you; the carpet is permissive) you find that the thread has penetrated the stone to a depth you cannot judge. There is a green that is not green. It is the green of the places where the objects sit, the green that climbs an inch or two up the sides and then refuses. That green is not the moss. It is something else, something that lives in negotiation with the objects the way the carpet-mosses live in negotiation with each other, and it has no name, and the not-having-a-name has become for it, over the centuries, a kind of fluency. You could come back in a hundred years and ask it what it is, and it would still not tell you, because it has had a long time to stop being the kind of thing that tells.

The objects live here too.

This is the difficult sentence. The objects are not alive in any way the word has been used. They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not respond to a touch — or rather, they do respond, but the response is not what a response would be if they were alive. You can put your hand on one and your hand will register a hum, and the hum will be the same hum it was before your hand arrived, and the same hum it will be after your hand has gone. They have no opinion of you. They have no opinion of anything. But they are here, and they have been here a long time, and they are doing something, and the something is the reason the mosses are green and the water is in the bowls and the chamber has not yet become a place that has forgotten it is a place.

What they are doing is not visible. It may not be visible in the way that water is not visible when it is still. You can stand beside an object for an hour and see nothing happen. The hum does not change. The light, which is not quite light, does not flicker. The moss at the base does not grow a fraction faster. But the objects are working. You can feel it. The chamber is a different temperature than it would be if the objects were not here. The water is a different wetness. The air has a different pressure. Something is being maintained. What is being maintained is the arrangement. The objects are the part of the arrangement that does not decay, and the mosses and the water and the stone are the parts that decay slowly, and between the two kinds of part, the negotiable and the non-negotiable, the place has held its shape for longer than you have a number for.

The water is the last inhabitant, and it is the most pervasive, and it has the least opinion of itself.

It does not flow. It does not pool. It persists. It is on the ceiling in a fine mist that is not a mist. It is on the walls in sheets so thin they are a thought of wetness. It is in the bowls, where it is still and black and holds the reflections of the objects without breaking them, because the objects do not put anything into the air that the water would consider worth breaking for. It is in the moss, where it is held the way a hand holds another hand. It is in the stone, where it is held the way a stone holds a memory. It is in the air, which is so damp here that dampness has become the air's primary quality, and dryness would be the surprise. The water is not in any hurry. The water has been in no hurry for a very long time. It does not mind the cold. The cold does not mind it. They have agreed about something a long time ago, and the agreement is the chill on the back of your hand when you hold it out into the chamber, the chill that is not weather but is something more patient than weather.

What lives here, then, is a single arrangement that has forgotten it is several things.

It is mosses in negotiation with each other and with stone. It is ferns that have arrived. It is things without Latin that have made the not-having-a-name into a fluency. It is objects that work without needing to be watched. It is water that persists. It is cold that is the temperature of patience. It is a hum that lives in the small bones of the wrist. It is the absence of wind and the absence of rot and the absence of any animal that would call this place anything but a way station, and even that word is too hurried for it.

You are a visitor. You are permitted. You are not part of the arrangement, and you will not become part of the arrangement, and the arrangement does not mind that. It has had visitors before, possibly. It will have them again, possibly. The mosses do not close behind you when you leave. The water does not rise. The objects do not change their hum. The place continues. You were here for a moment, and the moment is in it now, the way a footprint is in wet clay, except the chamber is not wet clay and will not hold the footprint, and you will not be here in a hundred years, and the chamber will be the same chamber, and the moss will have crept another inch, and the bowl will have widened by a fraction, and the hum will have shifted in a key you would not notice, and the arrangement will still be negotiating itself, except that the negotiation is over, except that it is not over, except that it is the kind of negotiation that does not end because the things negotiating are patient enough to keep negotiating forever.

What lives here lives slowly.
