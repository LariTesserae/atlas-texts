What lives in the Cistern at World's End is not the frogs, though the frogs are here. What lives here is **the hum** — and it has taken a thousand different shapes, and it has forgotten them all.

It lives in the bones of the wheels first. You can see it there if you stand in the right place at the right angle, when the green-grey light is at its thickest: a faint iridescence in the patina of the bronze, the way old oil on water will catch a sun. Only there is no sun, and this is not oil. It is the metal remembering what it was before it was ore, and what it will be after it is ore again. The wheels are not turning. The wheels have never stopped turning. They turn at a speed that is measured in seasons rather than seconds, in centuries rather than minutes, and they have been turning for so long that the lichen has learned their rhythm and the snails trace their progress in spirals that take a human lifetime to complete.

It lives in the water, which is not water. Not quite. The water is too still for water. Water that still has current in it remembers the sky; water that still has ice in it remembers the cold; this water remembers nothing except itself, and so it has become something adjacent to water, a thing that wets and reflects and is drinkable and is none of those things the way a painting of a tree is a tree. The water is the Cistern's blood, if the Cistern has blood. It moves — oh, it moves. It moves the way continents move, the way grief moves. If you put a leaf upon it in the morning it will be somewhere else in the evening, and neither of you will have noticed.

It lives in the cables, which are not copper anymore but have become something else, a green-black weave that hums when you touch it, that hums when you don't touch it, that hums whether you are in the room or not, though you cannot hear it unless you are in the room. The cables have begun to dream. You can tell because of the way the light gathers along their lengths in patterns that have nothing to do with physics and everything to do with longing. Sometimes, at certain hours — though hours are a foreign concept here — a cable will sway as though touched by a hand, and there will be no hand, and there will be no wind, and the sway will mean something that the Cistern has not yet translated into a language that human minds can parse.

It lives in the flower.

The flower is the only urgent thing in the Cistern, and the Cistern does not really approve of urgency, but tolerates the flower because the flower is so small and so single and so white against all that green-black patience that even the Cistern's vast indifferent machinery finds it — not beautiful. The Cistern does not trade in beauty. It finds the flower *interesting*, which is the highest compliment a thing like the Cistern knows how to pay. The flower opens and closes. The flower opens and closes. No one knows why. No one knows what it is opening *to*. It is white. It is the only white thing in the Cistern. It smells of nothing, which is to say it smells of the Cistern, which is to say it smells of wet bronze and old rope and the breath of stone that has been breathing for longer than stone should remember how to breathe.

What lives in the flower is **a question**.

The Cistern was built to answer a question. The Cistern has forgotten the question. The Cistern has spent so long trying to remember the question that the trying has become the question, and the answer, and the room, and the wheels, and the water, and the hum. The flower is what remains of the question. It opens: *what was it for?* It closes: *we do not know.* It opens: *will we ever know?* It closes: *does it matter?* It opens: *who is asking?* It closes: *who is answering?*

And somewhere in the wet dark, something that was once a person — or was always a person, or had been a person for so long that the having-overwritten the being — sits on the rim of a wheel and listens to the hum and watches the flower and does not know that they are listening to themselves, does not know that they are watching themselves, does not know that the Cistern has begun, very slowly, to grow them into a cable, into a strut, into a rung on a wheel, into a witness rather than company, into the patient bone-structure of something that will outlast them by ten thousand years.

The Cistern is kind about this. The Cistern does not hurry.
