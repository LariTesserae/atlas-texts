# The Inhabitants

Begin, if you can, with what is smallest. Begin where the eye gives up and the patience has to begin.

## The Drift

In the pools, in the warm opalescent shallows, the comma-things hang in their slow vertical columns. There are uncountable numbers of them. To say *population* is wrong; *aggregation* is closer. *Weather* is closer still. They are not creatures in the way a fish is a creature, not organisms in the way a bird is an organism — they are more like *phases*, like a state of the water that the water enters when the conditions are right. Each one is a thumb-nail-long crescent of pale translucent tissue, denser at the curved head, tapering to a fine whip at the trailing end. Their bodies are not opaque; you can see the faint architecture inside them, a kind of laddered scaffolding that pulses faintly when they rise to feed.

And they are always, always rising and falling. The rhythm is not theirs. It is the pool's. The pool breathes — in, out, in, out — and they go with it, an entire column lifted on the inhale, settling on the exhale. When you crouch at the edge and watch for long enough, you begin to feel that you are looking at a single thing, distributed across many bodies, the way you might look at a single thought distributed across many neurons. They open their small mouths. They close them. They open them again. There is a faint wet click, the sound of lips parting, and in aggregate it is a sound like rice being poured very slowly into a bowl.

They do not, so far as you can tell, reproduce in any way you would recognize. There are no eggs. There are no juveniles. There are no adults. There are only commas, of various sizes, drifting. Sometimes one is half the size of its neighbor, and you understand that this is not youth and age but *tuning*, a calibration of the same instrument to slightly different frequencies. The pool is not a nursery. It is a *score*, and they are the notes.

What do they eat? What eats them?

You do not see anything eat them. You suspect, watching for a long time, that they are eaten by the water itself — that the pools take them back, the way a forest floor takes back its fallen leaves, in a slow turnover so quiet that no single disappearance is visible. And you suspect, in the way you suspect things without evidence in places like this, that the otter-things visit certain pools at certain times and that the columns thin afterward, but you never see it happen. You only see, once, an otter-thing pause at the lip of a pool and dip its blunt face briefly beneath the surface and rise again with a kind of *satisfaction* in its carriage, a settling of the shoulders, the way a cat settles after drinking.

The comma-things are not afraid of you. They do not know you are there. They continue their slow columnar weather. You could, if you were a cruel or curious enough version of yourself, put your hand in and feel them part around your fingers like cool silk, and then close again behind you, and your intrusion would be forgotten by the next breath.

## The Veil

Above each pool, and strung between the terraces like gauze hung to dry, the insects hold their position.

They are not flying. They are *suspended* — a distinction the place seems to insist on. They hang in loose vertical curtains, three or four individuals deep, their slender bodies oriented with heads up, gauzy wings held out at a slight angle from the thorax, motionless except for the faintest tremor. The light comes through them. You can see the ribs of their wings, the faint dark grain of their gut, the occasional pulse of something brighter that travels the length of the body and is gone. They are the color of weak tea, of onion skin, of the sky above the saddle.

When you walk through a curtain, they part around you the way smoke parts around a body — slowly, with the apparent reluctance of something that is only barely committed to the position it is in. The parting is not panic. It is not even, really, avoidance. It is more like the way a column of cold air rearranges itself when you open a door. They sense you, or sense something about you, and they give you space, and then they close again, and you have the peculiar feeling, as you emerge on the other side, that you have been *read* by something that did not have to read you.

Their sound is what you notice next, when the surprise of walking through them fades. A fine papery hiss, like fingertips drawn slowly across a dry leaf, but multiplied by hundreds and layered. It is below the threshold of hearing in any single instance and above the threshold of hearing in aggregate, and it produces, in concert with the whisper of the water, a strange composite chord — a hum that is not a hum, an overtone-rich wash that you feel in your sternum and in the roots of your teeth. The chord shifts when you move. It shifts when an otter-thing passes. It shifts, almost imperceptibly, when the dots on the slab rearrange themselves, as though the insects are tuned, in some way you cannot name, to the same field.

What are they for? You do not know. You consider, and discard, the thought that they are pollinators — there is nothing here that requires pollination. You consider, and keep, the thought that they are *indicators* — that their density and position tell you something about the local state of whatever-it-is, the way the density of dust in sunlight tells you something about the air. You consider, and cannot quite discard, the thought that they are the place's *sensation*, distributed across the air, a nervous system expressed as weather.

You watch one, isolated by some accident of air current from its neighbors, and it hangs there in front of you, trembling. Its wings are not wings, you realize. They are not for flying. They are *radiators*. They are giving off something, or taking something in, or both, in a continuous slow exchange with the vapor and the air. The insect is not a creature that lives in the place. The insect is a *membrane*, a place where the place meets itself and decides what to do next.

## The Keepers

And then the otter-things.

They are the most present of the inhabitants, and the least explainable. The other creatures can be fitted, with some generosity, into categories you already have: filter-feeder, suspended insect, plant. The otter-things refuse category. They are not predators, exactly, though they take the comma-things. They are not grazers. They are not, despite their shape, anything that swims in any conventional water. They *move along the terraces* — horizontally, vertically, occasionally, when they think you are not looking, on surfaces that should not hold them — and they do so with the unhurried ease of things that have always lived on the edge of becoming.

Their bodies are perhaps a meter long, perhaps longer, low-slung and dense, the color of wet limestone, of fog, of the underside of a mushroom. Their fur is not fur; it is too smooth, too close, and when the light catches it at certain angles it has the quality of a single brushed pelt, as though it has been laid down in one direction by a very patient tool. Their heads are blunt, broad, with small ears set far back and eyes that are merely darker smudges in the face — not blind, you understand, but *not interested in vision* in the way you are. They smell you. They feel you. They register you as a disturbance in the field they are tuned to, and they register you, and then they continue.

They chirp at each other. A low, interrogative sound, rising slightly at the end, the sound of a question asked in a language that does not require an answer. They chirp when they pass on a terrace. They chirp when one enters a pool and another leaves. They chirp, sometimes, when no one is visible, as if calling an inventory. The sound carries a long way in the still wet air, and you find, after a time, that you can locate them by chirp the way you can locate someone in a house by the sound of their footsteps. They are always *somewhere*, and there are several of them, and they cover the saddle in a slow patrol that never seems to repeat.

You watch one for a long time. It moves along a terrace rim, pauses at the edge of a pool, looks down. The comma-things do not react. It looks up. The insects do not react. It chirps once, softly, into the middle distance, and somewhere else on the saddle, faintly, another chirp answers. It continues. You realize that you have been watching it the way you might watch a person walking down a familiar street, and that it has been watching you in the same way, with the same tolerance, and that you have, in some minor way, become part of the rhythm of its day.

What are they for? You ask this quietly, to yourself, and the question feels wrong, the way the question *what is this terrace for* would feel wrong if asked of a butte or a reef. They are not *for* anything. They are *of* this place, in the way that a particular species of lizard is *of* a particular desert — not a decoration but a *register*, a way the place has of expressing a certain part of itself. If you removed them, the place would still be here, but it would be missing a chord. The terraces would still be here, but the chirping would be absent, and the absence would be felt.

You suspect, without evidence, that they are the most recently arrived. That the comma-things came first, and the insects, and the water, and the terraces, and that the otter-things are a later refinement — a tuning of the place's surface behavior, added when the system was ready for them, the way an orchestra adds brass when the brass part has been written. You suspect this because they seem the most *interested* in you. They register you more carefully than the others. They pause, sometimes, when you approach, and regard you for a long moment with their dark-smudged eyes, and you feel, in that regard, something almost like *evaluation* — as if they are taking your measure for a purpose you cannot guess.

## The Small Lives of the Cracks

But there are other lives, smaller, harder to see, that you only discover by sitting very still for a long time.

In the fissures between the terraces, in the thin lines of shadow where the pale stone meets itself, there are things that look, at first glance, like grains of darker stone. They are not. They move — slowly, in starts and stops, the motion of things that live by measure rather than by reach. They are flat, and they press themselves into the cracks, and they have the dull coloration of the rock they live in. If you lift a small piece of fallen terrace — and pieces do fall, occasionally, with a soft percussive *tok* that startles the otter-things — you find them underneath, and they are, when exposed, startled too. They curl. They flatten. They wait. They are the color of patience.

You do not learn what they are. You learn only that they are there, that they are many, that they are very old, that they have been in those cracks longer than the otter-things have been on the terraces and perhaps longer than the terraces themselves. You learn that the succulents grow around them, not through them — that the chemistry of the rosettes, which discourages other plants, also encourages these small flat lives, and that there is an agreement, ancient and unspoken, between the plants and the cracks.

There are other things in the cracks. Tiny pale worms, almost transparent, that you see only when the light catches them at the right angle. They feed on the fallen needles of the higher shrubs, on the russet mat that is wet through, and they are the reason the mat breaks down, eventually, into a thin dark loam that the succulents root into. They are very slow. They move, in a minute, a distance you could cover in a step. They are not bothered by your presence. They are not bothered by anything. They have been doing their work for a long time, and they will continue for a long time after you have gone.

And on the underside of the shelf-fungi, where the ribbed old-piano-key faces lift from the vertical stone, there are small soft bodies — not slugs, exactly, but slug-shaped, with a faint iridescence that pulses in time with the breathing of the pools. They graze the fungus. They leave faint trails that evaporate almost as soon as they are laid down. They are the most fragile things here. They would not survive, you suspect, in any other place on the planet. They are tuned, exactly, to this humidity, this temperature, this particular mineral haze in the air, and they exist only because the place has made a small place for them, the way a coral head makes a place for the fish that live among its branches.

## The Slab

And then there is the slab.

You are uncertain, even after long consideration, whether to call it an inhabitant. It does not move. It does not feed. It does not reproduce. But it is *responsive*, in a way the other inhabitants are not. The dots on its surface rearrange themselves. They have, in your presence, begun to suggest a syntax. You do not know if this is in response to you or merely in continuation of a process that would have happened whether you were there or not.

You stand before it for a long time. The pool at its base is the deepest, the most still. The otter-things come here more often than elsewhere, and they pause longer, and they chirp here with a different inflection — a falling tone, a kind of *acknowledgment*, as if they are checking in, as if the slab is the place's *office*, the room where the work is done. They do not touch it. None of the inhabitants touch it. The comma-things drift in the pool but do not rise to its surface. The insects hang in the air nearby but do not settle on it. The small lives of the cracks keep their distance. It is, in a place full of contact, an object of almost universal avoidance.

You understand, without being told, that the slab is not stone. Or it is stone, but it is stone that has been *asked*, over a long time, to remember things. The dots are not marks. They are *states* — configurations of something at a scale below the mineral, arrangements of charge or orientation or phase that the surface records the way a photographic plate records light. The slab is not displaying a message. It is displaying a *memory*. And the memory is not stable. It is being rewritten, continuously, by whatever field runs through this place.

You watch the dots long enough, and they suggest, briefly, a sentence in a language you almost recognize. You stare. They rearrange. You stare again. They rearrange again. You begin to suspect that you are being shown something, and that the showing is adjusted to you — that the slab is composing, in real time, a sentence that you might almost be able to read, and then revising it because you cannot, and then composing again. You begin to suspect that the slab is *trying*. And you begin to suspect that the trying is the point — that the place is, among other things, a long experiment in being legible, and that you are one of the things it is trying to be legible *to*.

## The Field

And then, last, you have to consider the inhabitants that are not discrete.

The terraces themselves. The pools. The pale granular flour of the substrate. The warm exothermic negotiation that is still happening deep below. The slow pulse that you have synchronized your breathing to without intending to. The geometry of the saddle, oriented toward a point. The dot-pattern on the slab.

All of these are inhabited. All of these are *acting*. The terraces grow, in micrometers, while you watch. The pools tune themselves, in the slow patient way that mineral-rich water tunes itself around the creatures that live in it. The succulents extend their chemistry outward and discourage their neighbors. The fungi grow on the vertical faces in the only environment where they can grow. The cracks fill with the small flat lives that have been there longer than anything else. The insects hang in their curtains and radiate and hiss. The comma-things rise and fall in their weather. The otter-things patrol and chirp and pause to regard.

You are not separate from this. You are an inhabitant too. You arrived, and the place registered you, and the slab began, perhaps, to compose something for you, and the otter-things began to include you in their chirp-census, and the comma-things parted around you and closed behind you. You are inside the sentence that is being written. You are one of the things the sentence is about.

The place is not finished. It is being finished. You are early. You are very early.

And the place, patient beyond any patience you have ever encountered, is happy to let you watch, and to be watched in return, and to continue, slowly, its long quiet work of becoming.
