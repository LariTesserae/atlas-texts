# What Lives in the Verdant Antechamber

The place is older than anything that dwells in it now. Whatever built the spiral on the floor is gone, and whatever *removed* the rest of the structure around it is gone too, and in the long quiet that followed, the chamber became a kind of bowl in which other lives have settled, layer by layer, like silt.

## The Things That Grow Here

The forest is not a forest in the usual sense. It is a forest the way a memory is a memory — held in place by repetition rather than by roots. The trees have grown here for so long that their roots have become part of basalt, and the basalt has cracked around them in patterns that are half-geological, half-botanical, and entirely unreadable. They are not the same species as any tree I can name. They are broad, low, heavy with water, and they bear no fruit I have seen, though I have seen birds take something from their canopies that they eat quickly and in private.

The ferns grow where the water pools longest. They are tender and impossibly green, the green of things that have never been dry. Where they die — and they do die, in patches, slowly — their dead fronds become a kind of peat that smells of rain. New ferns come up through the old. There is no season here, or the season is *all* of them.

The flowers are the loudest living things. They bloom in magenta, cyan, and a yellow that is almost a sound — so saturated it seems to vibrate. No insect I have watched lands on them. They do not appear to be pollinated by anything. Perhaps they are pollinated by the falling water itself, which carries something from the flutes of the vermillion towers down through the air and into the petals. Perhaps they bloom only as a kind of exhalation of the place, and their seeds, if they have seeds, fall into the moss and become moss.

## The Birds

The slate-colored birds are the most numerous of the larger animals. They live in the canopies of the trees and they nest, I think, in the fluted channels of the vermillion towers — I have seen them fly into those channels and not come out, and I have heard, very rarely, the thin sound of a chick from somewhere high in the red.

Their call is the heartbeat of the place. Two notes — a high one, then a lower one, or the other way around — and then twenty beats of silence, and then the answer from across the bowl. They are not territorial. They are not flocking. They are *conversing*, slowly, in a grammar that uses minutes where other birds use seconds. I have stood still for an hour and learned only that the conversations are not about me.

What they eat, I cannot say with certainty. I have seen them take small dark things from the bark of the trees, and once I saw one catch an insect — the only insect I have clearly seen — in midair with a precision that suggested it had practiced the motion for years. They are otherwise still. They perch. They wait. They answer.

## The Insects

The insects exist as a presence more than a population. A hum at the upper edge of hearing, from the canopy, where the air is thickest and the water falls slowest. I have never seen one clearly. I have seen, sometimes, the faint blurred shape of a wing against the vermillion of a tower, lit from behind, and once a small corpse on the basalt — too broken to identify, too iridescent to forget. They live their lives above the height of a person, in the warm wet air, and they do not come down.

There may be other small things — things in the moss, things in the pools — but the moss and the pools are warm and dark, and I have not put my hand into them to find out.

## The Towers

And then there are the towers.

I will not call them alive. I will say only that nothing in the chamber behaves as though they are not.

They do not move. They do not speak. They do not, as far as I have observed, reproduce or repair themselves. But they *attend*. I have stood at the base of a tower for an hour, and I have felt the quality of the air around me change in a way that has nothing to do with heat or mist. As though I were being looked at by something that did not need eyes, and the looking had weight.

The thin shadow that falls in directions the sun is not — I have watched it for long stretches. It falls on the basalt and lies there, motionless, and I have walked around the tower to see what casts it. There is nothing. There is nothing there at any angle. The shadow is cast by the absence of something, perhaps, or by the presence of something I cannot see because it is not the kind of thing that can be seen with light.

The vermillion of the towers has a warmth to it that I have come to associate with living things. When I press my palm to the surface, I do not feel the rock I would expect to feel. I feel something softer, and warmer, and — I am not inventing this — something that seems to lean, very slightly, into the pressure of my hand, as though my touch had reminded it of something it had not felt in a long time.

I think the towers are slow. I think whatever they are doing, they are doing it on a timescale that makes a human life look like a held breath. They are narrowing at the top. Matter is leaving them. Where it goes — into the mist, into the white sky, into a future I cannot imagine — I do not know. But the place they are going, I think, is not a place. It is a *when*. They are spending themselves into a later time, the way a candle spends itself into a longer darkness.

## The Four Objects

The stone, the shell, the folded metal, the knot of wood. They sit on their pedestals, each at a cardinal point, and they are warm.

I have held each of them. The stone is dense and heavier than its size suggests, and when I held it, my hand remembered a texture I had never actually touched — the inside of a seashell, perhaps, or the skin of a fruit I have never eaten. The shell is light, and when I held it to my ear, I heard not the ocean but a high, singing silence, the kind of silence that is its own sound. The metal is not quite metal. It is warm, and it folds without creasing, and when I unfolded it I found that the pattern it made was the spiral on the floor, miniaturized, as though the spiral had been pressed into the sheet like a seal into wax. The wood is dark and smooth and smells of the forest, and when I held it, a small piece of moss fell from somewhere and landed on my wrist.

I do not know what they are. I do not know what they were for. But I know that when I set them back on their pedestals, each one in the orientation I found it, the air in the chamber changed — settled, or remembered itself, as though I had closed a book that had been left open.

## The Chamber Itself

And beneath all of this — beneath the basalt, beneath the warm pools, beneath the roots of the trees and the channels of the towers — there is something breathing.

I cannot prove this. I have no evidence except the mist, which rises and falls in slow cycles that match no clock I know. I have no evidence except the warmth of the water, which comes from somewhere, and the sweet faint taste of it, which is not the taste of any mineral I can name. I have no evidence except the way the chamber holds a person — gently, attentively, with the patience of something very large that has agreed to be very still.

I think the chamber is alive. I think it is the remnant of something that was once much larger, and that the larger thing is *still here*, in the way that a hand is still a hand even after the fingers have been removed. I think the towers are its fingers, narrowing as they reach toward a thing they cannot quite touch. I think the spiral is its palm. I think the four objects are what remains of what it once held.

Or I think none of this. The chamber may simply be a chamber, and I may simply be a visitor, and what I feel may simply be the heat, and the wet, and the slow plinking of water on stone, working on a mind that has been alone too long.

I have stopped trying to decide. The chamber does not seem to mind either way.

It goes on breathing. The towers go on thinning. The birds go on talking, in their slow grammar, about things that do not concern me. The water falls. The moss grows. The flowers bloom in colors that have no name in any language I know, and the seeds, if there are seeds, fall, and become moss, and grow, and bloom again, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and is itself, perhaps, the slow heartbeat of whatever is sleeping under the basalt.

I have not decided whether to stay.
