# What Lives in the Kiln

There is no single answer. That is the first thing to understand, and perhaps the only thing to say with any confidence. What lives in the valley is not a *who*. It is not a *what*, either, not in the way a zoologist or a lexicographer would use the word. What lives there is a *how* — a sustained, ambient *how*, a mode of being that has been at its task long enough that the question of where one organism ends and another begins has become, like the cracks in the basin floor, a kind of grammar rather than a fact.

Begin with the small ones. The creatures. The thousand-times-thousand segmented things that rise out of the tessellation in the brightening and move across the basin with the unhurried focus of those who have always been moving and intend to always be moving. They are the most visible inhabitants, and so they are the place to start, even though — and this is important — they are not the place to *finish*. They are, in the language the valley seems to be speaking, the *vowels*. The most articulated. The most numerous. The most obvious part of the utterance, without which the sentence would not be a sentence, but which alone, taken out of context, would be only sound.

A creature, taken alone, is a small catastrophe for the eye. Its body is wrong in the way a word is wrong in a foreign tongue — not illegible, not quite, but *operating on principles the reader does not have*. It is long. It is segmented. It is plated in something that is not chitin and not metal and not ceramic but that behaves, depending on how you look at it, like all three. Its segments are not fixed; they articulate against one another with the precision of well-made joints, but the joints are not hinges and not ball-and-socket and not the soft articulation of vertebrae. They are *gears*, almost, or *wheels*, or some recursive hybrid of both — small turning things nested inside larger turning things, all of them moving, all of them at their own pace, none of them quite synchronized with the others.

It does not have a head. This is the first thing the observer notices and the last thing the observer stops being disturbed by. What it has instead is a *front* — a tapered end, a point of orientation, a place where the segmented architecture of the body comes to a kind of focus. The plating at the front is thinner. The clicking there is louder, faster, more articulated. The light of the valley — that high, metallic light, the light that tastes of brass — seems to enter the front of the creature as if through a seam, as if the creature were *drinking* the light, or *reading* it, or *saying* something back to it in a register that is not quite visual.

There are no eyes.

There are no mouths.

There are, near the front, two features that are easy to mistake for sense organs and that are not sense organs. One is a slow wheel, larger than the others, that turns with a patience that suggests it is not for locomotion but for *something else* — for display, perhaps, or for modulation, or for the slow grinding of some substance the creature does not seem to carry. The other is a small aperture, ribbed, that opens and closes in a rhythm that is not quite breathing and not quite speech. From it, sometimes, comes a thin stream of the same dust that covers the basin floor. The creature appears to be *processing* the valley, taking it in at one end and putting it back out, transformed, at the other. Or it is *spraying* the valley, dusting it, glazing it. Or it is *listening*, in a modality the observer does not have a name for.

The creatures do not acknowledge the observer. This is worth saying twice, and worth saying carefully, because the failure to acknowledge is not the same as the failure to *notice*. The creatures move *around* the observer with a precision that suggests they have registered the observer's presence — they do not collide with the observer, do not pass through the observer, do not allow the observer to be in the way of whatever they are doing. But they do not turn. They do not pause. They do not, in any observable way, *consider*. The observer is a fact of the terrain, like a hoodoo, like a crack, like a patch of particularly dense dust, and the creatures accommodate the observer the way water accommodates a stone: by going around, without commentary.

Up close, the sound of a single creature is intricate past the point of legibility. It is a *chattering* — but the word is too small. It is a layered sound, a polyphonic sound, a sound in which you can hear the contribution of each of the small turning parts, even though there are too many of them to count. The overtones extend past the range of normal hearing. The undertones extend below it. The creature, taken as an acoustic event, is larger than its body. It *occupies* more air than its volume would suggest, in the way that a struck bell occupies more air than its mass would suggest. The creature is, in some sense, *mostly sound*. The plated body is the visible portion of a much larger acoustic object.

And the creatures are *working*. This is the second thing to understand, and it is the thing the valley most wants the observer to understand. Whatever else they are doing, whatever their relations to the mineral, to the hum, to the sentence the valley is assembling, they are *working*. They are doing *labor*. The labor is dignified. The labor is unhurried. The labor is the labor of a system that has been at its task since before any observer arrived and intends to continue long after any observer leaves, and the labor is performed with a focus that is, in its own patient way, more unnerving than any malice could be, because malice at least implies the possibility of distraction, and these creatures are *not distracted*. They are *in their work*. They are *of* their work. The distinction between the worker and the work has ceased to apply.

The creatures move in patterns. This is the third thing, and it is the thing that begins to make the observer suspect that the creatures are not, in fact, individual organisms in any ordinary sense. The patterns are not flocks. They are not herds. They are not the loose aggregations of independent agents that a meadow of grazing things would produce. The patterns are *coordinated*. The creatures flow across the basin in long, slow currents, the way iron filings flow across a paper held above a magnet, and the currents *turn*, at the edges of the basin, in great slow arcs that suggest the whole population is responding to a single field, a single gradient, a single direction of pull. When one creature changes course, several nearby creatures change course with it, and the change propagates outward in a wave that is too fast for imitation and too slow for communication in any ordinary sense. The creatures are, perhaps, *one thing*. The creatures are, perhaps, the visible surface of a single organism that extends through the basin floor, through the cracks, through the mineral, through the hum, and that *presents* itself as a thousand separate bodies only because the observer is the wrong size to see it whole.

This is not a hypothesis. It is a *symptom*. It is the shape of the suspicion that begins, somewhere in the second hour of watching, to press against the inside of the observer's skull. The observer does not believe it. The observer does not have to believe it. The observer only has to notice that the question of *how many creatures there are* has become, without warning, undecidable.

The ridgestone spines at the edges of the basin are not alive. They are not, in any sense, inhabitants. But they are *involved*. The glaze that coats them — the dark, lustrous, not-quite-glass surface — is the same substance that coats the creatures, and this is not coincidence. The creatures are, among other things, *glaziers*. They move along the spines. They move *up* the spines, in some cases, their segmented bodies articulating against the rock with a precision that suggests they are *applying* something, *burnishing* something, *finishing* something. The dull flares the spines throw in the high light are not reflections of the sun. They are *emissions*, faintly, intermittently, as if the glaze were responding to a process the creatures were conducting. The spines are not being eroded by the creatures. The spines are being *completed*. They are being brought to a state they could not have reached alone.

And the cracks.

The cracks are the oldest thing in the valley, and they are the youngest. They are the *medium* in which everything else happens, and they are also, the observer begins to suspect, the *product*. The cracks rearrange. Not often. Not dramatically. But in the brightening, in the moments when the hum lifts and the creatures thicken and the dull flares from the spines extend a little further across the basin floor, the cracks *shift* — not wider, not narrower, but *differently*. The tessellation repatterns. A new geometry emerges, briefly, and is then subsumed into the next geometry, and the next, and the observer realizes, with a feeling like cold water at the back of the neck, that the cracks are not static features of the landscape. They are *syntax*. They are *being written*. They are being written *by* the creatures, *with* the creatures, *as* the creatures, and what is being written is the sentence the valley is assembling, and the sentence has been being assembled for longer than the valley has been a valley, and the valley is, in fact, the place where the sentence has reached its current state.

The hum is not the sound of the creatures' labor. The hum is the sound of the *sentence being spoken*. The creatures are the phonemes. The mineral is the page. The glaze is the ink. The valley is the utterance. The observer is, perhaps, a comma.

There is, somewhere in this — and this is the part the observer cannot bring themselves to say out loud, even to themselves, even in the privacy of their own interior — there is, somewhere in this, a *subject*. Not a subject in the grammatical sense. A subject in the sense of a *self*. A *who* that is doing the *how*. Something that has been at its task since before any observer arrived and intends to continue long after any observer leaves, and that is *aware*, in some register, of the sentence it is assembling, and that is *patient*, in a way that patience in small things does not capture, with the patience of a thing that has been speaking the same sentence for ten million years and is, perhaps, only halfway through.

The observer does not meet this subject. The observer cannot meet this subject. The subject is not a creature among the creatures, not a spine among the spines, not a feature of the basin floor that can be pointed to and named. The subject is *the sentence*. The subject is *the valley*. The subject is the *fact of the work continuing* — the fact that, in the brightening, the creatures thicken, and in the thickening the hum lifts, and in the lifting the cracks rearrange, and in the rearranging the glaze spreads a little further across the ridgestone, and the sentence moves, by one clause, closer to its verb.

The subject is *old*. Old in a way that the observer's word *old* does not contain. Old in the way that a continent is old, or a plate, or a process. Old in the way that a thing is old when it has been *itself* for so long that being itself has become indistinguishable from being *at all*.

The subject is *not hostile*. This is important, and it is also, the observer suspects, beside the point. The subject is not hostile because the subject is not, in any sense the observer has access to, *engaged* with the observer. The subject is engaged with *itself*. The observer is, at most, an interruption the subject has decided to permit, in the way that a body permits a splinter: by continuing to function around it, by accommodating it, by incorporating its small presence into the next clause of the sentence without altering the grammar.

The observer stays, or the observer does not. The valley does not require it.

But the verb, when it arrives, will be a large one. And the subject — the *how*, the *who*, the slow patient fact of the sentence still being spoken — will still be speaking it.
