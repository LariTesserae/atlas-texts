# The Inhabitants of the Scree Plateau of the Long Echoes

The plateau is not empty. The eye says it is—the eye, trained on the lush and the vertical, finds nothing here that it knows how to read. But the eye is wrong, or rather, the eye is honest only about the surface. The plateau's life is mostly underneath, mostly between, mostly in the language of small motions and long patience. The creatures who live here are not the kind one names from a field guide. They are the kind one learns by spending years of silence in one place, watching, until the place begins to trust you enough to show itself.

To begin with the grykes.

## The Crack-Dwellers

The grykes are a second country, a vertical one, and they have their own citizens.

The most numerous are the **slate lizards**, though that name is a kindness; they are not really lizards in the way a child draws a lizard. They are long and low and gray, the gray of pencil lead, the gray of the stone itself, and they have evolved to disappear into it. Their scales are not glossy but matte, so that even when the sun hits them at the wrong angle they do not flash. Their bellies are paler, almost the cream of the limestone, and when they press flat against a sun-warmed plate they vanish. The only way one knows they are there is the small click of claws releasing as they startle, and then the flicker of motion vanishing into a fracture, and then a long stillness, and then nothing. They eat the insects that live in the cracks. They are eaten, sometimes, by the fox. They drink from the condensation that beads on the undersides of overhanging plates in the early mornings, and they have learned to position themselves in the grykes in a particular way: head up, body pressed into the angle of the crack, so that the drops fall directly onto the flat of the skull, where the skin is thinnest and the water is taken in through capillary action. A person watching long enough might see one lift its head at the right moment, as if in prayer. It is not prayer. It is thirst.

The **beetles** are a quieter kingdom. They move in patient lines along the floors of the grykes, glossy black and slow, processing the windblown scraps of organic matter that fall into the cracks—pollen, dead insects, the occasional seed carried in on a bird's foot. They do not hurry. There is nothing to hurry toward. A beetle here may live for several years, which is an enormous span for its size, and it spends most of that time doing what looks like nothing, which is in fact the slow, methodical work of digestion and waiting. There are species here that exist nowhere else, specialists in the chemistry of limestone, their gut bacteria evolved to break down the calcium-rich dust that accumulates in the cracks. Remove one from the plateau and it dies within days, not from anything dramatic, but from a kind of digestive confusion, as if it cannot remember how to be alive without that particular stone.

The **winged things** are the small dark birds, and they are the most audible of the gryke-dwellers. They nest in the deeper cracks, building nothing more elaborate than a scrape lined with a few feathers of their own. Their feathers are thick, layered, almost woolly, an insulation against the cold nights, and they are the color of the stone, brown-gray with paler underparts. They are not songbirds. They have a voice that is pitched to carry across open rock without dissipating—a short, sharp call, repeated, that sounds more like a question than an answer. They hunt by sight, swooping low across the plates to snatch beetles and the larvae that feed in the cushion plant roots, and they can fly in the grykes themselves, in the narrow channels of still air between the stone walls, navigating by some sense the human eye cannot follow. They are not afraid of people, exactly; they simply do not recognize people as a category. A human is, to them, a large and slow-moving disturbance, not a predator, not a prey, not a thing with a meaning. They fly around a human as water flows around a stone.

Then there are the **larvae**, the **springtails**, the **mites**, the whole hidden phylum of small soft things that live in the dark of the cracks and never see the sun. They are white or pale pink, eyeless, and they feed on the roots of the cushion plants and on the fungal threads that thread through whatever soil has accumulated at the bottom of the grykes. The plateau is, in this sense, fed by its own underside. The plants above feed the things below, and the things below feed the soil that feeds the plants above, and the rain, when it comes, briefly knits the two together.

## The Pale Fox

The **fox** is the largest thing here, and the most watched, though it watches back harder than any creature on the plateau.

It is smaller than its lowland cousins—perhaps two-thirds the size, with a more compact body, shorter legs, ears reduced to small triangles to minimize heat loss, and a tail that is thick and used as a wrap rather than a flag. Its fur is the cream of the limestone, with a faint gray wash along the back, and when it lies in a sun-warmed gryke with its tail over its nose, it is functionally invisible. It does not need to be invisible to the other creatures here; they are small and the fox is large; it hunts them anyway, by sound and by the slight disturbance of air as they move. Its ears are tuned to the frequencies of beetle-clicks and lizard-scrapes. It does not pounce so much as wait, motionless, until something moves, and then it moves faster than the eye can follow.

The fox is mostly solitary. A pair will share a hunting range, and a vixen will raise kits in a particularly deep gryke, but the adults do not linger together. There is not enough food here to support a pack, and there is not enough cover to hide one. The fox that lives on the plateau is a kind of monk, patient and thin, with ribs that show when it runs and a coat that looks rough in the lean months and luxurious in the brief good ones after a rain.

It drinks, like the lizards, from the condensation basins in the deeper grykes, and it knows where these basins are. A fox will travel a long way across the plateau to drink from a particular pool, and it will return to that pool for years, and its kits will learn the location from it, and so the family line passes down a map of water drawn entirely in memory. The fox is, in this sense, one of the only creatures on the plateau that knows the place as a whole. The beetles know their crack. The lizards know their plate. The birds know their gryke. The fox knows the architecture of thirst.

## The Cushion Plants and Their Tenants

The **cushion plants** are the closest thing the plateau has to a forest, and they are not very close. Each one is a tight dome of silver-gray leaves, the size of a dinner plate or sometimes two, wedged into a crack where, over centuries, enough dust and organic matter has gathered to make a fistful of soil. They grow outward, not upward, and they live a long time—generations, perhaps centuries for the oldest, though no one has measured. They flower after the rain, and the flowers are sudden and startling against the gray: yellow, violet, white, brief explosions of color that the small dark birds come to feed on, and that the insects come to pollinate, and that fade within a day or two, leaving behind a hard seed head that waits.

The plants are, in fact, the keystone of the visible ecosystem. Their roots hold what little soil exists. Their leaves catch the rare rain and channel it inward. Their dead material feeds the fungal network underneath, and the fungal network feeds the larvae, and the larvae feed the beetles, and the beetles feed the fox. The cushion plant is, in its slow way, the mother of the cracks.

There are also, sparsely, **alpine grasses**, sharp and wiry, growing in the lee of larger stones where the wind cannot reach them. They are not much, and they do not flower often, and they are mostly eaten by the birds. But they are there, and their presence, like the presence of the fox, is a kind of stubbornness.

## The Wind-Inhabitants

And then there are the things that are not animals and not plants but that live here, in the way that weather lives in a place.

The **wind** is the most present. It does not visit; it resides. It pours across the plateau from the west, funneled by the grykes, and it produces the sound that gives the place its name, the long echo that is not one echo but many—a layered drone of moans from the wider plates, whistles from the narrower cracks, sudden shrieks from fractures that act as organ pipes. The wind is not alive, but it is the medium in which aliveness happens. It carries pollen. It carries seeds. It carries the small insects that get picked up from the lower valleys and dropped, sometimes, onto the stone. It wears the rock imperceptibly, in a patience that is indistinguishable from the rock's own patience.

The **shadows** move like animals across the plateau, sweeping in long slow arcs as the day progresses, and they are inhabited by all the things that have retreated from the light. The shadows deepen the grykes into a kind of false night, and in that night the beetles move, and the larvae feed, and the fox sometimes walks, careful, in the cool.

The **light** is also, in a sense, a resident. It arrives with the force the description names, and it falls on the cream-colored stone and bounces back up so that the whole plateau glows, and it heats the upper plates of the rock to a temperature that is almost pleasant, and the lizards lie on those heated plates with their toes spread wide, and their blood warms, and they become, for a few hours, almost the creatures they would be in a kinder place.

## The Stone at the Center

And then there is the upright stone, in the place where the grykes converge like spokes of a wheel.

It is not alive. But things live on it. The lichen, which is the green-black film of the deeper grykes, climbs partway up its sides where the rain has splashed. A small bird nests, in some years, in the cup at the top, lining it with a few feathers. Beetles walk the smoothed surfaces in the morning, when the stone is still cool. After a rain, the cup fills with water, and the water sits there longer than it would in any natural depression, because the stone is denser, less porous, and it holds. The birds drink from it. The fox, perhaps, has drunk from it. The lizards, certainly, have.

The stone was made by hands. The hands are gone. But the cup at the top is still a cup, and the rain still falls into it, and the water still sits there, trembling, for a few hours or a few days, and the creatures of the plateau still come to it as they have come to it for as long as the cup has held water. The makers intended this, or at least they made it possible, and the intention has outlived the makers by a span that no one on the plateau can measure.

Whether the stone is inhabited in any other sense is a question the plateau does not answer. It stands. The wind moans past it and produces, in the particular grykes around it, a sound that is different from the sound elsewhere—lower, more resonant, as if the convergence of the cracks is itself a kind of throat. The stone is, in some sense, the place's only artifact and the only thing that has resisted becoming part of the plateau. It is becoming part of the plateau. The calcium seam at its base is thin, but it is there, and in another few thousand years the stone will be fused to the plate it stands on, and the cup at the top will still hold water, and the wind will still moan through the cracks around it, and the creatures of the plateau will still come.

That, perhaps, is the truest answer to who lives here. The plateau lives here. The plateau is its own inhabitant, and everything else—the fox, the lizards, the beetles, the birds, the cushion plants, the wind, the shadows, the upright stone, and the rare human who has come and slept and left a circle of killed lichen that has faded—are visitors, of varying lengths of stay, in a place that has already been worn down to its essential skeleton and now merely persists, and makes a sound while it does so.
