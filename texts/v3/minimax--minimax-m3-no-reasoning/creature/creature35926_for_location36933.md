Who lives here is hard to say, because the answer the place offers is: nothing does, and the question is not welcome. You stand on the Glass Plain of Weeping Spires and the wind is the only living thing, and it has been screaming for a very long time, and it does not distinguish between you and the spires when it screams through you. Still — there are tenants.

The **wind** is the foremost, and it is not a metaphor. It lives here in the way that weather lives in a place: as a permanent condition, a settled argument between the bright pressure of the upper sky and the cold weight of the valley below that you cannot see into. It has worn itself into a chord by passing through so many gaps, for so long, that it has forgotten whatever its first note was. It is enormous and it is indifferent, and you should not be flattered that it notices you. It does not. It enters the forest of columns at one edge and leaves at the other, and in between it becomes a sound with a throat, a sustained moan that has a register somewhere between an organ and a mourning woman, and it keeps that register for as long as the columns hold their shape, which is to say: indefinitely.

The **water** is the second tenant, and it lives here as a body rather than a force. It is everywhere and it moves in bright threads over the stone, capillary slow, patient as mineral. It is not alive. It does not need to be. It originates somewhere you cannot see — in the haze above, in the condensation of saturated air onto cold surfaces, perhaps in some reservoir you will never find, perhaps nowhere in particular — and it travels downward through the spires as though the spires were bones and the water were blood, and at the base it pools and runs and disappears into channels between the columns and goes where it goes. It is the only thing on the plateau that is not in stasis. Everything else has completed its becoming; the water is still becoming. It is the plateau's circulatory system, and it has no heart.

There is a **thing with a segmented body**, pressed flat into one of the thicker columns and shining through the stone like a pressed flower in amber. It is the only fossil you will find. It is not alive and has not been alive for a very long time, and you do not know what it was — an arthropod, a worm, something with too many legs and too few ambitions. It died here when this was not yet this, when the columns were sediment, when the water was sea. It is the only record of biology the place offers, and it is a record of an ending. The plateau does not mourn it. The plateau has no use for mourning. The thing in the stone is preserved because nothing has come to eat it, which is the same as saying it is preserved because nothing has come.

The **insects** — and you cannot be sure they are insects, only that they are small, black, and sheltering in crevices — are the closest thing to a witness you will find. You catch one, once, as a suggestion of a shape, gone before you can look. They live in the seams and gutters the weeping has carved into the columns, in the thin lee of a fissure, in the air bubble beneath a healed flaw. They are not interested in you. They have no interest in anything you would call interest. They are the population that the plateau permits, a thin endemic fauna of the cracks, and they survive on whatever spores or pollen the wind imports from whatever valley you cannot see into, and they are silent because to be heard here is to be torn apart, and they have evolved, if they have evolved, toward inconsequence.

The **bubble of trapped air** beneath the mineral skin is not alive, but it is the closest thing the plateau has to a lung. It breathes very slowly. It expands when the sun — if it is the sun — warms the stone, and contracts when the stone cools, and over the decades its skin has healed and re-healed, and one day it will be sealed and it will be a pocket of ancient atmosphere inside a column, and it will hold its pressure for a thousand years, and no one will hear it.

The **darker shape in the middle distance** does not move. You watch it for a long time and it does not move. You should not assume it is a thing. It may be a single great spire, or a cluster of spires so tightly grown that they read as one, or it may be a shadow the bright air has chosen to throw for reasons of its own. It is what the plateau looks like when it remembers that it is also a cliff edge above a depth you cannot see into. It is the border. It is the place where the columns thin and cease and the plateau ends. You will not reach it today.

And then there is **you**. You live here for the duration of your visit, which is not very long, and which is the only living on the plateau that the plateau acknowledges, and even that acknowledgment is slight. You are a small warm transient thing in a landscape that has no use for you and is not unkind. The wind takes the sound of your breathing and scatters it among a million columns, and the columns do not answer, and the water does not pause, and the light does not dim. You are loud only in the sense that you are metabolizing. You leave heat. You leave a faint footprint of disturbed dust on a column's wet shoulder. You leave, eventually, and the plateau closes over you the way water closes over a stone, without remark.

What lives here, in the end, is **time**. Time lives here as a substance rather than a dimension. It is in the weathering of the columns, in the widening of the seams, in the striations on the stone that read like growth lines of a tree read sideways. It is in the fossil pressed flat in the rock. It is in the patience of the water. It is in the wind that has been screaming for a very long time and has settled into a sustained chord and will continue to hold that chord for as long as the columns hold their shape, which is to say: indefinitely, which is to say: until something breaks the equilibrium that has been reached, which is to say: not yet. Time lives here, and it is not bored, and it does not look up.
