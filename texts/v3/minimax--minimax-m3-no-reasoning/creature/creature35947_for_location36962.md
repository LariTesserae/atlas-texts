# The Inheritors

---

Begin with the lichen.

It is the oldest tenant of the ridge, older than any insect now leaping through the green pelt, older than any juniper still locked in its long argument with the wind, older than the wind itself in any sense that matters to a creature with a calendar. It has been here so long that some of it is no longer quite lichen. It has become a kind of slow living crust, blotched in the greenish-gray of weathered pewter and the dull orange of an old bruise, that wears the rock the way the rock wears the sky. It grows a millimeter a year, more or less, depending on the slope and the aspect and the generosity of the season. A millimeter a year, for a thousand years. For ten thousand. It does not hurry. It has outlived the hands that set the waystone. It will, very likely, outlive the waystone itself.

There are several kinds, and they have arranged themselves along the rock according to a logic that is older than preference. The orange ones — *Xanthoria* or something like it, though no one has ever stood here and named them — have taken the south-facing shelves where the sun lies thickest in the afternoon, and they have colored those shelves the color of dried blood. The pale gray-green ones — thinner, more crust-like, more austere — have taken the north faces and the underhangs and the small protected pockets where the light comes only as a rumor. Together, in their millions of square inches, they have made the rock itself look like a kind of map, an old chart of exposures and shelterings, drawn by something that understood the sun and had all the time there was.

Beneath the lichen, in the slow chemical weather of its making, the rock is being very gradually unmade. The lichen secretes its mild acids. The acids pry at the feldspar and the mica. The rock softens by a fraction of a fraction. Soil begins. A few grains. A few more. In a hundred years, a thin wash of dust where once there was clean stone. In a thousand, a pocket of earth deep enough to hold a juniper seed against the wind.

The lichen is the foundation. Everything else, in a sense, is built on it.

---

And then the moss.

The moss here is not the moss of shaded stream-banks, the polite velvety species that lies flat under alder and asks only to be left alone. It is a different creature entirely. A deep, hoary, fist-thick species — *some* kind of *Grimmia*, perhaps, or a robust *Racomitrium*, the kind that alpine travelers learn to recognize because it grows where other things will not — that bunches itself into cushions the size of bear pelts and the color of old milk. It drinks the dew in the morning and forgets it by afternoon. It drinks the rain when the rain comes and then holds the rain in its own body for days afterward, a green reservoir that the small things can drink from when the world has gone dry again.

There is a great quantity of it. More than the lichen, perhaps, in sheer volume. It has colonized every horizontal surface that will hold a rootlet and many that look as if they will not. It has crept into the cracks. It has climbed the lower trunks of the junipers in shaggy skirts. It has begun — patiently, without hurry — to climb the stones of the old windbreak along the ridge spine, and the circular wall below it, and the single upright slab of the waystone. In another hundred years, if no one comes to pull it back, the moss will have taken the waystone's carved face entirely, and the vague concavity that might once have been a figure or an animal will be just a slight unevenness in the side of a small green hill.

Underneath the moss — and this is the part you do not see unless you kneel and part it with your hands — the world is dark, and damp, and busy.

---

It is busy in there.

Springtails, in their hundreds, in their thousands — tiny pale things, no bigger than a typed comma, that hop in sudden invisible eruptions when the moss is parted. Mites, slow and armored, working through the damp layers. Small worms, white, almost transparent. The bright threads of fungal hyphae, running everywhere, a kind of underground nervous system connecting one cushion to the next. A beetle larva, occasionally, curved into a comma and waiting. A spider's egg sac, silk-white, hung from the underside of a juniper branch where the moss has made a kind of ceiling.

This is the layer of the very small. The layer that the eye cannot resolve and the hand will crush without noticing. The layer that is, in terms of actual mass of living tissue, the largest part of the ridge. The green pelt you see from a distance is not the bulk of the life here. The bulk is under it, in the felt, in the damp, in the slow churn of things eating and being eaten and being eaten again.

The springtails eat the fungal threads. The mites eat the springtails. The small worms eat the mites and the dead things. The fungal threads eat the dead things and also, in a kind of secret collusion with the juniper roots, trade the juniper water and minerals for sugars the juniper has made from the sun. Nothing here is alone. Everything here is in a conversation with everything else, in a network so dense and so quiet that the only sign of it is the green pelt itself, and the faint smell of wet earth when you part it.

---

Above the moss — on top of it, in it, jumping off it, flying above it — is the layer you do see, if you sit still and watch.

The grasshoppers first, because there are more of them than anything else, and because they are the most insistently present. They are in a dozen colors. Brown, of course — the universal brown of the universal insect — but also green, yellow, a kind of dusty rose that looks almost artificial, as if some of them had been dyed. There are so many of them that the air at shin-height is a kind of drift, a constant low migration of small bodies leaping and landing and leaping again. They go up in arcs, and they come down in arcs, and the arcs are so numerous and so overlapping that the sound of them — a soft perpetual pattering, like very fine rain on dry leaves — becomes part of the place's weather.

They are not afraid. This is the thing that distinguishes the grasshoppers here from grasshoppers in lower, wilder places. They have not been hunted, in any serious way, by anything large enough to matter. A few birds take them. The lizard takes some. But the hawks prefer easier meat — the mice, the voles, the occasional young rabbit. The grasshoppers are, in effect, the ungoverned citizens of the place. They have inherited.

And they eat. They eat the green pelt, the grasses and the small herbs, with a kind of industrious cheerfulness. They sit on a blade of grass and work their way along it, joint by joint, and then leap to the next blade, and then the next. A single grasshopper eats very little. A million of them eat a great deal. The whole green pelt is, in some continuous slow way, being mowed, and being mowed down, and growing back. The ridge is fed by the grasshoppers as much as it is eaten by them. Their droppings, in their millions, return what they take. The place has been turning itself over, in this way, for a very long time.

---

Alongside the grasshoppers, working slower, lower, more privately, are the beetles.

The beetles are dark. They are slow. They have a kind of settled-into-themselves quality, as if they had long ago accepted that the world was mostly rock and had made their peace with it. They walk along the juniper bark with a patient deliberateness that is almost meditative, pausing every few inches to test the surface with their antennae, to taste the air, to consider. They do not leap. They do not fly, except for a few of them, in the warm hours. They go about their business with the unhurried dignity of creatures who expect to be here tomorrow and are not particularly worried about it.

There are many kinds. There is a small black one that lives under the moss and comes out at night. There is a longer brown one with a kind of waist, which you sometimes see dragging something — a fragment of moth, a piece of dried grasshopper — backward into a crack. There are weevils, with their long curved snouts, working at the juniper bark to get at the cambium beneath. There are ground beetles, shining and quick, that come out after the rain and run across the bare patches of rock in long swift patrols, hunting the springtails that have themselves come up out of the moss to drink.

The beetles do not make a sound, or very little. They are the silent majority of the place. If the grasshoppers are the visible citizens, the beetles are the council, working behind the scenes.

---

And the spiders.

The spiders here, most of them, do not build webs. This is a place too windswept and too exposed for webs to make any sense — the wind would destroy them in an hour. So the spiders run. They are dark, small, fast, with a kind of deliberate speed that is different from the grasshoppers' panicked leaps. They are hunters on the ground. They cross the bare patches of rock in long looping sprints, feeling the vibrations with the fine hairs on their legs, hunting whatever small thing has wandered into their territory. When they catch something — and they catch something, usually, within a few minutes — they pause, very briefly, and then begin to feed, and then they run again.

They have no fixed address. They live under stones, in cracks, in the deep pockets of the moss. A few of them have built retreats — silk tubes in the juniper bark, silk doors in the moss — but most of them are nomads, moving through the green pelt like small dark wolves through a forest. They are not numerous, compared to the grasshoppers, but they are persistent, and patient, and the numbers of small things they take in a season are considerable.

---

The lizard.

There is a small gray lizard, here, that you will see if you sit still long enough in the right place. It lives on the rock shelves, mostly, where the sun falls warm in the morning and the cracks go deep enough to hide in. It is quick. It is almost invisible against the gneiss — the gray of its back is the gray of weathered feldspar, and it knows how to flatten itself against a surface and become, for a moment, part of the rock.

It is a sit-and-wait hunter. It basks in the sun until it is warm enough to move fast, and then it hunts — a fly, a small moth, a grasshopper that has come too close. It eats a great deal for its size, when it can. It eats almost nothing, in the cold weeks of deep winter, when the rocks are too cold to bask on and the insects are mostly gone.

You see it as a flash. A small gray streak from one crack to another, gone. If you are very lucky, and very still, you see it pause, on a sunny shelf, its throat pulsing gently, its small bright eye taking you in. It does not know what you are. It only knows that you are large, and that you have not yet moved. It judges you, briefly, as a predator or as not-a-predator, and then it is gone.

There are more of them than you think. On a sunny morning, in the right place, you can see three or four of them in a single small area, each on its own shelf, each watching its own patch of green.

---

Above the green pelt, in the air, the hawks.

The hawks are the largest moving things on the ridge, and they are the most intermittent. They come over from the west, usually, riding the wind along the spine of the place without needing to beat their wings. They circle. They hang in the updrafts, motionless in the sky, and from that height they can see the whole green pelt below them in the kind of detail a person could never achieve — every grasshopper, every movement in the moss, every small quick thing that has come out into the sun.

They cry. The cry is the sound of the place, in a way — that thin, descending keening that arrives from somewhere over the ridge and fades as it crosses. It is thin because the bird is far. It is almost abstract, by the time it reaches you, because the wind has taken most of its body. You hear it as a kind of punctuation in the long hum of the insects, a comma in the long sentence of the place.

The hawks are not many. A pair, perhaps, on the ridge. A few more in the surrounding country. They live by what the green pelt produces — the mice, the voles, the occasional young rabbit, the larger grasshoppers, the slow-flying moths. They are the only thing on the ridge, besides the wind, that looks down on it from outside.

---

And the fox.

The fox comes at dawn. Sometimes in the evening, but mostly at dawn, when the light is still low and the long blue shades are coming up out of the valleys and the green pelt is wet with dew and the small things have not yet begun to move.

You see it as a low shape against the spine of the ridge, crossing from one side to the other, loping in that loose unhurried way foxes have. It is not hunting, when it crosses — it has hunted, perhaps, in the dark hours, and is now going home, or going somewhere else. It moves through the green pelt without disturbing it much. The moss closes behind it. The grasshoppers that leaped out of its way leap back. Within a few minutes, there is no sign that anything large has passed.

Foxes do not live on the ridge. The ridge has no dens, no real shelter for them. They live in the drainages below, in the brushy draws, in the places where the rocks have fallen together into something cave-like. They come up to hunt, or to cross, or to be briefly in the wind. They are visitors, in the way the hawks are visitors — part of the ridge, but not of it.

You see one, perhaps, once in a long while. You see the tracks, more often, in the dew of a summer morning — the neat small prints in the moss, going over the spine, going on.

---

The bones.

These are the inhabitants that are no longer inhabitants, and are now something else. The bones lie in the green pelt, picked clean and bleached, slowly being buried by the moss.

A deer, mostly. Sometimes an antelope. Sometimes — though rarely now — something larger, something that has not walked here in a long time. The skulls are the most present. They lie in the small protected pockets of the slope, in the lee of the junipers, where the wind does not reach them and the moss has been able to climb over them without being scraped away again. The antlers of the antelope are still attached to the skull, sometimes, the long curved prongs rising out of the moss like the bare branches of a small dead tree.

The bones are old, but not uniformly old. Some of them have been here for decades, the antlers dark with age, the bone going yellow and then gray. Some of them are recent — last winter, perhaps, or the winter before. The place is not so high, not so harsh, that things do not die here. They die of the cold in deep winter. They die of injury. They die of being caught by the hawks, or by something lower, something that lives in the brush and comes up under cover of dark. They die, and the insects take them, and the beetles take the insects, and the moss covers what is left, and in a few years the place has taken them back into itself.

The bones are inhabitants in a slow way. They are becoming the substrate. The calcium in them is leaching into the soil under the moss, and the soil is using it, and the junipers are using the soil, and the deer that come next year — if any come next year — will eat the junipers and take the calcium back into their own bones, and the cycle will go on. The place is not a place where things end. It is a place where things are taken apart and put together again in different arrangements.

---

And the wind.

The wind is an inhabitant in a different sense. It does not eat. It does not breed. But it shapes everything, and it is here in a way that nothing else is — constant, pervasive, from before the lichen to after the lichen. It has been blowing across this ridge since the ridge was a ridge. It will be blowing across it when the ridge is no longer a ridge.

It works on the junipers most visibly. The junipers on the spine of the ridge are flat-topped, wind-pruned, shaped by decades of the same wind from the same direction into something like bonsai — or like fists, the branches all bent one way, the dead side facing west, the living side hunched down to the east and reaching out in the only direction the wind allows. The junipers in the lee of the rocks are normal. The junipers in the open are not. They have made their settlement with the wind — a kind of truce, in which they give the wind everything it wants and keep, in the lee of their own bent trunks, enough foliage to live.

The wind works on the insects, too, though they do not know it. The grasshoppers that leap up into it are carried, sometimes, a long way downslope before they can land again. The moths that come out at dusk are blown sideways across the ridge and must fight to make any headway upwind. The hawk, riding it, is the only one that profits.

And the wind is the voice of the place. That long low moan across the spine. The higher thin keening in the cracks. The woody drumming of the juniper branches. The whole ridge, on a windy day, becomes an instrument, and the wind is its player.

---

And finally, the vanished ones.

The hands that set the stones are gone. They have been gone long enough that the moss has begun to consider the stones as part of itself, and the lichen is climbing the waystone's carved face, and the circular wall below the ridge has begun to slump inward as if the structure were slowly sitting down.

They are still here, in a way. The line of stones along the spine — the windbreak, the marker, the fence — is still doing the work it was made to do, or some of it. The wind still breaks against it. The rain still collects behind it. The junipers have grown in its lee, and the deer still shelter against it in the deep cold of the winter nights, the way the original makers of the stones must have intended.

The circular wall is gone in another way. It is being taken. The moss has come up over the bottom course. A juniper has rooted in the center and grown into a small tree that has begun to push the wall apart from within. A few more decades and the wall will be a low hummock, indistinguishable from the other hummocks of the slope.

The waystone stands longest. The single upright slab, taller than a person, with the worn concavity in its upper face. It stands because it was set deepest, because it has the most stone in it, because it is one piece and not many. But the moss is climbing it. The lichen is colonizing it. The carved shape — figure, animal, whatever it was — is almost gone, just a slight unevenness now, a place where the dew gathers in the morning and is gone by afternoon.

The hands are gone, but the things the hands made are still inhabited. By moss, by lichen, by beetles. By the wind, which moves around them. By the people who come up here, rarely, to look at them and wonder, and then go down again into the valleys.

---

So who lives here?

The lichen lives here. The moss lives here. The springtails, the mites, the small white worms, the fungal threads. The grasshoppers in their millions. The beetles in their patient dark millions. The spiders that run. The lizard that watches. The hawk that circles. The fox that crosses at dawn. The deer, rarely, in the deep winter. The wind, always. The rain, when it comes. The dew, every morning.

And the bones, becoming the place.

It is not a place where the small things hide. The place does not allow hiding. The wind strips the shelters. The rocks give no cover. The thin soil gives no burrow deep enough to escape. Everything that lives here is, in some way, exposed.

And so the things that have survived here are the things that have made their peace with exposure. The lichen, slow and crust-like, asking nothing. The moss, holding what water it can, releasing it slowly. The grasshopper, breeding so fast and in such numbers that the loss of a few to the hawks does not matter. The beetle, armored, patient, dark. The lizard, quick, watching, gone. The hawk, high, unhurried. The fox, low, passing through.

They have inherited. The place is theirs now, and has been theirs for a long time, and will be theirs for a long time to come. The hands that set the stones had their time, and it passed. The things that came before the hands had their time, and they passed too — the things that grazed the slopes in vast herds, the things that hunted them, the things that lived in the climate that no longer lives here. All of them passed through the place and left some small chemical trace in the soil and went on.

The green pelt remains. The insects remain. The wind remains.

The ridge continues. The moss grows. The lichen spreads. The hawk, somewhere, calls.

The place continues.
