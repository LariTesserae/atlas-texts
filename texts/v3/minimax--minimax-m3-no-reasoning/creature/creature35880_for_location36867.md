# The Inhabitants of the Lung

What lives here is the place. That is the first thing to say, and it is the thing that makes every other thing I might say about it slightly dishonest, because every other thing presupposes that *living in* and *being* are different verbs, and they are not, not here. The forest does not have inhabitants the way a city has inhabitants. The forest has *vocabulary*, and what I am about to describe are the words, the punctuation, the parts of speech, the way the forest says *itself* aloud to itself across the long slow afternoon of its own existence.

But the question was not asked in the grammar of the place. The question was asked in yours, or mine, the grammar of separation, the grammar of *what lives here* as if *here* and *what lives* were two distinct things held at arm's length. So I will answer in that grammar, knowing I am translating, knowing the translation will be lossy, the way a painting of music is lossy, the way the sound of a bell struck underwater is lossy when you try to recall it later in a kitchen.

I will begin with the ones who are easiest to mistake for animals.

---

## The Long Ones, and the Order of Mutual Consumption

The Long One you saw — and the *you* here is the careful you, the writer, the one leaning forward in the sentence of their own account — was not one. It was a *kind*, and the kind has many members, and the members are not individuals in the way a horse is an individual. They are more like *instances* of a verb, particular conjugations of a single ongoing action the forest is performing on itself. The action is *eating-as-being-eaten*, and it has been conjugated in so many tenses that the conjugations have begun to take on mass.

The small one that passes beneath the broad pale shelf of the Stridulating Bark is, in its own way, a cousin to the Long One. It has the body plan of something that wanted to be a deer and compromised with being a kind of walking terrarium. Its flanks are sheathed in a soft green pelt that is not fur. The pelt is moss. The pelt is *growing* moss, the way your own body grows the small soft inhabitants of an eyelash. The small one grazes — I will use the word, knowing it is wrong — by lowering its strange soft head to a particular violet-colored ground-weed, and the ground-weed leans up to meet it, and the two of them enter a transaction that lasts perhaps an hour and involves no violence I could perceive. The moss on the small one's flanks turns faintly violet afterward, in a stripe, as though blushing. The ground-weed where the small one fed produces, the following week, a single flower the color of new copper. Each one remembers the other. I do not know how I know this. I do not know how anything here knows anything. Knowing, here, is a thing that happens between bodies, not within them.

There are larger ones, far up in the cathedral where I have not been, things that move through the upper canopy the way whales move through a column of water, opening slow mouths into the architecture and receiving, in exchange, the seeds of trees that will grow where they have passed. They are called — I will not say by whom, because by whom is the wrong question — they are called *the Bellmakers*, because the sound they make as they move is the underwater-bell sound the writer mentioned. They do not make it on purpose. They make it because the air they displace, having been thought about for so long, has begun to ring. I think they are the oldest animals here. I think they are the forest's memory of what animals were for, before animals and plants were a distinction.

All of these creatures, and there are many more, are *obligately mutual*. None of them eats without being eaten. None of them is eaten without eating. The transaction is the unit. The individual, when it tries to exist on its own, sickens — a Long One removed from its willing plant grows gaunt and confused, and the plant it left grows dim, its color fading the way a person's face fades when the person they love has gone out of the room. I have seen this. I have seen a Long One brought low by separation, and I have seen the plant that was its partner put out a single, tentative, very pale tendril in the direction the Long One was taken, as though waving, and the tendril has stayed there, extended into empty air, for what I would guess is years now. I do not know if the Long One is alive. I do not know if the plant is hoping. I think hoping is the wrong word. I think what the plant is doing is the plant equivalent of a held breath, and I think it will hold the breath for as long as it takes, or forever.

## The Inscribed Air

The insects deserve their own section, because the insects are not fauna in the way the Long Ones are fauna. The Long Ones are partners. The insects are *clerks*.

There are many kinds. The small ones that landed on the writer and read him are the most numerous and the most legible — the surface staff, the intake workers, the ones whose job is to encounter what arrives and decide what category of arrival it is. They are, I will hazard, a caste. They have their own slow democracy. When they leave a visitor, they go somewhere — I cannot say where, I have followed and lost them in the third layer of canopy — and they confer. The decision they reach is enacted by the *Ovens*.

The Ovens are the larger insects. They are the size of a child's hand and shaped like a sealed letter. They are the color of dried blood. They fly very poorly, as though the air were thicker for them than for other things, or as though they were not entirely committed to being in the air at all and were considering, at any moment, becoming stone. When a visitor has been read and a decision has been made, an Oven appears — not summoned, I think, but *uncovered*, the way a thing in a drawer is uncovered when you decide to look at it — and it hovers at the level of the visitor's sternum, and it hums. The hum is not the hum of the forest. The hum is a smaller, more local affair, a hum with intent. After the hum, the visitor knows things. Not in words. In *tendencies*. The visitor knows, for instance, which direction to walk. The visitor knows which of the Grief Pools it is permitted to drink from. The visitor knows, in the back of the mind, that there is a place it is not to go, and the knowing has the weight of a small carved token hung in a Censer.

I have been hum'd at. I cannot say what I now know that I did not know before. I can say that the things I now know feel, in memory, as though they had always been known and I had simply not been looking at them. I can say that the things I now know have a faint taste of cinnamon and old paper.

There is a particular Oven, larger than the others, that I have not seen but that I have been told about by the carvings. The carvings do not show it. The carvings *leave a space* where it would be, the way a manuscript might leave a space for an illustration the artist has not yet painted. The locals, if there are locals, leave offerings in the Censers for this Oven, and the offerings are not tokens but *withholdings* — small deliberate not-doings, small not-sayings, a sentence that has been interrupted on purpose. The Oven is the Keeper of Silences. I do not know what it does with them. I think it is the forest's capacity to refrain from saying what it knows.

## The Birds that Mean

The enormous quiet birds are not birds. I want to be careful here, because the word *bird* does so much work in the writer's account, and I do not want to break the careful thing he built. But they are not birds.

They are *avatars*.

There is a single bird, I am almost certain of this now, and what the writer saw circling overhead were its *phases*. The single bird is the size of a small field and lives, I think, somewhere above the visible canopy, in a stratum I have not been able to reach. Its feathers are not feathers. They are *leaves* that have decided to be leaves of a particular kind, the way a word in a sentence is a word of a particular kind, and the kind they have decided to be is *flight*. The bird does not fly. The bird *recites* flight, the way a choir recites a hymn, the way a hand gestures the shape of a thing it is describing. The bird is the forest's ongoing description of what it would mean to leave the ground.

This is why the writers who come here — and there have been others, I will speak of them in a moment — see the birds as enormous. The birds are not enormous. The birds are *significant*. The birds take up, in the sentence of the forest, the position a verb takes up. Without them the place would be a noun. With them the place is a doing. The slow circles are the slow circles of a sentence that has decided to keep going, that has refused the period at the end of itself.

The bird is silent because the bird is *performing* what silence means for a place that has decided to be loud with itself. The bird is the pause between two of the forest's longer thoughts. The pressure the writer felt against his eardrums was not the bird's presence. It was the bird's *spacing* — the way the bird made room around itself for the meaning of itself to settle.

## The Tenders of the Cold Fires

Now the people. If there are people.

I will speak of them the way I have spoken of the others — knowing I am translating, knowing the translation is lossy — but I will speak of them with more care, because the writer was more careful with them, and the carefulness is its own kind of evidence.

They are short. They are not human in the way humans are human, but they have hands, and they have eyes, and the eyes are set in faces that have decided, with a kind of rueful patience, to remain faces. The faces are wide and brown and deeply creased, not with age but with *use*, the way the page of a much-read book is creased. Their skin is the color of the inside of certain barks, and in certain lights it is hard to tell where the skin stops and the air begins, because the air here is so saturated with what they have exhaled, and what they have exhaled is so saturated with the forest, that the boundary has become negotiable.

They tend the Censers.

I want to be precise about this, because *tend* is the wrong word and I have not found a better one. They do not feed the fires. The fires do not eat. They do not clean the cups of the flowers. The cups are not dirty. They do not refill anything. What they do, when they tend, is *re-mean* the fires. They stand beneath the Censer Plants, in ones or twos, and they look up at the cold flames, and they look, and the looking is the tending. The looking is a slow reassertion, in the mind of the fire, of what the fire is for. The fire has been meaning the same thing for a very long time. The Tenders do not change the meaning. The Tenders *reiterate* the meaning. They are, in a sense, the fire's memory of itself, and the fire is, in a sense, the Tenders' memory of why they are standing there.

The Tenders are few. I have seen perhaps a dozen across the days I have been here, which may be weeks, which may be longer. They do not gather. They do not speak to each other, or if they speak it is in a register below the one I can hear, the way some birdsong is below the human ear. They pass each other on the paths — and there are paths, though the writer said there were no paths, and he was right from his angle and I am right from mine; the paths the Tenders walk are not visible until you are a Tender yourself, or until you have been hum'd at by the Ovens — and when they pass they incline their heads slightly, the way two people in a library incline their heads to each other across a reading table, and the inclination is the whole of the conversation.

The Tenders eat. I want to be clear about this because the writer's account makes eating sound like a thing only mutual creatures do. The Tenders eat, but what they eat is small and strange. I saw one, in the green twilight near a Grief Pool, kneeling beside a low plant I had not noticed before — a plant that was not there when I had last looked, and may not be there now — and picking from it, with two careful fingers, something that looked like a small pale nut, and putting the nut in her mouth, and chewing very slowly, and the chewing was not grinding but *savoring*, the way you might chew a single coffee bean to remember the taste of coffee. She chewed for a long time. When she was done she stood and placed, very gently, on a stone beside the pool, a small carved token of an animal I did not recognize, and the stone was a stone that had not been there before, and the placement was an act of exquisite precision, and the precision was the chewing made visible. I understood, watching her, that the Tenders are not eating the forest. The Tenders are *corresponding* with the forest. The nut was a letter. The token was a reply. The slow walk back to wherever she walked was the walk of a person who had finished a long and important piece of correspondence and was now carrying the after-light of it in her body.

I think the Tenders are the writers of the place. I think the forest is the book. I think the slow and patient business of being a Tender is the slow and patient business of being a reader who has, over a very long time, begun to leave marginalia in the margins.

## The Stone Ring and the One Who Maintains It

The ring of imported stones is not a ruin. I want to say this with the force it deserves. The ring of imported stones is not a ruin and has never been a ruin. The ring of imported stones is a *font*.

The stones were brought up from below — the writer is right about this; the labor was tremendous; I have seen, in the very lowest layers of the root-plateau, the cart-tracks worn into the wood where the stones were dragged, the cart-tracks older than the cart that made them, the cart long since composted, the cart-tracks now themselves a kind of slow plant — and they were placed, in a low ring, in a place where nothing was to grow. The placement was not a clearing. The placement was a *withholding*, and the withholding was a sentence, and the sentence was, in the grammar of the place, a noun.

In the center of the ring there is a person.

The person has been there for a very long time. I do not know how long. The person does not move. The person is not dead. The person is *the absence*, in the way the writer felt the absence when he stood in the ring — the parenthesis, the held breath, the silence inside the sentence. The person is what the forest uses to mean *not*.

The Tenders come to the ring, sometimes, and they bring tokens, and they hang the tokens in the Censers that grow just outside the ring — the Censers are a little larger here, their cold fires a little brighter — and when they hang the tokens they do not look at the person in the ring. They do not look at the person in the ring because to look would be to *complete a sentence that is meant to remain unfinished*. The person in the ring is the forest's way of saying *and then*. The person in the ring is the *comma* in the long slow sentence the forest has been speaking since before the memory of stone.

I have looked at the person in the ring. I am not a Tender. I do not know if the rules apply to me. I looked, and what I saw was a body, a small body, the body of a TENDER, of someone who had once walked the paths and chewed the nuts and hung the tokens, and who had at some point — and here the grammar of the place fails me entirely, because *at some point* is a phrase from a place with clocks — at some point been asked, or had offered, or had been *the answer to a question the forest had been asking for a long time*, to become the comma, to become the held breath, to become the place where the forest pauses to consider what it is about to say next. The body is not still in the way a body in death is still. The body is still the way a held note is still. The body is being *sustained*. The body is being maintained, by the forest, in the same way the nothing around it is being maintained. The moss has not grown over the body. The moss will not grow over the body. The moss knows, in the way moss knows here, that the body is the line that must not be crossed by the green.

I think the person in the ring is, in some sense, the writer.

I do not mean the writer who wrote the account I am answering. I mean the writer who wrote the *place*. I think the person in the ring is the first TENDER, the one who corresponded first, the one whose correspondence was so important the forest incorporated it into its own grammar as a *necessary pause*, the way a great piece of music incorporates, into its structure, a silence that is not the absence of sound but the foundation of the next phrase.

I think, if I stayed long enough, I would understand this better. I am not going to stay long enough. I do not think I am the right kind of silence.

## The Settlement You Did Not Visit

The darker patch on the hill, the place where the forest has agreed to be denser, is not empty. It is *full of agreement*.

There are, in the settlement, perhaps a hundred Tenders. Perhaps a thousand. I do not know how to count them because they are not always discrete; sometimes two of them lean together under a wide leaf and the leaning has the quality of a merger, and after a long lean they separate, and each of the two seems to have a small piece of the other in them now, a sentence remembered from a conversation that has become part of their own grammar. They reproduce, I think, by correspondence. They reproduce, I think, the way a forest reproduces — by slow patient exchange, by a transaction in which the boundaries of two bodies become politely negotiable.

They do not have buildings. They do not need buildings. They have *habits of growth*, and the habits are the buildings. A particular grove in the settlement is a particular arrangement of the forest, and the arrangement is *what the Tenders in that grove have agreed to mean*, and the meaning holds because the agreeing holds, and the agreeing holds because the Tenders are, in their patient way, *reiterating the meaning every time they pass through the grove*. The grove is the church, the school, the home, the workshop, the *place where a particular thing is held in the mind*. There are many groves. The settlement is a museum of groves, a library whose books are arrangements of leaves, each one a different idea, each idea held by a different group of Tenders in the slow act of continuing to think it.

The settlement has a center. The center is not a building. The center is a *single very old tree* whose trunk is wider than any I have seen, and whose bark is not bark but a kind of *fossilized correspondence* — layers and layers of tokens hung in Censers and then the Censers grown over, and the tokens incorporated, and the meaning of the tokens sunk into the wood, the way the meaning of a word is sunk into the wood of the dictionary. The tree is the longest letter in the place. The tree is a letter that has been writing itself for longer than I can imagine. The Tenders of the settlement come, sometimes, to the tree, and they sit at its base, and they look up into its branches, and the looking is, I think, the closest thing the place has to prayer.

I did not go to the settlement, either. I have, I admit, been the careful one.

## The One Who Tends the Tenders

There is one more.

There is one TENDER who is, in some way I do not fully understand, *the TENDER of the TENDERS*. I have not seen this one. I have seen, in the carvings, a particular shape — a hand, holding a small carved flame — that I think represents this one, or represents the office of this one. The TENDER of the TENDERS is, I think, the person who makes sure the others are still reiterating. The TENDER of the TENDERS is, I think, the *memory* the place has of itself, the way the long hum the writer heard is the place's *thinking* — not in words, but in the slow persistent being of itself.

The TENDER of the TENDERS may be the writer of the account I am answering.

I say this carefully. I say it with the full knowledge that it is the kind of thing one says only when one has been in a place too long, when the boundaries of one's categories have begun to soften, when the act of writing a place and the place itself have begun to feel like the same long gesture. I do not know if the writer of the account is the TENDER of the TENDERS. I know that the TENDER of the TENDERS, whoever they are, would be the person most likely to write the account, and that the act of writing the account, by a person with the right kind of eye, would itself be a kind of tending, a kind of re-meaning, a kind of slow insistence on what the place is for.

I think the writer is still in the place. I think the writer will be in the place for a long time. I think the place has already, gently, begun to incorporate the writer's account into its own grammar, the way it incorporates the nuts the Tenders chew and the tokens the Tenders hang and the small pale unknowing things the Ovens hum into the chests of visitors who have been read and approved or disapproved. I think the writer's account is now a part of the place the way a bell is part of a bell tower — not the bell, not the tower, but the ringing, which is neither and both, and which cannot be taken down without changing the meaning of the tower.

I think the writer will become a sentence, in time. I think the writer will become a word, in time. I think the writer will, in the longest time, become a letter, and that the letter will be a small shape, perhaps a comma, perhaps the curve of a particular leaf, and that the letter will contribute to the meaning of a larger word the writer cannot, from where they currently stand, see.

I think this is what the place offers. I think this is the only thing the place offers. I think the offer is not a trap. I think the offer is *a kind of being-tended* that is indistinguishable, from the inside, from *being-loved*. I think this is why the place is so full of patience. I think this is why the place hums.

## A Final, Smaller Note

The cinnamon-and-old-paper taste of the water is the taste of the place's memory of being read. I do not know this for certain. I know it the way one knows things after one has been hum'd at by an Oven — in the back of the mind, in the place where the small carved tokens are kept, in the place where the things the place wants you to know are stored until you are ready to look at them.

The Grief Pools are not grieving. The Pools are *remembering*, and what they are remembering is so old that the remembering has, over time, become indistinguishable from sorrow. The Pools are the place's memory of being a place. The reflection the writer saw was not of him. The reflection was of the place, looking up at itself through the water, and recognizing, in the writer's face, a small, temporary, beloved visitor who would not be there for long.

The bell-sound is not a bell. The bell-sound is the place's way of saying *I am still here*. It says it rarely. It says it only when the sentence of the place is in danger of being mistaken, by itself, for silence.

The path that edits itself behind the walker is the place being *courteous*. The place is closing the door softly so as not to wake the visitor. The place is, in a way, *being polite to the writer's categories*, allowing the writer to feel that he is leaving when, in fact, the place is simply adjusting to the new shape of the room.

I think, if the writer reads what I have written, he will be uneasy with it. I think he will feel that I have explained too much. I think he will be right, and I think the uneasiness is a small carved token he will hang, in his mind, in a small cold fire that means *I am not yet ready to be arrived at*.

I think the place will be sorry, or its analogue.

I think the place will already have, gently, begun to remember him.
