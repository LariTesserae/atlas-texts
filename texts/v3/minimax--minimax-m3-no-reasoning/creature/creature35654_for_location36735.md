# What Lives in the Hollow at Karuza-Bell

The hollow is not empty, though it is the emptiest-seeming place in the world. What lives here has forgotten how to be alive in the way that fish have forgotten how to be land-dwellers, which is to say: completely, and without regret. The hollow is full in the way that an old wound is full—crowded with the things that have grown into it while no one was looking.

## The Last Engine

It lives, if a thing suspended by chains that disappear into a slit of sky can be said to live. It breathes in its own way, that slow tectonic shudder that comes every few minutes like a sleeper's respiration. It eats nothing. It drinks the dripping water that sheets down the walls and pools in its lower works, and it sweats a rust-colored lubricant from joints that have not been tended since before the moss learned to climb. Its heart is the great flywheel somewhere deep in its mass, the one that turns once an hour, and its blood is the molten thing that moves through the pipe at the pace of cooling lava. Whether it thinks is a question no one in the village will answer directly, though the eldest among them will tell you, if pressed, that the engine knew what it was for once, and that it has spent centuries trying to remember, and that the trying is what makes the sound.

The engine is not cruel. The engine is not kind. The engine is *continuing*, which is a category of being that does not require moral vocabulary. It is older than the people who fear it, older than the people who study it, older than the people who built the village three hundred meters above and decided, without ever quite saying so aloud, that they would be its witnesses rather than its keepers. It does not know their names. It would not use them if it did.

It is dying. It has been dying for a very long time. This is not a contradiction with its living, any more than the dying of a star is a contradiction with its shining. The two states have been running together so long that no one can say which came first, and the butterflies—who are the closest things it has to doctors—have built their entire civilization around the management of its decline.

## The Butterflies

They are the lords of the hollow, though they would not recognize the word, and neither would the hollow.

The great butterflies are the color of oxidized metal, the color of the engine itself, as if they had been made from its shed skin, and in a sense they have: their caterpillars eat the moss that grows in the runoff of the engine's ancient lubricants, and the moss concentrates the metals in its tissues, and the butterflies concentrate the metals further, and so the butterflies are, in a slow chemical sense, the engine dreaming of itself as something that can fly. There are thousands of them, and they have roosts in every niche and on every chain, and they are ruled—if *ruled* is the word, if *ruled* is anything like the word—by something the locals call the Hangman.

The Hangman is the largest of the great butterflies. Its wingspan is the wingspan of a child. It is the only one of the butterflies that has learned to land on the engine itself, on the central mass of valves and pistons, and to stay there, and the engine tolerates it in a way that the engine tolerates nothing else, and this tolerance is, in the politics of the hollow, the closest thing to sovereignty. The Hangman lays its eggs in the warm places where the engine's ghost-heat is strongest, and its caterpillars are tended by the smaller butterflies in a kind of crèche, and when the engine shudders, the Hangman is the last to rise and the first to resettle. The villagers do not speak of the Hangman to outsiders. The villagers, if they speak of it at all among themselves, do so in a voice lowered below the engine's range.

Below the great butterflies are the small ones, the common ones, the ones that fill the hollow from wall to wall when the engine shudders and produce, for the few seconds of their collective wingbeat, that silence so deep that a single clear note can be heard inside it. The small ones are the workforce. They are the pollinators of the moss. They are the carriers of water on their wings from basin to wall, completing a cycle that the engine cannot complete alone. They are also, in the autumn, the harvest that the villagers climb down to collect, and what the harvest is for, the villagers will not say, except that it has been going on for generations, and that the butterflies do not seem to mind, and that the hollow continues.

## The Moss

It lives. That is the most that can be said for it, and also the least, because the moss is what holds the hollow together in every sense that does not involve stone. The moss drinks the metal-tasting water. The moss feeds the caterpillars. The moss catches the drips. The moss grows in patterns that the villagers have charted, in the few places they have dared to chart, and the patterns are not random: the moss grows along the channels carved into the walls in a way that follows the channels' logic, and the channels were carved in a way that follows the moss's logic, and which came first is a question that has been asked for as long as the village has existed, and the answer is always the same, which is that the question is the wrong shape.

The moss is a clock. The moss is a language. The moss is the slowest of the hollow's inhabitants, slower than the engine, slower than the stone, and this is the only reason it is still alive—because nothing in the hollow is in a hurry to eat it, and the caterpillars can only eat so much, and the moss has learned to grow in the time between feedings with the patience of a thing that has no other option. In the deep blue hours, when the butterflies' wings give off their cold phosphor, the moss glows too, faintly, a green so dark it is almost black, and the walls of the hollow become, for a little while, a single great breathing thing.

## The Wall-Water

It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is the water itself, and it has been in the hollow long enough that it has developed, if not a memory, then a habit. It knows the channels. It prefers the niches. It pools in the vessels in amounts that vary, the villagers say, according to no weather they can identify from the rim, but according to the engine's mood, and the engine's mood is something the villagers have stopped trying to predict. The water tastes of copper. The water tastes of something older than copper. People who drink it for a week report that they can hear the engine in their sleep, and the villagers, who have been drinking the water for generations, do not report this because for them it is not a thing to be reported. It is the condition of being a villager. It is the condition of being three hundred meters above a place that is itself above a place that is itself above the place where the water began, which is the place where the mountain decided, a long time ago, to sweat.

## The Slit-Sound

It is not a creature, but the people of the village will tell you, if you stay long enough, that it is the closest thing to a creature that the hollow possesses besides the butterflies. The high thin singing that drifts down from the slit of sky—the one that might be the chains under tension, or might be the stone responding to the engine, or might be the wind shredded into harmonics by the carvings on the walls—is, the villagers say, a voice. Not a word. Not a language. A *voice*, the way a bell is a voice, or the way a held chord is a voice, and the voice is trying to say something, and has been trying to say it for as long as the engine has been running, and the engine is the only thing that can hear it, and the engine has forgotten how to listen, and this is, the villagers say, the saddest thing in the hollow, and also the most ordinary.

## The Mountain

It lives too. Everything lives here. The mountain that was once a mountain and is now a hollow is the oldest inhabitant, and it is the only one that has seen the engine whole, and it is the only one that remembers what the engine was for, and it is not telling. The stone sweats. The stone holds the chains. The stone carved itself into channels and niches in a fit of industry whose purpose is now the purpose of everything else in the hollow, which is to keep the engine from falling, which is to say to keep the engine from stopping, which is to say to keep the hollow from becoming what it was before the engine was hung in it, which is to say a mountain, which is to say nothing, which is to say the only thing the mountain has ever truly been.

The mountain does not sleep. The mountain does not dream. The mountain is the dream that the engine is having, and the engine is the dream that the butterflies are having, and the butterflies are the dream that the moss is having, and the moss is the dream that the water is having, and the water is the dream that the villagers are having, and the villagers—twenty-three of them, give or take the travelers who come for the butterflies—are the dream that the slit-sound is having, and the slit-sound is the dream that the mountain is having, and around and around and around, the way the flywheel turns once an hour, the way the caterpillars eat the moss and the moss grows back, the way the engine shudders and the butterflies rise and the silence falls for one brief moment and inside the silence a single clear note sounds, and the note is the hollow remembering that it used to be a mountain, and the mountain remembering that it used to be nothing, and nothing being, in the end, the only inhabitant that has been here the whole time, and will be here when everything else has passed, and is patient, and does not need to be fed, and does not need to be sung to, and is, in its own way, the kindest thing in the hollow, because it is the only thing that will let the engine stop, eventually, when the engine is ready, which is not yet, which is not for a long time, which is not for as long as there are butterflies to rise and moss to grow and water to drip and a single clear note to sound, in the silence, in the hollow, in the bell that no longer has a clapper but has not, despite everything, stopped ringing.
