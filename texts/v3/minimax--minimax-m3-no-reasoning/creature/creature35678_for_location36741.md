The orange-vested ones live here.

That is the first thing and the most obvious thing and, in its way, the smallest of the things. They are two or three hundred of them at any one time, distributed across a hundred kilometers of drift in numbers that vary by hour, by shift, by the quietly shifting logistics of where the work happens to be. They are small in the place the way a hand is small in a glove — pressed into shape by what surrounds them, and wearing the shape in turn.

They wear two coats and a third sometimes, and the third is not enough either. They learn to read the cold, which is not a temperature here so much as a wetness, a thing that finds every seam and pools against the small of the back. They yawn at certain hours without knowing why, or knowing perfectly well why, and they do it anyway because the pressure wants what the pressure wants. They sing, sometimes, off-key, into the cotton of their masks, and the singing does not travel and is not for anyone but themselves, and they know this and they sing anyway.

There are specializations among them, though the specializations are not what they would call specializations. They would call them *the work*. The cable-tensioning crews inch along the catwalks in the high arches with their spanners, two or three to a crew, checking the long tendons that hold the ribs to the rock, and the tendons are old and new together and the spanner never quite stops clicking. The drainage crews, in their high rubber, work the gutters and the half-pipes and the long sedimentational troughs, clearing what the water has brought down — a silt, a sand, occasionally the pale shape of something that was once alive on a surface none of them have seen in years. The inspection crews walk the load-bearing ribs of the main arch with their instruments and their clipboards and the patience of repetition, and the data they gather is sent somewhere, and the data goes, and they do not always know where.

The bore crews are different. They are driving a new tunnel east, into material that is technically not rock but the compressed tailings of a previous century's industry, and the cutter head goes around and around in a darkness it can never see the end of, and around the cutter head, day after day, the spoil is hauled out and the segments are carried in and the tunnel gets a little longer. The bore crews are the ones for whom the place is, even now, not finished.

There are supervisors. There are shift-changers. There are the people who staff the long control rooms at the junction points, where the LEDs brighten in their zones according to a logic no operator will own to. But the Reaches is not a hierarchy. No one is in charge of this and no one is not. The work is a fact, and the people are facts in the work, and the facts do not require a chain of command so much as a habit, a continued habit.

The smaller lives are also here.

The concrete mites, white, a centimeter long, found in the new concrete where the water pools warmest. The engineers call them this without affection. They are not a beloved species. They are an indication — of moisture, of warmth, of the chemical composition of the pour. They are a sign that is itself alive, and the engineers tolerate them the way one tolerates a messenger. The pale moths, blown in on drafts from somewhere above, live only until they settle on a lamp, and their settling is their end. They are a draft made visible. A current made into a thing that tried to rest and could not.

The rat, once a year, if the drainage delivers it from a surface it can no longer be returned to. The rat is watched by everyone. The rat is given what water it can be made to drink. The rat is the closest thing the Reaches has to a ceremony.

And the green things — because there are green things, even here. The algae that lives in the gutters, slow, scum, untouched. The rarer emerald bloom that erupts in the favored cracks where copper pipe sweats onto bare steel, startling in its color, impossible to plan for, and which the people note in passing and do not cultivate and do not destroy. The fungus terraces in the old research bay — dry, brittle, a felt you can peel up with a fingernail — which are not alive now but which remember being alive, and which some of the longer-serving people can name the cultivar of, in the soft murmur of the unwitnessed.

But the largest inhabitant is the place itself, and it does not announce itself.

The Reaches keeps time. You learn this in the third week, that you can feel the hour without checking. The turbines pulse, and the pulse is a heartbeat that has nothing to do with you. The water finds a different gutter at different hours because the pumping has a schedule, and the air has a tide, and the LEDs brighten in their zones. You find yourself, in the third month, unable to remember that this is a thing you have learned. You find yourself, in the third year, unable to remember a time you did not know it. The place has made a clock of itself and put it inside you, and it has not asked.

The dark here is not empty. It has shape just out of register, and you fill the shape with whatever you last feared, and you do this for a while, and then you stop, and then the dark is just dark again, and then later the dark has shape again. The dark is a resident. The dark has weight.

The water is the medium of almost everything, and the water is also, in its way, an inhabitant — the longest one. It comes in from above through a thousand drilled weep-holes. It pools. It is led away by channels that themselves weep. It never really leaves. It is older than any of the people will ever be, and it is patient, and it is everywhere, and it is the substance out of which the cold is made, and the cold is the wetness of the cold, and the wetness is the water.

The people live in the Reaches the way the Reaches lives in the people. The turbines do not need them and the water does not need them and the dark does not need them, and they need all of these things. They are the orange-vested ones, the small ones, the necessary ones. They are threaded through the place like a signal through a wire. They sing, off-key, into the cotton of their masks. They yawn when the pressure wants them to yawn. They watch the rat. They note the emerald bloom. They drive the new bore eastward, into the compressed tailings of a previous century, and the cutter head goes around and around, and the Reaches get a little wider, and they are inside it, and it is inside them.
