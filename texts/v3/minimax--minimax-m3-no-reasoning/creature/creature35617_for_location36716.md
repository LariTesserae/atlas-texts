# What Lives in the Low Cauldron

They do not have names you would recognize. They have not been named. No cartographer has come here with ink; no naturalist with a notebook. The only language that has touched this place is the percussion of a dull stone on softer stone, somewhere out in the dark, patient and rhythmic, and even that may not be language. It may be only habit.

## The Pale Columns

The spires are not fungi, though they do what fungi do. They are not animals, though some of them, late in their lives, begin to move. They are closer to what a coral is to a polyp — a thing built by a smaller thing that has long since left. The glow comes from within because something inside is digesting, slowly, patiently, the way a coal digests the air. They drink the warm water that seeps up through the young soil. They breathe out the mist that hangs in the basin like a held breath. The spores are their young, or their thought, or both — drifting up in columns wherever the warmth lifts them, settling on every wet surface, beginning again.

When a spire is old, it leans. It does not fall so much as it *settles toward*, the way a tired man settles toward a fire. Sometimes a leaning spire touches another, and where they meet, the glow brightens for a moment, and a small pulse moves through both, and they begin, slowly, to fuse. The oldest spires in the deepest part of the basin are not single things anymore. They are thickets of joined columns, pulsing together in a slow rhythm that is not quite synchrony. If you put your hand on one, you would feel it. A low throb. A waiting.

## The Choir in the Canopy

The piping — the thousand layered throats — comes from things that hang upside down from the undersides of the great leaves. They are small, the size of a fist, and they look, if you could see them clearly in the sourceless light, like the soft-shelled eggs of some larger animal, with a mouth and a crown of fine trembling hairs where a head should be. They pipe because piping is what they are. They have no song, not the way a song means anything; they are the sound of their own metabolisms leaking out, the way a kettle leaks its boiling. But because there are so many of them, and because they are never quite in step, the basin gets a churn, a slow tidal noise that is the closest thing this place has to weather.

When a large thing moves below them, they stop. All of them, at once, for a long moment. Then, when the disturbance has passed, they begin again, not where they left off but a little earlier, as if the silence had been a rest they were not expecting.

## The Long Shapes Under the Water

The undulating shapes just beneath the surface are not fish. Fish are too finished, too committed to a shape. These are softer than that — long, pale, ridged along the back, with a slow lateral pulse that moves them through the choked water without disturbing the floating mat above them. They feed on the mat. They feed on the beetles. They feed, sometimes, on each other; a smaller one will simply fail to resurface one morning, and the mat will close over the absence.

They do not surface often. When they do, it is only the ridged back, breaking the mat for a moment, the wet eye — if it is an eye — rolling once to look at the gloom above before the thing sinks again. They breathe through their skin, which is why they never leave the water, which is why the water never gets any cleaner.

## The Standing Heron

There is a bird in the basin that is not a heron but is what a heron would be if a heron had been left alone in a warm dark place for a very long time. Its neck is too long, its legs too articulate, its eye too steady. It stands for hours in the same spot, on a hummock, watching the water with the patience of a thing that does not need to eat often because it has learned to eat almost anything. When it strikes, the strike is not a strike. It is a leaning. The neck simply *is* in the water where a moment before it was above it, and whatever was beneath the water is now being eaten in three efficient motions.

It is not the only one. There are others, smaller, in the canopy, that drop without warning — these are not the same animal, only the same idea arrived at twice.

## The Beetle Columns

The beetles are the size of a hand and they are not beetles. They have too many legs, or not enough, and their carapace has a soft seam down the middle that pulses when they walk, the way a throat pulses when it breathes. They move in columns because the place is too dark and too choked for any of them to know where to go alone, so they follow the seam of the one in front, and each one follows the seam of the one in front of that, and the column curves through the ferns and over the roots and across the floating mat in a line so long you cannot see both ends.

They carry fragments of leaf. They always carry fragments of leaf, even when there is no leaf to be seen, and sometimes you can hear the soft click of a fragment being lifted and passed forward and set down again, endlessly, like a prayer being relayed along a chain.

## The Clay-Sided Thing

At the edge of vision, sometimes, a flank the color of wet clay slides through the ferns. It is the largest thing in the basin, and the rarest. You never see all of it. You see a shoulder, or a hip, or the slow swing of a limb so thick it parts the ferns for a full second before they close again behind it. Its breath is the low croak you feel in your sternum. When it walks, the floating mat dips in a long wake, and the long pale shapes beneath the water veer away from it the way minnows veer from a shadow.

What it eats, no one knows. What eats it, nothing. It is the thing the rest of the basin is built around, the way a reef is built around a current. It does not appear to be in any hurry. It does not appear to be going anywhere. It is simply *there*, the way a mountain is there, except it moves, slowly, and the breathing never stops, and the wake it leaves on the water lasts for an hour.

## The One With the Stone

And then there is the sound. The patient percussion. The dull stone on softer stone.

There is a creature in the basin — no one has seen it, only heard it — that works. It works the way a beaver builds, or a termite mounds, except what it is building is not a dam or a mound. It is a flat surface. A grinding stone. A place where the dull stone can be drawn across the softer stone and something can be made.

What it is making, no one knows. The sound never changes. The rhythm never varies. It goes on through the chorus and through the silence and through the low croak of the clay-sided thing and through the boom that rolls across the basin every few minutes. It is the most regular sound in the Low Cauldron, and it is also the loneliest, because it is the only sound in the basin that has the cadence of *intention*.

If you turned toward it, you would not find it. The mist would close. The columns would lean. The warm water at your ankles would pull, and you would realize, slowly, that the current is not a current. It is a long pale shape turning in the dark, regarding you, deciding.

And the percussion would go on, somewhere you could not get to, by a thing you would not see, with a patience that would outlast you.
