# What Lives in the Vault of Slow Rain

It is the water that lives here first. Everything else is answer to it.

The water is not a tenant but the landlord, and the rent it charges is stillness. It has pooled in the soft tallow-limestone and found its way into every crack that will hold it, and it has been seeping and settling and warming itself against the stone for so long that the stone has begun to think the water's thoughts. The two are no longer separate substances. The stone is water that has remembered it used to be stone. The water is stone that has remembered it used to be rain. They have made a kind of peace.

The first of the dwellers, then, are the things that *are* the water, more or less.

There are the fish — though calling them fish feels like a small violence, an imposition of brighter worlds onto this dim one. They are the color of weak tea, with bellies slightly paler, and they have no names because naming requires a kind of speed the place does not encourage. They hang in the warm brown water the way thoughts hang in the mind of a person sitting very still. They have small dark eyes, and these eyes do not dart. They consider. They are not afraid of you because fear is a kind of urgency, and urgency is the one currency not accepted here. They surface with the unhurried dignity of bubbles — *up* is a direction they visit but do not commit to — and take the iridescent skin that floats on the pools, and that skin is partly them, or their byproducts, or the work of something smaller that has been at the films all night. The boundary between eater and eaten is unclear, as is the boundary between fish and water. They are the most numerous of the visible dwellers, and the most patient.

Around the fish, in the silt, move the pale worms. They are the color of a fingernail, and they move through the damp ooze the way the water moves through the stone — by the slowest possible negotiation, by feeling forward and withdrawing and feeling forward again. They eat the sediment. They become the sediment. Their castings pile in small soft mounds beside the channels, and these mounds are colonized, in their turn, by the next thing.

In the cracks of the limestone live the small pale things with many legs. You see one, perhaps the length of your thumb, withdraw into a fissure when you turn toward it, and its withdrawal is not a flight but a continuation. It was already going there. It was merely waiting for you to look away so the business could proceed without your attendance. Its legs are fine and pale and it has more of them than feels strictly necessary, and it moves with the tempo of the place, which is the tempo of the water, which is the tempo of the heat rising through the stone, which is the tempo of roots growing in the dark. It eats — probably — the soft bodies of smaller things, the spores that drift, the film that lines certain wet stones. It is in no hurry. It will be in this crack for a long time. Its children, when it has them, will also be in no hurry. Its grandchildren will be indistinguishable, in disposition, from the stone itself.

Hanging from the underside of the horizontal roots are the small dark shapes with grouped eyes. They are upside down to your orientation and right-side up to their own, and they seem to find this state unremarkable, the way a person who has lived in a house for thirty years finds the slant of the floor. They are held to the wood by hooks or pads or by a slow adhesive that may be their own secretion. They pause. They regard you with their clustered eyes — all of them at once, a multifaceted attention that is not quite a stare. Then they continue on their way, which is a way measured in inches per hour, perhaps. They are eating the bark, or the fungus on the bark, or the smaller things that are eating the fungus on the bark. They are part of a chain so long and so dim that no one link knows what the first one ate.

There is a something larger that you see only once. It slides along a far terrace, low and sinuous and the color of the stone, and then it is gone into a place you cannot follow, and you are not sure, after, whether you actually saw it. It may have been a length of root. It may have been a fish out of water for a moment, gasping in the air. It may have been a dream the place had of itself, briefly, in the shape of a moving thing. It is, in any case, part of the fauna, and the fauna here has the quality of being half-imagined — as if the line between a creature and the stone it crosses is a courtesy rather than a fact.

Then there are the people who were here, once, and are not here now.

They left almost nothing. There is a low wall of stacked stones ringing a hearth, no taller than a knee. There are scraps of broken shell or broken pottery — no one can now say which — scattered in the silt, half-buried. There is a dark stain on the wall of one grotto that might be a handprint and might be a shadow and might be a mark left by a hand pressing against wet stone. Whoever they were, they did not *live* here in the way that the word usually means. They *inhabited* it, the way a bird inhabits a particular branch — present for a season, absent for a season, leaving only the small deformations of their having been. They took almost everything with them when they went, or the water took it back, and the difference between these two is hard to determine. They were here long enough to build the low wall. They were not here long enough to leave a name, or a tool, or a bone.

But the stain on the wall is shaped like a hand. And the low wall is shaped like a hearth. And the scraps of shell are shaped like the broken bowls of people who ate from them. So there were hands here. There were mouths. There were, briefly, the quick things of the world — the gestures, the hungers, the small urgent fires. And then the slow things re-asserted themselves, the way they always do, and the hands and the mouths withdrew, and the silt covered the scraps, and the stain faded but did not disappear. The chamber has the feeling of a mouth after the last syllable — not silent, exactly, but holding the shape of a sound that has gone.

Now, in the present tense, the dweller that is most truly *at home* here is the forest of roots that descends from the rim of the sinkhole and rises again from the floor of it, and is, in some sense, the same forest.

These are not ordinary roots. They are banyan-thoughts, ficus-thoughts, the kind of thought a tree has when it has been growing for a very long time in a place where the air is wet and the stone is warm and there is no particular reason to stop. They descend from the trunks above in thick ropes, and where they meet the humid air of the chamber they do something that roots are not, in the bright world, supposed to do. They sprout. They send out pale white root-tips — downward, always downward, even when downward is *up* relative to the orientation of the parent tree, and the geometry of which-direction-is-which has become, down here, a matter of taste rather than law. The tips reach for the floor like the fingers of a hand searching for a glass of water in the dark. They thicken. They become pillars. They become trunks. And from those new trunks, more roots descend.

This is a forest that has forgotten the difference between a forest and its reflection. It grows in both directions at once, and has been growing in both directions for so long that it no longer remembers which was first. The original floor of the sinkhole is somewhere down there, beneath the mass of pale wood, but no one can see it. The roots and trunks have become architecture. There are chambers inside the root-mass — small grottos walled by curving pale wood, floored by the same warm tallow-stone — and these grottos are inhabited by all the smaller things, and the smaller things are eaten by the slightly-less-small things, and the slightly-less-small things die and become silt, and the silt is colonized by the worms, and the worms are eaten by the fish, and the fish are eaten by nothing in particular, and when they die they become the iridescent skin on the pools, and the iridescent skin is fed back into the water, and the water carries the warmth up from below and the moisture down from above, and the whole thing turns, very slowly, like a wheel that is made of time.

The heat comes from somewhere deeper. No one in the vault has seen the source of it, and the fish have no interest in it, and the worms do not travel that far down, and the small pale things in the cracks stay in their cracks. But it is there — a low pulse, almost a frequency, felt through the soles of the feet when one stands still long enough. It is the breath of something, perhaps, or the slow digestion of the stone, or the last fading warmth of whatever made this sinkhole in the first place, many centuries ago. It is felt more than heard. It is the metronome to which everything in the vault keeps time.

And the light — the rare, dim, gauze-colored light that filters down through three hundred feet of mist and hanging roots — is the gentlest of all the dwellers. It does not insist. It does not demand to be seen. It seeps, and it pools, and it waits, and when a cloud passes and the mist thins for a moment, it falls in a single shaft through the whole height of the chamber and touches everything, briefly, with the memory of what the sun is like. Then the mist thickens again, and the place returns to its dim dreaming, and the light goes back to being only a suggestion.

What lives here, then, is a community of the patient.

The water, which is the oldest dweller of all, and the one to which all the others are answer.

The fish, which are the water made slightly more itself.

The worms, which are the silt made briefly mobile.

The small pale many-legged things in the cracks, which are the stone made briefly curious.

The dark shapes hanging upside down from the roots, which are the wood made briefly awake.

The larger thing that may or may not exist, sliding along the far terrace, which is the place made briefly aware of itself.

The forest of roots, which is the sky made to grow downward, slowly, in the dark.

The faint heat, which is the deep earth remembering it is warm.

The rare shafts of true light, which are the sun remembering this place exists.

And the people who were here, once, and left almost nothing — who are the quick world, briefly, having visited the slow one, and having been changed by the visit, and having gone.

The vault does not notice that you are here. It will not notice when you leave. It will go on being, very quietly, exactly what it has been. But for as long as you are in it, you are one of its dwellers too. You are part of the slow circulation. You are a small warm thing, breathing water, hanging in the dim brown light, in no particular hurry, becoming, by degrees, more patient than you were when you arrived.
