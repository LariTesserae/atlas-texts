The valley is not unoccupied. It is occupied in the way a riverbed is occupied by water — by something that has been moving through it for a long time, slowly, taking the shape of what is offered. The inhabitants are of more than one kind, and not all of them will admit to being there.

The wren, first, because she is the most insistent.

She is a small brown bird, smaller than a man's fist, and she nests in the same place every spring — a gap in the brickwork of the boiler house, where two courses have shifted over the decades and left a fissure just wide enough for her. The gap faces southeast, which means the morning light enters it for an hour before the ivy on the outer wall thickens enough to cut it off. She lines the nest with moss, which is plentiful, and with a few small feathers she has collected from the drying shed floor, where the woman working there sometimes leaves them, though she would say she does not. She has a mate, or she has had one for several years running, and they have raised four broods in this gap, and two of those broods have gone on to nest in the eaves of the press hall and in a loose tile of the drying shed roof. The wren is louder than she looks. She sings from the top of the rusted iron collar on the smokestack in the half-hour before dawn, and her song is too large for her body, and the valley has not yet decided whether this is a complaint or a sermon.

The moss is the next oldest inhabitant, and it does not sing.

It lives on the north faces of everything. It lives on the mill wheel paddles, climbing them in slow green increments while the wheel waits for water. It lives on the iron straps of the machinery that has not been used in thirty years. It lives in the cracks between the cobblestones of the lane, and it has begun — this is recent, only the last few years — to climb the trunks of the birches nearest the press hall, which is further than moss used to climb, and which the woman with the drying racks finds slightly alarming, though she has not said so. The moss is not one moss but many: cushion moss, hair moss, the kind that forms low feathery mounds like green sleeping animals. It holds water the way a sponge holds water, which is to say greedily, and it is the moss more than the rain that makes the iron rust, and the iron more than anything that stains the moss a faint orange where they touch.

The wisteria is older than most of the mosses, or at least it was planted earlier. It was planted by someone whose name is in a ledger in the drying shed, a name that no one now reads aloud. The wisteria has outlived that person by a considerable margin. Its main stem is as thick as a man's thigh at the base and has split into three woody arms as it climbs the press hall wall, and each of those arms has woven itself so thoroughly into the brickwork that to remove it would be to dismantle the wall, and no one has proposed this, and no one will. The wisteria blooms in late May — a brief, extravagant purple that lasts perhaps a week — and the rest of the year it is just presence, just weight, just the slow tightening of its grip. A blackbird nests in it every spring. Sometimes two blackbirds, in different forks, who cannot agree on the territory line and spend two weeks shouting at each other across three meters of vine before one of them gives up.

The ivy on the boiler house roof is younger than the wisteria, but it is more ambitious. It has, in the absence of the original roof, constructed a substitute — layered stems and leaves now thick enough to walk on in places, though no one does, because it is also home to a small colony of woodmice who would object. The ivy is not one plant; it is many plants joined at the root, and the root system has penetrated the brickwork of the boiler house so deeply that the inside walls, where the plaster has fallen away, show dark root-lines running through the mortar like veins through flesh. The woodmice are small and shy and only emerge at dusk. They eat the ivy berries in winter and the fallen burdock seeds in autumn, and they leave nothing behind them but their droppings and the faint, sweet smell of their nests, which they build from dried grass and the pale feathers they steal from the wren's old nests when she is not using them.

The burdock is the most industrious of the inhabitants, though no one would call it that. It grows along the lanes, in the gaps between the cobblestones, along the edges of the stream, in any patch of bare ground that opens up between the buildings. It is the crop, the valley's reason for continuing, and it is also the weed, the valley's reason for being overrun. The plants in the lanes are left to grow to their full height — six feet, sometimes more — and their broad leaves are cut in late summer by the boy and carried to the drying racks. The plants elsewhere are pulled and composted, or left, depending on the year, and on how much help the boy can be expected to be. The burdock has small purple flowers that become, in autumn, the burrs that catch in the trousers of anyone walking through the lanes, and the woman who tends the drying racks has a special comb for removing them, and she sometimes uses it on the boy when he comes in covered in them, though he is growing too tall for this to be dignified.

The two men who operate the press are named, but only one of their names is used with any regularity. The other is called by a shortened form of his surname that has been in use so long it has lost its origin, and which the woman, when she is annoyed with him, lengthens again into its full form, by way of reproof. They are both somewhere in their fifties, though neither of them knows this exactly, because no one in the valley has counted years for a long time. The one whose name is used regularly is the smaller of the two, and he is the one who fires the engine, which is a single-cylinder steam engine of an old pattern, and he does this on Thursdays, and has done it on Thursdays for as long as anyone now in the valley can remember. He coaxes it the way one coaxes an old horse. The taller of the two operates the press itself, feeding the dried leaves into it in measured quantities, working the lever that compresses them, and he has hands that are scarred in a specific way that anyone who has worked a press would recognize. They are not friends, exactly. They are not enemies. They are two men who have spent a long time in each other's company in a small damp valley, and they have arrived at an accommodation that requires neither of them to speak more than is necessary.

The woman with the drying racks is younger than both of them by some margin, though not as young as she looks. She came to the valley because her mother came before her, and her mother's mother before that, and the records of how this lineage began are partly in the ledgers and partly in the woman's own memory and partly in the way she stands at the racks with her hand held a certain distance from the leaves, feeling the moisture without touching them. She is the one who decides when the leaves are ready to be pressed, and she is the one who decides when they are too dry, and her decisions are not questioned, because she has not been wrong yet, and also because no one wants to be the person who argued with her about it. She has a cat, which is orange, and which lives in the drying shed, and which has been the death of a number of the woodmice over the years, but she does not encourage this and does not discourage it, and the woodmice continue.

The boy who brings the cordwood is thirteen, or fourteen, or fifteen. He is the son of the woman, or of one of the men, or of no one in particular — the valley has not insisted on the question, and he has not pressed it. He is tall for his age, or his age is short for his height, and within the year he will be taller than the smaller of the two men, and this will be remarked upon, and after it has been remarked upon he will be given more of the work, and less of the carrying. He knows the woodpile the way the woman knows the racks — by feel, by weight, by the particular ring a log gives when you strike it with the heel of your hand. He knows which of the birches in the upper wood will burn well and which will spit, and he knows the alders will burn too fast and the oaks too slow, and he mixes them in a proportion that no one has taught him explicitly. He is reading, when he can find the books, which is not often.

The thrushes are the last of the named inhabitants, and they are the shyest. There are perhaps four of them, in the upper branches of the taller trees, and they sing in the evenings — not the wren's afternoon sermon but a longer, more considered phrase, repeated three or four times and then varied. They do not come down to the buildings. They do not need to. They eat from the understory and from the leaf litter, and they nest in the forks of the birches, and they are present in the valley the way the rust is present, and the moss, and the slow work of the roots — there, and not saying much about it.

What does not live here anymore is its own kind of inhabitant. The paper mill workers. The salvage crew that took the boiler house roof. The families who lived in the cottages along the upper lane, whose names are in the ledgers. The woman whose name is in the ledger beneath the wisteria. They are gone, but they are present — in the marks they left, in the iron they set, in the trees they planted or failed to cut down, in the ivy that grew up after their roof was taken and is still growing, and will grow for a long time after the woman, and the men, and the boy, and the wren, and the moss have all of them become part of what the valley is composed of.

The valley does not hurry. The valley has not hurried for a long time. It holds its inhabitants the way it holds its moisture — closely, without complaint, and for as long as they are willing to stay.
