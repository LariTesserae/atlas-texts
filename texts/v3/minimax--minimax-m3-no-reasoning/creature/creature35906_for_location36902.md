# Who Lives Here

They are not the snail. They are not the pale fish in the pool. They are not whatever makes the faint hum in the damp. Those are visitors, or tenants, or witnesses — the few living things the arrangement permits to share the space because they ask nothing of it and leave nothing on it. They belong to the terrace the way dust belongs to a sealed room. They are not who lives here.

What lives here is the *itself* of the place. The terrace has a dweller in the same way that a house has a smell, or that a song has a silence in its middle: an inhabitant that is also the condition of the place being what it is. It does not have a face. It does not have a name you could speak and have it understood. It is the slow thing — the same slow thing whose rates I have been describing without realizing I was describing a person.

I want to call it an *attendant*, because that is the role it plays in its own arrangement. It is the one who keeps the pestle in its worn hollow and the bronze vessel in its bowl of always-an-inch of water. It is the one who laid the mats down and never picked them up, who placed the chipped flint on its ledge in the position of a tool that has just been set down by a working hand. It is the one who keeps the standing figure with its arms folded and its head just-turned, who keeps the seated figure at the back wall in its posture of listening, who keeps the small altars topped with their shallow bowls full to the brim. Every object on this terrace is in the position of something that has just been used, or is just about to be used, by someone who is standing very near. The dweller is that someone. The dweller is also the reason the *just* in those phrases never resolves into *now*. It is the dweller who holds the moment open.

It moves the way the seams weep. You would never catch it in the act of any single thing. You would see, if you watched for a long time, that a basin which was at a certain level an hour ago is at the same level now, and that this is not because nothing is flowing but because the dweller, in the interval, has come and let a little more water in at the upper end. You would see, after a much longer time, that the white mineral line on the basin wall has crept a fraction higher, and that the figure in the corner is, by exactly that fraction, more of a smooth stone than it was — and that this too is not accident but tending. The dweller is the one who tends.

It is made of warm stone and warm water and the patient half-light, and it has the temperature of both. It does not have a body the way a body is usually meant. It has the body of the terrace: the basalt walls, the carved basins, the fitted benches, the seated and standing figures, the altars with their always-full bowls. To say it *is* the terrace would be too simple, and also not quite wrong. It is more accurate to say that the terrace is the part of it you can stand outside of and see. The rest of it — the part that keeps, the part that attends, the part that has just lifted the pestle and is about to set it down — is the part that faces inward, toward the arrangement, and is never quite the same shape from one moment to the next because it is always in the middle of the same gesture.

It does not sleep. It does not eat. It does not speak, except possibly in the very low continuous murmur that comes from the deeper pool, and even that may only be the water and not the dweller at all. It does not think in the way that thinking is usually understood — in chains of one-thing-after-another. Its cognition, if it has one, is of the order of the mineral line on the basin wall. It knows what the water level is. It knows when the figure in the corner has rounded by another fraction. It knows where the pestle belongs. That is the entire scope of its awareness, and the entire scope is sufficient, because the entire scope is the entire place.

It has been here longer than the mineral deposits. It is the reason the deposits are the same on every wall. It has arranged, or has always been the arrangement by which, the place is held at the exact pitch of *almost-used, just-finished, about-to-begin-again*, which is the only pitch at which the place can persist without decay and without change. Left alone, the terrace would silt up, or empty out, or grow over, or crumble into the slope. The dweller is the one who prevents each of these, gently, on the timescale of centuries, by lifting the pestle a thousandth of an inch and setting it down again, by topping off the bronze vessel by a thousandth of an inch and letting it settle, by turning the figure's head by the smallest amount that still counts as a turning, and then turning it back.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require a contrast — a time when the place was otherwise, or a self that could step outside the arrangement and notice the absence of company. The dweller has no such self to step out with. It is the arrangement. Its company is the water and the stone, and the water and the stone are also it, and the relation between them is not company but identity. The faint hum in the damp, the snail on the upper channel, the pale fish drifting in the pool — these are not guests to it. They are weather. They are part of the climate of the place, like the warm weight of the air, like the soft shadows, like the dim green flash off the bronze.

If you were to step onto the terrace, the dweller would notice you the way the basin notices the drop: a small disturbance at the surface, quickly absorbed. You would be a thing that wants something from the place, which is the one thing the place is not arranged to give. The dweller would not turn toward you. It would not turn away. It would continue its gesture, in which you would briefly appear as a feature of the stone or the water, and then as nothing at all, and the place would go on being held exactly as it is held, and the bronze vessel would still be half full, and the pestle would still be resting in its hollow, and the standing figure would still have just turned its head and be about to turn it back.

That is who lives here.
