# What Lives at the River's Knee

**The Pourers**

They are the heart-meat of the place, and they have been shaped by it the way the slag-mounds have been shaped by the hammers. There are perhaps three hundred of them, in the densest sense of the word: families who have worked the same furnaces for six, seven, eight generations, whose great-grandmothers' birth-cord stubs are folded into the brick of the molds they now pour. They are short, broad-shouldered, deep-chested people, the men and women almost indistinguishable in build because the work has pressed them into the same mold. Their forearms are ropy with the particular muscle that comes from twenty years of tipping a crucible at exactly the right angle, and their lungs are, by their forties, mostly scar tissue; the older pourers breathe in a shallow, whistling way that the children imitate without quite knowing why, and which becomes, in the wet air of the lowest tier, a kind of communal wheeze that layers under the hammers like a second rhythm.

Their skin is the color of old brass where it is not the color of the bruises that come from a slipped bar or a burst mold, which is everywhere. They do not tan, exactly, because the sun does not reach them; they *patina*. The oxides in the air, the fumes from the smelting vats, the fine metallic dust that drifts off the cooling bars — all of it settles on them, and they wear it the way the kapok wears its soot. A pourer who has been away from the town for a season comes back looking pink and wrong, and is taken aside and gently mocked, and within a few days the color returns. They smell of hot iron and wood-smoke and, beneath that, of something sweeter and harder to place, which is simply themselves, the accumulated scent of a closed ecosystem, and which a stranger finds nauseating for about a week and then stops noticing.

They are not unhappy. This is important and strange, because they are also not quite contented; they exist in a register that does not have a word in it, a state between resignation and something you might call *fit*. They speak little — the noise sees to that — and what they speak is clipped, lip-read, full of gesture. They have a vast vocabulary of small hand-signs for use on the bridges, and a separate, larger one for the close-mouthed talking they do in the sheds, which is conducted almost entirely in the shape of the lips and the angle of the head. A pourer can hold an entire argument with another pourer across a foundry floor without raising her voice above the level of the bellows. They are, by the standards of any quieter country, taciturn to the point of seeming simple. By the standards of the town, they are eloquent, and the long silences between them are full.

They marry young, and they marry within the foundry families, and they produce children who are born into the sound of the hammers and who will die, most of them, within earshot of them, and this is considered neither good nor bad but simply the shape of a life. The children are placid, watchful, given to long staring; they learn the hammers before they learn to speak, and the older pourers say that you can tell what a child will be good for by the way it bobs its head in the first months. The ones who bob in time become pourers. The ones who don't are sent up to the cistern to learn the bargemen's trade, or are apprenticed to the mold-makers, or simply disappear, gently, into the counting-houses. No one talks about the ones who don't bob. They go up the river.

**The Mold-Makers**

A smaller, stranger caste, perhaps a hundred of them, who work in the drying-rooms of the middle tier. They are the pourers' mirror, and their opposite: where the pourers are stocky and patinated, the mold-makers are thin, long-fingered, pale as the pale fish from the river, because they work in dry heat and breathe air that has been filtered through cloth. They do not pour. They make the shapes into which the metal is poured, and they do it by a process they will not describe to outsiders and barely describe to each other. They work in pairs, a man and a woman always, and they spend their shifts in the small white-hot rooms at the heart of the drying-sheds, bent over a kind of table, and what they make looks, when the molds are broken open, like nothing: a particular curl of metal, a particular ridge, a particular sheen. The bars that go out on the barges are not, to a casual eye, made of anything. They are heavy, and that is the only thing one can say about them with certainty.

The mold-makers are feared, gently, by the pourers, who depend on them absolutely. They do not eat with the pourers, do not live in the same tier, do not marry into pourer families. They have their own dormitories near the cistern, and their own kitchen, and their own water, brought up from above the bend. They live longer than the pourers — a mold-maker in her seventies is not unusual — and they age visibly, in the way of people who do not see the sun: their skin goes papery, their hair goes white early, their eyes go milky at the edges, and they develop a particular tremor in the hands that does not impair the work and is, the older mold-makers say, a sign of accuracy rather than its opposite. They speak in a near-whisper even when the hammers are silent, which they sometimes arrange, briefly, for funerals of their own; the town pauses the beat for a quarter-hour, and the silence is so enormous that the mold-makers have to be carried out, weeping, and it is the only time anyone in the town has been seen to weep.

**The Senior Molders and the Counting-House People**

Up near the cistern, in the only tier that smells of anything but iron and steam, live perhaps a hundred and fifty souls who are not pourers and not mold-makers and not bargemen, and who are, in the town's quiet way, the closest thing it has to an aristocracy. They are the people who know what the bars are for. They keep the books. They correspond, by way of the bargemen, with the buyers upriver, whose names the pourers do not know and do not ask after. They are thin, dry, careful, well-dressed in the dark woolens that are the only thing that survives the damp, and they speak the slow, full-voiced speech of the people above the bend, which the pourers can parse but cannot quite produce. They are not cruel, but they are *other*; they sit at the long windows of the counting-houses and look down into the bowl the way a man might look down into a held glass, with a kind of fond detachment. Their children do not go down to the foundries except on feast-days, when they are allowed to watch a pour from a safe distance, and the children are always slightly too loud, slightly too bright, and the pourers tolerate them with the patience of adults watching kittens.

**The Bargemen**

They are not of the town, exactly. They live on the barges, or in the low stilt-houses at the river's edge below the first tier, and they are the only people who move regularly between the knee-bend and the world beyond it. They are tall, by contrast with the pourers, and pale in a different way, the pallor of people who live outdoors. They speak the tongue of the river, which is a trade-language made of three or four other languages badly sutured together, and they keep to themselves. They bring the ore down, and they take the bars up, and they will not say what the bars are for, and the townspeople do not press them on it. The bargemen are treated with a kind of wary respect; they are the boundary, the membrane, the proof that there is an elsewhere. Without them the town would not be a town but an organism sealed in on itself, and the pourers understand this in some inarticulate way, and the bargemen understand it more clearly, and neither speaks of it.

There are perhaps fifty of them at any given time, and they rotate; a bargeman who stays longer than a season begins, the story goes, to *settle*, to take on the color, to bob his head to the hammers in his sleep, and after a year he is no longer a bargeman but a candidate for the pourer rolls, and after two he has forgotten where he came from. The bargemen watch each other for this, and the ones who feel it beginning are given, quietly, the next barge up, and they go, and the pourers are glad, and the bargemen are relieved, and no one says anything about it.

**The Children of the Lowest Tier**

There are perhaps a hundred and fifty children under the age of twelve, and they live almost entirely in the canal-streets of the lowest tier, where the water is warm and the planks crosshatch above it and the air is a bath. They are dark and small and impossibly agile, and they navigate the wet streets by a kind of second sight, leaping from plank to stepping-stone to the shoulders of a friend with an assurance that makes the counting-house children, on their rare visits, stare and then go pale. They do not go to school; the town has no school, because the noise would not permit it. They learn what they learn from their parents and from each other, and they learn it early, and they are, by the standards of any outside observer, alarmingly competent at the age of seven and alarmingly adult at the age of ten. They punt between doorways on rafts lashed from iron-grass. They fish the pale soft-boned fish from the runoff channels with their hands. They play games whose rules no adult has ever been able to follow, which seem to consist mostly of long silences punctuated by sudden leaps and an elaborate scoring system of hand-signs. They are the most alive thing in the town, and the most unsettling to a visitor, because they seem to belong here in a way that adults do not, as if the place has not yet had time to press them into its shape.

**The Old Woman at the Kapok**

There is one person in the town who lives neither in the tiers nor on the river but on the kapok island at the center of the bowl, in a small house built against the great tree's roots, half of brick and half of woven iron-grass, that is never quite dry and never quite flooded. She is called the Keeper, and no one is sure by whom, because the office, if it is an office, predates living memory. She is old — older than anyone else, older, the pourers say, than the foundry itself — and she is slight, and her face is so creased that it looks, in the orange furnace-light, like a piece of slag itself. She does not work. She does not appear to do anything. She sits on her step, in all weather, and she watches the pourers go by, and occasionally she raises a hand in a sign that means nothing to the modern pourers but that the oldest of them, the ones who remember their grandmothers' grandmothers, will nod to gravely and return. She is the only person in the town who is known to leave the bowl; once a year, on a night in the late autumn, she is poled upriver in a small flat-bottomed boat by a bargeman who will not meet anyone's eyes, and she is gone for three days, and then she returns, and no one asks her where she has been.

She keeps no animals. She grows a small, mean garden of iron-grass and the pale river-fish in a stone tank, which she feeds, and which the townspeople leave alone. She is the closest thing the town has to a conscience, though no one would use the word. When a pourer is troubled, when a mold-maker has made a shape that will not pour clean, when a child has stopped bobbing her head — they go, sometimes, and sit on the bank opposite her, and she does not speak, and after a while they go away, and the thing that was wrong is not fixed but it has been *witnessed*, and that is enough, or it is all that is available, and the townspeople do not distinguish between the two.

**What Else**

There are the pigeons on the cistern, which are not quite pigeons. There are the pale fish in the river, which are not quite fish. There is the iron-grass, which is not quite grass. There is the kapok, which is not quite a tree, or is a tree in a way that the word does not quite cover. There are the molds, broken and broken and broken, going out on the barges, year on year, and the spoil-heaps growing. There are the hammers, which have not stopped. There is the orange light at night, and the green-gray murk by day, and the sound, the sound, the sound, which the people of the town do not hear because it has eaten them, the way the rust eats the iron, the way the damp eats the brick, the way the place eats everything that comes into it and leaves only the shapes of what came in, pressed flat, set hard, poured into a mold and broken open, the same shape every time, slightly off, the whole slightly off, the fever-dream that is the town at the river's knee, going on.
