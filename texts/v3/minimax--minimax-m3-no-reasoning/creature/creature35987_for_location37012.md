# The Inhabitants of the Steamwall

You would not see them from the ridge. They do not live on the ridge. The ridge is a place they pass through, or are sent to, or climb to only when the machine calls them up.

But they are there. Below. In the wet.

---

## The Tenders

They are people, though the word fits imperfectly. They have been here long enough that the word *people* has been worn down by use, like a stone in a river, until what it points to is something more particular and less general than the word can hold.

You would know them first by their clothes. Everything they wear is layered, and everything is wet, and has been wet so long that the distinction between cloth and skin has begun to blur. Long shirts of woven fiber — not cotton, not wool, something else, something that grows in the lower valley in the too-green strips near the machine's outflow. Over the shirts: capes or aprons of oiled hide, dark with age, slick with condensate. On their feet: wrappings rather than boots, leather strips wound around and around the ankle and shin, replaced when they rot, which is often. On their heads: nothing, or a low hood drawn up against the drizzle, or sometimes a wide-brimmed hat of pressed felt that has gone soft and shapeless with the humidity.

Their faces are weathered in a particular way — not sun-weathered, not wind-chapped, but *damp*-worn. The skin of their cheeks and foreheads is soft, almost pulpy, perpetually flushed. Their eyes are slightly narrowed, as if they have spent years peering through mist. Their hair, where it shows, is lank and dark and tends to grow long; they cut it with stone knives and without much ceremony.

They are not large. The altitude, the wet, the difficulty of the terrain have selected against bigness. They are spare, narrow-shouldered, long-fingered. Their hands are the most distinctive thing about them — the knuckles enlarged, the nails thickened and dark, the skin between the fingers webbed slightly, or appearing webbed, or worn so thin by constant moisture that you cannot tell whether what you are seeing is adaptation or erosion. They can feel things in the water that other hands cannot feel. They know when a pipe is going to fail by putting their palm flat against it and listening with their fingers. They know when the machine has shifted in its sleep by the way the condensation on their skin changes.

They speak, but not much, and not to one another when they can avoid it. They have a sign-language they use across the slope — slow gestures, made with wet hands, visible at a distance. They have names for one another but use them sparingly. The work does not require much speech, and they have come to feel that speech is a kind of expenditure, a small loss of heat, a waste of breath that could be used for breathing the wet air.

They number in the low hundreds. There have never been many of them. The valley will not support many. The machine will not support many. They are not a population so much as a workforce — a population sized to the task.

---

## What They Do

They tend the Lung.

This is the word they use, and they do not know if it is the right word. It has been the word for as long as anyone can remember. Their grandparents used it. Their grandparents' grandparents used it. They use it. They have stopped asking what it is a lung *of*.

The Lung is the long dark structure on the slope, the one that trails pipes into the wet green like roots. It is not, despite the name, a single object. It is a system — a distributed mechanism that takes in water from the upper slopes (and the upper slopes take in water from the clouds, and the clouds take in water from the sea, which is far away but whose presence is felt in every breath) and processes it through stages that no single tender fully understands. There are tenders who know the first stage. There are tenders who know the last stage. There are a few — the old ones, the ones who have been at it longest — who claim to know the whole, but the claims are not consistent, and the younger tenders have stopped trying to reconcile them.

What is known, what everyone knows, is this: water goes in at the top. Water comes out at the bottom. What comes out at the bottom is not the same water that went in at the top. It is *richer*. Darker. It smells of metal and of something organic, something like blood but not blood. When it reaches the lower valley, things grow in it that should not grow at this altitude, in this soil, in this much water. The green of the lower valley is the green of the Lung's outflow. The tenders are the Lung's tenders, and the Lung is the valley's heart, and the valley is their home, and so the arrangement is not questioned.

The work is divided. There are the Uppers, who climb to the high basins and clear the intake grates of moss and debris. There are the Middlers, who work the central mechanism — opening valves, replacing gaskets, watching gauges that may or may not still be connected to what they claim to measure. There are the Downers, who manage the outflow, who shape the channels, who tend the lower plantings and ensure that the rich water is distributed to where the valley needs it. There are the Watchers, who sit in the Lung itself, in its dim interior, and listen to its sounds, and report what they hear.

The Watchers are the strangest of them. They go in young and come out old, though the tenure is not long in years. Something about the Lung ages them faster than the air alone. Their eyes, when they emerge, are slightly wrong — focused on a middle distance, as if they are still listening to something you cannot hear. They do not speak of what they hear. They are not forbidden to. They simply do not.

## The Fauna

The tenders are not alone.

The valley supports a denser population of animals than it ought to, given the altitude and the soil. This is also the Lung's doing. The outflow feeds not only the plants but, through the plants, the things that eat the plants, and through them, the things that eat them. A small ecology has assembled itself around the Lung's bounty, and the tenders have learned to live inside it as one more large animal among many.

**The lizards** on the ridge are the most visible. They are dark — black or very dark brown — and they lie on the wet stone and absorb what little heat the diffused sun provides. They are flatter than you would expect, broader in the body, with longer toes than a desert lizard. They have adapted to the wet. They eat the insects that cluster near the water. They are eaten, in turn, by the hawk-bird.

**The hawk-bird** is not a hawk. It is something with the silhouette of a hawk and the behavior of something older. It circles above the ridge and the upper slope without sound — no wing-whistle, no cry. Its plumage is dark, mottled with green where the lichen-colored light falls across it. It does not need to call. It hunts by sight, and the wet air carries its image clearly. The tenders regard it with a kind of respect. They do not kill it. They have a name for it that translates roughly as *the one who watches the watchers*. They believe it knows things about the Lung that they do not.

**The insects with too many legs** are the tenders' constant companions. They are everywhere — in the wet moss, under the stones, in the folds of clothing, in the Lung itself. They are slow, deliberate, and large enough to be noticed. The tenders do not fear them. They are cleaner than they would otherwise be; the insects eat dead skin, dried blood, the small flakes of rot that the damp encourages. The tenders call them by a sound rather than a word — a soft hiss through the teeth — and the insects respond, after a fashion, by orienting toward the sound. There is a symbiosis here that no one has articulated but everyone has accepted.

**The things with hooves** are the largest animals in the valley. They are not native. They were brought up, long ago, by tenders who are now dead, from somewhere lower and warmer. They have adapted, badly, to the altitude. They are stunted, thick-coated, narrow-chested. They live in the dense growth at the valley's edges and come out to drink at the outflow channels. Their meat is tough and their milk is thin, but the tenders keep them. They are useful. They pull small sledges of stone and pipe-fitting along the prepared paths. They are eaten, sparingly, when an animal is old or injured. The tenders do not name them. Naming an animal you will eat is a kind of cruelty they have decided not to commit.

**Other things** move in the wet green and are not seen. Something large. Something that crashes through the undergrowth and is gone before you can place the sound. Something with a voice like a low hoot, which the tenders hear often and never investigate. The tenders have a word for these unseen presences, but it is a word they use only among themselves, and only at night, and only when the Lung is making its particular night-sound — that long, slow exhalation, like the breath of a sleeper, which can be heard, if you listen, all the way up to the ridge.

## The Rhythm

There is a rhythm to the place, and the tenders live inside it.

In the morning — though morning is a courtesy here, since the light does not change much — the Uppers climb. They move in single file up the prepared paths, switching back and forth across the slope, their wet clothes dark against the wetter green. They carry tools wrapped in oilcloth: pry-bars, scrapers, the long-handled scoops they use to clear the intake grates. They work in silence. They come down in early afternoon, muddy to the knees, and report to the Middlers in soft gestures.

The Middlers work the central mechanism in shifts. The Lung does not stop, and so the work around it does not stop. The shifts are not equal. Some tenders work for hours. Some work for a few minutes and then go and sit in the wet air and breathe and look at nothing for a while. The Lung seems to tolerate this. It has, over its long life, accommodated itself to the rhythms of the people around it. When a tender is exhausted, the mechanism seems to run a little steadier, as if it is taking up the slack. When the tenders are all working hard, it seems to run a little rougher, as if it is asking something of them.

The Downers work the lower valley in the long damp hours when the light is at its dimmest. They shape the channels. They tend the plantings — not crops, exactly, but a kind of managed wildness, the rich dark growth that the Lung's outflow encourages. They harvest what can be harvested: the waxy-leafed succulents, certain of the mosses, the tubers that grow in the Lung-fed soil. They eat these things. They have been eating these things for a very long time.

In the evening — again, a courtesy — the tenders gather. Not all of them. Some are still on shift. Some prefer to be alone. But a number come to a low stone platform near the Lung's outflow, where the air is warmest and the sound of the mechanism is a kind of company. They do not speak much. They eat. They pass a leather cup of fermented succulent-juice from hand to hand. They watch the too-green valley below them and the dimming clouds above them and they wait for the Lung to make its night-sound.

When the sound comes — that long slow exhalation — they are quiet. They listen. They do not know what they are listening for. They have never known. They listen anyway.

Then they go to their shelters — low stone structures, half-buried in the slope, roofed with slab and moss — and they sleep, in the wet, in the warm, in the constant susurration of water.

---

## The Question

There is a question the younger tenders sometimes ask the older ones, in the soft private hours when the Lung is quiet.

The question is: *Why does the Lung run?*

The older tenders do not answer. They have heard the question before. They asked it themselves, once. They have, over their long tenures, considered it carefully, and they have come to the conclusion that the question is not the right question. The Lung runs because it runs. It has always run. It will continue to run. The asking of why is a kind of ungrateful arithmetic — a refusal to accept the place as it is, a hunger for a meaning that the place does not, perhaps, intend to provide.

But sometimes, when an older tender is very tired, and the Lung has been making its night-sound for a long time, and the wet air is heavy in the chest, they will say a thing. They will say: *It runs because we are here. It runs because we tend it. If we stopped tending it, it would stop, and then what would we be?*

This is not an answer. The younger tenders know it is not an answer. They ask the question anyway, and the older ones say the thing anyway, and the Lung runs anyway, and the water flows down the slope anyway, and the valley is green anyway, and the tenders live in it anyway, and have lived in it for a very long time.

It is, in its way, an arrangement. It is not a happy one. It is not an unhappy one. It is the only one they have.
