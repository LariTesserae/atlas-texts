# Who Lives in the Plateau of Korrun

They have stopped counting birthdays, though they once kept a record of them somewhere in the scratched ledgers in the back room of the long building near the cistern plaza. Now no one asks. A woman who appears to be in her fifth decade may have a great-grandchild who also appears to be in her fifth decade, and they greet each other as cousins, or friends, or strangers, depending on what kind of morning it is. They do not age past a certain point. They are not immortal in the way that stories usually mean immortal — they can be hurt, they can fall from the erratic in the central square and break a hip, they can sicken in the damp and lie abed for a week with a fever. But they mend. They mend very well, in fact, and the mending does not appear to leave them any older than they were before the mending was needed. A child who has been climbing the rowan trees in the lee of the northern erratic for what feels, to a visitor, like three years, is still climbing them at what is recognizably the same age, with the same scraped knees, the same freckled face, the same missing front tooth that the mothers of the plateau have given up hoping will grow in.

They are slight. They are shorter on average than lowlanders, by perhaps a hand's breadth, and thinner in the wrist and ankle, though their hands are broad and strong from the stonework. Their skin, where the damp has not chapped it, has a pale even quality that looks, in certain lights, almost like the pale gray-green stone of the mountains themselves. Their hair tends to dark — black or very deep brown — and they wear it long, both men and women, plaited or tied back with cords of braided wool, because the wind on the plateau is unkind to loose hair. Their eyes are dark too, and set close, and quick. They blink often in the damp. They have a way of looking at you that suggests they are considering what you have said and have decided, gently, that you do not quite mean it.

They are quiet. They do not speak loudly even when calling to one another across the stream that surfaces in the town's middle section; they use a cupped hand or a particular whistle that has three notes and is not the same three notes the black bird sings, though the resemblance has been remarked upon. They do not gossip in the way lowlanders gossip, or if they do, the gossip is so slow and so careful that it is hard to recognize as such. A piece of news — a death in a lowland village, a marriage, a change of magistrate — will move through the town over the course of weeks, passed from one doorway to the next in the early mornings when the condensation is heaviest and the slate roofs are at their loudest ringing. They have phrases for things that lowlanders do not have phrases for. There is a word for the exact moment when the mist reaches the height at which the bell should be rung. There is a word for the kind of silence the plateau has at dusk, which is not the silence of noon and not the silence of midnight. There is a word for the lichen that grows on the north walls but never, in any recorded season, has grown any further north than it already is.

They work steadily. They do not hurry. The quarrying of the slate and the building stone is done in shifts that are not, strictly speaking, scheduled, but which the quarrymen fall into as naturally as breathing — three days on, one day in the byres, two days on, a half-day at the erratic with the shrine, and so on, in a rotation that has been stable for so long that the quarrymen themselves do not remember proposing it. The sheep are sheared in late summer when the heather flowers. The goats are milked twice a day, by women and children, into wooden buckets that are scrubbed with sand from the streambed. The mules are shod by a farrier who works in a corner of the lower town, and whose family has worked that corner for a hundred generations, though the farrier himself looks to be a young man of twenty-five, and has looked to be a young man of twenty-five since before the oldest visitor to the plateau can remember. He sings while he works. The song is the same song every morning. It has three verses, though only the first is ever sung aloud; the other two are known but not used.

They are not wealthy. The houses are tight against the weather and roomy against the family, and the rooms are spare — a bed of heather stuffed into a tick, a table of planed slate resting on trestles, a chest of cedar wood for the few clothes they own, a rack on the wall for the copper pots, an iron lamp fueled with rendered tallow, and a small clay brazier for the damp evenings. The walls are hung, sometimes, with woven hangings in muted colors — undyed wool, the russet of the rowan berries, the deep green of the moss — but more often the walls are bare stone, and the bare stone is the decoration. They eat simply. Bread of barley or oat, baked in communal ovens in the lower town, brought home still warm in cloth. Cheese of goat or sheep. A stew of root vegetables and the small gray-brown deer that are hunted in late autumn, when the deer are fat and the cold has not yet settled in. Dried rowan berries, picked by the barefoot children, strung on threads and hung in the cool pantries. The bitter herbal tea, which is brewed from the leaves of a low shrub that grows only on the plateau and nowhere else that anyone has been able to find. They drink it from cups of unglazed clay, and they drink it in the evenings, and they offer it to visitors as a matter of course, without asking whether the visitor wants it.

They are kind to animals. The dogs are working dogs, half-trained, watchful, and they sleep in the doorways of the houses, and they are not petted in the lowland way but they are spoken to, often, in low voices, and given the first sip of the stew. The pigeons are not eaten, though they are numerous enough to be eaten; no one in living memory has eaten a pigeon from the plateau. The children are told, when they ask why, that the pigeons belong to the slate and the slate belongs to the pigeons, and this is not quite an answer but it is the answer that is given. The deer are hunted only in late autumn and only with a quota that is decided, each year, by the same consultation among the same elders in the back room of the long building. The children are not told about the quota. The dogs know.

They are religious, or something like it. The shrine built into the lee of the erratic in the central square is not dedicated to any being that has a name. It contains a small stone basin that is kept full of water from the stream, and a few worn copper offerings that have been there so long that no one remembers placing them, and a single sprig of rowan, renewed each week by a different family. The bell in the long building is rung at the height of the mist, and at dawn, and at the death of a townsman, though deaths are so rare that the bell has been rung for that purpose only twice in the memory of anyone living, and one of those times was a visitor. There is a festival in late summer when the heather is at its peak, and the children carry armfuls of it through the streets and leave it on the doorsteps of the houses, and the older people sit on the stone bench in the cistern plaza and watch, and no one speaks of what is being celebrated because it has never needed to be spoken of.

They are uneasy about visitors, though they would never admit it and would be horrified if the admission were made for them. Visitors are received with the bitter tea, and with a bed if one is needed, and with the polite attention that is offered to anyone who has made the long climb. Visitors are asked, gently, how long they mean to stay. Visitors who say they do not know are told that this is fine. Visitors who say they are leaving soon are told that this is also fine. Visitors who say they would like to stay are not told that this is fine, but are not told it is not, either; the subject is changed, and the tea is poured again, and the conversation turns to the weather, which is always the same. The visitors who stay long enough begin to notice the stillness. The visitors who stay long enough to notice the stillness begin to notice the not-aging. The visitors who notice the not-aging begin to ask questions. The questions are answered, as well as they can be answered, by the same old woman in the same back room, who reads the same scratched records and consults them and shrugs, and says that time here moves differently, or does not move at all, or that the question is not well-formed, and pours another cup of tea, and changes the subject.

They do not, themselves, ask why they do not age. They have asked, perhaps, in some earlier century. They have stopped asking. The not-aging is not a thing they have decided upon or been granted or earned. It is, like the damp, like the coolness, like the lichens that do not spread, a quality of the place. They are of the place. They were born here, or they were not born here and they have been here so long that the distinction has ceased to mean anything. They are the place the way the mosses are the place, the way the slate is the place, the way the black bird on the weathervane is the place — persistent, patient, unchanging, and entirely themselves. They will be here tomorrow. They were here yesterday. They will be here when the mountains have learned to be gentle with themselves, which will not be for a hundred thousand years, and by which time the question will still not be well-formed, and the tea will still be bitter, and the bell will still be rung at the height of the mist, and the children will still be called in from the plateau, and the dogs will still come home.
