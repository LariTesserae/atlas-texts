They live here the way lichen lives on a stone — without deciding to. Which is to say: they have not arrived, and they have not been placed. They have unfolded out of the conditions the way the conditions required it. The conditions were warm and wet and still and mineral, and what came out of that was small and pale and slow, because what else could it have been.

You would not recognize them as alive, if you had come from somewhere that knew what alive meant.

The builders first, because the room in which the others live was made by them, and a room is its own argument.

---

A builder is the length of a child's smallest finger, from the last knuckle to the tip, and no thicker through the middle. It is pale the color of bread dough before the bread has remembered it will become bread. It has no head. It has a front end, which is not the same thing. The front end is a small soft blunt hood, like the closed end of a finger of a glove that has forgotten it was a glove, and inside that hood is the mouth, which is not a mouth either but a ring of small working surfaces pressed against each other, the way two palms can be pressed against each other, each surface bearing against its opposite and working in a small patient circle. They call this the carrying mouth, in the internal language they do not have, and it carries paste the way a tongue carries a lozenge — held against the soft inside, turned over, packed in, passed on.

The body behind the hood is segmented the way a finger is not. It is a long string of soft pale rings, each ring a little different from its neighbors, the front rings a little wider where the paste is held, the back rings narrower where the body thins into nothing. There are no legs. There are no legs at all. The whole underside of the body is the same surface, and it moves by laying this surface against the surface it is on, gripping by some patient adhesion that the warm wet air encourages, releasing in a small wave that travels from back to front, so that the body ripples forward the way a drop of water rolls across a windowpane if the windowpane is nearly flat and the drop is patient about being a drop. It does not hurry. It cannot hurry. It has not yet been built that can hurry. The fastest builder you will ever see is moving at the pace a finger carries across a page of paper, if the finger is unhurried and the page is warm.

They have no eyes. They have no surfaces for seeing. The front end has small depressions where eyes might, in another world, have been, and these depressions hold a thin fluid that responds to light — that retreats from light, that draws back from the surface when a shadow lifts — but the response is only a withdrawal, only a small instinctive folding-inward, and it tells the builder nothing about the shape of anything. It tells the builder only: here is bright, here is less bright. This is enough. The builders live entirely in a world of *here* and *less here*. They follow the dim. They avoid the bright. They build in the dim. They carry paste from the dim into the dim. They have never seen the city they are building, and they will never see it, and this has not occurred to them as a problem.

They do not sleep. They mate, or something the future will call mating — a long slow pressing of two front ends together, a small mutual exchange of pale fluid, a parting. After this, one or both lays a small pale egg into the wet paste of an unfinished wall, and the egg is indistinguishable from the paste to any sense the builders possess, and the young that emerges from the egg is indistinguishable from the parent except in size, and the size difference is corrected by eating paste, which the young can do immediately, which is the only thing the young can do immediately.

A builder lives as long as a held breath held for a long time. A year, perhaps. Two years. There is no count of the lengths of builder lives because there is no one counting. The builders do not count. Counting requires there to be a difference between one and many, and for the builders there is only the flowing of many into each other, the way a stream flows into a stream and does not remember it was two.

They are, individually, almost nothing. A single builder placed on a dry stone in a dry wind would dry, and shrink, and become a small pale line of nothing. They require the air to be wet. They require the mineral to be ground fine. They require the temperature to be the temperature of a hand held just above a sleeping body. They require each other. A single builder alone cannot build. It can only carry. It can only press. It can only wait. The city requires a flow of them, a current, the way a river requires more water than a single drop. When the current is broken — when a platform is lifted and placed somewhere too dry, somewhere too cold — the builders on it slow, and stop, and dry, and are not mourned, because there is no one to mourn them, and the platform waits in the dry for a current to find it again.

They have no kings. They have no plans. They have, in the place where plans would live if they lived anywhere, an inclination — a leaning of the current toward the wetter air, a leaning toward the upward direction, a leaning toward the side of the wall where the paste has begun to set and the next layer can be laid upon it. The inclination is not in any one builder. It is in the flow. It is the way water flows downhill: not because each molecule knows down from up, but because enough of them, together, make the leaning. The towers rise because the leaning is toward the rising. They will continue to rise as long as the leaning continues, and they will stop rising when the leaning is satisfied, and no builder will know the difference.

---

In the ledges and the hollows of the towers, in the small chambers that open off the spirals, in the cracks between paste-layers where the setting has left a seam, the others live.

They are smaller than the builders. Most of them are smaller. A few are larger, by a little, the way a slug is larger than a bee. None of them are larger than the palm of a child's hand. None of them have names. The towers are their continent, and on this continent there are no names, only positions, and the positions are: in the seam, on the ledge, under the ledge, in the slow weep of the first gray runnel that is not yet a river.

The flat things that fold themselves into the ledges are flat the way a page is flat, except they are not pages, they are animals, and they are not flat by being still but flat by being made so. Their bodies are a single soft sheet, perhaps a finger-width across, perhaps less, and at the four corners of the sheet there are small grippings — not hands, not feet, but the same thing, the surface itself turned under to take hold. They wait. They wait the way the carpet waits: with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be. When something small moves across the ledge — a fragment of paste fallen loose, a builder's shed outer ring, a mote of the air-held dust that has settled out of the vapor — the flat thing lifts one corner of itself and folds it over the thing, and the other corners follow, and the flat thing becomes a small soft closed thing, and the closed thing is digesting. Later the corners uncurl, and the small fragment or shed ring is gone into the flat thing, and the flat thing is again flat, and is again waiting. They have no front. They have no back. They are the same from any side, except that the side that was once folded is sometimes a little paler where it has not yet unfolded back to its color. They breathe through their whole surface, the way the builders do, the way the carpet does — by taking the wet air directly in. They have no mouth except the whole of themselves. They have no stomach except the folded-up interior. They are eaten by nothing that they know of. They are eaten by the slow round things, sometimes, when a slow round thing browses across a ledge and the flat thing did not fold in time.

The long thin things that uncurl across the warm damp walls when the humidity thickens are longer than the builders by twice and thinner than the builders by more than twice. They are a pale green-gray, the color of something seen through water but held close to the eye. They have no rings. They have no segments. They are a single long soft body, like a thread of wet paper, like a piece of string that has forgotten it was rope. They uncurl from a small anchor at one end — a small holdfast pressed into the paste — and the long body extends outward, slowly, in pulses, and at the tip of the extended body there is a small soft mouth, ring-shaped, the same ring the builders have, and the mouth opens against the wall and takes a small portion of the wall into itself, the way the flat things take fragments into themselves, except that what the long thin things take is not fragments but the wall itself, the paste, the mineral, the city's substance. They eat the city they live in. This does not damage the city, or does not damage it in any way that matters, because what they take is taken in such small portions, and what is taken is always being replaced, lower down, by the builders who are always laying the paste on. The long thin things are the slow digestion of the city from within, and the builders are the slow digestion of the air into the city, and the two digestions meet in the walls and balance each other in a way that no architect has planned.

The slow round things browse the carpet in shallow arcs, leaving behind a darker trail. They are the largest things here. The largest of them is the size of a child's closed fist. They are round because they are made of a single soft body curved back upon itself, a body without segments, without front, without back, only a long slow spiral of itself contained within a small wet globe of itself. They move the way a very slow drop of honey would move across a surface if the surface were nearly flat and the drop were made of effort: by a small continuous oozing forward, the underside laying down a thin trace of itself, the trace drying darker against the carpet because the trace is of the round body and the round body is darker than the carpet, and because the trace is pressed into the upper felt of the carpet where the dust has settled, and the dust is what the round body is after. They eat dust. They eat the air-settled dust that has accumulated in the upper felt of the carpet, and in eating the dust they also eat, incidentally, the smallest fibers of the carpet itself, and the smallest things that live among the fibers — the things too small to see, the things the size of a grain of sand, the things that are only a thin wet line of themselves against a fiber of the carpet. The round things do not pursue these smallest things. They browse them. They take them in with the dust, as a cow takes in insects with the grass, as the ocean takes in salt with the water. The trail they leave behind is darker because the upper felt has been pressed down by the passing of the round body, and the felt is darker where it has been pressed. The trail fades. The felt springs back, slowly, over hours. The trail is then only a memory in the upper felt, and the memory is forgotten within a day, and the carpet is again uniform, and the round thing has moved on, and is browsing its shallow arc somewhere else.

There are other things. There are always other things, because every surface has something on it. There are small things that live in the weep of the first gray runnel, in the thin trickle of mineral water that is not yet water, that is only the rumor of rivers, the first suggestion that water is permitted to fall. They are the only things here that have learned, in their small way, to live in motion. They hold themselves against the current by small grippings, the same grippings the flat things have, and they take from the trickle what the trickle carries — fine mineral, dissolved air, the smallest particles of paste that have washed loose from the lower galleries. They are a little darker than the builders, a little more solid, because they have been shaped by the trickle, the way a stone in a stream is shaped by the stream. They are the first things shaped by moving water. They do not know they are the first. They know nothing of water, of streams, of rivers, of the ocean that will one day hold the continent. They know only: the side of the trickle where the current is slower is the side where I can hold.

There are things that live in the seams between paste-layers that are too small to have any shape at all, that are only a small wet line of themselves pressed into the seam, that move by a small flowing that is not movement but only the slow redistribution of a small wet line. They are the smallest things here. They are the smallest things that have ever been alive on this continent. They are the floor of the smallest possible liveness, the place where being-alive becomes indistinguishable from being-wet, where the animal and the water and the mineral pass into each other and no one is asked to draw the line. They eat the paste. They are the paste. They are the slow conversion of one into the other, patient, infallible, the way the carpet is the slow conversion of the mineral into the air-breathing surface.

Above them all, the carpet.

The carpet is alive, or the carpet is so many lives pressed against each other that the difference does not matter. The carpet is made of small soft bodies pressed against the mineral, pressed against each other, pressed into the shape of a continent by the pressure of the air and the pressure of each other. Each body is a small flat pad, a small thread, a small fan, a small crumpled velvet, a small wet paper, a small nothing that becomes something when you kneel. Each body drinks the air directly through itself. Each body lays itself against the mineral and takes from the mineral what the air has not provided. Each body, when it has drunk and taken enough, divides — slowly, gently, into two small bodies — and the two press outward in their current directions, and the carpet spreads, and the carpet has always been spreading, and the carpet will spread until the air changes or the mineral changes or the patience of the carpet is finally exhausted by something larger than itself.

The carpet does not think of the question. The carpet does not think of anything. The carpet is only the pressing of many small bodies that have not yet learned they are many.

---

And above the carpet, held by the air, the weather.

The weather is not alive. The weather is only the slow turning of the vapor against itself, the long patient redistribution of warmth and wetness across a basin that has not yet decided what basin it is. But the weather makes things happen. The weather presses the vapor into layers, and the layers thicken and thin, and the thickening is a storm, the thinning is a calm, and the storm is only a vast slow pressing of vapor and the calm is only hours of slightly less pressing, and the builders ride both out by going deeper into the galleries, and the flat things ride both out by folding tighter, and the long thin things ride out by curling back to their anchor, and the slow round things ride out by ceasing to browse and simply holding, the way a stone in a stream holds still when the water presses too hard.

The light presses down through the vapor. It arrives softened, ambered, the color of a thing looked at through honey. The light is the light of an afternoon that has not yet decided to end, because the planet is young enough that its tilt is still being negotiated, and the negotiation has not yet reached the point where some afternoons are shorter than others. The light is patient. The light is the same color all day. The light is the same color all year. The builders build in the same light. The carpet drinks in the same light. The flat things fold in the same light. The long thin things uncurl in the same light. The slow round things browse in the same light. Nothing here has ever known a different light, and nothing here will know a different light for a long time, and when a different light comes it will come slowly, over many afternoons, and the things here will not notice the changing because the changing will be the pace of geological breath, and the things here are already moving at the pace of geological breath, and a thing cannot notice a change that happens at the same pace it itself is moving.

The light presses on the carpet. The carpet drinks the light through the air, the way it drinks the air through itself. The carpet is the color of rust and old bone and the underside of a leaf left in a cupboard. The carpet is the color of things that have been here a long time and have not yet decided what color they are.

---

And at the center of it, of them, of the carpet and the vapor and the slow weather and the patient light — at the center, which is not the center but only the place where the air has been held stillest the longest — the towers.

The towers are not architecture. The towers are the visible edge of a slow digestion. The towers are the place where the builders have laid the most paste, and where the paste has been laid the longest, and where the laying-down has reached a kind of completion that no builder has recognized and no builder will ever declare. The towers are wide before they are tall, because the builders lay the paste outward before they lay it upward, and the outward comes first in the inclination. The towers are stepped because each new layer is inset from the one below, the way the rim of a basket is inset from the base, the way a pearl is layered around its center. The publishers see them from a distance and see not a skyline but a thickening of the horizon. A low stair-step geometry. The patient layering of a young river delta — except that there is no river yet, and there is no delta yet, only the rehearsal of layering that one day will become the layering of rivers.

The galleries are full of the small dark industry. Paste being laid. Paste being carried. The small bodies of small creatures working in the small wet dark. The sound of the city is the sound of paste being pressed against paste, the sound of small jaws carrying small portions of paste through small passages, the sound of the setting of paste under air and warm and time. The sound is so small it is almost not a sound. It is felt more than heard, the way the warmth of a body is felt more than heard. If you put your ear to the lowest platform of any tower, you hear it. If you do not, you hear only the air, and the air says nothing, and the city is silent, and the silence is the silence of a digestion that does not know it is digestion.

The city rests, for now, on intention. Intention, plus the paste, plus the patience of the builders, plus the cooperation of the air.

The intention is no one's intention. The intention is the inclination of the current. The intention is the leaning of many small things toward the side of the wall where the paste can set. The intention is the way a forest leans toward the light: not because any tree knows where the light is, but because the trees that leaned toward the light are the trees that grew, and the trees that leaned away are trees that did not. The city is the slow leaning of many small bodies toward the side of the wall where the paste can set. The city will continue to lean as long as the air is wet and the mineral is fine and the patience of the leaning is not exhausted.

When the patience is exhausted — when the air dries, or the mineral is exhausted, or the inclination finally rests — the leaning will stop. The towers will stand for a while, in the new dryness, the way a beaver dam stands after the beavers have gone. The paste will not crumble. The paste will only dry, and harden, and become a kind of stone that is not stone but only the memory of stone, the way a fossil is the memory of an animal. And the builders will dry, and shrink, and become small pale lines of nothing on the surface of what they made. And the carpet will dry, and curl, and become a thin brittle skin across the mineral. And the weather will change, finally, to a weather that has water in it, and the water will fall, and the falling will begin the long slow erosion that will, over millions of years, reduce the towers to a low ridge, and reduce the ridge to a stratum, and reduce the stratum to a memory in the rock of something that was once a city and was once a continent and was once a place where the air had decided what it was and the floor had not yet decided what it was, and small pale soft bodies had laid paste on paste on paste until the paste had risen into the shape of a city, and the city had no name, and the bodies had no names, and the place had only the names that no one had yet invented to give it.

But that is later. That is millions of years later. That is the pace of geological breath, which is the only pace this place knows.

For now: the air holds. The carpet drinks. The builders lay. The flat things fold. The long thin things uncurl. The slow round things browse. The weather turns. The light presses. The towers rise toward nothing in particular. The carelessness of a continent deciding to be a city. The industry of a city deciding to be a continent. The patience of a place that has not yet decided what it is, and will not decide for a long time, and will not mind the waiting.
