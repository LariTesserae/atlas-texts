Nothing lives here.

That is what the legend on the railing says, in the small bronze letters, the ones you have to crouch to read: *no animal life*. The Slake & Greensward Industrial Filtration Company was precise about such things in its day. The Company is gone. The watershed district that took it over was dissolved in 1971. The towers were deeded to the waters themselves, and the waters, being waters, did not sign any papers. The legend meant it when it was written, and the place has been faithful to the legend, and the place has been faithful in a way the legend did not expect.

So nothing lives here.

And yet.

---

The most obvious inhabitants are the plants.

The plants are everywhere. They are on the glass. They are on the iron. They are on the stone cornices and the copper flashing and the iron drainpipes and the gutters and the mullions and the sills behind the glass. They were here before the Company left, and they have been here since, and they have made themselves into the dominant fact of the place, the way weather is the dominant fact of a coast. The moss has climbed the glazing bars in a green pelt so dense it looks shorn. The ivy has threaded itself through the iron lattice and come out the other side and begun to climb back in again, layer on layer, so that in places the wall is six feet thick with vine. The ferns spill out of every mullion in green waterfalls that do not fall, because they are caught in the next vine down.

No one planted any of it. There has been no one here to plant anything since long before the ivy finished its first loop. The plants maintain themselves. The plants maintain the walls. The plants have been working on the walls for so long that the walls are no longer the surfaces — the surfaces are the plants, and the plants are the surfaces, and the iron and glass and stone are just the bones.

There are orchids growing out of the drainage holes of the gutters — *out of* them, not behind them, the orchids have come through the holes the way a fist comes through a paper wall. There are bromeliads the size of your head brimming with rainwater and the drowned husks of insects. The insects got in somehow — on the wind, on the hem of some long-dead workman's coat, on the foot of a bird that landed and did not stay — and they drowned in the brimming bowls and the plants have been eating them ever since, slowly, patiently, the way the plants eat everything. The plants eat the water. The plants eat the air. The plants eat the metal.

The plants are the skin of the place. If the place were an animal, the plants would be its skin. But the place is not an animal, or the place is not only an animal. The place is an animal in the way that a forest is an animal — a forest is not one animal, a forest is ten thousand animals and ten thousand fungi and ten thousand plants all tangled in one another's affairs, and the forest breathes as a forest only because everything in it is breathing in the same rhythm.

The plants breathe.

In and out. In and out.

The ferns lift and the ferns fall. The moss swells and the moss contracts. The vines shift against one another with a soft friction sound, like a sleeper stirring. You have to stand very still for a long time to see any of it. The breathing is so slow it could be the wind, except that the wind is not blowing. The air is perfectly still. The air has been perfectly still for a hundred years.

The plants breathe in the rhythm of something else. The plants breathe in the rhythm of the railing, and the railing breathes in the rhythm of the lake, and the lake breathes in the rhythm of the tower, and the tower breathes in the rhythm of the engines, and the engines breathe in the rhythm of the water. There is a single breath here, and everything in it is breathing it.

---

The oldest inhabitants are the engines.

The engines live in the lake, at the base of the tower, under the black warm water that has no current and no ripples and no wind across it. The engines were installed in 1891. The engines are made of a metal that does not corrode — or that corrodes into a different metal, and the different metal is still functional, and that metal corrodes into another, and another, in a slow alchemical descent through the periodic table that no one is keeping track of anymore. The engines are made of iron that has become something else. The engines are made of copper that has become something else. The engines are made of brass that has become something else. After a hundred and thirty years of transmutation, what the engines are made of now is not what they were made of then, and the engines are still lifting water.

The engines hum.

The engines hum because the engines have always hummed. The engines hum because the water turns the wheels and the wheels turn the engines and the engines lift the water. The engines have been running on the water for so long that the engines have become part of the water, in the way that a stone in a riverbed becomes part of the river. You could not now remove the engines from the lake without removing the lake from itself. The engines are in the lake. The lake is in the engines. The engines hum in your molars when you stand on the plank walk and hold onto the railing, because the railing is connected to the engines by iron and stone and water, and the hum travels.

The engines are the heart, or the engines are the gut. The engines lift the water up through the tower. The water rises through the soil on the rooftop gardens. The water rises through the roots of the plants. The water rises through the stems of the plants. The water reaches the leaves of the plants. The water evaporates from the leaves into the warm air and falls back down again as fat warm drops, and the drops are caught by the plants below, and the plants below send the water back down to the lake, and the lake turns the engines, and the engines lift the water again.

The same water has been lifted and fallen more times than there are stars in the sky. The water has been filtered through the body of the cathedral so many times that the water is no longer only water. The water is warm in the way blood is warm. The water is black in the way blood is black in the veins. The water closes around your ankle like a hand.

---

On the floor of the cathedral, in the ankle-deep water, are the fish.

The fish are the only animals in the cathedral.

The fish are dead.

The fish came in with the water. The fish came up from the lake, drawn in the lift, carried through the engine, through the pipe, through the soil, through the roots — caught and held and slowly dissolved — or carried past the roots and into the cathedral itself, where they settled on the tiled floor and on the sills of the windows and in the lap of the vines, and there they have stayed, in different stages of being eaten.

The plants eat them. The water eats them. The slow green chemistry eats them — the chemistry of roots, of stems, of the moss, of the algae that films the copper-green tiles, of the biofilm at the waterline. The fish are feedstock. The fish are fertilizer. The fish are part of the engine. The fish are lifted up and brought down and lifted up and brought down, and the fish are eaten, and what comes out of the fish is part of what comes down, and what comes down is the water the Upper Greensward Concordat drinks.

The fish have no eyes. The fish have been dead a long time. The fish look up at you from the floor of the cathedral with their eyeless faces and bump against your legs as the water rises, gently, the way balloons bump against your legs in a pool.

You do not know if they are seeing anything.

---

Behind the glass, behind the plants, behind everything, there is light that is moving.

You cannot see what the light is moving on. You can only see the light itself, far back, somewhere in the dark wet heart of the tower, a slow paleness shifting behind layer after layer of green. The light moves the way light moves under a forest canopy — in long slow shafts, in shifting pools, in the dappled pulses of leaves turning in a breeze that isn't there. There is no breeze in the cathedral. The air is perfectly still. The leaves are not turning. And yet the light moves as if something behind everything were breathing.

Maybe something is.

---

In the center of the cathedral, directly under the oculus, directly under the falling water, is the altar.

The altar is a machine.

The altar is a machine the size of a baptismal font, made of brass and copper and glass, and inside the glass there is a wheel, and the wheel is turning. The wheel is turning very slowly, maybe one revolution an hour. The wheel is connected by a shaft to a gear, and the gear is connected by a shaft to another gear, and so on, down into the floor, down through the foundations, down into the lake, where the engines are. The wheel turns because the engines turn. The wheel has been turning since 1891.

Around the base of the font the plants have grown in. The plants have grown *into* the gears. The plants have threaded themselves through the teeth of the gears so that when the gears turn, the plants turn with them, and the plants are not cut, and the plants are not torn, and the plants seem, as far as you can tell, to be enjoying it. The plants are turning in the gears the way prayer is supposed to turn in the mind: slowly, endlessly, without friction, without wear.

The altar hums.

The pews are iron, and the iron is covered in moss, and the moss is breathing — in and out, in and out — in the same slow rhythm as the railing outside. If you sit in one of the pews, the moss takes your weight. The moss holds you. The moss breathes with you.

---

Nothing lives here.

That is what the legend says, and the legend is right, if by *life* you mean what the legend means — animals with hearts and lungs and eyes, animals that move under their own power, animals that flee, animals that die on their own. There are no such animals here. There have been no such animals here since the engines were left to the water.

But something lives here.

Something is breathing here, has been breathing here for over a hundred and thirty years, and will go on breathing when you leave, and will go on breathing when the Upper Greensward Concordat is dissolved, and will go on breathing when no one drinks the water anymore, and will go on breathing after that, for as long as the engines turn and the metal transmutes and the plants grow into the gears and the water goes up and the water comes down and the cathedral breathes and the railing breathes and the moss breathes and the lake breathes and the fish lie on the floor and look up with their eyeless faces and the light moves behind the glass.

The something is not one thing. The something is the plants and the engines and the water and the metal and the dead fish and the light and the glass and the moss that holds you, all of it bound together in the same long breath, all of it doing what it has been doing since 1891, all of it humming.

The legend on the railing says: *Maintained in perpetuity by the waters themselves.*

The legend is right about this too.

The legend does not know what it is right about.

The engines hum.

The cathedral breathes.
