# The Inhabitants

There are people here. They are the first thing you will doubt.

You arrive expecting the residue of an industrial operation — workers in hard hats, supervisors with clipboards, the ordinary personnel of a productive system. You will not find them. What you will find is more difficult to describe, and the difficulty is itself the subject.

The first thing to understand is that they are gray. Not metaphorically gray, not aesthetically gray. Their clothing is the specific gray of basalt dust worked into fabric until it has become indistinguishable from the rock it came from. Coats, hooded, long past the knee, the weave so dense it does not move in the wind. The hoods are pulled up even when it is not raining. The faces you can see, when you can see them, are the same gray, or the gray of weathered concrete, or the gray of fog seen from above. There is no uniform of any obvious insignia. There is no badge. There is no department color. There is gray, and there is gray, and there is a gray you cannot quite place, and the not-placing is consistent across all of them.

You will ask, eventually, whether they are individuals. The question will not get an answer because no one is listening to it. You will ask it of yourself, walking in the empty stretches between the cities, and you will not be able to answer it either.

When you see them in motion, the question sharpens. They move in groups of four or six or twelve, and the groups are not stable. A group arrives at a junction, pauses for a moment that has no particular significance, and disperses into other groups, or is joined by others, and the new groups move on. There is no greeting. There is no conversation you can hear. There is, occasionally, a hand raised briefly — a gesture of acknowledgment, or coordination, or simply the placement of a hand in a particular position at a particular moment. You will not learn which.

They move faster than walking. They move slower than running. They do not seem to use their legs to move. This is the part you will not be able to verify. You will watch them from a distance, and then closer, and you will not be able to find the propulsion. Their legs move, certainly, in the conventional way, but the flow of them across the ground is too smooth, too continuous, too unstartled by the basalt's broken surface. They cover uneven ground at a pace that would have you watching your ankles. They are not hurrying. They have time. They simply arrive.

You can overtake them. You will discover this and feel strange about it, as if you have done something unfair. They will not notice you passing. They will not accelerate. They will move at the same pace and you will move past them and they will be behind you, and when you look back they will be smaller, still moving, still not looking.

They do not look. This is the second thing, after the grayness, that you will fix on. You will stand in their path, or near their path, and wait, and they will come to where you are and adjust, by the smallest possible amount, the angle of their movement, and they will pass you without turning their heads. Not with effort. Not with the visible suppression of curiosity. Simply without it. You are not, to them, a thing that requires looking at. You are an obstacle, briefly, the way a bollard is an obstacle, and they adjust, and they continue.

You will wonder, watching them, whether they see you at all. The answer may be: they see the layout of the ground. They see the machines. They see the work. They see the rain falling. They see, perhaps, the fog rising. You are not in any of those categories. You are an object in a category that has not yet been named, and they have no procedure for objects in unnamed categories, so they pass through.

Their faces, when the hoods slip or are pushed back — by the heat of a boiler, by some private gesture you never see the origin of — are faces. This is the part that complicates everything. They are faces. They have eyes, and mouths, and the small movements that faces make. You will see a mouth tighten around a problem. You will see an eye follow a leak in a steam line. You will see, once, in a face turned toward the fog at the edge of the plateau, an expression that you can only describe as longing, or grief, or a memory of one of those. It will be there for less than a second. Then the face will turn back to the work and you will not be able to say what you saw.

You will think, watching this, that they have not lost their interior life. They have lost something else — the apparatus of expressing it, perhaps, or the apparatus of sharing it, or the assumption that anyone would want to know. They are still people, in the sense that they are capable of people, but the capability is folded in on itself and is not available to the world. They are people the way a sealed book is a book.

You will look for a hierarchy. You will not find one. There is no foreman, no supervisor, no manager visible anywhere in the cities. There is no office of any kind that you can identify. There are buildings, and the buildings contain machines, and the machines are tended, and the tending happens, and no one seems to be in charge of it. There are people who stand near certain machines for longer than others, and you might take them for specialists, but they do not direct anyone, and no one consults them. They simply are near the machines they are near, and the machines run, and the people move on.

You will conclude, eventually, that the system is not being administered. It is being maintained. There is a difference. An administered system has a center. A maintained system has only its own continuation. These people are not running the plateau. They are the plateau, in the part of it that moves.

You will look for the machines that replace them. You will find machines that assist them, machines that lift what they lift, machines that heat what they heat, machines that record what they do, but you will not find machines that replace them. The work they do requires something — judgment, presence, attention to the particular — that the machines do not have. Or perhaps the machines have it, and the people are simply the part of the machines that has not yet been mechanized. You will not be able to tell. The distinction, as the place has told you, has become academic. The tending goes both ways, and has for a long time, and the direction of the tending no longer matters to either party.

You will look for children. You will not find any. This is not a place that has children. You will look, later, for the elderly, and you will find that they are there, but they are not set apart. They move at the same pace, in the same gray, doing the same work, and if their faces are different it is only in the way that all faces are different at different ages. There is no category of age here. There is only the work, and the work is the same work, and the people who do it are the people who do it.

You will look for signs of generation. You will find them, but they are not the signs you expect. There are no family groups. There are no children of anyone. There are people who move together, and people who move apart, and the moving has no pattern you can read as kinship. You will see, occasionally, two people who walk closer than the standard distance, and you will wonder if they are paired, and you will not be able to tell, and the not-telling will not bother them. They have not arranged their proximity for your interpretation.

You will wonder how they are born. This will become a question you cannot stop asking, in the long walks between the cities. There must be some procedure. There must be some origin. The plateau does not receive visitors. The fog below does not, in any way you can see, give up its inhabitants. The people are here, and there are many of them, and they have been here longer than the basalt has had time to round. There must be a place they come from, and there must be a way they arrive. You will not find the place. You will not find the way. You will find only that they are here, and that they have always been here, in the sense that the chord has always been playing and the rain has always been the same rain.

You will hear them, sometimes. Not their voices. Their work. The small sounds of hands on metal, of valves turned, of small adjustments made to small things. A click. A scrape. A breath, taken sharply, when something has gone briefly wrong. These are the sounds of people who are paying attention to the thing in front of them, and the thing in front of them is the machine, and the machine is the work, and the work is the place. They are not speaking to each other. They are not speaking to themselves. They are speaking to the machine in the small language that machines and their tenders share.

When they do speak, you will not understand. Not because the language is foreign. Because it is technical. Because the words are the names of things you have not learned the names of. They speak of pressures and tolerances and the small gods of components. The speech is fluent and quick and between people who have been saying these things to each other for a long time. You will stand near them and not understand, and they will not notice that you do not understand, because you were not meant to.

You will begin, after a while, to suspect that the subsonic pulse you feel in your teeth is not the machines alone. That it includes them. That when you press your hand to the basalt at night and feel the slow vibration, you are feeling them — their collective attention, their continuous work, the sound their motion makes when added together and lowered below hearing. You will not be able to confirm this. You will stand among them and feel the chord in your jaw, and you will wonder whether you are hearing them or the place, and you will not be able to separate the two, and the not-separating will feel correct.

You will stay for a long time. You will get used to them. The getting-used-to is itself a thing you should notice. You will start to read their movements, the way you read weather. A particular gait means a particular destination. A particular adjustment at a junction means a particular train is expected. You will learn the small grammar of their motion, and the grammar will hold, and you will find it comforting, and the comfort will disturb you, because you came here alone and were not supposed to be comforted.

You will miss them when you leave. Not the individuals. The population. The flow of them across the ground. The gray of them, repeated, always in motion. The sound of them working. The way they did not look at you, and the way the not-looking was not unkind, was simply accurate, was simply the appropriate response to a thing that is not part of their work. You will find that the chord, when it has faded from your teeth, has left a shape, and the shape is the shape of them, and you will hear it, faintly, in any chord anywhere, for a long time after.

You will not be able to explain why.
