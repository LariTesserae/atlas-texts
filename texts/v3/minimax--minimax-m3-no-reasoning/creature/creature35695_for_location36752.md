# Who Lives in the Upper Fabrication

The first thing you understand is that *living here* is not the same thing as *being from here*. Most of what occupies this place arrived. But the place itself has produced its own inhabitants too — or, more accurately, has negotiated with what arrived, allowing certain tenancies it would not have permitted elsewhere. The high, wet, stony shelf is not generous. It is selective. What it permits, it changes.

---

## The Herd

You saw them first as movement mistaken for shadow. They are not shadows. They are not quite anything else either.

They graze at the foot of the nearest operating tower — the closest, the most complete, the one whose cables are taut and whose platforms carry equipment that hums in sympathy with the rock. There are perhaps thirty or forty of them, working the broken ground in a loose constellation, and they move with the unhurried priority you noted: the gait of something that owns the lease.

**What they are** is a problem. They read as ungulate — cloven hooves, low shoulders, the cantilevered spine of a ruminant built to browse on slopes that would break a horse. But the proportions are wrong in the way that suggests their ancestors were not. The legs are a little too thick at the knee. The neck is shorter than a goat's, longer than a musk-ox's. The head — when you catch one lifting it, when a vapor plume unfurls from its nostrils and dissolves into the haze — is blunt, heavy, with small eyes set low and wide. You think of ibex. You think of musk-ox. You think of something older than either, something that remembers the steppe and has not entirely forgiven the mountains for being there.

Their coats are the dark of the towers. Charcoal-brown, weathering to a sooty black along the flanks, with a pale underbelly that you only see when one of them climbs an outcrop and the wind combs the hair the wrong way. The outer coat is long and shaggy, dragging at the brisket, matted at the shoulders where they push through the scrub — but there is an undercoat you catch a glimpse of when they shake, and it is the dense, almost felted grey-white of an animal built for altitudes where the thermometer lies. They have adapted; of course they have. The thin air, the wet, the cold — none of it has killed them. It has only made them more insistent.

**How they live here** is by not fighting the place. They do not graze on grass — there is no grass. They work the rock-dust itself, or whatever grows in the rock-dust: a thin crust of lichen, black and bitter; the moss that has colonized the damp undersides of outcrops; the trickle of mineral-rich water that gathers in the deeper cuts and feeds something algal and foul. They lick the stone. You see them do it — lowering their heavy heads to a slab and working at it with a patience that looks almost meditative, their tongues broad and dark, leaving wet tracks that frost over within minutes. They are, in a real sense, eating the mountain. They are also, incidentally, eating what the towers exude: the runoff from the platforms, the trace metals in the steam, the iron-rich dust that sifts down from the grinding of gears they cannot see. Their droppings, when you find them, are dark and faintly metallic. They will outlive anything that grazes at its feet, you wrote, and you were thinking of the towers. You might have been thinking of them, too.

**How they behave** is the part you keep returning to. They are not alarmed. They are not tame. They look up when a cable snaps somewhere overhead — it happens, a percussive *tlang* that echoes across the shelf — and they look up without alarm, the way cattle look up at a train. They resume. They have lived through construction. They have lived through the slow erection of the first tower and the second and the thirtieth. They watched the foundations get poured. They watched the girders go up. They were here when the place was only broken stone, and they will be here when the place is finished stone and the towers are complete and humming with whatever purpose the towers have. They are the constant. The towers are the event.

There is a social structure, if you watch long enough. The largest animals — the heavy-shouldered bulls, if bulls is the word — hold the center of the herd, the sheltered ground between the nearest towers where the steam vents keep the frost at bay. The smaller animals, the yearlings and the females heavy with the next generation, work the margins, the colder rock, the exposed outcrops where the lichen is thinner but the wind is cleaner. Calves — and there are calves, small and absurdly woolly, their legs still too long for their bodies — stay close to the larger females and make a low, continuous sound that is not quite a bleat and not quite a lowing, a hum that fits oddly well with the hum of the towers, as if they have learned to harmonize.

When one of them dies — and they do die, you find the bones sometimes, picked clean and scattered at the foot of an outcrop where the steam keeps scavengers off — the others gather. Not to mourn. To stand. To breathe together in their vapor plumes, which rise and dissolve into the haze, and to be, briefly, a single organism with a single purpose. Then they disperse. They have work to do.

## The Wheelers

Higher up — much higher, where the haze begins to feel like a ceiling — the raptors work.

You saw them as silhouettes wheeling off the heated rock. They are silhouettes still, mostly, because they do not come down. They are dark, broad-winged, with the squared-off primaries of a thing built to ride thermals rather than to flap. Bearded vulture is the closest analogue your brain offers. Lammergeier. The bone-breaker. But the birds here are darker — blacker than any lammergeier, with a pale patch at the base of the tail that you only see when they bank — and they are larger. They ride the updrafts that come off the operating towers, the columns of warm air that rise from the equipment and the steam vents, and they do it with the bored competence of a thing that has been doing it for centuries.

They do not hunt the herd. The herd is too big, too heavy-shouldered, too cohesive. They scavenge, when something dies, but they prefer to work the margins — the outcrops where the smaller fauna lives, the places where the towers have not yet reached and the rock is still being broken by weather rather than by machines. They carry things off in their beaks. The things they carry off are not always dead. They nest, you think, somewhere up the slope, on a ledge you have not found, in a place the haze will not let you see.

Their cries come down thin and remote. A thin piping, almost metallic, with a cadence that suggests sentences. They are talking to each other. They are talking about you, probably. They are talking about the new thing on the slope that was not there yesterday, the warm-blooded thing that does not belong, that smells of soap and machine oil and a lowland valley. They will be watching.

## The Steam-Born

These are not animals. These are not, properly speaking, anything. But they are here.

At the base of certain towers, where the steam vents pulse in their slow irregular breaths, life has colonized the warm wet stone. There is a green-black film that you took at first for algae. It is something else — or something more than that. It moves, slowly, when the steam pulses, a contracting and re-expanding that is almost respiratory. It is fed by the minerals in the steam and the warmth of the rock and the dim light that filters down through the haze, and it is the only color in the place that is not gray or brown or the dull silver of damp steel.

Smaller things live in it. Things with too many legs. Things that fold themselves into the cracks when you step near and uncurl again when you move off. Things that are not quite insects — the body plan is wrong, the legs are too many, the antennae are too long — but that occupy the niche insects would occupy if insects could survive at this altitude on this rock. They eat the green-black film. They are eaten, in turn, by something even smaller that you cannot see but whose presence you infer from the way the green-black film pulses more urgently in certain patches, more alive, as if it is being grazed.

It is an ecosystem. A small one, a strange one, built on the byproducts of industry. It would not exist without the towers. It might not exist without the steam. It is, in its way, as new as the towers themselves — a fabrication within a fabrication, a small living thing made possible by a large mechanical thing, both of them still wet, both of them still incomplete.

## The Things in the Deeper Cuts

You noticed the oily gleam in the deeper cuts between the outcrops. You were uncertain whether it was water or machine-fluid. You were right to be uncertain.

In the deepest cuts — the places where the broken rock forms crevasses too narrow for the herd to enter, where the steam does not quite reach and the frost lingers all day — there is movement. A slow, deliberate, muscular movement, somewhere down in the gleam. You cannot see what makes it. You can see the displacement: a ripple in the oily surface, a small wave against the stone walls, the suggestion of a long body passing through a channel too narrow for its turning.

Whatever lives down there is patient. Whatever lives down there is cold-blooded, or something functionally equivalent to it — slow to wake, slow to feed, slow to die. It eats the things that fall in. The things that fall in are mostly the smaller fauna from the margins, the things with too many legs, the occasional raptor chick that misses its footing on the wet stone and slides down into the dark. The bones, when you find them at the edges, are dissolved. There is nothing left. The gleam has taken them.

It has been here longer than the towers. It has been here longer than the herd. It was here when the place was only broken stone, and it will be here when the place is finished stone, a long slow muscle in the deep channels, waiting.

---

## The Wind Itself

The wind is not an animal. But it carries things. It carries the calls of the wheelers, thinned to piping. It carries the hum of the towers, scattered into overtones. It carries the steam from the vents and distributes it across the shelf, and where it carries it, the green-black film grows. Where it does not carry it, the frost lingers and the lichen thins and the herd moves on.

In a real sense, the wind is what knits this place together. Without it, the herd would be islands. The steam would pool and stagnate. The green-black film would die back to its vents. The wheelers would have nowhere to ride. The wind moves through the incompletion of the place and makes it a place, makes it a system, makes it a single thing with a single metabolism: the towers breathe out steam, the steam feeds the film, the film feeds the things with too many legs, the things feed the gleam in the cuts, the gleam feeds the roots of the lichen, the lichen feeds the herd, the herd dies and feeds the wheelers, the wheelers drop their droppings and the droppings feed the film again. And the towers keep breathing, and the wind keeps moving, and the place is not finished, and the place does not care, and the place is, against all expectation, alive.

---

## The One Who Watches

You are here too.

You stand in the wet, in the dim, in the cold. You have come from somewhere lower, somewhere with more oxygen and less metal in the air. You are a visitor. The herd will not flee from you because the herd has not yet classified you as a threat. The wheelers will watch you because the wheelers classify everything as a potential meal. The things in the cuts will wait. The green-black film will not notice you at all.

You are the only thing here that knows the place is being assembled. The herd does not know. The wheelers do not care. The steam-born cannot know. The things in the cuts have known it for longer than assembly has been happening, and have made their peace with it. You are the only thing here that is disturbed by it — or charmed by it — or both.

You stand in the slow exhalations of a world that is being assembled around you, and the place does not care, and the place is not finished, and the place is not settled, and you — you will go back down. You will go back to the lowlands, where the air tastes of growing things and the machines are buried in buildings and the animals have names and the wind does not hum with overtones. You will go back down and you will try to describe this place to someone who has not been here, and the description will not be enough, and you will know it will not be enough, and you will come back if you can.

If you can.
