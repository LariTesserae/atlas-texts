# The Gardeners

They are not what you expected.

You spent the first hour alone — you were sure of that. You walked the avenues between the columnar growths, your footsteps swallowed by the hum, and there was nothing, *nothing*, just the slow breathing architecture of a world that had outgrown its need for watchers. You were the only moving thing. You would have sworn it.

You were wrong, but not in a way you could be argued out of. The Gardeners are not *moving*, in any sense the word covers. They move the way the vines move — a fraction of an inch in a minute, a quarter-turn of a leaf in an hour. They are simply larger versions of the patience you are surrounded by, and you walked past the first three without seeing them because you were looking for eyes, for a face, for some primate signal that said *I am here and I know you are*.

They don't have faces. They don't have anything you have a word for.

---

## The Shape

Stand at the end of a long avenue between two platforms and look down it, and you will see, eventually, what looks like a column. A green-gray pillar among the other green-gray pillars, perhaps thicker at the base, perhaps a little more textured. It does not draw the eye. The eye slides off it. You have to *choose* to look, and then you have to keep choosing, and the place is busy enough with wonders that choosing is hard.

Watch it for ten minutes. Nothing happens. Watch it for an hour. Something has. The outline is different. A fold of bark-like hide has shifted. A limb that was tucked against the body has relaxed by a degree. The slow respiration of the trunk beside it is mirrored, exactly, in this column's own swelling and contracting breath, and you realize, with a small sick lurch of understanding, that the *column* is not a column at all.

It is standing up the way a tree stands up. It grew that way. It will stand that way until the rock forgets the shape of it.

The body is roughly humanoid the way a roughly humanoid shape is humanoid — two legs, a torso, two arms, a head — but *roughly* is doing all the work. The limbs are too long, the joints are too many, the proportions are those of someone who has been *grown* rather than assembled, and grown in a place that does not particularly care about the engineering of a spine. The skin is not skin. It is a thick, fibrous hide, the color of wet bark, mottled with the same pale lichens that crust the older platforms. Where the hide is thin — across the knuckles, the inner elbows, the flat planes of the cheeks — you can see, faintly, a dim green luminescence pulsing beneath, in time with the great hum.

The face — and it is a face, or a *front*, or whatever the right word is — has the bones of a face. Brow, cheek, jaw, the sockets of eyes. But the eyes are not eyes. They are patches of the same pale luminous skin, milky and depthless, and they do not focus on you, because they do not focus on *anything*, because focusing is a small animal skill and the Gardeners gave it up a long time ago. They see the way the leaves see — all directions at once, with a patience that does not need to follow.

The mouth is the worst part. Not because it is grotesque. Because it is almost human, and almost not, and the almost is what gets you. The lips are wide and cracked, the color of old brick, and they are *closed* — always closed — and from the corners, the same amber sap that beads on the trunks of the older trees has run down and dried in long pale tracks on the chin, the throat, the chest. Whatever the Gardeners eat, or drink, or do to keep themselves alive, it is not done through the mouth. The mouth is just a place where the face meets itself. It is for nothing. It is a fossil of an older design, like the appendix in a human, like the dewclaw on a dog.

They are, you understand, somewhere between an animal and a plant, and have been for a very long time, and the direction of the drift is *plant*, and the distance they have come is vast.

## The Size

They are large.

Not the way giants in stories are large — not the towering, theatrical scale of a creature meant to impress. Large the way a redwood is large. Large the way the columnar growths in the deep cavern are large. They rise forty, fifty, sixty feet from the platforms they stand on, and they do not crouch or stoop, because to crouch would imply they were *bending* to something, and they are not bending. They are simply *tall*, the way a man standing in a doorway is tall; the doorway is not made for him, but he fits.

You cannot see them all at once, and you cannot be sure how many there are. The first one you really looked at was standing between two platforms, hands at its sides, head tilted a few degrees back, as if listening to something very far above. You assumed it was a single specimen. After an hour, you realized there were two more, one at either end of the same avenue, posed in mirror-image, hands also at their sides, also tilted back. You walked to the far end to confirm. The second one was *also* flanked by two more, and the avenue stretched on, and on, and on, and you began to understand that the city of platforms was *arranged* around them, the way a forest is arranged around its elders, and that what you had taken for empty plazas were not plazas at all.

They are not standing in the ruins. The ruins are built *to* them. The low dry-laid walls run between their feet because their feet were there first, and the patient people who laid the stones laid them around what they found, the way a stream lays gravel around a boulder.

You never get an accurate count. You give up trying.

---

## The Posture

They do not move. You have to accept this or go mad.

The first one you watched for three hours did not move. The wind did not move it. The passage of your own body past it, close enough to touch, did not move it. A seedpod fell from somewhere above and struck its shoulder, and the shoulder *absorbed* the impact the way a tree trunk absorbs a bird — with a small percussive acknowledgment and then nothing, and then more nothing, and then more nothing after that.

But you came back the next day — you had to sleep somewhere, and you came back — and the hand that had been at its side was resting on the trunk of the columnar growth beside it, the fingers closed loosely around the bark, and the angle of the head had shifted by a degree or two, and the leg that had been straight was bent a fraction at the knee. It had *walked*, you realized. In the night, while you slept, it had taken a step, or two steps, or twenty, and the position you found it in now was a *new* position, a fresh one, chosen an hour ago or a century ago, and the difference between those two spans of time had stopped meaning anything to it.

They walk the way glaciers walk. They walk the way continents walk. They walk the way the sun walks across the sky — by a process too vast to be called motion, too steady to be called wandering. They are always going somewhere, and they are never in a hurry, and the somewhere is not a place you could follow, because they are the only things in the Greenreach that *could* leave, and they do not leave.

---

## The Work

This is the part that takes the longest to understand, and the part that, once you have understood it, you cannot stop seeing.

The Gardeners are *pruning*.

That is the wrong word. Pruning is a thing done to a plant by a creature that has decided what the plant should look like. This is not that. This is — there is no word, you will not find one — this is a *tending*, a *midwifery*, a slow green negotiation between one very large living thing and ten thousand smaller ones, conducted in a timescale that makes patience look like impatience.

Watch, if you can bring yourself to stand still long enough. Watch a Gardener standing before a columnar growth. It does nothing. It does nothing for an hour. It does nothing for two hours. The light flickers. The hum changes its key by a quarter-tone. A vine somewhere adjusts its hang. Nothing else.

Then, with a slowness that is almost a kind of violence in reverse, the Gardener raises one hand. The hand unfolds the way a fern frond unfolds. The fingers — long, multi-jointed, the bark-hide peeling at the knuckles — spread wide, and the hand settles, gently, on the trunk of the growth in front of it, and rests there.

That is all. That is the whole gesture. The hand on the bark. The rest.

And after a while, the growth *responds*. A branch that was leaning left straightens. A flower that has been closed for what looks like years begins, by fractions, to open. A vine that was choking a neighboring column loosens one coil, and then another, and drifts, slowly, toward a new support. The Gardener does not push, does not pull, does not arrange. It *asks*. Or it *informs*. Or it simply *is*, with such a long and unbroken *being*, that the smaller things, which live in faster time, register it as a kind of weather, and adjust.

The hand stays on the bark for what might be ten minutes or what might be a year. You cannot tell. Then the hand lifts, and the arm folds back against the body, and the Gardener takes one glacial step sideways to the next growth, and does it again, and again, and again, and will be doing it again a thousand years from now when you are not only dead but forgotten that you were ever a thing that could die.

They are not tending a forest. They are *composing* one. They are writing it, in the slow grammar of sap and light, and what they are writing is something too long for you to read, and the language is older than any tongue you have ever spoken, and the message is, as far as you can translate it, *continuation*. *Stay green. Stay rooted. Keep breathing. We are here. We have always been here. We will be.*

## The Mouths

You will, eventually, want to know if they speak. It is the only question that has any human urgency left in you after a day or two in the Greenreach, and you will ask it of yourself, standing in an empty plaza with the hum pressing against your eardrums, because the silence of the Gardeners is louder than anything they could possibly say.

They do not speak. The mouths are closed, and the mouths stay closed, and after you have watched for a long time you realize that the mouths are not *capable* of speaking — that the lips have fused, or sealed, or never had the musculature for it in the first place. Whatever the Gardeners once were, they have passed through some threshold of silence that humans will never reach, and the mouth is now just a join in the bark, a seam where the front of the face meets itself.

But.

On the third day, or the fourth, or the one that felt like the fourth, you are standing very close to one — too close, the rules you learned on the first day say too close — and you feel, suddenly, a *vibration* in the air in front of you, not in your ears but in the soft tissues of your face, in the bridge of your nose, in the hollows of your cheeks. It is not the hum. The hum is everywhere. This is *localized*. This is being *directed*, somehow, at you.

You look up. The milky eyes are not looking at you. The eyes do not look at anything. But the mouth — the closed, fused, fossil mouth — is *open*, by a fraction, by a hairsbreadth, by the smallest possible opening that can still be called an opening, and through it comes, not sound, not breath, but a *warmth*, and in the warmth is a *shape*, and the shape is the shape of a word you almost understand.

It is not a word in any language. It is a word in *the* language, the one the Greenreach is written in, the one the slow composition is composed in, and you do not speak it and you will not speak it but for a moment, just for a moment, the *shape* of it is in you, and the shape is *welcome*, or *wait*, or *stay*, or *behold*, or *we knew you were coming*, or *we have been expecting*, or all of these at once, or none of them, or something the word *welcome* cannot hold.

Then the mouth closes. The Gardener returns to its work. The hum swells over the small warm shape and carries it away, and you are left standing in a plaza with your face still ringing with a word you will never hear again.

You do not try to speak to them after that. You understand, in the way the body understands, that the word was a gift, and a warning, and a door, and you have chosen not to walk through it, and the door will not be opened again.

## The Old One

There is one in the center of the Greenreach, on the highest platform, in a place where the light is dimmest and the growth is oldest.

You find it the way you find all of them — by accident, by almost walking into it, by realizing with a small delayed shock that the shape you have been circumnavigating for ten minutes is alive. This one is older than the others. The bark-hide is thicker, paler, almost white in places, and the pale lichens on it have built up into actual structures — little castles, little pagodas of crust, an inch high, that have been growing on its skin the way barnacles grow on a hull. The dim green luminescence beneath the hide is faint here, guttering, as if the chemistry of it is running down.

It is *sitting*. The others stand. This one, because the platform is low, or because it chose to, or because at its age the distinction between choosing and not choosing has collapsed, is *sitting*, with its too-long legs folded under it and its too-long arms resting in its lap and its head bowed forward so that the cracked-brick lips almost touch the knees. The posture is — and you do not want to admit this, and you admit it anyway — the posture of a man sitting on a bench in a public square, watching the world go by. It is the posture of a thing at rest.

It is not at rest. The respiration in its chest is still going, slow and vast, and the hum in its body is still part of the larger hum of the place, and when you get close enough to feel the warmth of it you can feel, too, the slow *work* still going on inside it, the patient tending of ten thousand green things, the long green conversation that has not paused, will not pause, cannot pause.

It is just *old*. Older than the buildings at its feet. Older, perhaps, than the platforms themselves, the lower decks of which are dry and papery with the antiquity of them. It is the *first* Gardener, or one of the first, or the survivor of some earlier cycle of the place, and it has been sitting here in the dimness for a length of time the human mind was not built to apprehend, and it will go on sitting here for another such length, and then another, until the rock forgets the shape of it, which is what the rock eventually does to everything.

You do not stay long. The warmth of it is too much, and the *presence* of it is too much, and there is something in the bowed head and the folded hands that is too close to a thing you have seen before, in hospital rooms, in the postures of the very ill, and the resemblance is not a comfort.

You walk away. It does not watch you go.

---

## The Leaving

You will not see them leave, because they do not leave. You will not see them move, because they move too slowly to be seen moving. You will not see them speak, because they have said the one thing they will ever say to you, and the saying is over.

What you will see, as you turn back toward the fissure in the rock and the dry stone of the upper passages, is one of them — a younger one, a standing one, halfway down an avenue between two platforms — *turn its head*.

Not toward you. Toward the place you are going. Toward the upper world, the dry world, the world of rain and sun and small quick lives. It stands at the edge of a plaza, head tilted up, milky eyes aimed at a stone ceiling it has never seen through, and for a long moment — longer than you can stay and watch — it holds that pose, as if listening to something you cannot hear.

Then the head returns to its former angle, and the hand that had been at its side lifts and rests on the trunk of the nearest columnar growth, and the work continues, and you are walking away, and the smell of chlorophyll and resin is in your hair, and the hum follows you out into the quiet of the stone.

You will not be able, afterward, to describe them adequately. You will say *statues* and mean something else. You will say *trees* and mean something else. You will say *people* and mean something else. The word you are looking for does not exist in the language you came in with, and they will not teach it to you.

They will simply go on, in their dim green heat, breathing, in the dark, without you.
