What lives here. Not much. Not quite nothing. Something between the two — a presence that has learned to be present without insisting on itself.

Start with the films, if you can call them that — the green-black growths in the deeper pools, in the wetter seams. They are not quite plants. They photosynthesize, or something adjacent to it, in the dim light, but the light is so dim that the photosynthesis is more a formality than a process. They consume dissolved iron. They excrete tiny amounts of oxygen that rise and join the general atmosphere of the place and disappear into the larger volume. They grow in layers. Each layer is a year, or a decade. The deeper ones have compressed into a thin black stratum that, if you cut through it, looks like a page from a very patient book — a record kept without a reader. They are the closest thing the place has to a memory of itself, and they do not know it.

The fern-things are a step up. They are not ferns — no true leaves, no vascular system in the strict sense, no roots. But they have the fern's persistence, its willingness to occupy the smallest available crack and wait. They grow in the laps of machines, in the angles of girders, in the corners where metal meets metal. They are small — a hand-span, rarely more. They reproduce by means that are not quite spores and not quite seeds. Their color is the dark green of old copper, which is appropriate. They die when the surface they grow on rusts through and they lose their purchase. They are replaced. There are always others. They are not aware. They are simply there, in the way a stain is there, in the way a watermark is there, in the way a thing that has no opinion about itself is there.

The machines — and here I must slow down, because the machines are the most difficult thing in this place, and the most inhabited. They were built. They were poured, rolled, cut, bolted, welded. They have running parts. Some of them still run. The question is whether they are alive. The honest answer is that the question has stopped being well-posed. A machine that has been running for centuries, that has settled into its foundation, that has begun to corrode in patterns that match its own function — that machine is not a machine in the older sense. It is a thing that has outlived its category. It runs processes that no one remembers setting. It produces outputs that no one collects. But the running continues. The outputs continue. The machine has begun, very slowly, to be what it does. In some deep sense it has become a way for the place to be the place. The machines are the place's metabolism. They are not alive. They are also not not-alive. They are what you get when something built persists long enough to develop habits, and the habits become the thing, and the thing outlasts the reason it was made.

There are creatures in the water. Not animals — nothing with a body, nothing with limbs. But the water carries things. Spores, cysts, tiny motile forms that swim in the film of moisture on every surface, feeding on the dissolved metal, on the oil, on the slow exhalations of the machines. They are the color of the water. You do not see them. You see their effect — a slight greening where they have been, a small redistribution of the corrosion. They are not individuals. They are populations. They flow with the water, settle, rise, flow again. They are the closest thing the place has to fish, and they are not fish. They are the closest thing the place has to anything at all, and they are barely anything, and there are a great many of them, and they are everywhere, and no one has ever looked at one.

And — this is the strangest, and I am not certain of it — there is a figure. Singular, or perhaps plural; it is hard to tell, because the figure moves slowly, and is seen rarely, and is seen at the margins of vision in the failing amber light. It is not human, or not quite. It is the size of a human, or roughly so. It has the gait of a thing that has learned to walk on metal that is sometimes slippery and always unreliable. It wears something — a covering, or a coating — that has the color of the rust and the algae and the dark water, so that when it stands still against a housing it disappears into the housing, and when it moves it leaves a stain of absence on the place it was. It carries nothing. It moves among the standing structures with a familiarity that suggests long acquaintance. It pauses at the larger machines. It places a hand — if it is a hand — against the casing and holds it there for a while, as though listening. Then it moves on. It does not appear to eat, or drink, or sleep, or at least no one has ever seen it do these things. It has the quality of a thing that has forgotten how to die, or has decided not to, or has simply not been informed that it should. It is the only moving form in the place that is larger than a fern-thing. It is not the master of the place. It is not the servant. It is something between — a witness, perhaps, or a relic, or a habit the place has acquired.

There may be more than one of these figures. It is hard to know. The fog comes in, and the lamps fail one by one, and the central sound is so constant that it erases detail. There may be a small population — a few, a handful — moving through the vast wet plain at their own slow pace, each one alone with its long acquaintance. They do not appear to interact. They do not appear to speak. The language of the machines is not a language anyone still speaks, and this may include them. They may be the last technicians, or the only ones, or the descendants of technicians, or things that grew up where the technicians had been and took the shape of what was left. They tend nothing. They repair nothing. They simply go on being present, and their presence is part of the place, and the place is what it is partly because of them, and they are what they are partly because of the place, and the difference between them and the machines is not as clear as it used to be.

And finally — and this is the answer that is hardest to give, because it is not really an answer at all — the place itself is inhabited. By itself. By the slow accumulation of its own processes. By the water that keeps its patient accounting of every surface. By the lamps that fail and are not replaced. By the central sound that turns at its constant speed. By the rust that is also a kind of breathing. By the fern-things that creep another millimeter along a girder in a year. By all the small changes that, over centuries, amount to a kind of staying.

The place does not need anything else to be present, because presence is what it does. What lives there is what is left when everything else has decided to leave, or has been forgotten, or has worn itself down to its constituent patience. What lives there is the place, persisting. It is wet, and cool, and dim, and roaring, and old, and made, and sparse, and strange, and quietly active, and it is there, and it continues to be there, and the things that live there are the things that have not yet stopped.
