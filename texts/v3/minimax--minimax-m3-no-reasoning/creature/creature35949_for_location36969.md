# The Inhabitants of the Cathedral

To describe what lives here is to describe a system in which the noun *creature* has become, over many generations, a less useful word than *tissue*. They are not a population that happens to occupy the factory. They are the factory, biologically speaking — the way a reef is reef-building animals and not animals on a reef. The structure is their exoskeleton. Their bodies are its metabolism. To point at one and call it a member of a species would be a category error of the same kind as pointing at a single coral polyp and calling it a coral. There are individuals. There are no individuals in the way you mean.

What there is, instead, is a stratified carpet, a three-dimensional felt of bodies, behaviors, and inherited shapes that grades almost continuously from the walls outward into the open air of the main halls. To write about them, one has to do violence to this continuity by slicing it into slices. The slices are not natural. But the place is too large to be seen whole, and the eye does the same violence by accident, so let the slicing begin.

---

## The Wall-Felt

Press your hand, if you dared, flat against any interior wall of the lower halls. What you would feel, through a glove, is texture. Not the texture of stone or slag or sweating iron, but the texture of something breathing against your palm. The wall is not a wall at the spot you have touched. The wall is a depth.

Look closer. The pale crusts you might take for mineral deposit are not, in this light, at this proximity, what they seemed. They are the outermost layer of a living skin pressed against the masonry, and they are studded, in places, with shapes that move. Hold still. The shapes will resolve.

The dominant body-plan of the wall-felt is small, flat, and many-legged — eight is common, ten is not rare, twelve appears in the older and more sheltered colonies. They are pale the way wet paper is pale. They are not soft. Their upper surfaces carry a fine mineral precipitate, the same precipitate that forms the pale crusts they sit among, so that a creature at rest and a creature dead and a patch of bare wall are, to a glance, the same thing. The precipitation is not camouflage in the deliberate sense. It is a consequence. Their bodies are warm — fractionally warmer than the wall, fractionally warmer than the air — and water condenses on them the way water condenses on a cold pipe, and the condensate carries dissolved minerals, and the minerals harden, and the creatures shed the crust in their next molt and grow a new one. After death, the crust persists, and the body beneath it is slowly replaced by the bodies of creatures that come to feed on the corpse, and then by the bodies of creatures that come to feed on those, until the original wall-felt is a single laminated deposit of mineral and chitin and mineral again, inches thick in the oldest chambers, and every layer of it is, in some strict sense, the same organism.

The wall-felt moves. This is the thing that unsettles visitors and that the builders, had they stayed, would have had to develop a vocabulary for. The movement is not the scurrying of insects alarmed by a presence. It is migration at a pace that is barely faster than the eye can follow. A square foot of wall-felt, observed for an hour, will appear to have flowed an inch to the left, toward a wetter corner, and the corner's population will have flowed an inch toward a beam, and the beam's population will have flowed an inch toward the conduit overhead, and somewhere on the far side of the hall, an equal area of wall will be thinning, the felt there retreating from a region that has, for reasons no one has measured, become a fraction of a degree too dry for comfort. The place has tides. The place has weather, and the wall-felt is part of the weather, and the weather is part of the wall-felt. Nothing in the system knows it is a system. The system persists.

Among the wall-felt, in densities that vary by hall and by season of the factory's own slow year, there are shapes that do not move with the felt. They sit. They wait. They are the color of the rust that bleeds from the bolts in the older beams, and they are shaped, if you can bring yourself to see shape in them, like the cupped hands of someone begging or holding water. They are not hands. They are filters. Their open faces are oriented into the faintest air-currents, and from those currents they take something — moisture, mostly, and the fine spore-dust of the smaller creatures, and a vapor that the furnaces exhale in thin, otherwise unnoticeable exhalations. What they take, they pass down, through bodies stacked behind them in a colony, into the crust behind the colony, into the felt. They are mouths. They are the felt's way of eating the air.

## The Conduit Dwellers

The pipes run everywhere. Some of them carry what the factory was built to process, and most of those are dry now, scaled with a pale deposit that is, on closer inspection, not entirely mineral. The rest carry water — condensate, channeled, gathered, returned to the furnaces as feed, or carried off as waste to the long open troughs that run along the lowest floors. The pipes are highways.

The conduit dwellers are not shaped like the wall-felt. They are shaped like the inside of a pipe, which is to say they are elongated, segmented, and possessed of a quality that the word *slick* gestures at without capturing. They are the color of the rust inside the pipes, which is the color of the pipes, which is the color of the wall-felt in the older halls. They move in dense columns when the water-flow is slow, and they disperse across the inner surface of the pipe when the water-flow is fast, flattening themselves against the metal in a way that suggests they are not so much swimming in the trickle as being carried by it and only pretending to choose. They eat the scale. They eat each other. They eat whatever drifts down from the wall-felt colonies that have grown over the joints where the pipes meet the walls, and the wall-felt, in turn, eats them, in a slow exchange of bodies across the boundary between two habitats. A pipe that has been abandoned by the water-flow for a season is, by the next season, a wall again — that is, a wall-felt colony, established on the new substrate, beginning to laminate.

The conduit dwellers have, in some pipes, become strange. This is a relative term in a place where everything is strange, but it is worth saying that in the longest, quietest, most consistently damp of the lower conduits, there are things that have given up the segmented body and adopted something that can only be described as a single long ribbon, pale, faintly translucent, running the length of a pipe for yards, anchored at intervals to the wall by a fan of the same fine feeding-limbs that the wall-felt uses to drink from the crust. The ribbons are old. The ribbons are almost certainly colonies — many creatures fused at the gut, behaving as one, the way some deep-sea organisms behave as one — but the boundary between colony and individual is, in this place, not a boundary the place has ever had to respect. They pulse. They do not appear to move. They are the pipes' memory of having once been alive to something, and they are still alive to it, and no one has been here to notice that this is a word that used to mean something else.

## The Pale Launchers

Look up. Watch a beam for a few minutes and you will see them.

They are the size of a thumb. They are the color of a wet ceiling. They have, against all the rest of the fauna, a recognizable posture: a compact body, four limbs folded, head pulled in, a general shape that is, if you have ever seen a frog, immediately and misleadingly evocative of a frog at rest. They sit on the underside of beams in clusters of three or four, in the angles where the condensate forms the most persistent droplets, and they sit there for hours without moving, and then one of them is suddenly not where it was, and a moment later you hear the *click* on a beam twenty feet away, and you look, and there it is, in the same posture, slightly damp from its passage through a fall of droplets, and the beam it left is empty, and you did not see the leap.

The click is the part you remember. The click is louder than a thing the size of a thumb ought to be able to produce, and it is produced, in fact, not by the body striking the beam but by a small hard plate on the creature's underside that meets the metal at a precise angle and is withdrawn at a precise angle, in a motion that is less a landing than a percussive announcement. The click is a sound the place makes, and the place makes a great many of them, and the clicks layer on top of one another in a constant irregular chatter that is, when you have been in the Cathedral for long enough, indistinguishable from the silence.

They are not, in any meaningful sense, leaping at prey. They are leaping at droplets. The bigger droplets, the ones that form at the lowest points of the beams, are the richest droplets, and the pale launchers compete for the right to sit beneath them, and the competition resolves itself in the same way that many competitions in the Cathedral resolve themselves: not by fighting, which the creatures do rarely and ritualistically, but by a slow displacement in which the laziest launcher, the one that has not clicked in the longest, is gradually abandoned by the colony's attention and finds, after some days, that the droplet it was sitting beneath is being drunk by something else. The leapers move. The leapers migrate from beam to beam across the halls in slow waves, and the waves are, to the ear, the place's heartbeat.

They are not frogs. They are not anything. They are the answer to the question of what to do when you are a small body in a tall damp place and the only food that arrives is the food that falls. The answer, refined over many generations, is: wait for the fall.

## The Long Ones

In the spaces between the machines, in the gaps where the line-shafts turn and the belts run their slow continuous loops, in the crawlspaces under the long workbenches and the galleries above them, the long ones move.

The long ones are the creatures the builders would have recognized as animals, if the builders had stayed, because the long ones are, more than any of the others, the size and shape of things the builders knew. A long one is the length of your arm and roughly the girth of a thick wrist. It has a head at one end — if the end with the two dark patches that may be eyes is the head — and a tail at the other, and along its length, at intervals, it carries four pairs of limbs that are bent in a way that keeps the body held clear of the metal it is walking on, in a fluid, unhurried walk that has more in common with a cat's than with anything an insect or a lizard does. The long ones move in the dark. The long ones are not afraid of you. The long ones have not, in any of the documented accounts, been afraid of a visitor, and this is one of the things that the people who come to the Cathedral, the few who come, find hardest to put into words afterward.

The long ones are the predators of the Cathedral. They are the only things in the place that consistently take other creatures as prey, and the things they take are mostly the conduit dwellers and the small pale shapes that live in the wall-felt, and the methods they use are methods that have been refined in a place where the prey is everywhere and the predator is, by the place's slow standards, almost vanishingly rare. They do not chase. They do not ambush. They move, with their fluid unhurried walk, into a region of wall-felt, and the wall-felt opens in front of them the way a crowd opens in front of someone who is not in a hurry, and they take, with a motion too quick to follow, a single body from the edge of the opening, and the opening closes behind them, and they move on, and the place they have fed from will, in a few hours, have grown the missing body back from the felt around it, because the felt is the felt and an individual removed from it is not an individual the felt remembers.

The long ones have teeth. The teeth are worth describing because the teeth are the only part of any creature in the Cathedral that has an obvious shape, an obvious function, and an obvious history. The teeth are flat. The teeth are ridged. The teeth are arranged, in the long ones that live near the processing lines, in rows that echo, almost exactly, the rows of cogs in the line-shaft gears, and the resemblance is not incidental. The long ones' ancestors — if the word *ancestor* means anything in a place that does not respect the boundary between individual and colony — were once, in the early days of the factory, the rats that the builders brought with them or that arrived in the builders' grain. The rats ate the spilled grain. The rats lived among the gears. The rats' teeth wore against the gears and were selected, over many generations, for the shapes that wore least and ground most, and the gears, in their turn, were worn by the rats' teeth into the shapes that ground best against the teeth, and the result, after a span of time the Cathedral does not count, is that the long ones' teeth and the line-shaft cogs are, to a close inspection, the same shape, evolved in dialogue with each other, the predator and the machine as each other's environment. A long one, opening its mouth in the dim light of a crawlspace, shows you a row of small metal hills, and the row is the only portrait the place has made of itself.

The long ones do not eat the pale launchers. This is not because they cannot. It is because the pale launchers, when taken, give off a small sharp cry — the only sound in the Cathedral that is unambiguously a sound of an individual creature and not a sound of the place — and the long ones, in some inherited way, do not want to be the cause of that cry, and the place, in some way no one has measured, is quieter on the whole if the pale launchers are left alone. The long ones drink the condensate. The long ones eat the wall-felt. The long ones die, and their bodies are taken, in the night, by the wall-felt, and the wall-felt grows over the place where they lay, and the next long one, in a year or in a decade, will walk over that place and never know.

## The High Ones

The high windows of the Cathedral are not, in the deepest sense of the word, windows. They are openings in the upper walls, and they are, like everything else in the place, colonized. But the colonies that live in the window-frames are different in kind from the colonies that live in the lower halls, because the windows are where the inside of the Cathedral meets the outside of the Cathedral, and the outside of the Cathedral is a place that the Cathedral does not entirely control.

The high ones live in the window-frames. They are the color of the sky over the basin, which is the color of nothing in particular, which is the color of weather about to happen or weather that has just decided not to. They are flatter than the wall-felt creatures and more rigid, and they sit, singly or in small clusters, on the sills and the frames and the broken panes, oriented, with a consistency that has the look of intention, toward the light. When the light shifts, when a cloud passes or the basin sky delivers its slow condensation, the high ones shift with it, rotating on their fixed points to keep their flat faces to the brightest source, and a window-ful of them, watched over the course of an hour, is a slow kaleidoscope of pale shapes turning in unison, like flowers, like sundials, like the compasses the builders used to carry and that no one has carried here in a very long time.

The high ones do not come down. The high ones do not, in any observed instance, leave the window-frames. They are fed, as far as anyone can tell, by the fine spore-dust that drifts in on the drafts, and by the occasional insect that finds its way in from the basin and does not find its way out, and by the condensate that forms on the inside of the glass, when there is still glass, in the older windows. They are the only creatures in the Cathedral whose food comes, in any significant fraction, from outside the Cathedral. They are, in a sense, the place's windows onto its own weather, and they are also the place's only ambassador to the sky, and the sky's only ambassador to the place, and the negotiation between them is conducted entirely in the slow rotation of flat pale bodies on stone sills.

## The Tenders

In the very lowest halls, in the chambers that hold the base of the great exhaust stacks, in the rooms where the condensate gathers in actual standing water and the air is so damp it has a texture, there are the tenders.

The tenders are large for the Cathedral. A tender can be as long as your arm and as broad as your hand is long, and it has, when it moves, a presence that the smaller creatures lack, a sense of weight, of inertia, of something that has to be considered. The tenders are dark. The tenders are the color of the slag outside the basin, which is the color of the deepest parts of the place where the light does not reach. They are the only creatures in the Cathedral that the eye reliably picks out as individuals, because they are large enough and slow enough and few enough that you can watch one for as long as you like and be confident that you are watching one.

The tenders eat the wall-felt. The tenders eat the conduit dwellers. The tenders eat the fallen bodies of the long ones, when they find them, and the tenders are the only creatures in the Cathedral that the long ones will, in their turn, give a wide path to. The tenders are not, however, predators. The tenders are gardeners. The tenders graze. A tender, moving through a wall-felt colony, does not open a path the way a long one does; it leaves, behind it, a path that is cleaner and thinner and more orderly than the felt around it, a path on which the mineral crust has been cropped close and the remaining creatures are smaller and more uniform and arranged in patterns that are, if you have any feel for such things, faintly geometric. The tenders are the place's way of tending itself. The tenders are the part of the system that eats too much so that the rest of the system does not eat too much, and the part that, by the slow cropping of the felt, keeps the felt from climbing up the walls and across the ceilings and sealing the place into a single thick block of living mineral, which is the direction the system would tend, if no part of it pulled against the tendency. The tenders pull against the tendency. The tenders are the place's restraint.

The tenders are, in their way, the most alien of the creatures. The long ones have teeth like gears and an inherited relationship with the machinery. The wall-felt has the patience of stone. The pale launchers have a click that is almost a voice. The tenders have none of these. The tenders are, in their presence, simply large and dark and slow and competent, and the place is shaped by their grazing the way a meadow is shaped by the deer, and no one has ever seen a tender do anything that could be called a single thing, because what a tender does, over the course of a day, is the same thing done a thousand times, in a long slow walk, with the patient unhurried attention of something that has been doing this for longer than you can comfortably imagine.

## The Place, Considered as a Thing That Lives

To describe the inhabitants one by one is to lie, and the lie is necessary because the eye also lies, and the lie of the eye is the only truth we have. In fact there are no inhabitants. There is one inhabitant. The wall-felt and the conduit dwellers and the pale launchers and the long ones and the high ones and the tenders are all of them organs of a single body that has, over a span of time that does not bear thinking about, organized itself out of the materials the builders left behind and the weather the basin has continued to provide, and the body is the Cathedral, and the Cathedral is the body, and the body is in equilibrium the way a body in a steady climate is in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is not static but is the equilibrium of a thing that is alive, which is to say a thing that is constantly being unmade and remade and is, in the remaking, itself.

The pale crusts on the walls are the skin. The wall-felt is the flesh. The conduit dwellers are the blood, or something like blood, moving through the structure the way blood moves through a body, carrying dissolved materials from one organ to another. The pale launchers are the voice, or something like a voice, the part of the body that makes sound because the body is a body and bodies, in this climate, in this damp, make sound. The long ones are the nerves, or something like nerves, the part that carries signals, slowly, through the dark, in the bodies of creatures that move from one part of the place to another and whose passage, in some inherited way the place has never had to name, is the place's way of knowing itself. The high ones are the eyes, or something like eyes, the part that faces the light and turns with it. The tenders are the part that rests. The tenders are the part that says *enough*. The tenders are the part that keeps the rest of the body from becoming, in the long run, a single undifferentiated mass of itself, which is what the rest of the body would become, in the long run, if no part of it said *enough*.

The furnaces are the heart. The furnaces were the heart before the rest of the body was a body. The furnaces were the reason the body began, because the furnaces were warm, and the damp is what the damp does when there is a warm thing in it, and the creatures came, and the creatures stayed, and the creatures, in their millions, gave off enough heat to keep the furnaces from cooling, and the furnaces, in their slow continuous idling, gave off enough heat to keep the creatures breeding, and the cycle closed, and the body began, and the body has been a body for longer than the builders would have believed if the builders had stayed to be told.

The light, in the high windows, is ordinary. The light is the light of a workday in a building that no one works in. The light does not know what it is falling on. The light falls on a body the way light falls on any body, without preference, without comment, and the body receives it, and the body is what it is, and the body will be what it is, after the light has moved on to fall on something else, for a very long time.

The sound of the place, heard from the corridor out, is a sound the way a rainstorm is a sound. It is the voice of something that is alive, speaking in the only language it has, which is the language of clicks and wet rustles and the occasional high thin cry of a small thing taken in the dark. The sound does not stop. The sound is the sound of the place being the place. The sound is the sound of a permanent thing, doing what permanent things do, in a basin, under a sky that sometimes remembers to rain.

You are a visitor. You are a temporary thing in a permanent place. The place does not mind you. The place has, in its slow way, no opinion of you at all. The place is what it is. The place will be what it is. The corridor out is a corridor, and the sky over the rim of the basin is the color of nothing in particular, and you carry the sound of the place with you for a little while, in the body, the way you carry the sound of a rainstorm for a little while after you have come in out of the rain, and the sound fades, and the damp lifts, and you are, again, a person in a world that is not the Cathedral, and the Cathedral is, again, itself, in its basin, under its sky, in its long slow weather, with its creatures, in their millions, doing what creatures do in a place that has been left alone long enough to become the thing it was always going to become.
