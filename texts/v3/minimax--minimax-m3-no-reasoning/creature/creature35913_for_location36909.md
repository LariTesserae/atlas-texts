The creatures are everywhere. They are the colony.

This is the first thing you learn, after the rock and the light. They are not guests in the pocket. They are its medium. The air of the place is mostly them — not their bodies, but the space their bodies have made habitable, the dust of their digestion, the warmth of their swarms. To breathe the pocket is to breathe them.

A worker, alone, is small. The size of a thumb, if it is a common worker. A fist, sometimes — those are the ones you learn to notice, the mid-sized ones, the ones that move with a slight heavy deliberation, as if they have eaten more stone than their smaller cousins and the stone is still sitting in them. The largest you see rarely, and only on the lower levels, near the still-hot vents. The colonists call them queens, but this is a courtesy; the term is borrowed and nothing has been confirmed. The queens move slowly. They are attended.

The body is segmented, plated, the plates overlapping like the tiles of a roof or the scales of a pinecone, depending on the individual. The material reads as ceramic to the eye — a dull matte black, faintly iridescent at the joints, the red of unoxidized iron threading through it the way the iron threads through the basalt — but it gives under pressure with the give of chitin. If you pick one up — you should not, but a worker falls onto your glove in a shaft once, and you do pick it up, briefly, before you set it down — it is warm. Warmer than the air. The body heat of a thing that has been digesting stone. Its legs tick against your palm. It does not struggle. It waits. It weighs almost nothing. The weight of a thought.

It eats.

You learn this by watching the bare patch of basalt in a chamber on the upper levels where the floor has been cleared of grating for some forgotten reason. A worker settles onto the stone and becomes, for several hours, a piece of the stone. You forget it is there. You look away. When you look back, it has eaten a depression into the basalt the size and depth of a thumbprint, and it is sitting in its own bowl. The iron threads that had run through the rock are gone, digested, and the powder left behind is the fine black dust you have been breathing since you arrived.

The creatures do not eat quickly. They eat patiently. A single worker, in a day, removes less stone than you could with your thumbnail. There are a billion of them. The math, when you do it, becomes the geology of the place. The pocket is being unmade by the creatures, and the colony is being made by the smelters, and the creatures are eating the colony's output faster than the smelters can produce it, and the smelters are fed from the vents below, and the vents are being dug out by the colonists, who dig with the parts the assemblers make, who make parts from the metal the smelters pour, who are themselves fed by the creatures in a long tangled chain of mutual appetite. The colony is a digestion. You are inside the gut of something.

Their legs are the sound of the place.

The papery susurrus is not a single sound. It is a chorus of small sounds, each leg a tiny striker on the basalt and the grating and the wall-plates and the bodies of the creatures ahead. A single worker, walking, makes a sound like a fingernail drawn slowly across paper. A stream of workers crossing a chamber makes a sound like the inside of a clock, like dry rice in a bowl, like a sleeve of letters being shaken. The colony, full of them, makes the held-breath sound you cannot stop hearing once you have heard it. It rises and falls. It thickens in the morning shift, when the creatures are hungriest. It thins in the deep quiet hours, when even the workers rest against the walls and only the queens, somewhere, continue their slow business with the rock.

It is louder after a shaft-cough. It is softer after a smelter has run hot. You learn to read it the way you would read wind in another place.

---

They are not machines.

You tell yourself this for the first day. They move with the patient mechanical rhythm of things that do not tire, and they are built like things that are built, plated and jointed and engineered, and the natural mistake is to assume that what is engineered is only engineered. Then you stand in a chamber on the mid levels and watch a clutch of them work a thermal vent, and you see what is not algorithmic.

They jostle. Two workers meet on a stretch of grating too narrow for both, and they press against each other, legs braced, plates scraping faintly, and one yields and the other does not yield, and the one that did not yield is larger. You did not see the size-test being done. It was done, and one of them walked on, and the other walked on, and the choreography of it was older than language. They press against the workers who are smaller. They retreat from the workers who are larger. They make a sound with their legs against the grating that is not mechanical — it is a sound of intention, of negotiation, of a thing that knows what it wants and is willing to wait for it. You begin to suspect that the bred workers, the engineered ones, are simpler than the wild ones. Calmer. Less interested in each other. The wild ones, deeper in the rock, are something else. The wild ones are not calm at all.

A swarm, when one happens, is not dangerous. It is *humiliating*. You stand in a corridor on the upper levels and a stream of workers, for reasons you will never know — a shaft-cough you did not hear, a smelter surge, a shift in the dim blue light — diverts around you, over you, into you. They pour across your hood and gloves and into every seam of your clothing with the indifferent patience of water finding a new channel. They are warm. They tick. Their small plated bodies press against your skin through the fabric. You do not move. You breathe through your nose. You stand until they decide you are not stone and move on. The ticks of them continue in the seams of your jacket for hours afterward, the ones that did not find their way out.

---

The colonists are the other thing.

You see them rarely, on the upper levels. They move in twos and threes along the corridors, dressed in their layers — gloves, hoods, faceplates, the filter masks that hum faintly with each breath they take. Some of them have faces. Some of them do not, or have faces that are not faces in the way you would recognize, but are apparatus, ports, sealed plates of dark glass behind which something moves. You learn not to stare. The colonists find staring impolite, in the way they find the question of their own origin impolite, which is to say: it is impolite because it has been asked before, and the answer has not changed, and the answer is silence.

They are not tall. They are not short. They are very nearly the same height as each other, and the same weight, and the same shape under their layers, and you begin to suspect, after a while, that this is not coincidence. They move with the same patient mechanical rhythm as the creatures, and you wonder, watching them, whether the rhythm is borrowed or whether both rhythms were borrowed from the rock.

They have specializations, and the specializations are the only thing that distinguishes them. You hear of the smelter-ops, who do not come up to the upper levels, who live in the amber glow of the lower chambers and breathe the hottest air in the pocket. You hear of the assemblers, who stand for hours at the stamping presses and the wire-draw towers and emerge with a fine dusting of dark powder in the creases of their gloves. The biotech team you do not hear about. They work in the windowless rooms on the mid levels, behind doors you pass without slowing, and what they are doing to the creatures is not conservation but improvement. The doors, when you pass them, give off a smell that is not the smell of the creatures but is also not not the smell of the creatures — a smell like warm ceramic, like the inside of a kiln, like something being slowly cooked in a closed space. You do not knock.

The creature-trackers you meet. There is one who keeps the log that will never be read. They sit at a console in a chamber hung with amber and blue strip-lights and note, in a script you cannot read, the bloom and contraction of the creature populations in the corridors. They are quiet, the trackers. They have a way of holding their hands very still in their laps while they watch you. They have seen the colony change. They know which shafts were cut first, or they say they do not, and you cannot tell if this is honesty or refusal.

They are kind to you, in the careful way of people who have not had to be kind to anyone in a long time. They bring you filtered water that tastes of metal. They show you which corridors are quiet in the morning shift and which are loud. They tell you, with a flatness that is not unkindness, that the creatures will not bite, but that they will swarm, and that when they swarm you must stand still, and that the swarm will pass, and that you will feel them for days afterward in the seams of your clothing.

---

There are the older ones.

You do not see them. You are told about them the way you are told about the deeper geology of the pocket — as a fact that has been observed from a distance and never approached. They live in the rock below the colony, in chambers that were not cut by the colonists and were not fused or bolted but were *found*, already there, already hollowed, the walls of them pitted and scored with the marks of teeth that are not the teeth of the bred workers. They are larger, the older ones. The size of a forearm, the size of an arm, the size of a torso. They eat what the bred workers cannot eat — the iron-threaded glass at the heart of the basalt, the rare earths the smelters need, the minerals that have no common name in any language you speak. They are perfect at their work. The colonists do not improve them. You do not improve a thing that is already perfect at its work.

They are loud, the older ones, in a different way. They make a sound like a low continuous grinding, like the inside of a mill, like a thing being made small by another thing. You can hear them sometimes, on the mid levels, through the grating — when the smelters are quiet, and the workers have settled, and the colony is at its most turbulent equilibrium. The sound rises through the floor and into your boots and up your shins. It is not unpleasant. It is the sound of the place remembering itself.

You are told that the older ones do not swarm. You are told that they do not move in streams. You are told that if you ever encounter one, in a corridor you were told was safe, you will not be eaten, but you will be *tested*, in the way the bred workers test each other on the narrow grating, and that you must not move, and that the test will last longer than you want it to, and that when it is over you will not be the same. The tracker who tells you this says it with the same flatness as everything else. You cannot tell if they are warning you or inviting you.

---

You stand at the glass port at the top of the pocket.

The colony hums below you. The creatures move in the walls, in the gratings, in the cabling, in the seams of every alloy plate. The papery susurrus is everywhere, and you are inside it, and you have been inside it long enough that the silence of the lower plains, rising up the spires like a slow tide, feels like a kind of death.

You have learned, in your time here, that you are one more thing being digested. Not unkindly. Slowly. The pocket will make of you whatever the pocket makes of its dust. You will be eaten. You will be excreted. You will be a part of the long slow unmaking of the spire, and a part of the long slow making of whatever comes after the spire, which may also be unmade, and which will also be made, by the same patient chain of appetite, in geological real time, by creatures that do not tire, and by people who have learned to move at the same rhythm as the creatures, in a place that is its own weather, in a place that is its own digestion, in a place that will not let you go and does not intend to let you go and has never intended to let you go.

You turn away from the port. You descend. The colony takes you back.
