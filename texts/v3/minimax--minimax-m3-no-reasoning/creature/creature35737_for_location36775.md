They are the only moving things in Mishtu-Rek except the engines and the algae curtains stirring in their troughs, and they have made this fact into a kind of founding principle. Not loudly, not as doctrine, but the way a people absorb the conditions of their place: as something simply *known*. A Mishti raised on surface stories will hear of animals with the polite blankness of someone being told about a foreign currency. They will understand the words. They will not understand why anyone would want one. The Hollow is clean. The Hollow has no chickens in the alleys, no rats in the granaries, no flies in the wound. The Hollow has only humans and machines and the slow almost-life of the cultivated things, and this, the residents feel, is the correct ordering of a city.

# What the Heat Has Made of Them

The Mishti are small and damp-skinned. Generations of warm mineral air have not made them tall. The bones of their grandparents are softer than those of surface people; the algae gives them something like calcium but not enough, and the Mishti you meet in the upper hollows are a hand's breadth shorter than the surface average, narrow in the shoulders, with forearms that look slightly too long for their bodies. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, the irises a deep maroon-brown that catches the dim red light and gives their faces an almost wooden look at a distance. Up close you can see the pupil, wide as a cat's, and the small movements that compensate for a world without sun. They do not squint. They barely look away.

Their skin is the complexion the prompt you might have seen in a forge: not tanned, because nothing here has tanned it, but weathered, reddened, oil-stained, faintly glossy. The mechanics are leathery in a way surface workers can never quite be, because the heat is constant and the work is hot and the air is wet, and the combination — wet heat, hour after hour — does something to skin that dry heat does not. It leaves it plumped, almost swollen, like fruit that's been kept in water too long. A Mishti hand has the texture of something that has been gently steamed for years.

They wear little. Loose shirts of undyed linen or cotton, when they wear anything at all, and aprons for work. The mechanics are bare to the waist. The bathing-house attendants are bare to the waist. Children run nearly naked until they're old enough to be embarrassed, which is late. Footwear is rare and precious — leather rots in the damp, and the only leather that lasts is the treated kind the wealthier houses import through the descending paths. Most Mishti go barefoot or wear thick sandals of layered cloth, and their soles are calloused almost to leather themselves. They walk on warm stone all their lives. Their feet look like the feet of people who have never been cold.

Hair responds strangely to the wet heat. Without wind or dry air to give it shape, it tends to hang limp and heavy, or to frizz into a halo if it is fine. The Mishti have, over generations, evolved a small repertoire of solutions: oiling it back against the skull with rendered fat, braiding it wet, capping it in close caps of quilted cloth. A bareheaded Mishti is either a child, a bather, or someone who has just lost a relative. Hair unbound and uncombed is a sign of mourning. The funerary cap is gray and plain, and a Mishti who removes it in public has performed the act the way surface people pour out a glass.

# What They Do

The chief industry of Mishtu-Rek is gas, and the chief class of the Mishti is the engine-tender. The volcanic gases that seep up through the basal floor are colorless, bitter, and slightly narcotic if you stand over a vent too long. The tenders trap them in copper bells above the fissures, run them through condensers, compress them in small tight engines, and bottle them in iron cylinders that are hauled down the switchback paths to the markets of the lower hollows. A tender is a person who has learned to listen to pressure the way a musician learns to listen to pitch. The rooms where they work are cramped — the engines were not designed for the spaces they occupy, and were fitted in afterward, the way furniture is fitted into an attic. A tender can be off by a tone and know it. They communicate in the dark, by the small sounds of joints. They are pale even by Mishti standards, because they work in chambers lit by a single gas mantle and lit further by the blue flicker of the pilot flames. Their eyes are red-rimmed. They take salt tablets when they can get them.

The second industry is stone, and the stone-cutters are an older craft, more artisanal, more respected. The banded ochre-and-rust ceiling-stone splits cleanly along its old flow lines, and a cutter who knows her seam can bring down a slab the size of a doorway in three careful blows. The slabs are sold by weight to the lower hollows but also used in the city itself: the wealthier households panel their receiving rooms in banded stone, and the cutters earn a small, unhurried wage and are considered somewhere between a tradesman and an artist. They have callused outer edges of their palms and a peculiar grip — fingers slightly bent inward, as if still holding the chisel. They are among the few Mishti who go outside the city proper, into the upper hollows where the rock is younger, and they are therefore among the few who have seen the upper tunnels and the place where the daylight from the surface comes in as a pale broken thread. They don't talk about it. There's nothing there for them.

The algae-tenders are the agricultural class, if a city without fields can be said to have agriculture. The algae grows in maroon curtains in the reservoirs, fed by warm mineral water and tended the way surface gardeners tend roses. Tenders learn to read the curtain — when it has grown thick enough to harvest, when it has thinned to the point of starvation, when a bloom has gone wrong and the water must be changed. The work is wet, smelly, and unromantic. The algae stains the hands red-brown, permanently, and an algae-tender ten years in has the brown-handing that an engine-tender has the red-rimming. They are, however, the keepers of the staple. Without them, the city starves. They are treated with a respect they don't much enjoy.

The fungus-growers work in the deepest cellars, where it's hottest and dampest, and they are a smaller, quieter class. The fungi they cultivate are pale, fleshy, sweet in a way that takes getting used to. They are grown on slabs of warm stone in stone boxes that are kept at constant temperature with the runoff from the pipes. The fungus-growers know the temperature of every slab in their care the way the engine-tenders know the sound of every joint. They are thin, patient, and modest. Their product is considered a luxury, though no one would call it that in their presence.

The bathhouse attendants are a class apart. The mineral waters of the Hollow are warm, sulfurous, iron-rich, and there is no better thing to do at the end of a shift than to sink into one of the bathing pools and let the heat meet the heat and the iron meet the skin. The bathing-houses are social centers: people meet there, conduct business, gossip, arrange marriages, mourn in private, draft petitions, settle disputes. The attendants are confidants. They know your body and your news. They are trusted. They are also, in some interpretations, the actual power in the city, because the chamber councils meet in their bathing rooms when they want to talk without being overheard. The senior attendants of the largest bathing-house are addressed with a formal title that translates roughly as *person-who-knows*.

# What They Eat, What They Drink

The Mishti diet is monotonous by surface standards and not at all monotonous by their own. Algae is bread. Algae is porridge. Algae is the cake eaten at name-day observances. There are ways of preparing it — pressed into slabs and dried on warm stones, fermented in clay jars with mineral water, mixed with the seepage from the fungal cellars to make a thick reddish broth — that the Mishti learn from their mothers and grandmothers, and that they consider an inheritance. A grandmother who knows twelve ways to prepare algae is a grandmother whose name is remembered.

There is no surface-style alcohol. There is fungus-wine, a thin and strange fermented liquor made from the sweetest of the cultivated fungi, drunk at celebrations and considered faintly decadent. There is mineral water, of course — warm, slightly bitter, the daily drink, served in clay cups that sweat in your hand. There is a tea made from dried algae, bitter and brown, that is drunk at the quiet hour and is thought to settle the stomach. You can tell a Mishti by the cup: surface cups are glazed and smooth; Mishti cups are unglazed and develop a faint crust where the mineral water leaves its deposit.

There is no milk, no butter, no cheese, no honey, no fruit. A Mishti child raised on surface food would consider it the strangeness. They have no word for apple. Their language has no word for apple.

# What They Know and What They Don't

The geologists are few. There are perhaps a dozen people in Mishtu-Rek who have made a study of the edge, of the way the basalt falls away in episodes every few years, of the rate at which the city is being consumed. They are not formally educated — there is no academy — but they are readers of the rock, and they have a small body of knowledge passed among them. They know the edge has crept inward ten feet in living memory. They know the quarry above the city has retreated at twice that rate. They know, without quite saying it, that in some number of generations the easternmost street will have to be abandoned. They mark the rock face with a private sign that moves as the rock moves, a notch that registers the retreat, and they speak of this notch in low voices and not at all in public.

The rest of the city knows the edge. It is not a secret that the easternmost street is partly gone. What the geologists are not allowed to say is what the loss implies. There is, instead, the foundational story that the elders and the storytellers tell, that the Hollow was always this shape, that it was *given* in its present form, that the heat and the moisture and the steam are a benevolent endowment. The geologists are tolerated as people who do useful work. They are not consulted on cosmology. Mishti children grow up with the givenness of the Hollow as a kind of weather — a thing that is simply there, that does not require a history, that does not invite a question. To ask why the Hollow is the shape it is would be, for many Mishti, a category error: like asking why water is wet.

# What They Believe, in the Way They Believe

The Mishti are not a religious people in the surface sense, but they have rituals. There are the drips. There is the quiet hour. There is the bathing. There is the small ceremony performed at the edge by an old-timer once a season (and seasons are an artificial imposition here, marked by the almanac the geologists maintain against the rotation of the world above, which only a few of them have seen). The edge-ceremony is not somber. It is a kind of thanks. The old-timer speaks to the cliff. He names the street that has just been lost, if any has been lost, and he pours mineral water over the lip, and the water falls into the red glow of the lower hollow and is gone. Children watch. This is the only place in Mishti life where something is given away and not accounted for, and there is a faint sense of waste in it that older Mishti enjoy and younger Mishti find strange.

# What the Children Are Like

Mishti children are small and loud. The upper tiers — the higher tiers, slightly cooler, slightly drier, where the wealthier houses are paneled in banded stone — are where children grow up, and a tier where children are growing up is recognizable by the noise. They climb. The tiered construction rewards climbing, and the children are good at it, agile in a way that startles visitors. They also have an earlier sense of the dangerous edge than surface children do of, say, the road. There are songs taught to them about the cliff before they can walk properly. The phrase *don't go past the gutter* is in their vocabulary before any other phrase. A Mishti child who goes near the lip is brought back by anyone, not just their parents. The edge is the one communal fear.

Children drink mineral water from a cup. They eat algae-cake. They watch the drip in the high curves of the plaza and they learn its rhythm the way surface children learn rainfall. They also, because the cellars are warm and damp, accompany their parents into the fungus rooms early, and they grow up among the fungal slabs. There is a smell of childhood that Mishti remember when they are old, and it is the smell of the sweet pale fungus and the wet stone and the warm air.

They have never seen a tree. They have never seen a cloud. They don't have words for these things, except as borrowings from trade-pidgin, used at a distance.

# The Bather at the Edge

At the quiet hour — that dampened-down interval when the engines sigh and the industry is throttled — a Mishti of middle years, with brown-handed fingers and a quilted cap pushed back from her forehead, walks to the eastern edge alone. She has come from the bathing-house; her skin is slick and faintly rust-colored where the iron water has left its mark. She stands at the lip, where the basalt ends and the red glow begins, and she looks down.

Below her: the receding strata, the falling-away into the depths, the distant lit hollows of other cities, the plumes of vapor where the deep springs vent. The view is long. It is the longest view she will ever take. There is no horizon, but there is distance, and the distance is falling.

She does not speak. There is nothing to say to it. She has done this many times. She stands until the quiet hour ends and the engines resume their murmur below, and then she walks back into the tiered dark, into the heat, into the drip, into the smell of iron and steam and the cultivated almost-life of the city's only crop.

This is who lives in Mishtu-Rek. Not a people of the deep in any mythical sense. Just people, in the place they were given, doing the work they were raised to, eating what will grow, listening for the pressure in the pipes, and pouring mineral water over a cliff that is slowly, quietly, taking their city back.
