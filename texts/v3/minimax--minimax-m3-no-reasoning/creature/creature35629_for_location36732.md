# What Lives on the Plateau

The plateau has been lived on for so long that the boundary between the place and its inhabitants has blurred. The lichens that crust the rock in their orange and grey-green plates are some of the oldest living things here — slow, patient, drinking the air itself, dissolving the stone one molecule at a time into the pale powder that gathers in the lee of every outcrop. Moss comes where the water lies oftenest, thick and dark, and within the moss the smallest invertebrates — mites, springtails, things so small they are almost weather themselves — go about their business in the perpetual damp. The cyanobacteria that stain the wettest stones a kind of verdigris have been here since before anything else.

The amphibians are at the heart of the place. The frogs — many kinds, layered through the pools from the deepest to the film-thinest — begin their chorus in the deep dusk and run it through the night. Each species has its pitch, its rhythm, its particular phrase, and the place is so full of water in so many different volumes that the whole spectrum is filled. The largest of them — a heavy, slow bullfrog kind of creature, mottled brown and olive — sits in the deeper pools and adds a low note to the chorus that you feel in the chest before you hear it. The smallest of them, the size of a thumbnail, are the ones whose voices stitch the upper register together in a constant high weave. The tadpoles fill the margins of every pool in such density that the water moves with a life of its own, and the surface becomes a moving skin. Frogs here do not migrate. A given pool is its own nation, its own bloodline, generation after generation, and a frog born into a pool will likely die near it.

The fish are small, finned, dark, and they live where the pools connect in wet years — little elongated things that move through the temporary water in a slow dispersal when the rains come, then get caught in their pools when the water withdraws and live out the rest of the year in the smallness they have been given. They are eaten in turn.

The reptiles sun themselves on the stones all day, taking what warmth there is from the diffused light, their long bodies laid along the seams of warmer rock. There are lizards — small, swift, insectivorous, with a brightness along their flanks in the breeding season — and there are the larger slow things, the snakes, which move in S-curves through the shallows and into the grass tufts and eat the frogs and the small mammals and the eggs. They breed in the warm season and the females lie out on the sun-warmed stone in the late afternoons of the wetter months, gravid and still, and the eggs hatch in the soil pockets between the rocks when the warmth has done its work.

The insects are the place's true population. They are everything. The cicadas — if they are cicadas, or something that fills the same niche — emerge from the ground in their billions after their long subterranean years and climb every vertical surface they can find to split their nymph cases and unfurl the damp wings that are still soft. For a few weeks the air is full of their husks and the males' long mating whine fills every hour of daylight. Then they die, and their bodies fall, and the water of every pool takes them in, and the frogs and the fish grow fat on the windfall. The dragonflies are the predators of this abundance — long, colored bodies, wings that hold still in the air like drawn swords, sudden and exact. They hunt over every pool. The mosquitoes are a cloud, and always have been. The midges, when they hatch, fill the air in pale columns that look like smoke rising from the water.

The grazers are the most visible of the larger beasts. They are heavy, thick-legged, slow, with the patient look of animals that have to range widely for what they eat. They move in loose family groups — a bull, several cows, a juvenile or two — and they know the plateau the way a person knows a house. They know which tufts of grass will be tallest in any given week, which depressions hold water in the dry weeks, which overhangs give shelter from the rare hard rain. They are dark-colored, short-coated, with hooves that are wide and splayed to walk on the wet stone without sliding. Their calves are born in the wet season when the water is everywhere and the grass is most willing, and the calves can stand within an hour and walk within the day. They live a long time, the grazers. They are the dominant visible thing.

There are predators. Something crouched and brindled, low to the ground, hunts the grazers in the deeper wet — an ambush hunter that hides in the reeds and takes calves and the young and the sick. It is solitary except in the breeding season and it ranges widely. It has been the dominant predator here for a long time. Its tracks — the wide, four-toed ones — are everywhere in the soft margins of the pools, and the grazers know the smell of it and the alarm-bark rises from the herd when the wind brings it across the water.

There is a scavenger. A hunched, dark, patient thing with a bare head and a long way of standing very still in one place, waiting for something to die. It is not numerous but it is always somewhere, walking the marsh edges, watching the pools.

The wading birds are legion. They stand for hours — really, for hours — in the shallows, motionless, on legs so thin they look like they were drawn in ink, and then their heads come down fast and they come up with a small silver twist in their bills. They nest in the few trees, in the low platforms of sticks that the same species have been adding to for years. The young of these birds are fledged in the wet season and learn to stand the long still hours in the shallows before they are old enough to breed themselves. There is a smaller, darker bird that runs along the wet stone and eats the insects it finds there — quick, businesslike, with a long curved bill. It is everywhere and it is not numerous anywhere.

The mammals besides the grazers are small. There are rodent things in the grass tufts, breeding fast, eating fast, eaten faster. They are the food base for everything else — for the snakes, the birds, the crouched predator. There is a swimmer, sleek and dark, that lives in the larger flats of water and takes fish. It is rarely seen. There is a thing that flies at dusk — a small membrane-winged hunter that comes out when the light fails and takes insects on the wing in the damp air.

There are no people. There have never been people here, or if there were, the place has taken them back and there is no remaining mark. The place is entirely itself, has been entirely itself for a long time, and what lives here has settled into the wet stone and the brown water and the loud, layered, persistent hum of being alive in a place that is at the end of its wearing.
