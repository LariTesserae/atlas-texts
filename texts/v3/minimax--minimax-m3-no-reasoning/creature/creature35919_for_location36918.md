# The Inhabitants of the Lower Cataract

Who lives in the throat of a fall.

---

## The Stair-Keeper at the Top

There is an old woman who sits in a wooden booth at the head of the descending stair, where the rail-line ends and the phosphor-moss cages begin their vegetal glow. She is not the only stair-keeper who has held this post, but she is the current one, and she has held it for thirty-one years. She sells tickets downward and tickets upward and small waxed-paper twists of fried dough that she makes in a cast-iron pan over a coal brazier, and the dough is warm and the tickets are punched with a small brass clip that she wears on a chain around her neck. She knows everyone who lives in Lower Dyssen, which is to say she knows nineteen thousand people by face and another several thousand by the sound of their boots on the first hundred steps.

Her name is Pern, which is short for something longer that she no longer uses.

She does not go down anymore. She has not gone down in eleven years, since her husband died at the lip of the fall. She sends her daughter-in-law down every morning with a tray of doughnuts and a ledger to be signed at the foundry office, and the daughter-in-law comes back in the evening smelling of hydraulic oil and ozone. Pern asks about the cliff-edge. The daughter-in-law tells her. Pern marks a number in a small notebook she keeps beside her brazier, a notebook going back forty years, full of numbers, the retreat measured in centimeters per year, the slow arithmetic of a town being eaten by its own reason for existing. She does not show the notebook to anyone. She adds to it every evening by lamplight, and the lamp is phosphor-moss in a cage, and the moss has been trained to her hand, and the cage has been in her family for three generations.

She is the only person at the rim of the cataract who cannot hear the fall. She has lived with it so long that her ears have made their accommodation, the way the ears of a miller adjust to the mill. She hears, instead, the tram-bells of Lower Dyssen, transmitted up the cliff by the iron rails and the water in the pipes, and she considers these bells the music of her town, though she has not heard them directly in eleven years. Once a year, on the anniversary of the founding, she stands at the rail of the booth and listens for the bronze bell from the museum above, the rescued bell of the original town, and waits the twenty-two seconds for it to fade.

She waits the full twenty-two seconds. She has a watch.

## The Penstock-Crews

They are the young men and the young women who go down into the bedrock to dredge the penstocks.

The penstocks are the eight great throats cut through the cliff at a lower elevation than the quay. Each one is a tunnel the size of a cathedral, lined with iron, and each one carries the full vertical-mile pressure of the falling column down to a turbine. The water inside them moves at a speed that is not a speed you can swim against. It is a speed that turns steel into something resembling cloth. It is a speed that, if the iron lining ever failed, would cut a man in half before he understood he had been cut.

The penstock-crews go in anyway.

They go in by the access shafts that run parallel to the tunnels. They wear canvas suits soaked in tallow, and they carry long iron scrapers, and they work in pairs, one above the other on the wall of the penstock, on a scaffold of rope and slat, scraping the silt and the algae and the drowned branches that come down from the watershed. They work in four-hour shifts because the noise inside the penstock is not a noise you can work in for more than four hours without something in the chest giving way. They emerge from the access shafts with their ears ringing and their fingers white and a particular look on their faces that older residents of Lower Dyssen can identify from a hundred meters away. The look is not exhaustion. It is the look of a person who has just been inside a sound and is now outside it and is not yet sure which is the real place.

There are perhaps two hundred penstock-crews in Lower Dyssen, which is to say roughly a thousand people who do this work in rotation, four hours on and twelve hours off, day and night, in pairs. They are paid more than anyone else in town. They die younger than anyone else in town. They are buried in the cemetery on the highest ledge, which is the last ledge the town still occupies before the upper quarry begins, and the headstones there are not marble but basalt, the local stone, and the inscriptions are carved in a script that looks from a distance like writing but from close up is the small, precise notation of the retreat — the centimeter count, the year, the name. The penstock-crews have their own calendar. They age in penstock-years. They retire at forty-five if they retire at all.

They have a song. The song has no words. It is a low, humming note that two people hum in alternation, the lower note and the upper note, and the alternation creates a beat that is felt in the ribs rather than heard. They hum it before they go down the access shaft. They hum it at the bottom. They hum it on the way back up. It is the only song in Lower Dyssen that is older than the cataract itself, and no one knows where it came from, only that it is what you hum when you are about to enter the throat.

## The Cats

The cats own the alleys.

This is not a metaphor. Lower Dyssen is, by the last municipal count, home to perhaps eleven thousand cats, and the cats are not pets. The cats are not strays either. They are something else: a feral-but-resident population that has lived in the town longer than any human family, longer than any of the four relocations of the town, and that has adapted to the place with a thoroughness no human has matched.

The cats of Lower Dyssen are small and dark and long-bodied. They have waterproof fur, which is not a feature of domestic cats but is a feature of these cats, acquired over the ten thousand years of spray and rain that have made up the climate of the lower cliff. They have slightly webbed feet. They have unusually small ears, which is an adaptation to the constant low-frequency vibration of the cataract, and which gives them a fox-faced look that visitors find unsettling until they understand it is only the ear-bones.

They eat the rats that live in the talus. The rats of Lower Dyssen are themselves remarkable: they are large, they are pale, and they nest in the ruins of the older towns, the terrace-walls built on terrace-walls, and they come out at night to forage in the cloud-forest below. The cats come out at the same time. There is a long, slow, nightly negotiation between cats and rats that plays out in the alleys and on the rooftops and in the cantilevered beams that hold the houses out over the void. The negotiation does not end in extinction on either side. It has been going on for centuries. It has, in fact, reached a kind of equilibrium that the town's biologists have written papers about, and the papers are kept in the museum above, and they argue, among other things, that the cats and the rats of Lower Dyssen are no longer quite the cats and rats they were when they arrived. They have coevolved. They are each other's climate.

The cats do not meow. They make a different sound, a high thin chirp, almost a whistle, that carries well over the cataract-noise. Visitors hear it and think it is a bird. It is not a bird. The cats of Lower Dyssen have evolved a vocalization that occupies a frequency band the cataract does not fill. They speak, in effect, in the only quiet space the place affords.

They are watched over by the municipal cat-keeper, a position that has existed for six hundred years. The current cat-keeper is a thin, silent man named Voss who walks the alleys at dawn carrying a bucket of fish-heads and a long pole with a small brass bell on the end, which he rings softly to announce his approach. The cats come when they hear the bell. He feeds them. He counts them. He treats the injured ones in a small clinic at the back of his house, a clinic that is the only veterinary surgery in the region for a hundred kilometers in any direction. He knows each cat by the pattern of its face. He has named perhaps a hundred of them. The rest are known by description. He will tell you about the cats for hours if you let him, and his descriptions are extraordinarily detailed, and the residents of Lower Dyssen consult him on matters that have nothing to do with cats, because he is a man who has spent a long time paying attention.

He does not own a cat himself. He has never owned a cat. He considers this appropriate.

## The Cloud-Foresters

Below the town, in the dripping, slumping chaos of the talus, there are people who live in the forest.

They are not numerous. There are perhaps three hundred of them, spread across a series of small clearings on the landslide slopes where the talus has stabilized enough to build on. They are not squatters. They are not outcasts. They are the cloud-foresters, and they have a trade, and the trade is the forest.

They harvest moss.

The moss of the lower cataract is not ordinary moss. It is a particular genus, a thick, pale, almost luminescent species that has evolved in the perpetual spray of the fall, and it has the property of holding phosphor. It glows faintly in the dark. It glows brighter when it is wet. It glows brightest when it is handled, because the touch of a hand disturbs the surface chemistry in a way that releases a soft pulse of light. The phosphor-moss is harvested by the cloud-foresters and sold to the town above, where it is used to light the stair-cages, the alley-lamps, the inside of the foundry where the iron bands are cut, the reading-lamps of the school. It is the only light-source in Lower Dyssen that does not depend on the turbines. If the turbines failed, the town would go dark within hours. The phosphor-moss would still glow. The cloud-foresters know this. They are quietly proud of it.

They live in houses built on stilts in the talus, the stilts driven into the landslide rubble and lashed with ironbark rope, and the houses lean at the same angle as the trees around them, which is to say they lean outward, away from the cliff, because that is how things lean in the spray-wind. The cloud-foresters' children grow up leaning. Their spines curve. The visitors from above find this alarming. The cloud-foresters do not. They are taller, on average, than anyone in Lower Dyssen, and their hands are stained permanently green from the moss, and their eyes are slightly pale, which is an adaptation to the diffuse grey light of the spray-zone.

They speak a dialect. It is not the dialect of the town above. It is closer to the dialect of the high plateau, which is where their ancestors came from seven hundred years ago, when a particular family decided that the forest below the town was where they belonged, and walked down the stair and did not walk back up. Their descendants have not walked back up either. They go up for market, and they go up for the festival of the bell, and they go up when a child is to be born, because the town's midwife is on the third ledge from the top, and she insists. Otherwise they stay below.

They have their own marriage customs. They marry by walking together into the forest and not coming back for three days. The bride and the groom. The forest. The moss. The pale green frogs. When they come back they are considered married. They do not always come back. The forest is large, and the cloud-foresters are not numerous, and the marriage by absence is sometimes an absence of a different kind. The town above finds this unsettling. The cloud-foresters do not explain it.

## The Pale Frogs

There is a frog in the forest.

It is small and bright green and it lives on the lichen on the moss on the bark of the trees on the rocks on the talus below the town. It has no common name because no one has ever been close enough to name it with affection. The cloud-foresters call it the bell-frog, because its call is a single clear note that sounds, in the constant roar of the cataract, like a bell heard from very far away. It is not a loud frog. It is a small, persistent, almost silent frog, and it is the only voice in the forest that is not the burble of a spring or the hiss of spray.

The frogs of the cloud-forest are everywhere. There are millions of them, perhaps tens of millions, distributed across the talus in a density that no biologist has ever properly counted, because the counting would require going out at night in the rain in the dark under a kilometer of mist, and the biologists who have tried have generally given up after a few hours and gone back to the town above, where the lamp-light is reliable and the rain does not come through the roof.

The frogs eat the insects that breed in the wet moss. The insects eat the moss. The moss eats the mist. The mist is the cataract. The frogs are, in a sense, the cataract made audible, and on still nights, when the wind from the fall dies down for a few minutes, you can hear them from the town above, a faint, glassy tinkling, ridiculous, like wind-chimes at a furnace.

## The Turbine-Minders

Down at the bedrock, in the cathedral-sized chambers where the eight great turbines turn, there are people who mind the machines.

They are the most skilled workers in Lower Dyssen. They are the most respected. They are also, by long tradition, the most silent. They do not talk about their work. They do not talk about the turbines. They do not talk about what it is like to stand in a chamber the size of a cathedral while a vertical mile of water spins a machine that generates enough power to light a city, and to feel, through the soles of your boots, the entire cliff vibrating at a frequency that is just below the threshold of human hearing but that is, over the course of a shift, perfectly capable of rearranging the bones of your inner ear.

The turbine-minders work in pairs. One minder stands at the console in the chamber. The other minder walks the perimeter, checking the iron bands that reinforce the rock around the turbine, looking for fatigue, looking for the hairline cracks that mean a band is about to fail. The bands are replaced every forty years. The minders know when the forty years are up. They feel it. They feel it the way a sailor feels weather.

There are sixteen of them, eight pairs, one pair per turbine. They serve for life, or until their hearing goes, which is usually the same thing. When a turbine-minder retires, he or she moves up the cliff to a house on the highest ledge, the ledge nearest the cemetery of the penstock-crews, and there they sit, in rocking chairs, in the spray-shadow of a cantilevered roof, watching the cliff-edge retreat in its slow centimeter-per-year way. They are the only residents of Lower Dyssen who can sit still. The rest of the town cannot. The rest of the town is in motion, has always been in motion, will always be in motion. The turbine-minders, after their years below, are the only ones who have learned to stop.

They are consulted, in emergencies, by the town council. The council meets in a chamber on the second ledge from the top, a chamber lined with basalt and lit by phosphor-moss, and the council consists of twelve people, six from the foundry, three from the penstock-crews, two from the cloud-foresters, and one from the cat-keeper's office. The cat-keeper's representative is Voss himself. He attends because the cats are a constituency. The cats cannot vote, but the rats can, and the rats live in the ruins of the older towns, and the ruins are the property of the municipality, and the municipality is represented by the cat-keeper, and the cat-keeper's representative is himself. The logic is impeccable. No one has ever successfully argued against it.

The council's primary function is to decide, each year, how far the town must move back from the cliff-edge. The measurement is done by a surveyor named Orta, who is the last of a line of surveyors going back to the founding of the town, and who goes out each morning to the lowest ledge and measures, with a brass instrument of his own manufacture, the distance from the ledge to the lip of the fall. He records the number. He sends it up to Pern at the top of the stair, who records it in her notebook. He sends it down to the council, who records it in the municipal ledger. The three records are compared once a year, on the anniversary of the founding, after the ringing of the rescued bell, and if they agree, the town does not move that year. If they disagree, the town moves.

The disagreements are getting more frequent. The retreat is getting faster. The town is moving back, on average, one ledge every century, which is to say four ledges in the four hundred years since the last move, but the recent moves have been closer together, and the next move, everyone agrees, is coming soon.

Orta does not say how soon. He is a surveyor. He reports what is.

## The Children

There are children in Lower Dyssen, of course. There are children everywhere there are towns.

The children of Lower Dyssen are unusual only in that they grow up accustomed to the cataract-noise at an age when the rest of the world's children are still being startled by thunder. They do not startle at the cataract. They startle at quiet. They find the silence of the plateau above unbearable, and the silence of any room with thick walls oppressive, and they will, if they can, sleep on the cantilevered porches of their houses with the mist on their faces and the cataract in their ears. They are dark-haired and pale-eyed, like the cloud-foresters but darker. They speak the dialect of the town, which is the dialect of the high plateau modified by the spray and the tram-bells and the long habit of shouting across voids. They are educated in a schoolhouse on the third ledge from the top, where the teacher is a woman named Mael who has taught for forty years and who uses a pointer made of ironbark and a blackboard made of slate and a curriculum that includes, in addition to reading and arithmetic, the measurement of cliff-retreat, the identification of moss species, the names of the cats in the alleys nearest the school, and the singing of the penstock-crew's humming-song, which is taught to all children whether or not they will become penstock-crews, because it is considered a song everyone should know.

The children are watched over, when they are out of school, by an informal network of older siblings and retired turbine-minders and the cloud-foresters' children who come up from below for lessons and go back down in the evenings. They play in the alleys and on the cantilevered beams and in the ruins of the older towns, which are off-limits but which they enter anyway, because the ruins are the best place to play, the ruins being full of the broken stairs of older buildings and the rusted wheels of older pumping stations and the bent skeletons of older cranes, all of which make excellent forts and hiding-places and launching-pads for the small rockets the children make from cardboard tubes and powder scraped from the forge-floor.

The rockets are a problem. The rockets go up. The rockets come down. The rockets occasionally land on the cantilevered roofs of houses lower down the cliff, and the residents of those houses complain, and the complaints are taken to the council, and the council considers the matter and generally decides that the rockets are a tradition and that traditions are to be respected, and the children are allowed to continue launching them as long as they do not launch them at the turbines.

No child has ever launched a rocket at the turbines. The children know better. The turbines are not a thing you launch a rocket at. The turbines are a thing you live beside, and you mind, and you listen to, and when you grow up you may serve, and when you have served you retire to the highest ledge and you sit in a rocking chair and you watch the cliff-edge retreat, and you have earned the right to be still.

## The Bride Who Did Not Come Back

There is a bride of the cloud-foresters who walked into the forest for her marriage and did not come back.

This was seven years ago. Her name was Tila. She was nineteen. The groom was a young forester named Bren, who came back alone after the three days, with moss on his hands and a look on his face that the town above found difficult to interpret.

He said she had walked into the forest on the second morning and had not come back by the third evening. He said he had looked for her. He said he had called. He said he had heard, very faintly, far away in the trees, the bell-note of a single frog.

The cloud-foresters searched for her. The townspeople searched for her. The penstock-crews searched for her, on their days off, in the talus below the access shafts. They did not find her. They found her footprints, going down the slope, into the deeper forest, into the part of the forest where the talus gives way to the older landslide and the older landslide gives way to the cliff-foot proper, where the spray is so constant that the moss grows on the moss on the moss and the trees lean so far outward that their crowns touch the ground. They found her footprints. They did not find her.

Bren married again, two years later. He married a woman from the town above, a daughter of the foundry, who came down to the forest and who walks the stilts with the practiced ease of someone who has learned to lean. They have a child. The child has green-stained hands already, although it is only three.

Tila's footprints are still there, in the moss of the deeper forest, preserved by the perpetual wet. The cloud-foresters go and look at them sometimes, and they say the footprints are getting fainter, not sharper, as if the moss is slowly growing over them, the way the moss grows over everything in the spray, and that in another hundred years the footprints will be gone.

The bride will be gone too, they say. She will have become moss.

The town above does not know what to make of this. The cloud-foresters do not explain. Bren does not talk about it. His new wife has never asked.

## The Visitor

There is a visitor in Lower Dyssen. There is almost always a visitor.

The visitor has come down the stair and has taken a room at the inn on the fourth ledge, a room with a window that faces the fall, and the visitor is sitting at the window now, eating a meal of fish and flatbread, watching the cataract.

The visitor is here because of the stair. The visitor came down the stair and the stair did something to the visitor, the way stairs do, the way descents do. The visitor is not yet sure what was done. The visitor is watching the cataract and listening to the tram-bells and waiting to find out.

The visitor will be here for some time. The visitor has not yet learned to hear the tram-bells as separate from the cataract. The visitor has not yet learned to lean. The visitor has not yet been taught the humming-song, although the children in the alleys have offered to teach it, and Voss the cat-keeper has offered to teach it, and the bride who did not come back has, in a sense, taught it already, by the particular way the forest holds a sound.

The visitor is one of perhaps two hundred visitors who come to Lower Dyssen in a year, down from the tens of thousands who came a century ago when the tourist trade was at its height. The visitor is here for the same reason the tourists came. The visitor is here because there is a place where the world has cut itself open and the inside is visible, and the visitor wants to see it.

The visitor will see it. The visitor will stand at the railing of the quay and look down the face of the cliff and see the water gather and go over the edge and not come back, and the visitor will understand, for the first time, that there are places in the world where the rules are different, where the rivers do not stop, where the cliffs retreat, where the towns move, where the bells are rung once a year and take twenty-two seconds to fade.

The visitor will go back up the stair, eventually. The visitor will take the rail-line across the plateau. The visitor will return to a place where the rivers stop and the cliffs do not retreat and the bells are not rung, and the visitor will find the silence of that place unbearable, and the visitor will come back down the stair, and the stair will ring under the visitor's feet, and the phosphor-moss will glow, and the cataract will not stop.

Nothing in Lower Dyssen stops. The visitor is learning this. The visitor is, in fact, almost learned.
