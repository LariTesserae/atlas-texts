# What Lives on the Plateau of Dripping Iron

The settlement has a population that is hard to count, because so much of it is carved into the rock. The census takers go down into the basalt chambers with their lamps and their ledgers and come back with damp pages and figures that are always slightly out of date, because the people who live in those chambers have a way of emerging and submerging that does not correspond to any schedule the administrators recognize. A reasonable estimate puts the town at something like three thousand souls, though on certain nights — the nights of the half-moons, when the heat breaks a little and the air thins and the distant settlements on other ridges can be made out as smudges of orange light on the horizon — a man standing in the central plaza might be forgiven for guessing half that. And on other nights, when the ore-carts are running heavy and the temporary labor has come in from the salt flats below, he might be forgiven for guessing three times it.

The people who live here are not tall, on average. Generations of growing up in the wet heat have produced a certain economy of frame — a tendency toward narrow shoulders, long forearms, hands that are broad across the knuckles and prehensile in a way that makes them excellent with tools and with the small wriggling things that occasionally emerge from the rock seams. Their skin is darker than the prevailing fashion in lowland cities would prefer, and it carries a permanent undertone of the rust that stains everything here. They do not notice this about themselves. They notice it about visitors.

The visitor from elsewhere arrives pale and sweating and immediately becomes a problem, because the heat is not the kind of heat that announces itself and then relents. It is the kind that settles in around a person like sediment in disturbed water, and after a few hours the visitor's boots have left orange prints on the stone floors of whatever inn has taken him in, and the corners of his eyes are burning, and he has begun to suspect that the air he is breathing is heavier than the air he is used to breathing, that it has weight to it, that it is in some way *feeding* on him. The natives recognize this. They bring him a ceramic cup of something warm and faintly mineral-tasting and wait. The visitor either adapts or he leaves. Most leave, eventually, with the kind of relief that is indistinguishable from defeat. The ones who stay — and there are some, every year, a few — are the ones who begin, after a few weeks, to feel that the place has a logic to it that they had been missing in their former lives, a logic that has to do with the patience of iron and the steadiness of water and the long slow work of becoming part of a thing that was there before you arrived.

The adults of the town work, of course. The factories must be staffed, the ore must be brought down the cable-tram and smelted and poured, the pipes must be maintained, the cisterns must be cleaned of the mineral sediment that accumulates in them at a rate the engineers find alarming. The work is not loud but it is constant. A man might spend thirty years tending a single furnace, knowing its moods, knowing which of its seams will weep first when the humidity shifts, knowing the particular orange shade of its glow when it is running well and the slightly different orange shade when it is running poorly. There is pride in this, but it is the pride of someone who has married into a difficult family rather than the pride of someone who has conquered something. The furnace is not conquered. It is humored.

The women of the town do most of what the women of industrial towns elsewhere do — they keep the houses, they keep the accounts, they raise the children — but there is more of the outdoor work that falls to them here than in lowland places, because the heat is not gendered in its cruelties and because the menfolk are often below in the smelters for sixteen hours at a stretch. A woman on the Plateau of Dripping Iron might be expected to mend a section of pipework, to clear a cistern, to climb out along the iron-stained shoulder of the ridge in the early morning to check the lichen-traps her grandfather set forty years ago. She does this without comment. She has been doing some version of it since she was small.

The children are a quieter population than children elsewhere. They are not unhappy. They simply have less to be loud about. There are no games that require shouting, no sports that require cheering crowds, no quarrels that rise to the pitch of quarrels in the lowlands. A child here might spend an entire afternoon watching a single column of the pale fan-winged insects drift above a seep-line, or following the path of one of the eyeless worms as it traces its slow line through a wet crack in the stone, or sitting very still next to one of the slate-colored lizards and trying to outlast it, to see who will move first. The child almost always loses these contests. The lizard has been doing this longer. The children learn from this. They learn the value of stillness, and of attention, and of the long view.

The old people of the town are respected in the particular way that old people are respected in places where the young cannot easily leave. They are the keepers of the lore of the pipes, of the cisterns, of the particular places on the ridge where the water emerges and the particular places where it does not. An old woman on the Plateau of Dripping Iron can tell you, by the way a certain stratum of basalt weeps in a certain wind, what the weather will do for the next three days. She cannot explain how she knows this. She will tell you that her mother knew it, and that her mother's mother knew it, and that the knowledge is in the hands and in the feet and in the small of the back where the heat settles, and that it cannot be put into words without being lost. The young accept this. They will learn it themselves, in time, if they stay.

There are visitors, occasionally. The cable-tram brings them up from the basin below — engineers from the lowland cities, buyers of the ore, the rare cartographer or naturalist or curious person of independent means who has heard that there is a town built into a ridge of dripping iron and has come to see for himself. They stay at the inn at the upper end of the saddle, which is kept by a man named something like *Vakkren* and his wife, who is from elsewhere and came up on the tram twenty years ago and has never left and will never leave, because she has come to love the place in the way that only converts can love a place. She serves them the warm mineral drink and watches their faces and knows which of them will adapt and which will leave.

The ore-buyers come and go. They are not of the town. They never are. They stay at the inn, they inspect the smelted product, they sign their papers, they descend the tram with their samples and their ledgers. They are not unfriendly, but they are never quite present, either. They have come from a world where transactions happen quickly and decisions are made in rooms with closed windows, and they cannot quite believe that the slow accumulation of rust on every surface is not a sign of some failure of governance. They leave, usually, with relief.

The cartographers come less often. They stay longer. They are the ones who sometimes do not leave, or whose assistants do not leave, or whose grandchildren do not leave. They come because they have heard that the ridge is interesting, that the lichen-forms here are unlike the lichen-forms elsewhere, that the basalt columns of the upper face have weathered into patterns that do not appear on any other basalt outcrop they have studied. They bring their notebooks and their instruments and they climb the shoulder of the ridge in the early morning, before the heat has climbed to its full weight, and they measure and they sketch and they write, and they do not notice, at first, that they have begun to stand very still when they observe a thing, and to breathe very quietly, and to feel, when the sun comes over the lip of the ridge and the wet stones throw the light back at them, that they are being looked at by something that is older than they are.

The naturalist who came up the tram three years ago and who is now a permanent fixture of the inn's common room — a man of middling years and indeterminate accent, whose given name is something like *Ostren* and whose publications are beginning to be cited in lowland journals with increasing frequency — he is the closest thing the town has to a scholar. He has been cataloguing the local fauna for the three years he has been here, and he has produced a monograph on the fan-winged insects that is, by general consensus, the definitive work on the subject. He is working now on a monograph about the crabs without shells. He goes out in the early morning, when they are abroad, and he watches them with the kind of attention that the children of the town have learned to give to small things. He has been adopted, in a sense, by the town. The mayor has given him a small chamber carved into the basalt for his papers. The innkeeper's wife brings him his meals when he forgets to come in for them. He will probably die here. He does not seem to mind.

There is a priest, of a kind. The town does not have a temple of any of the lowland faiths, but it has a man who performs the functions that priests perform elsewhere — the naming of children, the blessing of the dead, the marking of the turning of the year. He is a tall thin man with a face that looks as though it has been carved from the same basalt as the ridge, and he speaks very little, and when he speaks it is in a voice that is so low and so even that it is difficult to be sure he has spoken at all. He walks the ridge at all hours. He is seen in the smelters and on the salt flats and in the lichen-traps and at the lip of the cistern where the runoff collects before it is sent down to the boilers. The people tip their heads to him when they pass. He tips his head back. Nothing further is exchanged. The arrangement seems to satisfy everyone.

There is no mayor, exactly. There is a council of seven, drawn from the senior workers of the smelters and the cisterns and the pipe-maintenance crews, and they meet in a chamber that is partly natural and partly cut, with walls that weep a constant fine mist and a floor that has been worn smooth by the boots of a hundred years of deliberation. The council does not govern so much as it ratifies. The town has its own momentum, its own slow accumulation of habit, and the council's job is mostly to formalize what the town has already decided for itself. Decisions take a long time. A proposal that would be debated and voted on in an evening in the lowlands might take a full season to work its way through the council here, and by the time it is ratified it has usually been quietly implemented anyway, by the people who saw the need for it.

The dogs of the town are a particular breed — or rather, they are not a breed at all, but a kind of dog that has made its own arrangements with the place. They are pale and short-haired and slightly smaller than the dogs of the lowlands, with narrow muzzles and eyes that are set wide apart and that have a particular quality of attention, as though they are always listening to something just beyond the range of human hearing. They do not bark much. They pant, in the heat, with their mouths open and their thin pink tongues extended, and they lie in the deepest of the available shadows, and they follow their people with the devotion that dogs everywhere follow their people, but they do it quietly, and at a slight remove, as though they have understood something about the nature of the place that the people have only half-understood.

The cats are fewer. There are cats in the upper settlement, around the inn and the council chamber, but they are thin and pale and they keep to the highest points, and they are not the friendly cats of the lowland cities. They watch. They do not purr. They have made some kind of separate arrangement with the ridge.

The town eats, of course. The food comes up the cable-tram from the basin below, where there is a small agricultural settlement that grows what can be grown in the salt-adjacent soil and trades it for the smelted iron and the cistern-water. The food is not bad. It is not exciting. It is the food of a place that has learned to feed itself from what is available, and that does not ask for more than that. There is a flat bread that is the staple. There is a stew of the eyeless worms, which is considered a delicacy and which the visitors find disturbing until they have tried it, after which they find it more disturbing, but in a different way. There is a fermented drink made from the gelatinous films that colonize the seep-lines, which is bitter and faintly phosphorescent and which the natives consume in small quantities and at long intervals. The visitors do not generally try it. The ones who stay sometimes develop a taste for it.

The dead of the town are buried in the basalt. There is a small chamber cut into the upper face of the ridge, above the smelters, where the bodies are laid in niches and left. The niches are sealed with stone. There is no marker. The names are kept in a ledger that is maintained by the priest and that is read aloud, once a year, at a ceremony that takes place at the lip of the ridge at the moment when the heat breaks for the half-moon. The names are read by a child. The wind takes most of them. The people who are present do not seem to mind. They have come to understand, after a few years, that the names are not for them. The names are for the ridge.

A man who lives here for a long time begins to acquire certain habits. He learns to move slowly in the heat, and not to resent the slowness. He learns to listen for the drip of the pipes and to find in it a kind of company. He learns to look at the iron stains on the cliffs and not to see them as stains but as the natural coloration of the stone, in the way that a man who lives by the sea learns to see the grey of the water as a color rather than as the absence of one. He learns the names of the small creatures — the names that the children give them, which are different from the names in the naturalist's monograph, and which are the names that matter. He learns to sit on the iron-stained shoulder of the ridge in the early morning, before the heat has climbed, and to watch the pale fan-winged insects drift above the seep-lines in their loose columns, and to feel, as he watches them, that he is watching something that has always been there and will always be there, and that his presence is incidental to it, and that this is all right.

He learns, eventually, to love the place. Not in the way that the lowlanders love their lowland places, with a passion that requires an object. He loves it in the way that a man loves his own body, or his own work, or his own long slow accumulation of years. He loves it because it is his, and because he is its, and because the distinction between these two things has, after a while, ceased to matter.

He does not leave. The visitors who adapt are the ones who do not leave. The ones who leave are the ones who could not stop thinking of the place as somewhere they had arrived at, rather than as somewhere that had, very patiently, accepted them.
