## The Polers

In the drowned quarter, the boat is not a vehicle. It is a way of being indoors.

Children learn to pole before they learn to swim, which is to say they learn to pole at four or five, standing in the bow of a skiff too small for an adult, pushing a sharpened pole against the coral-coated streets below. The pole finds purchase on the old pavement, on the thresholds that used to be doorsteps, on the curbs that used to edge avenues before the avenues became canals. The poler stands and the skiff moves and the poler does not fall in, because falling in is how you learn the depth of the streets, and the depth of the streets is known.

A poler of any skill stands the whole day. The skiff is flat-bottomed, nine feet long, planked tight. The pole is a length of straight-grained hardwood, often heartwood from one of the fig-trees that have died in the middle terraces and been hauled down — heavy, balanced, scarred with use. The business end is wrapped in pitch and iron banding where it takes the worst abrasion. The top end, where the poler grips, is rubbed smooth and dark with hand-oil over years.

The stance is wide. The knees are slightly bent. The pole goes down and finds the street and pushes and the skiff glides and the pole comes up dripping and goes down again. There is a rhythm to it. The rhythm is the poler's, not the boat's. A good poler can move a loaded skiff through a doorway without touching the sides. A good poler can pole backward. A good poler knows every street in the quarter by the feel of the bottom.

They are not taciturn, the polers. They sing, often, working songs whose verses are local and whose choruses are older. They call to one another across the water in the carrying voice. They argue about the depth of a particular corner. They keep their hair cut short and their feet calloused and their eyes, always, on the next few feet of water ahead.

## The Smokers

On the middle terraces, on every ledge wide enough to stand two people abreast, there is a smoker.

The smoker is a stone box, roughly waist-high, fitted with a grate and a chimney of clay tiles or of woven branches daubed with mud. The fire is built on the grate. Above the fire sits a flat stone, and on the stone sits whatever is being cooked, and around the stone sits the smoke, which is meant to be there.

The middle terraces are wet. The humidity is high and the rains come in bands off the western water and the vegetation is dense and the air is full of small crawling things that would very much like to eat stored food. The smoker solves this. The smoker cures meat. The smoker smokes fish brought up from the drowned quarter. The smoker dries the pods of the legume trees whose upper canopies shade the terraces. The smoker is a kiln, a larder, and a beacon, all at once — the smell of its smoke is the smell of the middle city, and a person who smells the smoke from far off knows they are approaching home.

A smoker-keeper tends the fire for an hour or two in the morning and again in the evening. The fire is fed with the pruned branches of the lianas and with the dried dung of the small pig-like animal kept in the upper pens. The smoker-keeper is usually an older person, often a woman, often the grandmother of the household, and the tending of the smoker is a job that is also a social position, because everyone comes to the smoker during its burning to talk.

The conversation at a smoker is the conversation of a place. News moves through the smokers faster than it moves through any official channel, because the official channels of the middle terraces are also the smokers — the same fire, the same stones, the same old woman watching the same smoke rise. A piece of gossip enters a smoker at morning and is, by evening, on every terrace in the city. A judgment pronounced at a smoker is, for practical purposes, a law.

## The Wallers

In the upper city, where the soil is inches deep and the rain is uncertain and the wind is a constant presence, the work that has to be done is the work of making places for things to grow.

The waller builds terrace walls.

The walls are three feet high, sometimes four, built of loose stone stacked dry — no mortar, no cutting, just the patient selection and placement of stones that fit. A waller carries stones in a sling at the hip. A waller can eye a stone and know whether it will lie flat. A waller can build a wall that will stand for sixty years without the top course shifting, and a waller can build a wall that will fall in the first storm, and the difference is the waller's hands and the waller's eye.

The waller is, by trade, a stone-person. The waller knows the basalt of the ridge — which layers split clean, which shatter, which hold water. The waller knows where the loose stones are and where the bedrock is and where the scree from higher up has collected in a usable pile. The waller works from the top of a terrace downward, building the retaining wall first and then filling behind it with the soil that is brought up in baskets from the lower terraces — leaf-mold and ash and the dark, fibrous compost of the middle city's pruned growth, carried up in the morning and laid behind the new wall in the afternoon.

A waller's hands are broad and scarred. A waller's forearms are ropy. A waller walks with the slightly bent-forward gait of a person who has spent a lifetime carrying weight on the hips. A waller is, in the upper city, the most necessary of the trades — more necessary than the baker, more necessary than the bell-ringer — because without walls, the soil blows away in the wind, and without soil, the grain does not grow, and without grain, the city does not eat.

The wallers are quiet. They work alone or in pairs. They are paid in grain, in shares of the harvest from the walls they have built, in the small but real respect of a city that understands what they do.

## The Children of the Drowned

There is a class of child in the city that is not found in cities built on dry land.

These are the children who grow up in the drowned quarter, who live in the upper stories of houses whose lower stories are coral and water, who learn the poling-stance before they learn to read. They are wiry and brown and quiet-voiced in a way that the children of the upper terraces are not, because the drowned quarter is not loud — the upper stories of the drowned quarter are quiet, by city standards, and a child who grows up there learns to listen before it learns to speak.

These children know the water. They know the tides, though the tides are weak. They know the fish — which ones are good eating, which ones have bones that must be cut a particular way, which ones will steal bait from a hook and which ones will not. They know the eels, and they are not afraid of the eels, and they will reach into a chimney and pull out an eel by the neck with the same unconcerned certainty that an upper-terrace child pulls a carrot from a terrace bed.

These children do not climb well. They do not need to. The drowned quarter is horizontal, navigated by pole, and a child of the drowned quarter who found itself suddenly in the middle terraces would be as lost as a middle-terrace child in the drowned — uncertain, off-balance, afraid of the wrong thing.

The children of the drowned grow up and become polers, mostly, or fishers, mostly, or the makers of the woven fish-traps that are set in the drowned doorways. A few go up. A few marry into the middle-terrace families and learn, slowly, to climb. But most stay, and the drowned quarter goes on being poled by its own children, generation after generation, in the same patient rhythm as the tide.

## The Watchers of the Waterline

There is an old man, or an old woman, in every terrace community whose job it is to watch the water.

The watcher walks the edge of the inhabited zone every morning and notes, on a tablet of soft stone kept in a niche for the purpose, the height of the water against the marked posts that the community has set at the boundary. The mark is scratched fresh each year at the lowest reading of the year, and the watcher notes the rise against that mark. The watcher does not speak of what they have seen. The watcher writes it.

The watcher is, by tradition, a person near the end of their life, because the work is patient and the work is long, and a young person would find it tedious. A watcher keeps the tablet for a decade, sometimes two, and then passes it to the next watcher, and the next watcher continues the record.

The tablets are kept in the upper terraces, in a small stone building whose door is never locked. The tablets of the previous watchers are stacked on shelves inside, hundreds of them, going back — no one knows how far. The oldest tablets are worn almost smooth. The marks on them are barely visible. But they are there.

The watchers of the waterline do not meet. They do not form a guild. They are simply the people who do the watching, in their own communities, in their own patient way, and the record they keep is the longest continuous record of anything in the city.

## The Coral-Tenders

In the drowned quarter, below the waterline, the coral grows.

It grows on the walls of the houses. It grows on the streets. It grows on the doorframes, softening the right angles, rounding the edges, making the drowned quarter, over years, into a continuous reef-like structure that is part architecture and part living thing. The people do not usually tend the coral. The coral tends itself.

But there is a class of person — usually a poler, usually a person of middle age — who pays attention to the coral in the way a gardener pays attention to a vine. The coral-tender knows which coral is fast-growing and which is slow. The coral-tender knows that the soft coral that encrusts the walls is good, in the sense that it protects the underlying masonry from the boring mollusks that would otherwise eat the mortar-less joints. The coral-tender knows that the hard branching coral that grows in the doorways is bad, in the sense that it narrows the channels the skiffs use, and a coral-tender will, with a hooked pole, break off the worst of it from time to time, and the broken coral will be hauled up to the middle terraces and burned for lime.

The coral-tender works in the morning, in the cooler hours, in the skiff. The coral-tender wears a wrap of woven plant-fiber and little else, and the skin of a coral-tender, below the wrap-line, is the color of old bone, bleached by years of sun on the water. The eyes of a coral-tender are good. The hands of a coral-tender are quick.

The coral-tenders are not numerous. A city of a few thousand souls might have twenty of them, working the drowned quarter in rotation. They are paid in fish. They are considered, in the older families of the drowned quarter, to be a kind of cousin to the polers, and a poler who shows an interest in the coral will sometimes be taken on by a coral-tender as an apprentice, and will learn the slow ways of the reef, and will in turn take on an apprentice of their own.

## The Climbers

The middle terraces cannot be reached by pole.

They can be reached only by climbing — by the narrow stone stairs that are cut into the basalt at intervals along the cliff, by the rope-and-rung ladders that bridge the gaps where the stairs have not been cut, by the lianas themselves, which the people of the middle terraces have trained into a kind of secondary stair system over generations. A person who lives in the middle terraces climbs, every day, and a person who lives in the middle terraces is, by the standards of the drowned quarter or the upper terraces, a kind of vertical animal.

The children of the middle terraces climb before they walk. They are taught to climb by their parents, who are themselves taught by their parents, and so the climbing ability of a middle-terrace child at three is the climbing ability of an upper-terrace adult at thirty. The middle-terrace child will go up a liana hand-over-hand with the unconcerned speed of a person going down a staircase. The middle-terrace child will cross a gap on a rope bridge with the easy balance of a person crossing a street.

The climbers are, by trade, the tenders of the vegetation. The climbers prune. The climbers train the lianas along their intended paths. The climbers clear the joints of the masonry where a root has begun to work into a crack. The climbers harvest — the fruit of the fig-trees, the flowers of the creepers, the pods of the legume trees, the leaves of the shade-trees. The climbers go up into the canopies of the middle terraces, which are dense and dark and full of the small climbing mammals with too many hands, and they work there in the green half-light, and they come down loaded with what they have gathered.

The climbers fall, sometimes. A climber who falls from a height dies, usually, and the death is felt in the household and in the terrace community and, by evening, in the smokers. The climbing is dangerous and the climbing is necessary and the people of the middle terraces do not pretend otherwise.

## The Wind-Listeners

The upper city is in the wind.

The wind comes off the western water, steady, salt-laden, strong enough to lean into. The wind is the upper city's constant companion, and the upper city's people have, over generations, learned to listen to it.

The wind-listener is a person who lies, in the early morning, on the upper path that runs the spine of the ridge, and who listens. The wind-listener is listening for changes. The wind shifts direction before the weather shifts. The wind drops before the rain. The wind rises before the squall that comes off the water in the late afternoon. A wind-listener who has been listening for an hour can tell, with reasonable certainty, what the weather will do for the rest of the day.

The wind-listeners are not formal. There is no guild, no apprenticeship, no tablet. The wind-listeners are simply the people who are good at it — who lie on the ridge in the morning and listen, and who tell the wallers whether to work the upper terraces today or whether to wait, and who tell the bell-ringers whether the morning bell will carry or be lost. A wind-listener is, often, a waller who has grown too old for the carrying of stones, and who has turned, by slow stages, to the carrying of weather.

The wind-listeners are quiet. The wind-listeners have good ears. The wind-listeners are, in the upper city, a kind of unofficial weather service, and a waller who has been told by a wind-listener that the weather will hold will work through the morning without looking up, and a waller who has been told that the weather will not hold will go home early, and both of them will be right, more often than not.

## The Bell-Ringers

At the hours, the bells.

There are four small towers in the upper city, set at intervals along the ridge, and in each tower hangs a bell — hand-cast, hand-rung, the bell-metal darkened with years of salt air. The bells are rung at the hours by the bell-ringers, who are an official post, appointed by the upper-terrace community, paid in grain and in the small honor of the office.

A bell-ringer is a person of middle age, usually, with arms strong enough to swing the bell and a wrist supple enough to control it. A bell-ringer climbs the tower in the morning and again at each hour, and rings the bell with a measured stroke — not the urgent clamor of an alarm, but the slower pulse of a city that is keeping time. The bells of the four towers are rung in sequence, the northernmost first, and the sound moves down the ridge, and the sound moves out across the water, and the sound is answered, distantly, from the other drowned ridges of the range.

The bell-ringers know one another. They are a small community within the city, four people, and they meet at the changing of the seasons to coordinate, and they meet at the changing of the bell-ringers to pass the office, and they are, in the quiet way of people who do a necessary thing well, the pulse of the city.

The bells ring at the hours. The bells ring at the death of a person. The bells ring, once a year, at the marking of the highest tide of the year, which is the city's longest day, and the bells ring then in a long continuous note, and the people of the city stop what they are doing and listen, and then the bells stop, and the people go back to what they were doing.

## The Pig-Keepers

In the upper terraces, in the stone pens built against the warmest walls, the small pig-like animal is kept.

The animal is not a pig. It is something else — something with a flatter face, something with a denser coat, something that browses as well as it roots, and that can be fattened on kitchen scraps and on the trimmed vegetation of the middle terraces and on the spent grain from the upper-terrace brewing. The animal is small, shoulder-high to a child, and it makes a low grunting sound that the people of the upper terraces find soothing, in the way that any constant low sound becomes soothing over time.

The pig-keeper is the person who tends the pens. The pig-keeper is, often, a child — an upper-terrace child of ten or twelve, who has been given the pens as a responsibility and who carries it out with the serious attention of a person who has been given something to look after. The pig-keeper feeds the animals in the morning and again in the evening. The pig-keeper cleans the pens. The pig-keeper knows each animal by its face and by its voice, and a pig-keeper will mourn, in the way that children mourn, when an animal is taken to be killed.

The animals are killed rarely. A family that keeps pigs might kill one a year, at the highest-tide festival, and the meat is shared out among the terrace community in careful portions, and the bones are boiled for stock, and the hide is tanned and used for the wraps that the upper-terrace people wear against the wind. The pig-keeper is not present at the killing. The pig-keeper is told afterward, and the pig-keeper is, for a few days, a quieter child than usual.

## The Children of the Stone

There is, in the upper city, a child who knows every terrace wall.

The child has grown up in the upper terraces, has followed the wallers since it could walk, has been given small jobs — carrying a stone, holding a line, watching a wall being built — and has, by the age of ten or twelve, learned to see what the waller sees. The child knows which stones will lie flat. The child knows where the loose scree is and where the bedrock is. The child can stand at the edge of a terrace and see, in the way the wall will need to be rebuilt, the bones of the wall that is there.

The child of the stone is not yet a waller. The child of the stone is an apprentice, and the apprenticeship is long, and the child will not be a full waller for another ten or fifteen years. But the child of the stone is recognized, in the upper terraces, as a child of a particular kind, and the wallers will watch the child with the attention of people looking at their own future.

The children of the stone are quiet. They are watchful. They do not climb well — the upper terraces are not vertical, and an upper-terrace child has not the climbing balance of a middle-terrace child. They are sturdy. They walk the upper paths with the sure-footed confidence of a person who has been walking narrow ledges since they could walk. They will, when they are grown, build the walls that hold the soil that grows the grain that feeds the city.

## The Old Women of the Smokers

There is, on every middle terrace, an old woman.

She is the smoker-keeper. She is the keeper of the gossip. She is the keeper of the small disputes and the small judgments. She is the person to whom the children are sent when they have done something wrong, and the person to whom the adults go when they need a thing decided that cannot be decided by themselves.

The old woman of the smokers is not, technically, in charge of anything. She has no office, no title, no tablet. She is simply the person who has lived the longest on this terrace, and who has tended the smoker the longest, and who therefore knows the most about the people who live here. The old woman is the memory of the terrace. The old woman is the continuity.

The old woman of the smokers is not always liked. The old woman's judgments are sometimes hard. The old woman's memory is sometimes inconvenient. But the old woman is always there, at the smoker, in the morning and in the evening, and the smell of her smoker is the smell of home, and the sound of her voice is the sound of the terrace, and when the old woman dies, the terrace weeps, and a new old woman takes her place, and the smoker goes on burning.

## The Visitors

A person arrives, sometimes, from the lowlands.

The lowlands are the drowned places the city has left behind — the flat coastal plains that the rising sea has covered, the river valleys that have become estuaries, the old farmlands that are now kelp forest. A person from the lowlands arrives in a boat, or on a raft, or swimming, if they are desperate, and they come to the city because the city is what is left.

The visitor is received. The visitor is fed. The visitor is given a place to sleep and a job to do, after a few days, when the visitor has rested. The visitor is not made to feel unwelcome, because the city was built by visitors once, and the city will be built by visitors again, when the upper terraces are themselves drowned and the people of that future have to go somewhere higher.

The visitor stays, or the visitor goes on. The visitor who stays marries into a terrace family, learns the poling or the walling or the smoking, becomes a person of the city in the way that all people of the city have become people of the city. The visitor who goes on goes further up the ridge, or further along the range, to one of the other drowned cities, where the same patient life is being lived against the same patient water.

The visitors are not numerous. The visitors are not common. But the visitors come, and the city takes them in, and the city goes on.

---

And the city, in its slow way, persists.

The polers pole. The smokers smoke. The wallers wall. The children of the drowned grow up into the water. The watchers of the waterline mark the rise. The coral-tenders tend the reef. The climbers climb. The wind-listeners listen. The bell-ringers ring. The pig-keepers tend the small pig-like animals. The children of the stone learn to see. The old women of the smokers keep the memory. The visitors arrive and stay or go on.

The water rises. The coral grows. The plants push into the masonry. The people retreat upward, a story at a time, in the patient rhythm of a city that has decided to last.

The city is loud. The city is slow. The city is green and stone and water and the sound of bells across the water and the smell of smoke and the taste of salt and the hum of insects and the cries of seabirds and the voices of people, talking, always talking, in the carrying voice, to be heard over the rest.

The city lives.
