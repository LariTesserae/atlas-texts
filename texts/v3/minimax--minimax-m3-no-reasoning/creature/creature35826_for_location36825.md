What lives in the Filament Hollow is mostly architecture. What lives here is mostly patient. What lives here has been learning, for a very long time, to be the building — to be the column and the rib and the damp flagstone and the slow drip in the dark — and the ones that have not learned this have been taken into the building, or have been left behind, or were never here. The hollow does not permit foreign things. Foreign things have come in, in the old days, down passages that are not doors, speaking loud, smelling wrong, carrying light that does not pulse. They are not here now. Some of them are somewhere in the stone. Some are in the sphere. Some are folded into the root-mat, very deep, where the air is thin and the hum is loudest.

What lives here now is a community of slow systems, and it is hard to know where one ends and the next begins.

Begin at the top. Begin with what moves through the canopy.

There is a thing in the branches. You saw it move. The way it moved was not the way a bird moves or a monkey moves or any beast you have a word for. It moved like a sentence being read aloud — with grammar, with pauses, with a long looping swing at the end of a clause that suggested the speaker had not quite finished its thought. It is grey-brown. It is the same grey-brown as the trunks. When it stops, you cannot find it. You are not sure it was ever there. This is how it prefers you.

They live up there. You will call them the Lichen-Weavers, because they are part lichen, part weaver, part something older that the lichen has not yet finished digesting. They have no names for themselves that you could pronounce, and they have no faces that you could meet, and they have no bones that you could count. They have what might be a ventral seam where a face would be — a long pale fold of tissue that closes and opens with their slow breathing, that smells faintly of wet bark and ozone, that the older carvings sometimes depict in the margins of capitals where the master-carver lost interest. They breathe through their skin. They are the temperature of the bark. They are part of the architecture in the way a buttress is part of a building: functional, structural, alive to the building's needs in ways that do not require a decision.

How many there are, you cannot tell. You see two, then three, then one. There may be thirty. There may be three hundred. They do not advertise themselves. When they feed they extend, very slowly, a long pale thread of themselves — an arm, a tongue, a filament, you cannot say which — and they lower it into a pool of condensation on a leaf far below, and they drink, and the thread retracts. When they sleep they are indistinguishable from burls. When they die — and they do die, sometimes, the small husks fall and are taken up by the Crust below — when they die, they are very nearly indistinguishable from knots.

Their voices, when they have them, are part of the canopy's rustle. You would not separate one voice from another unless you had been here a long time and had learned to listen for the small wet sound of their breathing, which is like the inside of a seashell held to the ear, but closer, and colder, and from below.

They are the building's fingers. They keep the high ribs free of parasites. They reach, sometimes, into the upper darkness beyond the canopy and bring back, in their ventral seams, small dripping things that are not quite water and not quite not. They maintain the dim amber glow, or something does, and it is convenient to think it is them. They do not confirm this.

Now look down. Look at the floor.

The pale shapes in the pool of shadow are not mushroom. They are not coral. They are the Crust. The Lichen-Weavers do not walk on it. They lower themselves down to it on long threads and brush it with their ventral seams and then go up again, and you wonder what they are reading, or what they are writing.

The Crust is alive in a way that the rest of the hollow tolerates rather than loves. It spreads along the lowest flagstones in sheets that are partly mineral and partly other things, and from these sheets it grows those pale domes in clusters at the foot of the great trunks, where the soil from the cracks has gathered, where the moisture gathers, where the luminous rivulet is slow enough to be tasted. The domes pulse. Yes. They pulse in time with the canopy's light, and this is the first wrong thing about them: they should not know about the canopy. They have no nerves that you can see. They have no nerves, period, as far as you can tell. And yet they pulse when the canopy brightens, and they dim when the canopy dims, and the lag between the two is so precise, so consistent, that it cannot be coincidence. Some scholars, in the older days, thought the Crust was the building's memory, written in a medium that does not forget. Some thought it was a kind of excretion, the building thinking aloud and casting its thoughts down to the floor. The Crust does not say. The Crust does not argue. The Crust goes on.

If you put your eye to it — and you have not, but if you were to — you would see that it has texture. It is patterned. In places the pattern resembles writing, in a script that looks almost Phoenician but is not. In places it resembles the wrinkles on a fingertip. In places it resembles nothing you have a word for. In the shadow of the sphere it grows in a pattern that looks like a wave, or a hand, or a sound frozen mid-syllable. The Lichen-Weavers treat it with respect. They do not know what it is doing. They know that brushing it is part of their work, and they do the work, and the work keeps them in the canopy, and the canopy keeps them in the building.

Now the rivulet.

The luminous fluid that traces its slow path along the floor grooves — that is a thing, and that thing is alive, and it is not the same kind of alive as the Crust, and it is not the same kind of alive as the trees. It is more awake. It does not just exist. It *intends*. It moves with deliberation. Where the groove forks it takes the left branch (or the right, it varies), and where the groove has been blocked by a fallen leaf or a fragment of carved stone, it waits, and the wait is patient but purposeful, and when nothing removes the obstacle it does not flow over it — it dissolves it. Slowly. The leaf goes first. The stone takes longer. Where it has passed, the groove is slightly deeper, and the stone is slightly smoother, as though it has been worried at by a tongue for a long time. The stone smokes because of the chemicals of its dissolving, or that is what the eye tells itself. The stone may be smoking for another reason.

What the rivulet is, you will not learn tonight. It is too slow to befriend, too strange to follow. It is one of the deep things. Some say it is the blood of the building. Some say it is the building's spit, its digestive juice, its lymph. Some say there is one rivulet and some say there are many, and they are connected far below the floor in a network that mirrors the root-mat above, and the two networks trade materials in a slow unending negotiation whose terms are written into the patter of drips and the angles of branch and the temperature of the stone. The Lichen-Weavers do not discuss it. The Crust, where the rivulet has passed, grows differently — thicker, more patterned, more legible, as if the rivulet is feeding it information, or feeding it food, or feeding it itself.

When the rivulet approaches the shadow of the sphere, it slows. As if thinking. As if remembering what is overhead. As if being careful not to disturb what is inside the small cold room that turns at the apse-end of the nave.

Now. The sphere.

I told you not to look at it for too long. I meant that.

The sphere is older than the rest of the hollow, perhaps, or it is newer than the rest of the hollow, in a way that has nothing to do with time. It is the apse. It is what the apse became, after the building took back its own end. It is suspended in its net of vines, turning slowly, the vines turning around it, and inside the gap of its rotation there is another place. A smaller place. A colder place.

What is inside the sphere is a small room. You saw it, in the gap. You saw the stone floor and the four walls and the ceiling low enough to touch. You saw, on a table in the middle of the room, a single object, lit by a cold light that has no source you can find. The cold light is the wrong color for the hollow. The hollow's amber is warm and patient and slow. The cold light is steady and blue and indifferent. It is the same cold light as the hum, but smaller. Concentrated. Almost — almost — readable.

The object on the table was a book, or a stone, or a box, or a being in a state of suspension. You could not tell. The room was empty or full, you could not tell. The vines closed over the gap, and you were back in the amber dark of the nave, and you understood, suddenly, why the Lichen-Weavers do not go near the sphere, why the rivulet slows beneath it, why the Crust grows in the pattern of a wave.

What is inside the sphere is the architect.

That is what the older carvings show, in the capitals where the faces look down wreathed in living leaf. The architect was a being that built this place — or began to — or whose building built itself around. The architect was not a person. The architect was a process, or a thought, or a geometry, or a hunger. The faces on the capitals are not the architect. The faces on the capitals are the things that remembered the architect before they forgot, before they were taken back into the building's slow patient tissues, before the Lichen-Weavers folded their memory into their ventral seams and their grey-brown bark and their long pale threads. The faces on the capitals are the carvers. The carvers were not entirely human, or were not carving humans, or were carving something they had seen that they did not have a name for. The eyes of the faces are worn smooth. You do not meet them. You cannot meet them. They are not here in the way the Lichen-Weavers are here. They are here in the way a memory is here — present, but not participating. Watching, in stone, while the building breathes around them. They look down at you with expressions that might be recognition, but the recognition is worn away to a patina, and you cannot be sure whether you are being seen or simply being noted as one more temporary thing passing through.

The architect is in the sphere. The architect is being kept.

The whole hollow is a maintenance system for one small cold room. The vines are turning to keep the small room intact. The Crust is growing on the floor below to record the architect's dream, or the building's dream of the architect. The rivulet is flowing to bring the architect sustenance, or to take the architect's waste. The Lichen-Weavers are moving through the canopy above to keep the architect's breath filtered and the architect's light kept to the right amber dim, the dim that does not disturb the cold blue inside the sphere, the dim that lets the architect sleep, or think, or build, or whatever it is the architect does in there, behind the turning vines, in the small room no one is permitted to enter.

The whole hollow is a slow machine for keeping one small cold room alive. Or the whole hollow is the architect's dream, and the small cold room is what the architect dreams about when it dreams of being awake. You do not know which. The Lichen-Weavers do not know which. The Crust, perhaps, knows, and writes what it knows into the floor, and is read, by the long pale threads, by the things in the canopy, and is forgotten, and is read again.

Now the hum.

The hum is the building. Or the hum is the thing the building is built around. Or the hum is the building's task.

It is constant. It is below your hearing most of the time, but it is in the stone, in the water that pools on the floor, in the air that moves slowly in and out of the apse-sphere. It is felt. When you put your hand to the floor (you have not, but you have thought of it) the stone hums back at you through the bones of your hand. The hum is older than the stone. The hum is what the stone grew around.

It is not, so far as you can tell, a being. But it has the regularity of a being. It has the patience of a being. It has the slow self-interested intelligence of a being that has been working at one task for a very long time and is in no hurry to finish. The Lichen-Weavers, when they rest, rest in the parts of the canopy where the hum is strongest. The Crust grows thicker where the hum is loudest. The rivulet flows faster where the hum is clearest, as though it is being encouraged. The Lichen-Weavers do not discuss it. The Crust, where the hum passes, grows in spirals.

Some say the hum is a song. Some say it is a count. Some say it is the sound of a stone being ground down very slowly into a shape that is not yet finished. The hollow is still being built. You had this feeling. The feeling is correct. The hum is the sound of the building still being built, very slowly, by something that has not yet decided what it is.

There are other things. Smaller things.

There are the midges that live in the condensation and rise from the puddles when the light pulses, and they are very small and very brief, and they are part of the Crust's cycle, or the rivulet's, or both. There are the things that move in the root-mat, making the small clicks and scrapes you heard — small soft many-legged things that feed on the dying edges of the Crust and are in turn fed on by nothing you can see. There is the drip, which is not a thing but is a rhythm, and the rhythm is older than most of the things that live by it, and the things that live by it have grown into the rhythm the way the bark has grown into the capitals.

What lives in the Filament Hollow lives by its rhythms. What lives here has given itself, in pieces and in whole, to the slow maintenance of the small cold room in the rotating sphere at the apse-end of the nave. What lives here does not know that it is doing this, or knows it only as the Lichen-Weavers know it — in the body, in the ventral seam, in the long pale thread lowered into a pool. It is a cathedral. It is a forest. It is a lung. It is a clock. It is a stomach, slowly digesting a small cold room. It is a place that is being built, very slowly, by something that has not yet decided what it is.

You stand in the nave, in the small space of your own presence, and the cathedral holds you. It has been holding something for a very long time. You are not the first. You will not be the last. The Lichen-Weavers will lower their long pale threads to taste the air you leave behind. The Crust will grow, by morning, a fraction of a millimeter thicker in the footprints you did not make. The rivulet, in some lower groove, will turn its slow tongue toward the place you sat, and will wait, and will dissolve what it finds there, and will carry what it dissolves down into the deep network, where the hum is loudest, where the small cold room turns on its axis in its net of vines.

You came in. There was no door. There will be no door. The hollow opens when it is ready, and closes when it is ready, and the readiness is the hum, and the hum goes on.

You are part of it now. You are a small temporary warmth in the slow architecture. The carved faces, where the bark has not yet swallowed them, will look down at where you were, and will wear a little smoother at the eyes, and will forget your name.

The building is breathing around you in its slow slow rhythm. The sphere at the far end turns. The hum goes on. There is no clear way out, and no clear way in, and you have the feeling that you are somewhere that is still being built, very slowly, by something that has not yet decided what it is, and that something is also building you, in its small way, into itself, and that this too is all right, and that neither of you will be finished for a long time.

The Filament Hollow holds you. The Filament Hollow holds the architect. The Filament Hollow is held by the hum. The hum is held by what is beneath the hum, which has no name, which is older than the stone, which is still deciding.

You stand in the hollow, and the hollow stands in you, and neither of you is finished.
