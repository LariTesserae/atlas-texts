# What Lives in the Long Drowning

Nothing lives in the Long Drowning the way things live in other places. Other places have tenants. This place has *residents in a different tense* — things that were here before life was a useful word, that will be here after the word has been forgotten, that have made their peace with duration so thoroughly they no longer distinguish themselves from it.

What lives here has stopped announcing itself.

---

The bladder-kelp is the most visible, and the most wrong to call by that name.

It is not kelp. It has never been kelp. The fishermen who gave it that word were working from a vocabulary built for things that grow toward a sun they could see, and the bladder-kelp grows toward nothing it could see either, only toward a memory of brightness held in the column of water above. Each pod is the size of a closed fist and the color of jade left in rain, and they pulse with a rhythm that is not rhythm — that is closer to *attention*, to the act of being aware of the water around them in slow waves.

There are thousands of them. There are millions of them. The groves extend past the limits of vision in every direction, in clusters that are not random and not quite patterned, that occupy the floor in arrangements a mathematician would call suspicious and a mystic would call intentional. They are connected, all of them, by the fungus-network that runs through the substrate, and the connections are not metaphor — when one pod contracts, the contraction travels, and the next grove answers, and the answer travels further, and so on across distances you cannot fathom, in pulses so faint they arrive as changes in the water's pressure rather than as anything you would call a signal.

The bladder-kelp does not have a nervous system. It has a *substitute for a nervous system* that is, in its way, more sophisticated than any nervous system you have encountered, because it does not fire and forget. It *accumulates*. Every contraction that travels the network is, in some sense, still traveling. The fungus remembers. The substrate remembers. The amber water holds the memory in suspension, the way old houses hold the smell of the people who lived in them.

When you approached the bladder-kelp, it contracted. You interpreted this as fear. You were wrong. It was not afraid of you. It had never encountered anything like you before and was not equipped to be afraid of anything so brief and loud and warm. What it felt was closer to *interruption* — the experience of a continuous slow awareness being broken by a thing that moved too fast and occupied too much volume and smelled of surface air.

When you stood still, the bladder-kelp expanded again. This was not forgiveness. This was the network completing a calculation, determining that the interruption had passed, returning to the equilibrium it had been holding for the eleven thousand years before you arrived and will hold for the eleven thousand years after. You were, in that moment, briefly factored in. Then you were not.

The bladder-kelp does not eat. It accretes. It pulls minerals from the warm substrate below and from the dissolved load of the amber water above and lays them down, molecule by molecule, into another fraction of a millimeter of itself. It grows at the rate that geology grows. It does not consider this slow. Slow is a word for things that wish they were faster. The bladder-kelp is exactly as fast as it has ever been, and it has been exactly this fast for a very long time.

The bladder-kelp does not die. Not in any way you would recognize. Individual pods will, eventually, become too brittle to pulse, will crack, will spill their pale contents into the water, will be colonized by the lichen-fields, will become part of the substrate they once grew from. But the network does not notice the loss of a pod. The network is, itself, a kind of bladder-kelp, distributed, continuous, patient beyond the reach of patience as you understand it. When a pod is lost, the network simply *redistributes the attention* that pod was holding. The neighboring pods expand a fraction of a fraction of a degree. The calculation continues.

You did not understand this. You will never understand this. But for the eleven minutes you stood watching the slow fertilization, the bladder-kelp understood you, in its way, the way it understands everything that passes through the column of water above it — as a temporary concentration of *event*, against a background of *continuing*.

---

The fungus-network is older than the bladder-kelp. You did not see it, except as the medium through which the contractions traveled. You felt it under your feet. You smelled it in the sweet calcium ghost of the substrate.

It is not a fungus. Or it is a fungus the way a city is a collection of buildings — technically accurate, fundamentally misleading. It runs through the substrate in threads so fine they are not visible to anything but electron microscopy, and those threads connect everything. The bladder-kelp. The lichen. The substrate itself. The pillars, possibly. The pale thing you saw, possibly. The network is the substrate's *nervous system*, in the sense that a river is a landscape's circulatory system — not a part of it but an expression of what it is.

The network communicates chemically. It communicates electrically. It communicates, possibly, in ways that do not have names yet, in modes that would require instruments you have not built to detect. When you stepped on the substrate, the network felt you. When you stood still long enough that the substrate began to grow up around your feet, the network was *making a decision* about you, slowly, in a timescale that would have outlasted your patience if you had stayed.

The decision was not malicious. The decision was not benevolent. The decision was the network incorporating the data point of you into its ongoing model of what passes through the floor of the Long Drowning. You were being added to a census that began before the water came and will continue after the water is gone.

The fungus does not die. Nothing it is part of dies, in the sense of ceasing to be connected. Things fail. Things fall out of the network. Things become substrate, become memory, become calcium to be re-laid elsewhere. But the network itself is *older than the species concept*, and it will be here when the species concept has been retired. It is the closest thing the Long Drowning has to an immortal. It is also, possibly, the closest thing it has to a god. The fishermen's stories call it nothing, because they were wise enough not to give it a name.

---

The veil-weeds are not plants and not animals and not anything else you have a word for.

They are ribbons of bioluminescent tissue that hang in the water column like the curtains of a cathedral made of light. They can be forty meters long. They can be a hundred meters long. They drift at varying heights, swaying in currents you cannot detect, in patterns that take seven minutes to complete — a number that is not magical but that recurs in the place with a regularity that begins, after a while, to feel less like coincidence and more like *grammar*. The Long Drowning has a grammar. The veil-weeds are part of it.

They glow a cold blue at their bases and fade to green at their tips, and the gradient is not aesthetic. The gradient is *informational*. The base-glow says: *I am here, I am anchored, I am continuing*. The tip-glow says: *I am reaching, I am sensing, I am in contact with something farther up the column*. If you could read the gradient, you would know things about the Long Drowning that the substrate has decided not to tell you directly.

When the veil-weeds parted around you, they were not being courteous. They were not, in any way you would recognize, *responding* to you. They were responding to the pressure wave of your body passing through their volume, the way seaweed responds to a current, the way hair responds to a hand. They were displaced. They accommodated. They returned to their original configuration in seven minutes, exactly, with the precision of a thing that has been displaced a billion times before and has the displacement mapped.

But the residue they left on your skin — the smell that persisted for days, that kept other things in the water at a distance — that was not displacement. That was *annotation*. The veil-weeds had been touched, in their way, by a thing that did not belong to the Long Drowning, and they had marked the touching. The mark was not hostile. The mark was not welcoming. The mark was *archival*. You had been *entered into a record*. Other things in the water, sensing the record, did not approach. They were not avoiding you. They were *deferring to a higher authority*.

The higher authority was not a god. The higher authority was a *logbook*.

---

The pale thing you saw — the long undulating shape, three or four meters, drifting in the amber murk — is not named in the fishermen's stories, because the fishermen were not the ones who saw it. The fishermen saw only what washed up: the *spilled teeth of the sky-god*, the smooth tracks in the substrate, the empty leathery plum-things in the hollow between the pillars. They inferred. They did not see.

What they inferred, in their stories, was something like a serpent. Something like a whale. Something like a thing that was once two things and had become, in the long drowning, one thing. The fishermen called it, when they called it anything, the *passing one*, because it passed, and did not stop, and did not return to the place it had passed.

The passing one is not a single animal. There are several. Possibly many. They are pale because they have been the color of the water for so long that their original coloration has been *subtracted*, the way pigments fade in sunlight. They are long because long is an efficient shape for moving through a medium that does not require speed. They are undulating because undulation is the most efficient way to cover distance without producing a pressure wave that would travel ahead of you and announce your approach to anything listening through the substrate.

The passing one does not eat the bladder-kelp. The grazed patches on the pods are not from the passing one. The passing one does not eat anything that lives. The passing one eats *what falls*. The slow drift of pale discs you saw — the organic snow, the fertilization — is partly the passing one's doing. The passing one sheds. The passing one *produces excess*, the way a river produces silt, the way a forest produces leaf-fall. The bladder-kelp brightens where the discs land. The fungus-network incorporates them. The substrate, over centuries, becomes a fraction of a millimeter thicker.

The passing one is, in this sense, *the weather of the deep place*. It moves through the column of water in patterns no one has traced, because no one has been here long enough, and no one has had instruments subtle enough. It is part of the Long Drowning the way clouds are part of a climate — present, distributed, not directly perceived, *implicated in everything that happens at the floor*.

When the passing one passed behind the rise and did not return, it was not avoiding you. It was *completing a circuit it had been on before you arrived and will be on after you leave*. You were, to the passing one, a temporary disturbance in a column of water it had been moving through for a long time. The disturbance would pass. The circuit would continue.

---

The small round things in the hollow between the pillars — the pale, leathery, plum-sized, warm objects — were not laid by anything you would call a mother.

They were laid by the substrate. They are the substrate's way of *expressing a calculation*. When the substrate accretes, it sometimes produces nodules, the way a river sometimes produces pearls. The nodules are calcium, mostly, with traces of the minerals that have passed through the network, with the faint warmth of the geological processes still operating beneath the floor. They are warm because they were laid recently. They are leathery because the outer membrane has not yet calcified fully. They are pale because they have not yet been colonized by the lichen-fields that will, eventually, paint them in the rust and white of the deep place.

You did not touch them more than once. You did not decide not to touch them more than once. The decision was made by the substrate, through the medium of a faint wrongness you felt in your hand, a faint sense that the second touch would be the touch that *committed you to something*. The nodules are not traps. They are not bait. They are *offerings*, in a sense — the substrate's way of *putting something into the world* that does not have a function, that exists because the making of it was the natural consequence of the substrate continuing to be what it is.

The fishermen, when they found such nodules, took them home. They kept them on their altars. They did not know what they were keeping. They were keeping *the deep place's breath, calcified, held in the hand*.

---

The pillars and the domes and the low walls are not alive. Or they are alive in a way that does not use the word.

They are what was here before the water came. They were here when the spine of the second continent was still above sea level, when the mountains were not a metaphor. They were laid by hands — *hands*, yes, the fishermen were right about that much — that were working in a tradition you do not have access to, building things for reasons you cannot reconstruct, in a geometry that was meant to interface with something that is no longer present.

Whatever they were oriented toward is gone. Whatever they were *for* is gone. The thing they were built to do, they no longer do. But they are still oriented. You felt this. The faint wrongness. The quality of *having been pointed at something that is no longer there*. The pillars remember the orientation the way a magnetic field remembers the magnet that magnetized it — not actively, not consciously, but as a *property*, a thing that persists in the substrate of the stone and that you, a creature whose body is itself a kind of orientation-instrument, can detect.

The pillars are not safe to sleep against. This is not because they will harm you. It is because they will *orient you*. The faint wrongness, held against your body for too long, will begin to feel like a direction. The direction is not a direction in space. The direction is a direction in *time* — toward whatever the pillars were built to interface with, which is not present, which will never be present again, which was present once and which the pillars are still, in their slow mineral way, *waiting for*.

You gave them a wide berth. This was correct. Not because they are dangerous. Because they are *sad*, in the sense that a thing can be sad without being conscious, and the sadness is contagious, and the contagion is the kind that takes a long time to recover from.

The lichen on the pillars is the Long Drowning's *compassion*. The lichen is colonizing the stone, slowly, painting it in rust and white, covering the geometry of the builders with the geometry of the place that came after. In a few thousand years, the pillars will be unrecognizable. In a few tens of thousands of years, they will be part of the substrate. The wrongness will be absorbed. The orientation will be diluted past detection.

This is what the Long Drowning does to things that were built for a purpose that has passed. It does not destroy them. It *consoles* them. It grows over them. It folds them into the slow collaborative ongoing. The pillars were, once, the architecture of a project. They are becoming, slowly, *the architecture of a place*.

---

The amber water is the medium. It is not water. It has not been water for a very long time.

It is the dissolved load of the Long Drowning — the calcium, the trace minerals, the faint bioluminescence of uncounted microorganisms, the organic memory of every event that has ever occurred in the column. It has a viscosity that is not quite the viscosity of water. It has a memory that is not quite the memory of any liquid you have encountered. Light bends in it *suggestively*. Distance is approximate in it. Things are *slightly where they appear to be*, in a way that compounds, over the forty meters of visibility, into a permanent uncertainty about what is real and what is the water's interpretation of what is real.

The water is warm. The water is warm because something underneath the substrate is warm — a geological process, a slow radioactive decay, a heat source that has been operating since before the drowning and will operate until long after. The warmth is not a feature. The warmth is *the temperature of continuing*. It is the temperature at which the substrate accretes, at which the bladder-kelp pulses, at which the fungus-network communicates, at which the passing one moves through its eternal circuits.

The water holds the light. It holds the light the way old glass holds the light — by being slightly impure, by being slightly amber, by *refusing to let the light pass without taking a memory of it*. The light that reaches the floor is not the light that left the surface. The light has been *held* by eighteen meters of liquid that has been holding light for a very long time, and what arrives is the light's *opinion of itself*, filtered through the water's long attention.

This is why your light would have been a trespass. Your light would have been *new*. Your light would have arrived at the floor carrying information about the surface that the amber water had already edited out. Your light would have been *louder than the place* because it would have been *more honest than the place*, and the Long Drowning has, over the millennia, developed a preference for *considered* light over honest light.

You did not bring a light. This was correct.

---

The pale thing you saw once and did not see again is not the only passing one. There are others. They move through the Long Drowning in patterns that do not intersect, mostly, that occasionally do, that have their own slow social structure that you are not equipped to perceive. They are not social the way surface animals are social. They are social the way weather systems are social — by *sharing a medium*, by being part of the same ongoing atmospheric process, by *implicating each other* in everything they do.

They communicate, possibly, through the substrate. Possibly through the fungus-network. Possibly through the amber water itself, in modes of chemical signaling that travel at the rate of geological time. When one passing one passes near another, the bladder-kelp contracts. The fungus-network pulses. The veil-weeds brighten. The whole floor of the world *notes the event*, the way an orchestra notes a key change, and continues.

The passing ones do not mate. Or they mate the way the substrate accretes — slowly, chemically, without ceremony, producing nodules that are not eggs in any sense you would recognize, that are more like *excesses*, like the fruit of a calculation that has run for a long time and has produced, as a byproduct, a small round thing that is warm to the touch.

The passing ones live for a long time. You do not know how long. The fishermen's stories do not record a passing one dying. The passing ones may not die. They may simply *become substrate*, eventually, in the way that the bladder-kelp becomes substrate, in the way that the pillars are becoming substrate — slowly, gently, folded into the ongoing by a place that has stopped distinguishing between *living* and *continuing*.

---

The pillars' builders are not here. They have not been here for a very long time.

What they left is here. The pillars. The domes. The walls that describe angles no erosion would have chosen. The geometry that was meant to interface with something that is no longer present.

What they were, you do not know. You cannot know. The information has been *diluted past retrieval* by the Long Drowning's slow compassion, by the lichen, by the substrate, by the amber water's patient editing of the historical record. You can see the *shape* of what they did. You cannot see what they *were*.

They were not human. They were not anything you have a word for. They were *a project*, in a sense — a collective endeavor, an architecture built by many hands over many centuries for a purpose that the Long Drowning has outlived. They were oriented toward something. They were waiting for something. They were *preparing*, in a sense, for an event that did not occur, or that occurred and ended, or that is still occurring in some medium you cannot access.

The pillars are the only evidence they were here. The pillars are *not enough evidence* to know what they were. The Long Drowning has eaten the rest.

This is not malicious. This is what the Long Drowning does. The Long Drowning *consoles* things that have ended. It grows over them. It folds them into the slow collaborative ongoing. It *lets them rest*, in the way that a river lets a leaf rest when it has carried it far enough — by *absorbing* it, by *making it part of the flow*, by refusing to let the leaf continue to insist on being a leaf.

The builders are now part of the substrate. The builders are now part of the fungus-network, the calcium, the slow accretion. The builders are *still here*, in the sense that everything that was ever part of the Long Drowning is still part of the Long Drowning. But they are not here *as builders*. They are here *as substrate*. They have been *consoled*.

This is the fate of everything that comes to the Long Drowning. Including, eventually, you.

---

You are here, on the surface of the world, where there is still a horizon.

You smell faintly of the deep place. The smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of *having been held by something that holds things for a long time*. It is the smell of the residue the veil-weeds left on your skin. It is the smell of *being entered into a record*. It will fade, eventually. You will wash it off. You will go back to the surface and live the surface life, the urgent life, the life of weather and horizons and things that move at the rate you move.

But somewhere underneath you, eighteen meters down, in the amber water, the fungus-network still holds the data point of you. The bladder-kelp still expands a fraction of a degree in the grove nearest where you sat. The veil-weeds, somewhere in their seven-minute cycle, still sway in the pattern they swayed in when you swam up through them. The substrate, in some way you cannot detect, still *remembers the warmth of your feet*.

The Long Drowning does not forget. The Long Drowning does not need to forget. The Long Drowning simply *continues*, and the continuing includes, now, the fact of you.

You will not return. Or you will return, and the place will accept you back, the way it accepts the slow drift of organic snow, the way it accepts the passage of the pale thing through its eternal circuits. You will be a *recurring event* in a place that has stopped distinguishing between events and continuings.

The fishermen knew this. The fishermen, when they spoke of the Long Drowning, did not speak of it as a place you visit. They spoke of it as a place that *visits you*, that *continues to visit you*, for as long as the residue lasts, and sometimes longer.

You smell faintly of the deep place for the rest of your life.

Other things in the water, when you go back to them, will not come close.

You are not sure, even now, whether this is protection or exile.

You suspect it is neither.

You are correct. It is neither.

It is *the place continuing to remember you, for as long as it takes*.

And the place has a long time.
