# The Inhabitants of the Upper Reach

The valley is not empty. It only appears empty to the kind of attention that was trained somewhere else. What lives here has been written into the place for a long time, and most of it does not introduce itself. You have to learn to read a surface the way you'd read a page — to see the script of cyanobacteria on a gneiss face and know it for what it is, to find the vole by the shape of a tussock it has chewed, to hear a wheatear in the turbine song and pick its phrase out of the wash. The valley keeps its tenants. It does not advertise them.

## The Crust

The first inhabitants are not inhabitants at all, in the usual sense. They are the ones who came before anyone who could be called alive, in any sense a child would use the word.

They are the lichens of the rock crust — *Rhizocarpon* in its pale yellowish maps, *Lecidea* in its black-and-grey freckling, the orange *Xanthoria* staining the bird-perch stones where the wheatears have been sitting for generations, leaving their guano to be mined. They are slow beyond any animal's patience, growing fractions of a millimeter a year, and they have been at work on these particular boulders since before the station was a line on a surveyor's map. Some of the colonies on the highest erratics are older than the machines that hum nearby, older than the cables that go into the rock, older than the men who tightened the bolts. They were here when the first engineer walked the valley with a theodolite and decided where the intake would sit. They will be here when the last engineer walks it the other way.

They do not move. They are not, in any ordinary sense, individuals. A single *Rhizocarpon* thallus is a city: a fungus housing an alga housing a yeast, sometimes a third partner, the whole federation holding its patch of stone and patiently dissolving it, molecule by molecule, into the beginnings of soil. The pale orange stain on the gneiss is not a stain. It is a digestion in progress, very slow, very thorough, the rock being eaten by something that intends to make a mountain out of what it ate.

In the morning, when the light comes in flat from the east and the frost has not yet lifted, the crust lichens are the only color in the valley that is not water. They are the writing on the page the valley is making of itself. The valley has been writing on itself, with these particular instruments, for a long time.

## The Mat

Above the crust, the mosses. The *Racomitrium* in its harsh cinnamon carpets, tough as wire, dry on top and damp beneath, with the special structure of it — the way each hair tip is hyaline, clear, a tiny lens that focuses the sun onto the green cell below. *Polytrichum* in deeper, taller turfs where the snow lies longer. *Sphagnum* in the wetter places, pale, almost cream-colored, holding many times its own weight in water, building itself into hummocks that will, in a few centuries, become the foundations of actual peat.

The mosses are the valley's second skin. They are what you are actually standing on, almost everywhere you stand. They are the floor of the place, and they are alive, and the fact that you do not feel them under your boots is only because the boots are too thick. The valley floor is, top to bottom, a single slow organism in some places, with its surface made of *Cetraria* and its middle made of *Hylocomium* and its lower made of *Sphagnum* and its underside made of peat, and the whole thing, when you look at it as a whole, is a kind of creature. It eats rock. It eats water. It eats light. It does not, in any meaningful sense, die; it just slowly thickens, and the dead lower parts become the foundation of the living upper parts, and so on.

The dwarf willows live in this mat as their substrate, not as their soil — *Salix herbacea* with its little coin-leaves barely rising above the moss, *Salix reticulata* with its veined leaves and its catkins no bigger than a child's finger, both of them woody only at the root, both of them creeping. They are trees that have decided, with great deliberation, not to be trees. They are willows that have been folded down to the ground by the wind until they have become, in effect, more ground. They flower in early summer, when the snow first pulls back, and the valley goes briefly white with their catkins, and then the catkins set seed in the cotton-grass wind, and by August the willows are just a slightly greener patch in the mat, and you have to look hard to see them.

The *Dryas* is similar but more assertive. It forms mats that are mat-forming; that is its whole strategy. It advances. It kills the moss beneath it by shading it out, and it occupies the space, and from that occupied space it sends out new rosettes, and they in turn advance, and the mat grows outward in rings, a few centimeters a year. The white flowers come in early summer, and they are not white the way a daisy is white but white the way paper is white, with eight petals (almost always eight; the species name is *octopetala*) and a yellow center, and they turn, in the long days, to follow the sun. A *Dryas* mat is a slow green animal, eating the valley one outward step at a time.

And among all of it — in the moss, in the *Dryas*, in the sedge tussocks — the cyanobacteria and the algae, working in the wet places, drawing nitrogen from the air and the rock and feeding it, in some forms fixed and in some forms not, into the rest of the federation. The valley's green is not, in any chemical sense, free. It is paid for, molecule by molecule, by the bacteria that figured out, very early, how to break the triple bond.

## The Pollinators

The insects are here, but not many, and you have to look for them.

The bumblebees are the most visible. *Bombus polaris*, the arctic bumble, and one or two of its cousins, moving low over the *Dryas* and the willow catkins, the saxifrage and the gentian, in the brief window of the bloom. They are very large for what they are, and very furry, and they fly in a way that looks effortful in the thin air, as if each wingbeat is being argued with. They are the only reliable pollinators at this elevation, and the plants have shaped themselves around them — shallow flowers, broad landing platforms, sugars in unusual concentration. The bees are the reason the *Dryas* is white. The bees are the reason the gentian is so blue it looks almost luminous. The valley's colors are, in a quiet sense, advertisements.

The flies are more numerous and less seen. There are dance flies on the *Dryas*, hovering. There aremos midges, hatching by the kettle holes in pale clouds that look, from a distance, like weather. There are the small parasitic wasps whose adult lives last about a day and whose larvae spend the rest of their time inside other insects, eating them carefully so as not to kill them too quickly. There are beetles, dark and small, moving through the moss. There are springtails in such numbers in the peat that the surface of any wet cushion, if you put a white card down on it for ten minutes, will be freckled with them when you pick it up again.

And the butterflies. There is always, in summer, a *Boloria* — the arctic fritillary — riding the wind along the valley, dipping occasionally to a flower, never landing for long, and there is, in the better years, an *Erebia* — the mountain ringlet — which is darker and which sits on stones with its wings closed and looks, when it does, like a piece of bark. They are not common. They are, in some years, almost absent. The valley is at the edge of their range and they come here as a kind of reconnaissance, to see if the conditions are still right, and if they are, they lay their eggs on the violet leaves near the boggy places, and the caterpillars overwinter under the snow, and the next summer there are more of them, or fewer, depending.

## The Birds

The wheatears are the valley's songbirds. A pair, sometimes two pairs, nesting in the stonework of the station — in a gap between the concrete blocks, in a crevice in the tailrace wall. The male is the one with the slate back and the black mask and the white rump, conspicuous in flight, almost invisible when he lands on a grey stone. He sings from the top of a transformer or a fence post, a phrase that is a quick clacking rattle followed by a clear note, repeated. He sings loud. He has to. The turbines are running, and the turbines are not going to stop, so the wheatear has raised his output to a frequency band that gets through.

He is feeding, in the brief summer, on insects — the bumblebees are too big, but the flies are his, and the small beetles, and the caterpillars he finds in the *Dryas* mat. The chicks, in the nest in the wall, get a steady stream of these, delivered by both parents, and they fledge, in late July, into a world that is still cold and still loud and still mostly not for them, and within a few weeks they are gone. The wheatear is a visitor. The valley is his summer. The rest of his year is spent in sub-Saharan Africa, on a different continent, in a different climate, eating different insects, and he is one of the most remarkable migrants on Earth, but he does not advertise this. He just turns up, one May, in the same gap in the same wall, and sings his song above the turbines.

The snow buntings are less committed. They come in small flocks in early summer, dropping out of the sky into the *Dryas* mats, and they feed on seeds — the willow catkin seeds, the *Dryas* seeds, the cotton-grass seeds, the seeds of the *Saxifraga oppositifolia* with its magenta flowers and its tiny capsule. They are white and chestnut and they move in a quick, rolling flight, low over the ground, and when they land they are almost invisible against the pale stones. The flock rises as one when you walk through, a soft whirring, and they circle and resettle fifty meters away. They nest in the boulder fields, in crevices, in the spaces between erratics. By August they are gone too.

The ptarmigan, if you are lucky, you will not see at all. They are here. They live in the boulder fields and on the upper slopes, year-round, eating willow buds and *Dryas* leaves and the occasional insect, molting from white to brown in the summer and from brown to white in the autumn, so that they are cryptic in every season. They are heavy-bodied, almost grouse-like, with feathered feet. They make a sound like a hoarse croak when they are flushed, and then they are gone, low over the rocks, and you will not find them again. They have been here through the ice ages. They were here before the ice. They will be here after the machines.

There is, occasionally, a ring ouzel, a large thrush with a pale breast, that nests in the cliffs and feeds on the insects it finds on the highest rocks. It is a bird of the high places in the lower latitudes, and at this elevation it is at the very edge of its range, and you might see one in a year, or you might not.

And the falcons. The gyrfalcon is here, sometimes, and the peregrine, and the merlin, and they are the reason the wheatear is quiet when he should be loud, and the reason the snow bunting flock rises as one, and the reason the ptarmigan does not want to be found. They are the upper limit of the food chain. They take the bunting, the wheatear, the ptarmigan, and occasionally — though rarely at this elevation — the small mammals. They are not common. They are not resident. They are, like the weather, a kind of punctuation.

## The Mammals

The vole is the most numerous mammal and the least seen. It is a root vole, or a tundra vole, or one of its near cousins, and it lives in the moss, in a network of tunnels and runs that you can only find by the shape of the tussocks it has clipped and the droppings it has left on the stones. It is small and brown and it eats grass and *Dryas* leaves and the seeds of the sedges, and it breeds in the brief summer, and it is eaten, in its turn, by the vole-eating birds and by the foxes and by the weasels that hunt in the boulder fields. It is the reason the weasel is here. The valley's mammal food chain, such as it is, is mostly voles all the way down.

The fox is the largest mammal that lives here full-time. The arctic fox, in the white coat in winter and the brownish-grey coat in summer, with its small ears and its bushy tail and its neat black footpads. It hunts the voles by sound, standing still on the moss and then pouncing, head first, into the snow or the tussock, with a precision that is almost mathematical. It eats the ptarmigan when it can catch one. It eats the snow bunting chicks from the nests in the boulders. It eats the dead fish from the tailrace, in the spring, when the smolts go through. It is everywhere, and nowhere, and you might see one moving along a moraine ridge at a fast trot, or you might not see one at all, and in either case it has seen you.

The weasel is smaller and rarer. A least weasel, perhaps, in its brown summer coat, hunting the voles in their tunnels, or a stoat, which is the same animal only bigger and with a black tip to its tail. It is long and thin and it goes into places the fox cannot, and it is, in its own small way, perfectly built. You will almost never see one. You will know it is there by the panic of the voles.

The reindeer are not here, though their signs are — the old antlers, weathered, in the boulder fields; the clipped *Dryas* on the high ridges; the droppings, very old, in the protected places. The valley is at the edge of their range in the summer, and they pass through, sometimes, on their way to the higher ground, and they graze the *Dryas* and the willow, and they leave, and they are not here.

The lemmings are cyclical. In some years there are none. In some years the valley is thick with them, and the owls — there are no owls, but the weasels and the foxes — get fat, and the population crashes, and the cycle resets. The valley is part of that cycle. The valley is, in a quiet sense, what it is because the lemmings are what they are.

The lemmings are not, contrary to the old stories, given to mass suicide. They are given to mass population fluctuations, which is a different thing, and the foxes get fat and then thin, and the plants get a year or two of not being eaten, and then the cycle turns again.

## The Men

The men — and they are men, mostly, and a few women, and the gendering of the shift work is changing, slowly — live inside the station. They are not, in any biological sense, inhabitants of the valley. They are transients. They come up on the funicular in shifts, in cars the size of small rooms, clanking slowly up the mountainside, and they work for two weeks and then they go down again, on the same funicular, and they are replaced.

Inside the station, they are the only warm-blooded things. The building is heated by the waste heat of the turbines, and the dormitory is at the back, and the kitchen is where the food they brought with them is reheated, and the control room is where they sit in front of the screens and the gauges, watching the load, watching the flow, watching the voltage, watching the machines do what the machines do. They read books they brought with them. They talk to each other in voices that are, necessarily, raised above the turbine song. They sleep in rooms that vibrate, gently, all night.

The men do not, in the usual sense, belong to the valley. They are, in ecological terms, an absence — they do not eat the plants, they do not pollinate the flowers, they do not disperse the seeds, they do not cycle the nitrogen in any useful way. They are, like the rocks, a feature of the substrate. They are part of what the moss is growing on.

But they have been here, in some form, for longer than the oldest moss currently growing on the foundations. The station has been running, in some form, for the better part of a century. The foundations are buried in the peat. The lichen has colonized the metal of the transformers, in pale grey crusts that have been growing for decades. The *Dryas* mat outside the dormitory door has advanced, in the time the men have been here, a measurable distance across the gravel. The men are, in this sense, a slow feature of the landscape. They are part of what the valley is. They are not going to be the part that is remembered, by the valley, when they are gone. The valley will go on. The turbines will go on, for a while, and then they will stop, and the moss will cover the foundations, and the *Dryas* will advance across the concrete, and the cyanobacteria will begin the slow work of dissolving the penstock steel, and the wheatears will continue to nest in the cracks, and the ptarmigan will continue to not be found.

## The Quiet Things

The place is sparse. This is the deepest fact about who lives here, deeper than the turbines, deeper than the men, deeper than the visible green.

The biomass is low. The number of species is low. The number of individuals of any one species is low. The energy flowing through the system is low, because the sun is bright but short and the growing season is brief and the soil is thin and the water is cold. The valley is not a forest. It is not a grassland. It is not even, in the usual sense, a tundra. It is a thin, persistent, almost geological living skin, draped over the bones of a valley that is still being uncovered by a retreating ice.

The things that live here live slowly. The lichens grow fractions of a millimeter a year. The *Dryas* advances a few centimeters a year. The moss thickens by a centimeter or two a year. The voles breed in a brief window in the summer and then are eaten. The wheatear flies to Africa and back. The ptarmigan stays and is white in the winter and is brown in the summer and is not seen in either season. The fox is a fast shape on a moraine ridge. The falcon is a punctuation mark in the sky.

The valley's tenants are patient. They are adapted to a place that is, in the long view, transient — the ice is still retreating, the plants are still advancing, the kettle holes are still filling with peat, the moraines are still being sorted by water. The valley is a young valley, geologically speaking, and its inhabitants are the first wave, the pioneer species, the ones that specialize in arriving early on a surface that has only just been made available. They are the first draft. There will be others, later, when the soil is deeper and the climate is different and the ice is gone, but those others are not here yet. The valley, for now, belongs to the things that can live in the cold, in the wet, in the thin air, in the bright unending light, on a substrate that is mostly rock and partly their own dead ancestors.

## The Whole

The valley is a federation. There is no other word for it.

The lichens dissolve the rock. The mosses hold the water. The *Dryas* advances across the moss. The willows creep within the *Dryas*. The bees pollinate the willow. The flies pollinate the *Dryas*. The bumblebees pollinate the gentian. The voles eat the seeds. The foxes eat the voles. The falcons eat the voles, and the buntings, and the wheatears. The wheatear flies to Africa and comes back. The ptarmigan stays. The lemmings cycle. The cyanobacteria fix the nitrogen. The peat accumulates. The kettle holes fill. The moraines erode. The water moves. The machines hum. The men watch the gauges and eat the food they brought with them and sleep in the vibrating dormitory and then they go down the funicular and the others come up.

The whole thing is, in some deep sense, a single slow process. The rocks are being weathered. The weathering is being captured by the lichens. The lichen-capture is being incorporated into the moss. The moss is being incorporated into the peat. The peat is being incorporated into the soil. The soil is not yet soil, but it will be, in a few thousand years, and by then the kettle holes will be proper bogs and the moraines will be proper hills and the valley will be, in the usual sense, a valley, and the ice will be gone, and the machines will be ruins, and the men will be a kind of memory in the stratigraphy.

But that is not yet. For now, the valley is what it is. The green is thin and the sound is constant and the light is bright and the wet is everywhere and the fauna is rare and the machines are loud and the men are inside. The valley is a place that is just barely made, and just barely inhabited, and just barely running, and the running is the point. The valley is a place that is, in some deep sense, *finished*, in the way a machine is finished, in the way a process is finished — already running, already operating, the only remaining events being the slow adjustments of an enormous system that has all the time in the world.

The tenants of the valley are the things that have made their peace with that. They are the things that live in the running, in the operating, in the long slow turn. They are not, most of them, in a hurry. The valley is not, most of the time, in a hurry. The ice is retreating. The plants are advancing. The water is moving. The sound is constant. The light is constant. The wet is constant.

The valley is, in the end, what lives in it. And what lives in it is patient, and thin, and persistent, and slow, and almost geological, and almost silent, except for the birds, and the bees, and the wind, and the water, and the machines.
