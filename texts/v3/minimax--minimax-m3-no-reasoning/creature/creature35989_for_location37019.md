The work-girls come in pairs, mostly, and they wear rubber aprons over clothes that are already wet when they put them on. Their hair is bound up in scarves of oiled cloth. They carry lanterns even in daylight — or what passes for daylight here, the green-grey seep from above — because the engines throw such shadows, and the steam makes everything into a suggestion of itself. They tend the smaller pumps, the condensate traps, the things that hiss and weep between the larger machines. They speak to each other in a shorthand that has more gesture than word. One holds up two fingers and tilts her head; the other nods and turns a valve and the hiss becomes a sigh becomes silence. They have done this a thousand times. Their hands are red to the second knuckle and never quite dry.

The boy with the iron rod is not a boy. He is a small man, or a man who never grew, and he has been given the gutters to keep because the gutters will keep no one else. He walks the parapets with his rod and prods at the blockages — leaves, mostly, and the nests of something small and wet that build in the downspouts — and he talks to himself as he walks, a low continuous murmur that is not quite language and not quite song. He knows every inch of the quarter's roofline. He has fallen twice and broken things and gone back. His boots are enormous on him. He is the only person who has seen, from above, how the quarter lays itself out: the patterns of condensation on the tiles, the way the steam drifts and pools, the way the moss advances a footmark in a summer.

The man with the oil-blackened arms is an engine-tender, and there are many of him, though each is one. They work in fours at the great pumps and sleep in shifts on cots in the engine hall itself, where the warmth is reliable and the dripping is known. They are broad and soft in the middle, the way people get when they have lived a long time in heat and steam, and they smell of the lubricant that is half their diet — they swallow a little of it with their bread in the morning, they say, to keep the lungs open. They are not unkind. They will show you the engines if you ask, and they will answer your questions, but they will not invite you in. Their world is the hall and the catwalks and the valve-floor, and the boundary between that and the rest of the quarter is one they enforce by ignoring the rest.

The woman on the gallery is waiting for someone. She has been waiting a long time. The damp has curled the posters she meant to take down, and the railing she leans on has rusted through at one point and been patched with wire. She calls down to passing figures and some of them answer. She is known to the quarter, and the quarter knows her rhythms: she comes out at the same hour each day, she calls the same name or a different name each time, and at dusk she goes back in. No one asks her in. She is not unhappy. She has arranged herself around the waiting the way the moss arranges itself around the brick.

The engineer who speaks of the old machinery in the canals is old himself, and he speaks of it because no one else will. He sits on a bollard by the widest channel and watches the slow shapes move below, and he tells anyone who stops that the shapes are the cranks and conrods of the first pumping works, drowned when the second city was built over the first but still turning, still turning, on the patience of rust and current. He has a glass-bottomed box he lowers sometimes on a cord. He looks through it a long time. He says he can see the maker's stamp on one of the wheels. The children who throw stones do not believe him. They believe something else. They believe the shapes are alive in a way that does not require machinery, and they are not wrong, and they are not right, and they throw stones anyway, because the quarter is the kind of place where you throw stones.

There is a child who has taught herself to swim the canals without sound. She drifts along the edges with her face just above the waterline, and she sees things the people on the bridges do not. She has seen the shapes close. She has seen them still. She has seen, once, what looked like a hand pressed against the underside of the ice that forms in the deep channel in winter — if it forms here, if anything that cold can be remembered here. She does not speak of these things. She is nine, or ten, or twelve — the quarter is not good at keeping ages — and she has not yet decided that they are worth speaking of.

There is a pair of old women who share a kitchen and argue about the soup. The kitchen is in a building that has no second floor because the second floor was never built, only the roof set on taller walls, and so the steam from the pot goes up and joins the general damp and the soup is always a little thinner than it should be. They have lived together longer than either can say. They are suspicious of newcomers but generous with the soup. Their window looks onto the engine hall's vine-curtained face, and in summer — if summer is the word — the magnolia petals come in on the draft and lie on the table among the bowls, and they do not sweep them up.

There is a man who sells phosphor-lamps from a cart. He is not from the quarter; he comes up from the lower town, from the place where the streets are still streets, and he brings his cart over the water on the ferry that runs at dawn and dusk. He is always a little cold here, and a little shocked by the green, and he sets his prices high and no one haggles because no one else makes the lamps. He does not stay. He goes back at dusk. He has never slept in the quarter. He says he has never wanted to.

There is a dog. There are several dogs. They are not pets; they are residents. They are thin and dark and they move through the quarter in the lanes between the buildings, and they know the rhythms of the place the way the work-girls know the valves. They eat from bowls that appear on doorsteps. They sleep on the warm stones around the engine hall. One of them, the oldest, sleeps on a rug in front of the relief-valve that vents every quarter-hour, and the steam lifts the edges of the rug and the dog does not wake. The children love this dog. The dog loves no one. It is at home.

There is a bell. Someone rings it. No one knows who. It is a single note, sustained, and it fogs at the edges because the air is wet, and it seems to come from no direction and from everywhere, and when you listen for it you cannot find it, and when you stop listening it is there again. The people of the quarter do not remark on it. It is part of the inventory. It has been ringing as long as anyone can remember. Perhaps it rings for the drowned first city. Perhaps it rings for no one. Perhaps it is the quarter's own heartbeat, made audible by some forgotten installation, a feedback loop in the engines that no one has traced and no one will trace.

You will not see all of these in an afternoon. You will see one or two, and the suggestion of others, and the sound of the rest — a voice in the steam, a footstep on a bridge, a child's laugh in a courtyard you cannot find. The quarter is full, and it is also, in its way, empty. The people move through it the way the water moves. They find their paths. They do not collide. They do not quite meet. They go on.
