What lives in the Basin of the Held Note is the chord itself, and the small things that have learned to stand inside it without breaking.

The lizard is the one you will see if you stay. *Sceloporus ferrum*, if it had a name. A rust-colored thing no longer than your thumb, with a throat patch the pale yellow of cooled slag. It lives in the cracks between terraces, in the millimeter-wide seams where the glass has fractured along the channels and the fractures have widened, over decades, by thermal cycling — the slow mechanical breath of stone that heats by day and cools by night. The cracks are its whole world. It is born in a crack. It hunts from the lip of a crack. It basks on the warm rim of a crack, belly pressed to the iron-sweet stone, and when something moves overhead — a wasp, a fly, a rare moth that has wandered in from the ridge — it does a thing so quick you only see the before and the after, a flick of the head and the insect is gone and the lizard is chewing, and its throat works, and it returns to stillness. It does not drink from the pools. It gets its water from the bodies of the things it eats and from the condensation that beads on its own scales in the small hours of the morning, the way dew beads on a wire. It has lived, its whole lineage, on the iron-flavored juice of wasps and on fog. It has never seen a green leaf. It does not know what green is. Its predators are nothing — nothing hunts it here, nothing larger than itself exists — and so it has no fear, and it will come out and look at you if you sit still, and its eyes are two small drops of the same obsidian the terraces are made of, and you cannot tell what it is thinking because the expression on its face is the expression of the stone.

The wasps are the next ones down, and they are the reason the wasp-eater exists. They are small, black, and they are not social, or if they were once they are not now. A solitary wasp, a hunter of spiders, which is strange because there are no spiders here either, but the wasp does not know this. It hunts in the thermal belt in the late afternoon, hanging in the air above the middle terraces at a height where the temperature gradient is steepest, where the warm air rising off the black glass meets the cooler air above it, and there is a thin band of updraft, and the wasp rides the updraft, and it does not need to fly because the air is doing the flying for it. It hangs. It is the most still living thing in the basin, more still than the lizard, because the lizard at least breathes visibly, and the wasp hangs in the warm column with its wings folded and its legs dangling, and from a distance you cannot see it at all, you only see, if you look, a slight disturbance in the air, a shimmer, the way heat shimmers above asphalt, except it is shaped like a wasp. It is waiting for a spider that will not come. The waiting is in its body. Its great-grandmother waited. The posture of waiting is what it is. Occasionally one will fly, suddenly, a few meters to a new position in the thermal belt, and this is its whole life. When it dies, its body falls, and the lizard eats it if the lizard is there, and the iron in its exoskeleton becomes part of the thin scatter of organic matter on the terraces, and a cyanobacterium will colonize the husk, and the husk will be black-green for a year and then gone.

The flies are more common and less dignified. They are small, dark, and they do not hang; they crawl. They are on every surface that has any organic matter at all, which is to say the cracks where lizard droppings fall and the channels where dead wasps accumulate and the rare spots where the biofilm has taken hold. They walk on the glass with the sure-footedness of things that weigh nothing, and they feed on the film, and they lay eggs in the dampest crevices, and the larvae are small white worms that live in the wet iron-sweet grit at the bottom of the channels. The flies do not fly much. There is no reason to fly. To fly is to leave the surface, and the surface is where the food is. They are the color of the stone. A fly on the black glass is invisible from three meters away. They are the most numerous animals in the basin, and they are the least noticeable, and they have been here long enough that their wings have begun, in some individuals, to be slightly vestigial, the flight muscles reduced, the preference for walking pronounced, because flight is expensive and the flies that did not fly had more children than the flies that did, over many generations, and so the basin has produced, slowly, a fly that does not fly, a fly that is a small dark shape on dark stone that is also, if you put a hand near it, capable of walking up your wrist and tasting the salt of you with its padded feet, and that will not leave, because leaving is not in its lineage anymore, because the lineage has decided, without deciding, that the surface is enough.

The biofilm is the oldest living thing here and the least an animal and the most a citizen. It is not one organism. It is a community — a cyanobacterium, several species of them, layered with a black fungus and a few species of iron-oxidizing bacteria, and together they make a crust that is black-green in the right light and pure black in the wrong light, and it lives only on the outer terraces, only on the weathered powder, only where the iron has begun to break down into a form the bacteria can use. It grows perhaps a millimeter a year. It is the only thing in the basin that is changing the surface it lives on. Everything else — the wasp, the fly, the lizard, the chord, the pools — is in equilibrium, is at rest, is the held note. The biofilm is composing. It is doing the slow, stupid, endless work of turning iron into iron oxide, of pulling the terrace down by a molecule at a time, of beginning, in geological terms, the process that will, in ten thousand years, turn the black glass into red soil and the basin into something that can hold a root. It is the only future in the place. The wasp has no future. The fly has no future. The lizard has no future, only an endless present. The biofilm has a future, and the future is rust, and the rust is coming.

The dust has things in it too, but the things in the dust are dormant, mostly. Spores, seeds blown in from the ridge and unable to germinate on the glass, lying in the channels with their patience intact, waiting for a year when the rain is heavy enough to wash a pocket of silt into a crack where a biofilm has already softened the stone. Some of these spores are a hundred years old. Some will germinate next year. Some will never. They are the ghosts of a vegetation that does not yet exist, sleeping on the surface of a place that is, in the most literal sense, not yet ready for them.

There is also, once or twice in a generation, a visitor. A bird, blown in low by a storm, lands on the fifth terrace in a confused moment and stands there for an hour or a day, and it cannot eat because the insects are too small and too well-camouflaged, and it cannot drink because the water tastes of iron, and it cannot perch because the glass is too smooth, and after a while it flies out, or dies, and if it dies its body lies on the terrace for a month and the flies colonize it and the biofilm follows the flies and the lizard gets fat for a week and then the bones are gone and the place is the place again. The bird is a rumor. It is not a resident. The basin does not count it.

What lives in the basin, in the end, is a small closed system tuned to a single chord. The chord is the sound of the wind through the channels and the channels are the negative of the vats and the vats are gone and the chord is the only memory of the vats that still has any force, and the small things that live in the basin have tuned their lives to the chord the way a musician tunes an instrument, and they are not aware they have done it, and they would not be able to do otherwise, and they are at rest in it, and the rest is total, and the biofilm is the only dissent, and the biofilm is very slow, and the basin will hold its note for a long time yet.
