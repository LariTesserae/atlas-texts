They speak in a small voice. This is the first thing about them, and it is the thing that takes longest to acquire.

A voice raised in anger is spent within a meter. A laugh is spent within two. The basalt and the concrete and the brine drink the way they're drinking, and what is left in the corridors is the careful voice, the next voice, the voice for the person beside you and no farther. To stand in the passages at the slow shift is to overhear, faintly, a kind of murmuring conventicle — the priest and the penitent, the mother and the child, the dredge-hand and the woman who hands him his towel — all of them pitched as though they were speaking into a well that had already accepted what it would accept. The voices have a quality of being slightly held back, slightly supervised, the way a heart lies between the ribs. They are voices that have learned they are not the largest thing in the room.

They are small, mostly. The children are smaller. There is a particular smallness to the children here, a particular compactness, as though they were finished early and the rest was allowed to set. They move the way adults move in tight spaces — they fold their gestures small, they reach at the angle that disturbs least, they set things down as if placing eggs. They play without raising their arms. Their games are conducted at the floor, near the ankles, in the kind of whisper that takes a visitor several shifts to notice is not a whisper at all but the ordinary volume of the place. They are quiet the way the lamps are quiet. A child here learns, before she learns to read, that the water beyond the windows is a kind of elder, a thing with patience and weight, and that the small voice is the courtesy owed to it.

Their clothes are layered the way the concrete is layered. There are, on any given body, three or four generations of fabric in evidence — a piece of sailcloth from the upper years, a strip of rubberized something, a knitted cuff that has been unknitted and reknitted three times, a patch that is itself a patch on a patch. The colors have retreated to muddy browns and the yellows of old paper and the indeterminate greys that come of many washings in brine. A woman I watched mending a child's sleeve had, on her own wrist, a band of crimson silk that was not patched, not repaired, not in any way continued, just there like a single kept letter in a language no one else speaks. I did not ask about it. There is a sense here that the things not asked about are the things being most carefully held.

Their faces are the only things in the place with any real color to them, and even that color has been worked on by the murk. The skin of the corridor-dwellers has the underlit quality of something kept too long in a cupboard — not pale exactly, but suppressed, as though the pigment were being slowly argued out of it. The dredge crews are different. The dredge crews come in from the water and come out, and the water, on their eyes, has been arguing harder. Their eyes are red. The lids are slightly puffy. The white has gone to the color of weak tea. They have a look, the dredge crews, of having recently been weeping, or of having recently been underwater, which is to say the same thing. They are given a different ration, which I take to mean a better one. They smell of the deep water — not brine exactly, but something colder than brine, something the brine has been holding at arm's length. They speak less than the others. They speak, when they speak, in a low voice that is not the careful voice of the corridors but a kind of aftervoice, the voice of someone still listening for something the water said.

The caste between the dredge crews and the corridor-dwellers is not spoken of as a caste. There is no word for it that I have heard. It is felt. The corridor-dwellers step aside for the dredge crews in the passages, the way one steps a kettle off a stove, not unkindly, with the small practiced deference of someone who has known many kettles. The dredge crews, in turn, do not seem to expect. They cross the corridors with a heaviness that is partly the heaviness of the dive and partly the heaviness of a man who has carried something and is now setting it down. They smell of the deep water and they have red eyes and they do not say where they have been.

They are watchful of me. This is the part I have been avoiding. The watchfulness is not unkind. It is the watchfulness of a cat at a door, the watchfulness of someone who has decided that what is on the other side of the door is a temporary phenomenon and is therefore not worth troubling to be rearranged past. I am a temporary phenomenon. They have not yet decided what kind. I move in their corridors, and they watch me move, and the watching has a quality of patience, the patience of a thing that has been watching other temporary phenomena move for longer than I have been alive. They are kind to me in the way that one is kind to a guest who is going to leave: with a courtesy that has a built-in end-date, a hospitality that does not require one to fall in love with the house. They are kind, and they are also watchful, and the watchfulness and the kindness are not in opposition but in some arrangement I do not yet understand.

There is an old woman who sits by the rice trays in the lowest of the rooms, in the place where the air is thickest and the warmth is most held. She wears a sari. The sari is the wrong word, perhaps — it is something that has the form of a sari, but the color is the color of everything else here, the color of bread, the color of the domes, the color of the silt. She wears it the way the crustaceans wear their embroidery — as if having inherited it. She has a face that is mostly forehead and eye, the rest of it having settled into the smaller features below, and the eyes are not red like the dredge crews but are a kind of pale grey-green that the murk has gotten into. She does not speak to me. She has spoken once. I have not pressed. I have been here eleven days. There is time.

She is the one who said *it*. The word was spoken the way one speaks the name of a thing one is not going to invite into the conversation. I do not know what *it* is. I have asked, in the small voice, to the people who will give me the small voice back, and I have been answered, in the small voice, with the kind of answer that is not really an answer but a kind of tilting of the head, a kind of slow blinking, a kind of returning of the question to the air it came from. There is something in the murk, or there is something in the settlement, or there is something in the basalt, or there is something in the silence, and the something has been referred to as the visitor, or the long one, or, by the old woman in the sari, simply *it*. I do not press. I have learned, in eleven days, that there are things here that pressing will not retrieve. The murk has its own way of giving things up, and the way is not asking.

The dog that I have not named has not yet been seen. There is a feeling, sometimes, when the lamps flicker and the pumps cycle up, of something moving at the edge of the corridor, at the edge of a furrow, in the very furthest reach of the sodium light. The feeling does not always come. It comes most often when the silt has been stirred, when the dredges have come back, when the murk has been newly disturbed. I have not yet seen what casts the feeling. I have not yet seen what the feeling is the feeling of. I have not yet asked. I have learned, in eleven days, that some questions here are a kind of food, and one does not ask for food before the host. The water has its own hospitality and it will reach, and what it brings up, it brings up.

The crustaceans are part of the dwelling, in their way. They have been here longer than the people, and they will be here longer. Their embroidery is patient and intricate and does not ask anything of the people, and the people have, in turn, made a kind of peace with them. They are not eaten. They are too slow to catch, and also, I think, the people have made a treaty with them that does not require a word — the treaty of small things with no other small things to make treaties with. The crustaceans are the neighbors. The crustaceans are the walls. The crustaceans are the patient and continuing decoration of a place that has decided to continue. When the people re-work the basalt, they scrape the crustaceans away in places, and the crustaceans, in their own time, come back. There is no war between the people and the crustaceans. There is a kind of joint tenancy, a kind of mortgage held in common, the kind of arrangement that requires no language because it has been going on for longer than the languages have.

The small pale fish come in when the silt is disturbed. They are edible. They are a sign, the residents tell me, of poor maintenance, of filters that have not been changed in some time, of a settlement that is, in its quiet way, falling behind its own standards. The residents eat them anyway. There is a kind of embarrassed fondness in the eating, the fondness of a hostess who has been forced to serve what was not intended to be served. The fish are not residents. The fish are visitors, in the way that I am a visitor, in the way that the long one in the murk is a visitor, in the way that anything in the settlement is either a resident or a visitor and the distinction is not a matter of duration but of intention. The fish do not intend to stay. The fish intend to be eaten, or to be filtered out, or to die in the corridors and be swept up by the digesters and go, in their small way, into the methane.

The rice is grown in cycles. The cycles are a kind of religion, and the religion has its priests, and the priests are the women who tend the trays. The trays are sealed. The air in the lowest domes is thickest, and the warmth is most held, and the rice grows in the sealed trays under the lamps in the thickest air the way a thought grows in a sealed head. The cycles are not performances. There is no ceremony that I have seen. There is only the work of it, and the work of it has a quality of being quietly in the right relationship to something. The work is what the work is for. The rice is what the rice is for. The cycles are what the cycles keep going. The religion is the religion of a thing that has decided to continue, and the continuing is not a faith but a kind of rhythm, a kind of breath, a kind of pulse held against the pulse of the water outside.

The kelp grows toward the lamps in a slow, blind reaching. The kelp does not know it is being tended. The kelp does not know it is reaching. The kelp is doing what kelp does, here at the bottom of an ocean, in the dim glow of sodium lamps, with a patience that I find hard to look at directly. The people tend it, and the tending is not a kind word but a kind of care, the kind of care one gives to a thing that is doing what one is also doing, which is to continue.

The mushrooms grow in the dark shelves in the wettest corridors. They are white and patient and smelling of loam. They are doing what mushrooms do. They are continuing.

The people, in their small voices and their patched clothes and their watchful kindness, are continuing.

The dredge crews, in their red eyes and their deep-water smell, are continuing.

The old woman in the sari, in her pale grey-green eyes and her one word, is continuing.

The crustaceans, in their embroidery, are continuing.

The thing that lives in the murk, that the long one is called, that the old woman called *it*, is continuing.

I have been here eleven days. I have learned, in eleven days, that the settlement is a kind of small and contained and continuing thing, and the small and contained and continuing things here are doing, in their own way, what the small and contained and continuing things do. They are feeding. They are resting. They are waiting, perhaps, for the long and patient morning that may or may not come. They are not waiting in any way that requires the morning to come. They are waiting in the way a lung waits, in the way a heart waits, in the way a place that has decided, with great quietness, to continue, continues.

The lamps will continue their small yellow work.

The dredges will go out and come back.

The rice will be grown.

The kelp will reach.

The silence, which is not a silence but a kind of dense and living quiet, will continue to hold everything in its plush and careful hands.

And the people, who are small and careful and watchful and kind and undecided about me, will continue.

I will stay, in the meantime, and learn their small voices, and learn the names of their corridors, and wait, perhaps, to see the long one, or to not see the long one, and to be, in my way, a small and continuing thing among the small and continuing things here, in the warm and dark and water-pressed settlement under the hush.
