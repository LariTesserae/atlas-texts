**What lives in Karst**

They live close to the engines the way shore-dwellers live close to a sea that never retreats — with an ear tilted always toward something they did not choose. You will not meet a Karstian quickly. They move as if their own weight were a consideration, as if every step were a small negotiation with the dust beneath them. Their boots are soled thick and re-soled often. Their clothes are layered against a chill that is not quite temperature — the kind of chill that lives in a throat, in the backs of hands. The thicker the layer, the more *settled* the person seems to wear it. A newcomer to Karst is anyone whose coat fits.

They are pale. Not the blanched pallor of deep-cave people, but the particular pale of those who live under an amber sky: a faint, persistent undertone, as if the skin has learned the color of the light and colors itself in sympathy. Their eyes are dark and quiet and seem to be looking at something slightly behind whatever they are facing. The children have the same eyes, but bigger in the face, and the faces are dustier. The old have the same eyes, but the lids have lowered a fraction, and the dust has gone into the lines.

There is a way they have of standing near a wall when they speak to you. Not leaning, exactly — *near*. As if the wall is a kind of consent, or a kind of witness. When two Karstians talk, they do not face each other directly. They stand at a slight angle, shoulders toward a shared wall, and the conversation moves between them like a slow current. You will notice this first as an oddity and then as a comfort. The angle of approach saves everyone the work of full attention. You can listen with half your face. You can answer with the other half.

They are practical. The buildings hold warmth. The water — such as it is, the reclaimed condensate from the cooling systems, faintly mineral, carried in long pipes — is used and used again. Nothing is wasted here. This is not asceticism; it is a deeper habit, the way one learned that in a place where the light must be made, one does not leave a lamp burning in an empty room. The Karstians are not frugal in the small things. They are scrupulous in the small things. The lamp ahead of you, beginning to glow on a timer before you arrive: that is the size of their conscience.

There is a slow commerce. Re-spun coils. Re-poured facades. The rolled alloy, in sheets, that goes into the new beams, and the older beams that are cut down and re-rolled. There is a foundry-quarter, and the foundry has its own caste of residents, and the caste is *almost* hereditary in the way that the door of a house is almost the door of a house: it can be changed, but no one is in a hurry to. The smelter crews work in shifts that are not day-and-night but warm-and-cooler. They come home smelling of metal and salt. Their children have a particular complexion, slightly grayed at the edges. The crews themselves are fond of the children, with a particular fondness, the way one is fond of anything that came out of what one has done.

There are dogs, and the dogs are old. There are five, in the end. There is a black one named Voss who belongs to no one and sits by the central obelisk in the late afternoons and watches the children with an expression that is, by long tradition, said to be *philosophical*, though it is more likely merely tired. There is a brown-and-white one in the southern quarter who still attempts, feebly, to bark at the rock-lice. There are three others in various states of dignified decline. No one breeds them. They will end, as all things in Karst end, by being *the last of*. The children are permitted to look at them. The children are not encouraged to love them. This is a kindness, not cruelty.

The cats are more numerous and less remarked. They keep to the engine-rooms and the cistern stations, where the warmth is reliable and the rock-lice are edible. They have the long, low bodies of cats that have lived for many generations in low light. Their eyes are very large. They regard visitors with the particular indifference of animals that have been the dominant small predator in their ecology for so long they have stopped noticing the race. There is a gray cat in the foundry office that has learned the location of the warmest shelf and that will, if you sit on it, sit on you. This is the closest thing Karst offers to a greeting.

The rock-lice come out in the warmer hours, in the seams between the buildings, in the thin strips of sun-warmed stone between the shadows. They are the size of a child's smallest fingernail and the color of the stone they rest on. They do not move when you watch them. They move when you look away. They feed on what the engines exhale. They are the only visible life in Karst that is not a person or a cat or the remains of a dog. They are not worshipped, but they are not disregarded. They are *present*. The civic religion, such as it is, would include them, if pressed.

The fliers — the tall pale fliers of dusk — are not, the consensus has finally settled, animals. They are too thin. They are too consistent in their behavior. They are warm metal, lifted, the working hypothesis goes, by thermals off the seven stacks. They may be sheets that escaped the foundry. They may be byproducts of the re-rolling that have found an unscheduled motion. They have been seen to *return*, which is the thing that settled it. An animal would not return. A loose sheet would.

There are visitors, but rarely. There is a southern trader who arrives twice a year by a route that involves weeks of walking across stone. She brings, when she can, a small quantity of food that is not synthesized — dried things, preserved things, things with an origin in water or in a soil that was once a soil. She is received with the particular Karstian hospitality, which is to say: a room is made ready for her in advance, and it is warm, and there is no conversation about why she came. She is given a lamp-coil at her departure. She does not speak of her journey. The Karstians do not ask. There is a custom, when she leaves, of walking beside her for the first hundred meters and then stopping, with a small motion of the hand, and then going back. This is not a farewell. It is *a completion*.

They are, in the main, an unhurried people. They do not gesticulate. They do not interrupt. They listen, when they listen, with their whole bodies — slightly turned, head a fraction inclined, the shoulder toward the speaker and away at the same time. They will not ask you to repeat yourself. They will not ask you why you have come. If you have come to see what Karst is for, they will show you the engines. If you have come to see what Karst is like, they will show you a room that is warm, and a lamp that has just been lit for you on a timer, and a cup of the mineral water, which is faintly metallic on the tongue and warm to the back of the throat. They will not press the question of why. The small civic religion, such as it is, would consider the question *incoherent*. They are what remained. They are what remains. The question of *why* has, by long settlement, become redundant.

What you will notice last, and what you will carry longest, is the quality of their attention. They attend to you. They do not study you. They do not assess you. They *attend*, the way one attends a low continuous sound — by holding oneself open to it, without effort, the way one holds oneself open to warmth. You will leave and you will be followed, with a low and unhurried gaze, for as far as a Karstian can see you on the pale stone. Then the gaze will lift, and turn, and become a gaze upon something else — a stack, a wall, a slow small movement in the seam between the stones, the place where a small pale creature has emerged to test the warmer hour.

The wind has had the better part of a million years to learn this place. The Karstians have had the better part of a hundred and sixty. They have settled into each other, the way the dust settles into the cracks.
