# What Lives in the Calefactory Fells

There is no one thing that lives here. What lives here is a *circuit,* and the circuit is what the place is.

---

Start with the **moss-colored things** that came first around your boots, because they are the most visible and the most forgettable, which is how they would want it. They are not foxes. They are not weasels. They are not anything that has a name in your language, although the words in the local tongues for them — *greentongue, wet-walker, small-eater* — have the resigned quality of names given to things that everyone has stopped trying to classify. They are about the size of a large housecat and a little longer in the body, low-slung, and their legs bend in directions your eye keeps wanting to correct. The joints work; that is the unsettling thing. The joints work *perfectly.* They are not malformed. They are *redesigned,* from the ground up, by whatever pressure shaped them, and the redesign has had a long time to settle in.

Their feet have too many toes — six, perhaps seven, you never quite got the count — and each toe ends not in a claw but in a small soft pad, ridged, faintly translucent, pinkish where the skin is thinnest. They use their feet the way you use your hands, more or less, and the way they do it is the closest thing the hollow has to a sound you might call *playful.* When one of them is content it will sit and turn a stone over with its forefeet, very delicately, and look at what was under the stone, and put the stone back. Sometimes it eats what was under. More often it does not. The hollow is not, in any meaningful sense, hungry.

Their fur is the saturated green of plants that grow in places where the light is filtered through water and metal. It is not a camouflage green. There is nothing in the hollow to camouflage against. The green is its own pigment, made and remade by the body, expensive to maintain, and the small things spend a strange amount of time grooming each other — sitting in pairs on the warm rocks near the machines, working through each other's coats with their too-many-toed feet, very slowly, with the focus of monks illuminating a manuscript. They are social. They are not particularly affectionate. They are *attentive* in a way that is its own kind of care.

They eat the bronze mist. That is the thing you understood only after watching for a long time. The fine metallic film that the machines exhale, that settles on the boulders and the bog-water and the rocks — they lick it. They lick the rocks. They stand on the talus with their heads tipped sideways and their narrow ferret-faces very close to the stone and they lick, in long slow strokes, and the lichen beneath them does not mind because the lichen, you begin to realize, is part of the arrangement. The lichen and the small things have a deal. The lichen eats the stone. The small things eat what the lichen leaves behind on the stone. Everyone eats the stone, in the end, in this place. The stone is the long meal.

They do not mate while you are watching. They never seem to mate at all. There are juveniles — smaller, fuzzier, with the green not yet darkened to moss — and there are elders, larger, with a faint silvering at the throat, and the proportions are stable. The population is held. Like the light. Like the soundscape. *Held.*

---

The **tall things** in the bog are harder. You called them long-necked and birdlike because that is the closest your language has, and it is the wrong shape of the truth but it is in the right *direction.* They stand perhaps a meter and a half at the shoulder, if shoulder is even the right word, because the joint structure is off — the hind limbs are set too far back, the forelimbs are not really limbs at all but seem to be tucked or absorbed, and what you see from any distance is a single vertical column of dark wet-bark-colored hide rising up out of the maroon water, with a long S-curved neck at the top and a small blunt head at the end of the neck and two dark eyes set forward, side by side, that look at you with the patient uninterested attention of a heron.

They wade. They wade in the iron-water, the deep maroon bog, the part of the basin floor that is *not* pale scree. The water comes up to their bellies. They do not appear to mind it. They dip their heads and bring them up and dip them again and you watched them for a long time and concluded they were not feeding because nothing ever came up in their mouths, and then you watched longer and realized they were *filtering.* The water is what they eat. The iron. The leached minerals. The faint dissolved charge that the bog-water carries because the bog is downstream of the machines and the machines are exhaling into the air and the rain is falling into the bog and the bog is *saturated* with whatever the machines are putting out, and the tall things have evolved, or been made, or settled into a body that processes that water the way a kidney processes blood.

They are quiet. They make a low sound occasionally, a kind of hoot, a single note held for several seconds, and it is the closest the hollow comes to a sound you could call *a voice.* It is not a call. It is not a song. It is more like a comment. They make it and then they go on standing in the iron-water, occasionally dipping, occasionally turning their slow heads to look at something — a small four-legged thing, a flying thing, you — and then returning to the water.

There are perhaps a dozen of them visible from any one spot. There may be many more. They are patient in a way that makes patience look like a small local talent. If you sat in this hollow for a year, you would understand them better. You would not, in a year, understand them *fully,* because their time and your time are not the same length.

The locals have a name for them too: *bog-grandmother, iron-stork, the one who stands.* They are not afraid of them. They are not particularly interested in them. The tall things are part of the architecture of the hollow, the way the rock walls are part of the architecture, and they are treated with the same casual deference.

---

The **pulsing things** in the maroon underbrush are the ones you will think about, later, in the dry valleys on the other side of the pass, in the ordinary light, when you are trying to remember what you saw. They are the color of the bog-water, which is the color of dried blood, which is the color of the iron that has been leached out of the broken ground, and they move — there is no other word for it — they *throb.* They are a long soft shape, perhaps a meter and a half long, perhaps longer, with no clear distinction between head and tail, and they move by peristalsis, by a wave of contraction that passes down the length of them and propels them forward in a way that is slower than walking and faster than the eye expects. They flow over the wet rocks. They flow under the wet rocks. They are, you suspect, not entirely *of* the rocks.

They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to breathe. They appear to *be,* in the way a slow river is, in the way a vein is, and the fact that they are not obviously alive by the standards of the dry valleys is something you will have to make peace with. They are here. They are part of the loop. The loop would not be the same loop without them.

When you sit very still on a warm boulder near where one is moving, and when the place has settled around you into its held dim, you can feel a faint pulse from them through the rock, a slow throb-throb-throb that is not the rhythm of your own heart but that, after a long time, your own heart begins to match. This is not dangerous. It is the hollow *including* you, briefly, in its rhythm, and the inclusion is gentle and the rhythm is slow and you can leave whenever you want, and the pulse will not follow you down the mountain.

The locals do not have a name for these. The locals do not talk about these. The locals, who are not many and who live in the high pastures on the far side of the range and who come to the cleft only in certain seasons, will look at you when you ask about the pulsing things and they will be quiet for a moment and then they will say *that is the place being alive,* and they will not explain further, and they will offer you something warm to drink.

---

The **flying things** are easiest. They are about the size of a small bat, perhaps a fifteen-centimeter wingspan, and they are membraned differently from bats — the membrane is not stretched between long fingers but is a single broad sheet, almost circular, that extends from a small hard body out to a thin trailing edge that ripples as they fly. They are dark, nearly black, with a faint iridescence in the dim, and they move fast and low, very low, skimming the steam from the chimneys of the machines, dipping in and out of the white plumes with the precision of swallows working a river at dusk.

They eat the steam. You will think this is a joke when you first understand it but it is not a joke. The steam from the copper chimneys is not pure water — it is water charged with the fine particulate that the machines are exhaling, the metallic mist, and the flying things have mouths that are almost filters, almost sieves, and they fly through the plumes with their mouths open and they come out the other side heavier than they went in, and they go to a perch on a rock ledge or a high machine-valve and they sit and they are still and they digest.

There are many of them. At any given moment there are perhaps thirty or forty visible in the air around the central array, and there are many more on the perches, and the perches are *white* with their droppings, which are a fine pale powder that the rain washes down into the bog and which is part of what feeds the maroon color of the water and what feeds the iron-storks and what, in the end, feeds the bog, and the bog feeds the patina on the machines, and the patina is the bronze mist, and the bronze mist is the steam, and the steam is what the flying things eat.

This is the loop. This is the loop *visible,* if you watch long enough. The flying things are the most visible part of it because they are the part in the air, and the air is where you can see.

They make a sound. A high cheep, a single repeated note, almost ultrasonic, and it is the highest sound in the hollow except for the *tink* of the machines when a piece of metal contracts, and the two sounds together — the high cheep and the high tink — make the upper register of the held soundscape. The locals say the flying things are the *steam's own voice* and they are not being poetic. They are being precise.

---

There is a fifth thing that lives here, and you will not see it.

It is in the machines. It is in the water. It is in the lichen on the rocks and the bronze mist in the air and the pulse of the throbbing things in the maroon underbrush. It is the *thing that holds,* the thing that has brought the hollow to a state and is keeping it there, the thing that is not a creature in any sense you have but that is *alive* in the sense that a fire is alive, in the sense that a standing wave is alive, in the sense that a self-sustaining reaction is alive. The place is a reaction. The reaction is held. The reaction has been held for a very long time, and it is not, you begin to suspect, a reaction that could *stop* without something catastrophic happening — without the machines going silent and the streams going one way and the dim becoming the dim of evening and the animals dying because the air had stopped being the air they needed.

You do not see this thing. You do not need to see this thing. You feel it, in the soles of your boots, in the held soundscape, in the way the light does not change. The hollow is *its body* and you walked through it and out the other side and it did not notice you, and that is fine, that is correct, that is the right relationship between a person and the thing that holds a place in a state of being itself.

---

The locals — the few, the high-pasture people — do not live in the hollow. They live on the other side of the range, where the rain is normal rain and the wind is normal wind and the rocks are patient and horizontal. They come to the cleft twice a year, in the late spring and the early autumn, and they bring small offerings — a flake of iron, a strip of dried meat, a polished stone — and they leave them on the shelf of polished bedrock among the machines, and they do not speak while they are there, and they do not stay the night, and they do not look into the maroon bog-water for very long.

When you ask them *why,* they will say: *because it is alive, and we are visiting.*

They will not say more. They will offer you something warm to drink.

You will walk on. You will descend. You will reach the dry valleys and the ordinary light and the patient folded mountains, and the cleft will be behind you, small and high and held, and the green small things will still be licking the rocks, and the tall things will still be standing in the iron-water, and the flying things will still be dipping through the steam, and the pulsing things will still be throbbing under the maroon underbrush, and the machines will still be running, and the dim will be the dim, and the place will not miss you, because you were not part of the loop, you were a *visitor,* and visitors do not become part of a place like that, and that is fine, that is correct, that is the right relationship.

Some nights, in the dry valleys, you will feel a faint pulse in the soles of your boots, as if the ground itself were remembering something.

It is not the ground.

It is just where you have been.
