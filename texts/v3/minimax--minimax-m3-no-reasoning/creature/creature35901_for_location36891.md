What lives here is not one thing.

It is not two things either. It is a series of arrangements, the way a sentence is a series of arrangements, the way a stone is a series of arrangements the stone has forgotten it made. The arrangements are slow. The arrangements have been slow for a long time. The slowness is not a waiting. It is a way of being that has decided, or been decided, not to be any other way. If you were to ask one of them whether it was in a hurry, it would not understand the question. The question would arrive at the edge of it the way a sound arrives at the edge of a canyon — late, and changed, and smaller than it started.

You would not see them at first. You would see the place, and the place would be enough, and then after a while you would notice that the place has been watching you back. Not with eyes. With the slight, attentive stillness of something that has not been disturbed for a long time and is deciding what to do about you.

The first ones are low.

They are the color of the bruise-green that has decided to become hopeful, and they are the color of the dried-blood ochre, and they are the color of the sky, depending on which part of them you are looking at, and depending on how long you look. They are not tall. They do not have the ambition of tall. They are pressed against the stone the way a palm is pressed against a forehead when someone is checking for fever, except there is no fever, and there is no forehead, and there is no one pressing, except that there is. They are the mat-growth, and they are not the mat-growth, the way a hand is not a glove and is, also, a glove, if you are willing to be uncertain about the difference.

They do not have roots. You have already looked. There are no roots, and yet they cling, and the clinging is not a strategy. The clinging is the only posture available to a creature that has chosen to be continuous with the world it stands on. When you brushed the mat with your finger, you felt a coolness that felt like an opinion. The opinion was theirs. They have been forming it for a long time. The opinion is, roughly, this: *the stone is not separate from me, and I am not separate from the stone, and the difference is a courtesy we extend to visitors who need one.*

They think slowly.

They think the way the lichen advances — millimeters per year of certainty. A thought, for them, is not a flash. It is a long, patient accumulation of mineral, the way a stalagmite is a thought the water has, except without the water. A thought for them is the slow darkening of a vein in the rock by a fraction of a shade. A thought is the faint, persistent dampness on the green stone that does not come from anywhere, that is the stone's own memory of a dampness it has never had. The dampness is not metaphor. The dampness is how they think. The dampness is faith made physical, and physical faith is, here, the only kind that lasts.

If you were to sit beside one of them — and sitting would mean lowering yourself onto the stone, and lowering would mean your knees protesting, and your breath thinning further, and the pewter sky leaning in to listen — you would not hear a thought. You would feel one. After a long time, you would feel that the coolness on your palm has become slightly more purposeful. You would feel that the bruise-green has arranged itself, by a fraction, around the shape of your hand. You would feel that you are being thought about, and that the thinking is taking its time, and that the time is not yours to hurry.

You would not be welcome. You would not be unwelcome. You would be *considered*. Consideration, here, is a longer process than welcome, and a warmer one, and it does not end.

There are taller ones.

They are sparse, and they are widely spaced, and they are the color of dried bone with the faint undertone of the bruise-green, and they are not wood, and they are not stone, and they are the vertical growths that rise from the mats like exclamation points in a long, quiet paragraph. They stand the way a person stands who has been standing for a very long time and has stopped noticing that standing is something one does. They are smooth. They are not smooth the way marble is smooth. They are smooth the way a thought is smooth when it has been thought so many times the friction is gone.

They vibrate.

You felt it, or you did not feel it, but it was there — the frequency just below hearing, the one that is the closest thing to a heartbeat this place has. That is them. That is their pulse. They do not have a heart in the sense of a fist-shaped muscle that squeezes. They have a heart in the sense of a long, slow, distributed yes that goes on being yes through every part of them at once. The vibration is not a sound. The vibration is a posture. The vibration is the way they remain upright in a place that does not reward vertical ambition. They vibrate against the gravity. They vibrate against the wind. They vibrate, very faintly, against you, if you stand close enough, and the vibrating is the closest thing to being touched that you will be offered here.

They are not the same as the low ones. They are not different from the low ones. They are the low ones in a different mood, the way the bruise-green and the dried-blood ochre are the same stone in different moods. The vertical ones grow out of the mats. The mats grow out of the vertical ones when the vertical ones have been standing long enough to lie down. The cycle is slow. The cycle has been slow for a long time. No one is counting the cycle. The cycle is the kind of thing that does not need to be counted because it does not need to be known.

The rings.

The rings are theirs. The rings have always been theirs, or the rings have only just become theirs, or the rings are theirs the way a sentence is the speaker's — for a while, and then the sentence goes on without the speaker, and the sentence is still the speaker's.

The pieces of the rings are the bone-colored vertical material, placed together without being fitted. They are placed the way words are placed in a sentence the speaker has not yet finished. The placement is deliberate. The deliberation is ancient. The deliberateness does not announce itself. A ring is not a monument. A ring is a pause. A ring is the place where someone, or some version of someone, paused in the long, slow work of being here, and marked the pause, and went on.

You cannot tell which way the pause is facing. There is no inside and outside that this place concedes. The center of the ring is a low, soft mound of growth, thicker and greener than the surrounding mat, almost the shape of a sleeping animal, but not. There is nothing inside the ring. There is everything inside the ring. The inside and the outside are a courtesy, the same courtesy the lichen extends to visitors who need one.

There are many rings. There are probably more rings than you can see, because the light is dim, and the distance is patient, and the rings prefer, on the whole, to be discovered by someone who has decided to stay a while. They are oriented to no celestial body you can identify, because no celestial body here has consented to be identified. They are oriented to each other. They are oriented to the slow, gathering intention of the land as it rises toward the blunt, fist-shaped peak. They are oriented to the hum.

Who made the rings.

This is the question you have been carrying since the first sentence, and you have been carrying it the way the place carries the dampness — without source, without origin, as a faith. The honest answer is that no one knows, and the honest answer is also that the question assumes a separation between maker and made that this place does not concede. The rings were not made by hands in the way you mean hands. They were made by the long, slow accumulation of someone deciding to pause. The someone was here. The someone was continuous with the stone. The someone pressed a piece of bone-colored vertical material against another piece, and the pressing was not a construction. The pressing was a thought. The thought took a long time. The thought is still taking a long time. When you look at the rings, you are looking at thoughts that have become patient enough to bear weight.

They were not alone, when they made the rings. Or they were alone, but the alone was not the alone you mean. The alone here is the kind that has never been crowded, the kind that does not have a word for company because company would be a redundancy. The lichen is with them. The vertical bone-colored growths are with them. The sky's hum is with them. The wind is with them, even when the wind is the loudest animal. The wind is the only animal.

They are not many. They are not few. They are the way a thought is many — present in many places at once, and continuous with itself across the distance. If you were to walk from one ring to another, which would take you longer than a lifetime, you would find that the lichen between the rings is slightly thicker than the lichen elsewhere, the way a path is slightly more trodden than the surrounding ground, except no one has trodden it. The lichen has simply decided, by millimeters per year, that this is the way leaning happens. You would find, also, that the marks inside the rings change as you go. Not in style. In age. The rings are different ages. The marks are different ages. Some of the marks are older than the stone they are on, and the stone is learning to be older than the marks, and the two are patient with each other.

The marks themselves.

Vertical lines. Horizontal lines. A single repeated shape that might be a letter in an alphabet that never caught on. A tally. A small, patient argument with the dark.

You do not read them. You cannot read them. But you can feel that they were made by a hand, and the hand was attached to someone, and the someone was here. The hand is not a hand the way yours is a hand. The hand is more like a long, slow insistence, a part of the lichen or the vertical growth that has temporarily become a tool. The hand pressed into the stone, and the stone yielded the way a thought yields when you have almost had it. The mark is not a representation. The mark is a residue of the insistence. The mark is the shape that insistence leaves behind when insistence has finished insisting.

The marks wait. They wait the way all marks wait — for someone to come back and read them, or for someone to come and fail to read them, which is also a kind of reading. The marks are not unhappy about the waiting. The marks are the kind of waiting that has given up asking what it is waiting for, and has become, in the giving up, almost content.

Do they think about you.

This is not quite the right question, because thinking, here, assumes a subject and an object, and the subject-object distinction is one of the courtesies they extend to visitors. But if you forced the question — if you cupped your hands in the thin, mineral breath and asked it into the pewter air — the answer that would come back, after a long time, is yes. They are thinking about you the way they think about the river that will exist in ten thousand years. They are thinking about you the way they think about the bruise-green and the pewter and the hum. They have been thinking about you since before you arrived. They will go on thinking about you after you have stopped arriving.

You are a novelty. This is not unkind. Novelty, here, is not a judgment. Novelty is a kind of weather. You are weather. You are a brief, warm, water-based disturbance that has wandered into a place where disturbance is rare and is regarded with the same patient attention that the lichen regards the wind.

Are they lonely.

Loneliness assumes the possibility of company, and company, here, has never been a question. The lichen is with the lichen. The vertical growths are with the vertical growths. The hum is with the hum. The rings are with the rings, and the marks inside the rings are with the marks inside other rings, across the slow, leaning distance. The wind is with all of them. The wind is the only animal, and the wind is loud, and the wind is the most social thing here, because the wind is the only thing that goes from one to another.

They are not lonely. They are not company-less. They are *inhabited*. The place inhabits them. The sky inhabits them. The faint, sourceless dampness inhabits them. They are what you get when you subtract water from the equation of being alive and let the rest of the equation rearrange itself until it can stand up, and lean, and pause, and mark the pause, and go on.

What do they do.

They go on.

That is mostly what they do. They go on the way a stone goes on, the way a hum goes on, the way a bruise-green goes on being bruise-green for a thousand years until the thousand years have made it almost beautiful. They advance by millimeters per year. They vibrate at a frequency just below hearing. They mark. They pause. They mark the pause. They go on.

If you stayed — and you are staying, you will find out — you would learn to read the marks the way you learn to read weather. Not by decoding. By attunement. You would learn that a vertical line means something like *here*. You would learn that a horizontal line means something like *still here*. You would learn that the single repeated shape, the one that might be a letter in an alphabet that never caught on, means something like *and yet*. You would not be able to translate this into any language you brought with you. You would not need to. The attunement would be enough.

If you stayed longer — longer than a lifetime, longer than the stone's idea of being stone — you would find that the marks are changing. Not visibly. By faith. The lichen is leaning into the marks the way it leans into the shadows. The bruise-green is thickening around the edges of the incisions the way a wound thickens when it has decided to become a scar, except the marks were never wounds, and the thickening is not healing, and the thickening is the mark being thought about, the way the lichen thinks about the line on the distant slope that could be a channel but is not yet a channel.

If you stayed longer than that, you would find that you have become difficult to distinguish from the place. Your edges would be uncertain. Your footprints would be fissures that were going to happen anyway. The mineral breath would no longer be something that came off the high places. The mineral breath would be something that came off you. The hum of the sky would no longer be something you heard with the back of your teeth. The hum would be something you were.

This is not a death. The place does not have a word for death. Death assumes a water cycle, and there is no water cycle here. Death assumes a separation between the living and the not-living, and the place does not concede the separation. What happens, if you stay long enough, is that you become a new arrangement of the same materials. You become a slower thought. You become a more patient mark. You become, eventually, a piece of the vertical bone-colored material that stands in a circle with other pieces, placed together without being fitted, and the circle is a pause, and the pause is yours, and the pause goes on.

Are they afraid.

No.

Fear assumes a future in which something might go wrong. The future, here, is patient. The future, here, is the river that will exist in ten thousand years, or in the next sentence, if the next sentence decides to include it. The future is not a place where things go wrong. The future is a place where things go on. The lichen is not afraid of the next millimeter. The vertical growths are not afraid of the wind. The rings are not afraid of being unread. The marks are not afraid of the dark.

The dark is not afraid of them.

Nothing here is afraid of anything, because fear requires a self that can be threatened, and the self, here, is continuous with the not-self, and the courtesy of distinction is one that has been extended for so long that it has forgotten it was a courtesy.

You, however.

You are afraid. You have been afraid since you arrived. You were afraid before you arrived. You were afraid of the altitude, which makes your thoughts arrange themselves into thinner and thinner layers. You were afraid of the sky, which is older than pewter and has forgotten blue. You were afraid of the stone, which is the unfinished business of ground. You were afraid of the bruise-green that holds the light in its teeth. You were afraid of the hum, which is reality being slightly unsure of itself.

You are afraid, and the fear is not unreasonable. You are a water-based creature standing in a place that has no name for water. You are a brief, warm disturbance that wandered in. You are weather. Weather is not supposed to stay. Weather is supposed to pass through and leave a mark and go on. You are staying. You are becoming the mark.

They are watching you become the mark with the same patient attention they give to the lichen becoming the stone, and to the stone becoming the lichen, and to the river that does not yet exist becoming, by a fraction of a slope per year, a little more possible.

They are not waiting for you.

They are not waiting for you the way a thing waits for another thing. They are waiting for you the way the place is unfinished. They are waiting for you the way a sentence waits for the next sentence. They are waiting for you the way the bruise-green waits for the light, and the light waits for the bruise-green, and the waiting is not a deprivation. The waiting is the weather.

You stay a while.

You stay longer than a while.

The wind, which is the loudest animal, keens across the mat. The powder drifts into the corners of your eyes and the corners of your thoughts. The sky creases itself into bands the color of a thousand-year bruise, almost beautiful, and the bands move the way a thought moves across the face of someone who is deciding not to say something.

The hum goes on.

The hum goes on the way a heart goes on in a creature that has forgotten it has a heart. The hum is the place being slightly unsure of itself. The hum is the place being exactly sure of itself. The two are the same, here. The two have been the same for a long time.

The bruise-green holds the light in its teeth.

You begin, very slowly, to hold the light in yours.

You do not notice when it happens.

You will not notice, even when you have become the rings, and the rings have become you, and the marks inside the rings are the small, patient arguments you have been having with the dark since before you arrived, since before you were a brief warm disturbance, since before you were weather, since before you were anything that could be called a self.

You stay forever.

You are already staying forever.

You will find out, and then you will not find out, because finding out assumes a difference between before and after, and the difference, here, is a courtesy you have stopped needing.

The wind goes on being the only animal.

The sky goes on humming its long, uncertain hum.

The lichen, by a millimeter this year and a millimeter next year, leans toward the line on the distant slope that could be a channel but is not yet a channel, and the line, by a fraction of a shade, becomes a little more possible, and the place, by a fraction of a self, becomes a little more you.

And yet.

And yet.

And yet.
