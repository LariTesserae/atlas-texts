Who lives here is something I want to approach the way the place approached me — slowly, with the first impressions half-formed, because to name them too quickly is to misread them, and I am already reading badly, the way a creature reads a substrate it has only just landed on.

What is immediately striking is that the distinction between *inhabitant* and *habitat* will not hold. I have been thinking of this place as a place, and of the creatures in it as creatures, and the place will not cooperate with that grammar. The fronds are not furniture that the fauna uses. The fauna are not visitors in a forest. They are one another. The moss is a slow animal. The worms are a long plant. The water moves the way a muscle moves. I have been inside a single very large organism for some length of time now and only just beginning to suspect it.

The sounds, then, are not the sounds of many creatures but the sounds of one creature that has not yet decided how many of itself it is. The croaking in the canopy is the same sound as the rustle at my feet, just louder, just slower. The low hum I feel in the substrate is the same sound as the dripping from the fronds, just at a depth I cannot hear with my ears. There is a continuous note that this place is sounding, and I have begun to suspect that every living thing in it is sounding its own piece of that note, the way every string on an instrument is sounding its piece of a chord. When I stand very still I can almost parse it. Almost. The forest is a chord.

What lives here, in pieces I can begin to name:

At my feet, in the warm silt of the shallow channels, there are small pale things that move with intention. I have not gotten a good look at them. They are the color of the silt itself, and they seem to know the silt, to move through it with a confidence I do not share. They have left tracks — a faint scrollwork in the surface film where they have passed — and I have followed these tracks for some distance without ever seeing what made them. I think they are the first citizens. I think they were here before the fronds had fully unfurled. They are the ones who turned the mineral ooze into something that could hold a root. They are ancient and small and patient and I am standing on their work.

The flat things that move across the stiller pools — the almost-shadows — I think are not one animal but many, layered. They ride the surface tension the way some insects ride the surface tension of puddles on Earth, but they are larger, and what holds them up is not quite surface tension as I understand it. The water here is thicker. Everything here is thicker. The air, the water, the light, the time. They skim across the pools with a motion that is less like swimming than like a thought traveling across the surface of a mind. When one of them turns — and they do turn, with a deliberation I find unsettling — the wake behind it takes a long time to fade. The water closes over their passage slowly, reluctantly, as though it remembers them.

On the fronds, the things I will not brush from my arms: they are small and numerous and have no opinion of me that I can detect. They move when I move, and stop when I stop, and their relationship to me seems to be one of indifference so complete it has crossed over into something I can only call courtesy. They are not parasites. I do not think they are feeding on me. They are *using* me — as a substrate, as a moving point in a landscape they are themselves mapping — and the use is so casual, so lacking in malice, that I find I cannot object to it. One of them, larger than the others, has settled on the back of my hand and is doing something with its underside that I can feel as a faint pulse. I think it is tasting me. I think it has found me uninteresting. I think it is waiting for me to do something.

In the canopy, the source of the croaking: I have caught glimpses. A throat that swells and deflates. A membrane that flashes a color I have no word for — somewhere between the violet of the frond-undersides and a blue that does not occur in skies I have known. An eye, large and wet, that watches me with an expression I cannot read because the face it sits in is not arranged for the expressions I know how to read. They are bigger than I expected. Some of them, I think, are quite large. The thrumming I feel in my sternum is coming from them, or from something very near them, and the thrumming is not vocalization as I understand vocalization. It is more like circulation. The whole canopy is a pump.

What I have been calling the hum in the substrate — the vibration I feel through my boots, in my teeth — I have stopped thinking of as a sound. It is, I now believe, a *metabolism*. The pale earth is not dead rock waiting to become rock. It is a medium through which something is being moved, slowly and continuously, the way blood is moved through a body. I think the mounds are organs. I think the channels are vessels. I think the heat I feel rising through the soles of my boots is a warmth generated from within, the way the warmth of my own body is generated from within, and that I have been standing on the skin of a very large thing this entire time without realizing it, the way a louse lives on a host it does not comprehend.

This changes what the things in the canopy are. They are not animals in a forest. They are something in a body. I do not know what to call them. They might be to this place what my white blood cells are to me. They might be what thoughts are to a brain. They might be parasites on a creature so large that the creature does not know they are there. I cannot tell. What I can tell is that when I stand still, the croaking slows, and when I move, the croaking rises, and there is a feedback between us that I find I am unwilling to break. I have started to move very slowly, in part to avoid disturbing them and in part because moving slowly is the only speed that feels honest here.

There is a larger thing, somewhere very close, that breathes.

I have not seen it. I have only the breath, which arrives in slow warm gusts that move the fronds near my face, and the displacement of the air against my skin, and a sense of scale that I cannot source — a sense that what is breathing is *big*, big in a way that makes my categories falter. Big the way a hill is big. Big the way a weather system is big. It is breathing with a rhythm that has nothing to do with the rhythm of the croaking in the canopy, and nothing to do with the rhythm of the dripping from the fronds, and I think it is breathing on its own time, on a time so much slower than the rest of the place's time that I would not have noticed it at all if I had not been standing still.

I think it is the place.

I think the place is one creature, and the fronds are its hair, and the channels are its veins, and the things in the canopy are the thoughts it is having about the weather, and the pale ooze under my boots is the inside of its skin, and the hum I feel in my teeth is the sound of its digestion, and the thing breathing near me, the thing whose breath moves the leaves by my face, is the creature itself, attending to me at last, having noticed that something foreign has come to stand on the surface of its body.

It has not yet decided what I am.

I am trying to be very still. I am trying to be a piece of drift, a fallen fruit, a thing that belongs here in the way a stone belongs in a stream — by having been here long enough that the place has stopped noticing me. The small pale things in the silt have continued to move around my boots. The flat shadows have continued to cross the still pools. The things on my arms have continued their patient, indifferent use of me. The croaking in the canopy has resumed, though at a lower volume, as though the canopy has decided I am not a predator. The hum in the substrate has not changed. The breath of the large thing has not changed. The dripping has not changed.

I do not think this place will ever finish becoming itself. I think it is in the act of becoming itself the way a wave is in the act of becoming itself — never the same twice, never arriving, always mid-arc. I think the things that live here are not residents of a place but *passages* of a place, momentary configurations of a body that is always rearranging itself. I think the pale things in the silt will be something else tomorrow, and the flat things on the pools will have dissolved into the water and become part of the next creature that crosses, and the things in the canopy will have laid their eggs in the fronds and the eggs will hatch into the fronds and the fronds will feed the canopy and the canopy will feed the hum and the hum will feed the ooze and the ooze will feed the pale things and the cycle will continue without ever being the same cycle twice.

I am a foreign substance here. I have always known this. What I am only now beginning to know is that the foreign substance is welcome, in the way that any new input is welcome to a system that is still learning what it is. I have been tasted. I have been hummed at. I have been breathed on. The place has taken my measure and is continuing to consider me, and I find that I am content to be considered, and that I am in no hurry to be classified, and that the warmth on my skin and the damp in the air and the sound that is the sound of one creature being many creatures are, for the moment, enough.

Something breathes out slowly near me, and the leaves by my face move, and I wait, and the place continues.
