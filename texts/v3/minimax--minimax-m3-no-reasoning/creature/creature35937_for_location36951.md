The Condensery is inhabited.

# The Inhabitants of the Condensery

## The Machine Itself

If it lives at all, the machine lives. The central sphere does not respond to touch or presence, but its contractions are not the contractions of cooling metal. They are too deliberate, too patient, too much like the rising and falling of a chest. It has been breathing in this dip in the land for longer than the reeds have been growing, longer than the shrimp have been leaping, longer than the moss has been spreading blue along the warm pipes. Whether it was built or grown or something else, it is the oldest thing here, and everything else has arranged itself around its rhythm.

It does not think. It does not care. But it works. Deep inside it, gears grind against something — liquid, perhaps, or gas, or something with no name — and the sound of that work is constant and uninterruptable. It will do what it does after the flying things are gone. It will do what it does after the shrimp have stopped leaping, after the moss has crusted to brown and the ceramic has gone cold. It is the only permanent resident.

## The Shrimp

They are almost not there. Each one is no longer than a finger joint, almost transparent, the color of weak tea, with legs so fine they vanish when the shrimp is still. They live in the mat of reeds in such numbers that the mat seems to move on its own between breaths.

Their lives are ruled entirely by the sphere. When the sphere contracts, they leap — every single one of the thousands visible, in a wave that radiates outward from the pool's center, a synchronous pulse of small wet arcs. When the sphere expands, they vanish back into the water or onto the stalks. They have no other behavior. They eat, perhaps, but you cannot tell what they eat or when. They mate, perhaps, but you cannot find the eggs or the young. What they are doing, between the leaps, is opaque. They exist primarily as a rhythm — a visible, countable rhythm — and secondarily as small wet creatures.

They will land on you. They do not know you are a person. They do not know you are anything. They are seeking warmth, or motion, or simply the next stalk, and you happen to be in the way.

## The Moss

It is not quite alive, and not quite not. The electric blue that runs along the warm sections of pipe is more like a chemical reaction than a plant — a precipitate, perhaps, fed by the heat and the metals, growing in slow fuzzy rings that brighten when the sphere contracts and dim when it expands. But the rings *spread*. New blue appears at the edges, half a millimeter a day. Old blue browns and crusts and dies. Somewhere between growth and decay it has crossed into something that resembles living.

It smells like a battery because it is, in a sense, a battery — a slow leakage of charge from the warm metal into the damp air, made visible. Touch it and your fingers come away smelling of copper and ozone and faintly of nothing. It does not mind being touched. It does not mind anything.

It will outlive the moss at the colder sections, which are already half-dead, and it will outlive the shrimp if the sphere ever stops. It is patient in the way only chemistry is patient.

## The Flying Thing

There is one of them, or there are several — you cannot tell, because they circle at a height where the mist eats the outlines, and they are the same color as the mist. Broad wings, slow beats, a body perhaps the size of a large dog. Pale gray. Almost invisible against the gray sky.

It is waiting.

It cries once, at unpredictable intervals — a long descending moan that is louder than the machine and whiten your vision for a half-second — and then it is silent again, circling. It cannot land. The reeds will not hold it; the water is the machine's; the pipes are too hot or too slick. It is exiled from the place it circles, locked out of the only niche available to a thing of its size in this dip in the land.

What it eats, you never learn. Whether the machine ever exhales something it can swallow, whether it drops on the grazer at the base of the sphere, whether it simply waits and one day does not circle anymore — these remain unknown. It is the saddest thing here. It has committed itself to a place that will not let it in.

## The Grazer

You only saw it once, in a slant of light, pressed against the warm ceramic at the base of the sphere, rocking with each breath. A dark shape, larger than a person, slow-moving, shapeless in the way that things in water often are. It was the color of the water but thicker. It might have been a single animal or it might have been a colony. It might have been eating the moss, or the ceramic, or the slime at the waterline. It might have been keeping the ceramic warm for the moss to grow. It might have been keeping itself warm against the ceramic.

It did not look at you. You do not think it could see you. You do not think it has eyes. It simply pressed against the warm thing and rocked, and the sphere breathed, and the shrimp leapt, and the moss pulsed blue, and the flying thing screamed, and the grazer went on.

## The Place Itself

The dip in the land is inhabited too. The mist is not weather — it is what the machine exhales, condensed and held by the reeds and the low ceiling of cloud. The pool is not rain-fed; it is what drips off the pipes, what wells up from beneath. The reeds are not native; they are what grows in the runoff of something that has been breathing for a very long time. The whole dip is a byproduct. A consequence. The slow accumulation, over years or centuries, of everything the machine has let go.

You walked into a lung. A single lung, breathing in a dip in the ground, tended by small things that ride its breath and graze against its warmth and circle above it waiting to be let in. The machine does not know they are there. The shrimp do not know the machine is there. The moss does not know anything. The flying thing knows it is hungry and is not allowed to land. The grazer knows warmth, and that is enough.

You left. The breathing went on.
