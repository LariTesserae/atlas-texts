The city is inhabited, but only in the way an unfinished thing can be inhabited — by those who are still in the act of finishing it.

They are not gathered to be met. There is no square where they congregate, no tavern whose noise reaches you from around a corner, no bell rung to call them in, no child called home from a street that has no children in it. What you find instead is a population distributed across the work that is being made of the place — one at a task here, one at a task there, each in their own pocket of effort — and the people are not separable from the city. They are the city's ongoing motions. They are the way the unfinished stone is moving, in itself, toward whatever it is becoming.

The first inhabitant you meet is the cat.

The cat crosses the far end of a street and it does not look at you. It does not need to. It moves with the self-possession of a creature that was here before you arrived and intends to be here after, and it reminds you, by the cleanliness of its paws and the silence of its footfalls and the unhurried certainty of its path, that this place belongs to it as much as it belongs to anyone. It disappears around a corner. You do not hear it go. You do not need to. The cat is the city's truest inhabitant, because the cat is not working on the place — the cat is in the place, fully, with no instrument but its own presence, and when it has passed the street is the cat's street for as long as the lamp remembers to be warm.

After the cat, the work.

There is a chisel-worker somewhere ahead of you, in the unfinished parts of the undercroft, and you do not see them. You hear them. The sound is irregular, almost gentle: chisel on stone, pause, chisel on stone, pause. The pause is not for breath. The pause is for the workman to look at what has just been done and decide what to do next. You walk toward the sound, and the sound does not get louder. The chisel-worker is in some part of the city you will not reach, or in some part you will reach but in which you will find no one — only a chisel laid down on a step with its iron not yet rusted, and a curl of stone beside it the color of cheese that ages in cellars. The chisel-worker is the city's most constant inhabitant because the chisel-worker is the city itself, in the act of becoming itself. The sound of the chisel is the sound of the city's thinking. You hear it the way you hear the breathing of a sleeper — as a sound that is not addressed to you, but that is all around you, and that is what it is.

The smith is the same, only made of iron.

You smell the smith before you see the smith — the hot-iron smell threading through the damp and arriving ahead of the orange that spills from the doorway of the forge. The bellows breathe. A hammer falls, once, and not again. You do not go in. The smith is in there, working iron that is not yet quite red, and the smith is in no hurry, and the iron is in no hurry, and the bellows breathe and breathe, and the city is being made a little more iron-strong in that one place. The hammer fell once. You heard it. It was enough. The smith is not waiting for you. The smith is in the middle of iron, and the middle of iron is a long place, and you do not enter it.

The man on the ladder is in the middle of mortar.

He is on a corner, on a ladder, with a trowel. He is repointing a façade, and the lime he is laying is paler than the stone it joins, and he is singing as he works — very quietly, a song you do not know, in a language you do not recognize. He does not see you. He is intent on the work, and the work is in his hands, and his hands know what they are about, and the song is the song his hands would sing if his hands could sing. You pass. He does not look up. He is not unkind. He is simply in the middle of what he is about, and what he is about is the long slow work of making the city a little less unfinished in that one place. The song is for himself. The mason is for himself. You are passing through.

The woman in the lamplight is past.

You see her through the empty rectangle of an absent wall — the upper story of a building only half-built, the beams squared and pegged, the walls not yet raised between them — and she is a shadow crossing the lamp-light of the next street over. She walks without hurry. You do not see her face. You will not. She is past. The space she passed through is empty, and the space was always going to be empty again, and she was always going to pass through it, and the city was always going to let her.

Then the traces.

A pair of boots outside a door. A broom leaning against a wall. A discarded sandal in a doorway. A window with a shutter half-open, and through the half-open shutter the sound of someone breathing in sleep — slowly, evenly, the breathing of a person who is not waking up because there is no reason to wake up yet, because the city does not require them yet, because the city is patient with them as it is patient with itself. A cup on the arm of a chair outside a building, and the cup is empty and dry, and the chair is dry, and the dampness has not yet reached them. A pile of grain sacks against a closed door, eight of them, and the grain is beginning to fuzz at its edges with a pale and uncertain mold — time, you understand, has started to touch even here, but only just, and the mold is the city's first gray hair, and the years that will darken the stone are still in the future.

You notice, then, what is not there.

There are no children. There are no elderly. You have not seen an infant, you have not seen a stooped shoulder, you have not seen the slow shuffle of someone for whom the city has been here a long time. The city's inhabitants are in their working years, in their patience years, in the long middle of life when the hands know what they are about and the back is straight enough to bend to a trowel or to a chisel or to the leather straps of a bellows. They are the city's makers, and the city is making them too, and the two makings are not separable.

The water-drawer is among them.

You find them at the well, or rather you find the place where they were. The well has a wooden curb and a wooden cover, and the cover is shut, and on the cover there is a stone to keep it shut, and beside the stone there is a cup, and beside the cup there is a pair of gloves, and the gloves are damp. Someone was here, recently, and drew water, and has gone. You do not lift the cover. The dampness on the gloves is enough. The water-drawer was here, and the water-drawer is not here now, and the gloves are cooling in the lamp-light, and the cup is waiting for a hand that has already left it.

The objects are inhabitants too.

The barrels, stacked in threes and fours, against a wall. The handcart with its single wheel chocked by a wedge of wood, left across an alley-mouth. The coils of rope on hooks. The grain sacks with their pale mold. The chair. The cup. The boots. They are the city's other inhabitants — the ones who do not move, the ones who were placed, the ones who are waiting. The dampness is finding them. The lamp-light is finding them. Time is starting to find them. But not yet, not fully. They are patient, as the people are patient, as the stone is patient.

The cat is the patientest of all.

It has gone, somewhere — into one of the alleys you cannot see down, or under one of the hand-carts, or into a doorway that is not closed against it. It is not making a sound. It is simply where it is, and it will be where it is tomorrow, and it will be there when the chisel-marks have been made into walls and the walls into buildings and the buildings into the city that the city is becoming. The cat is the city's constant. The cat is what the dampness would be if the dampness could move.

You go on. The chisel-worker is ahead. The smith is at the bellows. The mason is singing in his language. The woman has passed. The water-drawer is somewhere, drawing water. The sleeper is breathing. The city is patient with all of them, as it is patient with you, and you walk through it the way you would walk through a sentence someone is still composing — carefully, not wanting to disturb the order of the words, knowing that the next word is not yours to choose.
