**The Wheels**

Forty-three wheels live here.

They are not alive in the way that you are alive. They have no lungs, no blood, no hunger. They do not reproduce, do not dream, do not die in any sense a biologist would recognize. But they persist, and they have persisted for nine thousand four hundred and twelve years, and persistence in a machine is a kind of life — or is, at least, the only kind of life this place will admit.

They hang in the air. Each is suspended from an axle that runs through the ceiling and continues upward into a darkness no one has bothered to enter. They hang at different heights, in a rough spiral like the chambers of a nautilus shell, each one slightly larger than the one above, each turning at a slightly different speed. The smallest, near the apex of the dome, is the size of a dinner plate and turns too quickly to follow. The largest, in the center of the room at chest height, is the size of a millstone and turns so slowly that a single nick in its rim becomes your clock hand. You watch it. You wait for the nick to appear. You wait for it to disappear. You wait for it to appear again. This is how the room tells time, and this is how the wheel tells you it is still here.

They are made of a dark, heavy metal that is not quite iron and not quite stone — the grain of one and the temper of the other. They are old. The earliest of them, you can tell by the wear, by the patterns of nicks, have been turning since before the current workers' grandparents were born. Each face is inscribed with concentric rings, manufacturing marks, the lathe-work of a workshop that no longer exists. Each one has a different geography of damage. Some are nearly perfect — circular, true, holding their tolerances after millennia of rotation. Some look as though a chisel has been taken to them in anger, or grief, or some emotion the metal could not have provoked but that someone, evidently, felt. One, near the bottom of the spiral, has a wedge cut clean out of it and still turns, the missing piece compensated for by some stubbornness in the mechanism. It has the air of an animal that has learned to limp without complaint.

You do not want to say that they are alive. But you cannot quite say that they are not.

**The Specimens**

On the iron shelves that line the walls, in their sealed glass jars, the specimens live. They are the only things in the room that do not move.

They are not plants. They are not fossils. They are not minerals. They are the shape that life would have if life were made of glass and patience and given no water at all — small branching structures, geometric, the color of old honey or of bone. The earliest ones, near the entrance, are dark and curled and brittle; they have the look of things that were once alive in some forgotten way and are now dead in a more permanent way. The later ones, deeper in the room, are paler and have a kind of crispness — coral-like, frost-like, the architecture of growth without the substance of growth.

They are cataloged. They are labeled. They are dated. The jars are sealed with metal lids. No one opens them. No one looks inside, except perhaps the workers on their rounds, and even they, you suspect, do not look. The jars are a record of something — of what, you do not know. They are entries in a ledger that has been closed. They are what the room grows in the absence of a garden, in the absence of soil, in the absence of water. They are the room's quietest inhabitants, and the room's most patient, and they will outlast you.

**The Keys**

The keys live here too, although a different kind of life.

They hang from hooks beside each shelf, in rings, each labeled with a number. They are brass. They are tarnished. They have no locks that you can find anywhere in the room. They are not for opening. They are for *counting*.

A worker comes. You do not see them. You hear them, perhaps, in the corridor beyond the panel. They unhook a ring. They walk the length of the room. They walk it back. They hook the ring up again. This is the audit. This is how the room is known to be still operating — by the periodic movement of small brass objects across the floor. The keys are touched. The keys are moved. They are, in their small way, the most handled things in the room, the only things any hand picks up with any regularity. They have the patina of use. They have the dignity of routine.

You wonder, sometimes, who counts the counters. You wonder whether the workers are themselves counted, by some larger mechanism, by some even more patient audit. You decide this is not a useful line of thought. You stop thinking it.

**The Light**

The light lives here.

It enters through a small aperture in the apex of the dome, lined with glass, and falls in a single hard shaft onto the largest wheel at the center of the room. The light moves as the wheel moves. It travels across the face, illuminating first one concentric ring, then the next, in a slow rotation of brightness. The light is, in some sense, the wheel's companion. They have been turning together for longer than you can imagine. The wheel would perhaps still turn without the light. But it would turn differently. It would turn in the dark.

The light is not always the same light. When the weather is clear, it is white. When there are clouds, it is gray. When there is dust — and there is always dust, this high up — the light is amber, and the wheel turns amber with it, and the whole room takes on a brief warm quality that feels, in this cold and metallic place, almost unbearably tender. The amber light is the room's only kindness. You find yourself waiting for it. You find yourself grieving when it passes.

At night the glow-plates take over. They are not lamps; they have no filaments, no flames. They give off a sourceless bluish radiance, fed by something you cannot identify, and they have been burning since the day they were installed. They do not flicker. They do not warm. They are the room's quiet sentinels, the small persistent eyes that keep the dark at bay. You do not know what feeds them. You suspect no one knows. You suspect the question is the wrong shape.

**The Cold**

The cold lives here. It is specified. It is not a malfunction.

Five degrees, plus or minus a tenth. The cold of a wine cellar or a deep pantry — dry, still, slightly mineral. Your breath does not quite cloud. Frost does not form. But the cold is everywhere, and the cold is what keeps the metal honest. The wheels are turning against each other at fractional tolerances. If the room were warm, the metal would expand, the tolerances would close, the wheels would seize, and the machine must not seize. The cold is not an environment. It is a specification. The cold is the medium in which this machine can continue to exist — a kind of life-support for the wheels, ambient and impersonal, as steady as the rotation itself.

After a few minutes you learn the cold is not unpleasant. It is the cold of clarity. It is the cold of attention. After a few more you realize you are cold in a way that has nothing to do with temperature — cold with the room, cold with the metal, cold in a way the room accepts and has been accepting for a very long time.

**The Sound**

The sound is, perhaps, the principal inhabitant.

It is not loud. It is a condition. It is the standing pressure of forty-three bearings turning at once, layered and re-layered — tick-and-hum, tick-and-hum — of axles in sleeves, of the rack-and-pinion feed that governs them all, the inside of a music box being played by something with no hands. Underneath it is the groan: the groan of the sheathing, the dome, the floor, all flexing infinitesimally with each rotation. The mountain itself participates; the basalt beyond the sheathing carries the vibration outward, and you can feel it in the soles of your feet if you stand still.

The sound has a centering effect. After a few minutes your breathing syncs to it. Your pulse, perhaps, begins to follow. The sound is not in the room; the room is in the sound. The sound is the room's breath, its exhalation, the proof it is still here, still working, still turning. The placard says the noise is correct. The noise is the point. You do not request silencing. You would not, you realize, even if you could. The sound wants to be inhabited. You inhabit it.

**The Sheathing**

The sheathing lives here. It is the room's skin.

Bolted to the basalt in long overlapping bands, gray-green, rust-streaked. It hums when you touch it — a low, tired, working hum, the way the hull of a docked ship hums against its ropes. It has the look of some patient armored creature, scaled, weathered, patient beyond any biological patience. It is the membrane between the machine and the mountain, the second skin that keeps the cold in and the weather out. It is the oldest surface in the room — older than the wheels, perhaps older than the workers, perhaps as old as the room itself.

It is what you noticed first, on the approach: the metallic taste in the air when the wind scraped it, the way it disappears into the rockface as though it were never there once you leave the gorge. It is the room's interface with the world. It is the room's face.

**The Workers**

The workers live here. You do not see them.

You hear them, sometimes, in the corridor beyond the panel — footsteps, a tray, a cough, the faint mechanical yawn of someone beginning a shift. You sense them. You know they are there. They wear heavy coats and fingerless gloves. They move with the brisk economy of people who do not intend to linger. They count the keys. They update the ledgers. They keep the cold at 5°C. They keep the oil flowing. They keep the wheels turning.

They are the only biological inhabitants — the only things in the room alive in the way you are alive, with lungs and blood and hunger and the slow accumulation of years. And they are, somehow, the least present. They are the ghosts. They flicker at the edge of perception, heard and not seen, present and not witnessed. The wheels are more present than the workers. The sound is more present than the workers. The cold is more present than the workers.

You do not know how many of them there are. You do not know how long they stay. You do not know what they think about, in the chamber, when they walk the length of the room and back with a ring of brass keys in their gloved hands. You suspect they do not think about it. You suspect that thinking about it would be the wrong shape. You suspect that they are, like the wheels, doing what they are supposed to do, and that the doing is enough.

**What Lives Here**

What lives here is a community of persistence.

The wheels persist. The sheathing persists. The sound persists. The cold persists. The light, in its slow rotation, persists. The specimens persist in their jars. The keys persist on their hooks. The ledgers persist in their stacks. The workers persist in their shifts. The mountain persists around all of it.

Nothing here is alive the way a forest is alive, with growth and decay and the messy abundance of biological time. Nothing here is alive the way a city is alive, with movement and noise and the constant churn of human want. What is alive here is something else — something that has been working, continuously, for nine thousand four hundred and twelve years, and that will go on working, presumably, for as long as the mountain stands and the sheathing holds and the cold is maintained at its specified tolerance.

It is a life made of metal and oil and patience. It is a life made of routine and audit and the periodic movement of small brass keys. It is a life that produces nothing and that does not need to produce anything, because the producing is not the point. The point is the turning. The point is the noise, which is correct, which is the point.

You stand in the chamber for a long time. The light moves across the face of the largest wheel. The wheels turn. The sound holds you.

You do not, exactly, belong here. But you are, for as long as you stand, part of what lives here. Your breath syncs to the room. Your pulse, perhaps, begins to follow. You are a temporary inhabitant of a permanent place. You will leave. The room will not.
