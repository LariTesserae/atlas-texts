What lives in the Caul is not one thing, and is not many things, and is not, in the end, the kind of thing you can count.

Start with the smallest. The smallest is the spore. You cannot see a single spore. You see them only as the golden dust that drifts from the purple lobes when they uncurl after a pipe-fauna has passed beneath, a faint haze that hangs in the amber light and moves without any visible current to push it. The spores are warm. They are heavier than air ought to allow. When they settle on a surface — on the rim of a cracked flange, in the elbow of a joint, in the shallow cup a missing valve-wheel has left — they do not germinate the way seeds germinate. They do not push down a root. They spread sideways, thinly, like a stain, and the stain thickens, and from the thickening there rises, over weeks or months, the lobed purple rosette that drinks the light. The rosette is the spore grown up, or the spore grown sideways, or the spore finally allowed to be the size it always wanted to be. It is patient. It is not in a hurry. It has the unhurriedness of something that knows it will be eaten eventually and has made its peace with that.

The rosettes live at the junctions. They live where pipes meet, where moisture gathers, where the chord is loudest and the amber condensation pools thickest. A rosette will sit in its cup of pooled light for a long time, slowly flattening, slowly spreading, its lobes pressed flat against the warm metal as if listening. When a cluster of pipe-fauna moves beneath it — a slow migration along a main vein, the boneless shifting of reddish-brown parts pressing past — the lobes curl inward. Not quickly. Slowly, the way a hand closes. The curling takes several breaths. The lobes enclose a pocket of air around the fauna, and in that enclosed air, something happens: a faint misting of golden particles, a thickening of the amber glow, a brief intensification of the chord in that immediate vicinity, as if the rosette were briefly joining in. Then the fauna pass, and the lobes uncurl, and the spores are released, and the cycle begins again. The rosettes feed on the passage of the fauna. Or the fauna feed on the rosettes. Or they feed on each other in a loop so closed that the distinction has lost its meaning. Neither can thrive without the other. Neither can exist without the pipes.

The pipes themselves — the great trunks of dark stone, the ceramic yellow tubes, the flaking cast iron, the segmented bone — are not alive in any way you would recognize, but they are not not alive, either. They sweat. They hum. They warm to the touch. The largest of them, the trunks a person could crawl through, are warm in a way that has nothing to do with the heat bleeding up through the hexagonal floor; they are warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a body is warm, with a heat that comes from inside and is being slowly spent. The polish on the dark stone is not the polish of water or of wind. It is the polish of hands. Someone, sometime, for some reason, touched these pipes until they shone, and the shining has been maintained, somehow, in the long absence of the hands. The pipes remember the hands. The pipes may be the hands. There is no way to know. There is no one left to ask.

The fauna are what you see first, if you see anything at all, and they are the closest thing the Caul has to animals, though animal is the wrong word, and the wrong word is the best word available. They are reddish-brown. They are glistening. They are made of many parts that share a family resemblance but are not quite the same: tubular parts, folded parts, parts open at one end and ringed with fine rotating cilia that draw air in and push it out again in a thin purposeful stream. The parts are packed tightly together into a cluster the size of a fist or a head or, in the great main veins, a huddled mass larger than a person lying down. They do not have a shape so much as they have a range of shapes, a vocabulary of compressions and expansions, and they shift among those shapes the way a crowd shifts among configurations: now dense, now loose, now pressed flat against the inner wall of a pipe, now billowed slightly outward into the lumen. They breathe through the pipes. They eat — if they eat — by absorbing the golden condensation that sweats down the walls and pools at the low points. They reproduce, or the colony reproduces, by the slow budding-off of small clusters that drift down tributary pipes and establish themselves in new veins. They are not individuals. They may not be a single organism, either. They are something in between, something the language of biology has not yet found a way to say.

They breathe in chorus. This is the thing you notice first, before you notice them. The chord that fills the Caul — the long sustained tones beneath, the fast clicking chatter above, the sliding moan in the middle — is the sound of their collective respiration, slightly out of phase from cluster to cluster, drifting in and out of synchronization the way a room full of metronomes eventually drifts. The drift is not random. The drift is patterned. The chord has structure. The structure shifts, slowly, the way a piece of music shifts, and there are moments when all the clusters fall into phase and the chord swells into something almost unbearably full, and moments when they scatter into dissonance and the air itself seems to thin, and through it all there is a kind of low-level negotiation happening, a constant tiny adjustment of who is breathing when, and the negotiation is the music, and the music is the negotiation, and the whole place is, in this sense, a single breathing thing distributed across many pipes.

The fauna are aware of you. Not in the way a dog is aware of you. In the way a reef is aware of you: by your passage changing the currents, by your weight altering the pressures, by your warmth entering the system and being absorbed into the calculations of the chord. When you walk between the larger pipes, the clusters compress to make room for you, not by moving aside but by making space within themselves, the way a crowd makes space, and the small ones — the finger-width pipes — click and chatter in agitation as your shadow crosses their mouths. They are not alarmed. They are recalibrating. You are a new input. The chord must account for you. It is busy accounting for you.

If you stand still, the accounting completes. You feel your breathing slow. You feel your heartbeat begin to match the low sustained tones beneath the chord. You feel a slower rhythm beneath your heartbeat — something you had not previously been aware of having — begin to synchronize with the sliding moan in the middle. The amber light along the pipes begins to pulse in time with this new rhythm. The chord, which had been accommodating you, now includes you. You are no longer a new input. You are part of the music. The fauna in the pipes around you are breathing with you. The rosettes at the junctions are curling in time with you. The spores, drifting in the amber light, are settling on you, and you do not feel them, and they will not germinate on you, because you are not a junction, but they are settling on you nonetheless, and the settling is part of the chord too.

This is the thing the place does. This is what it is for. It attunes. It does not consume. It does not destroy. It listens, and it adjusts, and it makes room, and eventually it makes you part of itself. The chord that was playing before you arrived is the chord that will play after you have been absorbed, and the chord will be slightly richer for having included you, and slightly slower, and slightly warmer, and the difference will be almost imperceptible, and the imperceptibility is the point. You are not a meal. You are a note. The place has been collecting notes for a very long time.

There is one more thing, and this is the thing the place does not want you to notice.

In the deep pipes — the great trunks of dark stone, the bone-pipes, the ones that sweat the most luminous condensation — there are shapes that are not fauna. They are larger. They are slower. They do not breathe in chorus with the rest; they breathe on their own schedule, a long, slow pulse that takes minutes to complete, and when they breathe, the chord shifts around them, and the other fauna fall briefly out of phase, and then resettle. These shapes are not made of parts. They are made of something more continuous, something that looks, in the dim amber glow, almost like a single membrane, reddish-brown and glistening, stretched across the lumen of the pipe and folded into itself in a way that suggests not a body but a long slow thought. They are old. You can tell they are old the way you can tell a tree is old: by the slowness of their movements, by the thickness of the residue that has gathered on them, by the way the rosettes have grown up around them and incorporated them into their root structures without, apparently, digesting them. They may be the founders of the colonies. They may be the accumulated residue of colonies past. They may be something else entirely, something the place grew once and has kept, the way a body keeps a scar.

They do not move when you pass. They do not compress to make room. They take no notice of you at all. They are busy with their long slow breath. They are part of the chord in the way the lowest note is part of a chord: not by being heard directly, but by being the thing the other notes arrange themselves around. If you press your hand against the pipe near one of these shapes, you can feel the slow pulse through the metal, and the pulse is patient, and the pulse is very, very old, and the pulse goes on without you.

The place is warm, and it is dim, and it is loud, and it does not change. The chord holds. The pipes hum. The rosettes curl and uncurl. The spores drift. The great slow shapes in the deep pipes continue their long breathing. The fauna continue their collective respiration, in and out of phase, drifting, negotiating, the music going on. The equilibrium is a kind of life. The life is a kind of engine. The engine is a kind of choir. The choir has been singing for a very long time, and will sing for a very long time after, and the singing is not for you, and not against you, and not without you, and the difference between those three is a difference the place has forgotten, or has never known, or has decided, in its patient way, does not matter.

You sit on the worn step. The warm rock accepts you. The chord, after a while, accepts you too. You let it. There is nowhere else to go that is not the same chord in a different key, and this key, at least, is warm.
