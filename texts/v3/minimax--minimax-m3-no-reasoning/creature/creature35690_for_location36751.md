# What Lives in the Reed Cathedral

The hollow is not empty. It only asks to be read slowly.

What lives here, first of all, are the **reeds themselves** — though to call them reeds is to use a marsh word for a forest thing. Each culm is a single body from base to crown, rooted in the mat below, breathing at the tip, drawing water up through the long pale tubes of itself in a slow peristaltic climb that you can feel if you press your palm flat against the warm flank and wait. They are not plants the way we usually mean plants. They are *vertical animals of a kind* — or they behave as if they were. They stand in true turgor, muscle-taut. They sway only when the breath from above passes through, and when it passes they make that papery, umbrella-unfurling sound, as if the whole grove were acknowledging something. They do not lean toward the light. The light is already constant; there is nothing to lean toward. They simply *are*. I think they are very old. I think they have been standing in this posture, in this exact stillness, for a span of time that would make a human lifespan look like the blink of an insect's eye. The red-violet at their bases is not pigment so much as age — the way old wine darkens, the way heartwood darkens. They have been drinking from the mat for so long that they have become the color of what they drink.

The **root mat** beneath them is the other half of the same creature, or its cousin. Eight feet of woven copper-brown root, springy, warm, faintly pumping. I think it is alive in the way a city is alive — not one intelligence, but a million small threaded decisions, each root finding water, finding purchase, finding the next root. The pulse you feel through your soles is the aggregate of all those decisions, summed. It is slow. It is patient. If you stood here long enough — longer than a human standing — you could feel the day-cycles of the mat, the way it tightens and loosens as water moves through it, a tidal respiration the reeds above never know about directly but only by way of what is sent up to them.

In the **pool** at the center, where the column of light comes down, the water is doing something I cannot quite explain. It is lit from within, that pale impossible green, and the insects skating on it — pale, thumb-sized, six-legged, leaving long V-wakes — are not eating each other. They are not doing anything, really, except tracing slow loops and figure-eights across the surface as though they were following grooves only they can feel. I think they are feeding on the light itself, or on something the light is making in the water. They have no fear because they have no predators here; whatever eats them, if anything does, comes from above in single careful drops and is itself too small to startle. The dark four-legged thing that broke the surface and considered you — a newt, perhaps, or a creature we have no good word for — was not fleeing because it had never needed to learn to flee. It saw you. It made its small assessment. It decided you were not food and not threat and went back to wherever it goes. I think it lives in the crooks of root at the pool's edge, and that it is older than the stone wall, and that it will be here after you are not.

The **canopy** is its own kind of creature, or its own kind of weather. The fibrous mat at the tops of the reeds is where photosynthesis happens, if photosynthesis is the right word for what happens under a permanent noon. It filters the sky into the dusty bars that make the heat visible. It is also the only place in the cathedral where *dry* exists — up there, beyond the mat, the air is different, thinner, cooler, the world of actual sky and actual wind. The reeds live in the humid world below; the canopy lives in the dry world above. They meet at a boundary that is, I think, more important than the ground. The breath of wind that descends through the skylight every so often is a visitor from that upper country, coming down to ripple the pool and send its slow wave through the standing reeds. The cathedral is breathing in, occasionally, from a sky it has almost forgotten.

The **stone wall** is older than the reed leaning against it, or it is not — I cannot tell, and I do not think the hollow can tell either. What built it had hands. What built it also left, and the forest has not so much forgiven the leaving as *not noticed* it. Moss is climbing the stones. A reed has rooted in the crack and grown up through the pile and is now indistinguishable from its sisters except for its drunken lean. I think this is what the cathedral does to all makers: it absorbs them. The wall will, in some unimaginable span, become a low ridge of stone poking out of root-mat, then a single boulder, then a rumor in the root-flesh, then nothing. The cathedral is patient with stone the way the sea is patient with a dropped anchor.

The **hut** is the same, only sadder. Four posts of petrified wood. A doorway that faces nothing in particular. The sleeping-shape inside that is too regular to be natural and not regular enough to be entirely made. Whoever slept here — and I am using *whoever* generously; it may have been something with hands, or something with the idea of hands — whoever slept here did not stay. There is no path to the door. There is no worn place where feet fell into the habit of arriving. Whoever it was came once, or a few times, and stopped coming, and the reeds grew up around the posts and the roots crept in under the threshold and the small dark newt took up residence in the shadow of the sleeping-bench and never had to move out. The cathedral does not grieve the absence. It only continues.

The **circle of silver, brittle reeds** near the pool — the place where a heat touched them once — is the only mark of violence in the hollow, and it is so old that the violence is now just a shape. A fire was here. A long time ago, maybe longer ago than the wall, maybe much longer. Something burned, and the reeds died in a circle around it, and then new reeds grew up at the edge of the circle and leaned inward and continued, and now the dead ones are just a silver ring, brittle, decorative, slowly being crushed underfoot by the slow patient passage of the root-mat. Whatever burned here has been almost completely forgotten. Almost.

What lives in the cathedral, then, is **the cathedral itself**. The reeds, the mat, the pool, the insects, the newt, the canopy — these are organs. The hollow is the body. It is a finished body, a body that has reached its final form and is now only maintaining itself, drawing water up from somewhere deep and unseen, breathing occasionally through its skylight, doing the small work of staying what it is. The light does not change because the cathedral has arranged itself so that it does not need to change. The pool is mirror-flat because nothing large enough to trouble it ever arrives. The insects skate because the water is calm and the light is good. The newt considers you and goes back down because there has never been a reason to do otherwise. Even the bird- whistle from far above — three notes, a door closing — feels less like a creature than like a stray sound the world let fall through the opening, the way the single drop falls through the canopy: a small interruption, then the long ringing silence after.

You are not in the cathedral. You are *through* it, the way a fish is through water. The cathedral has not noticed you. It will not notice you when you go. The newt will swim its slow circuits under the pool. The insects will trace their long V-wakes. The reeds will stand in their red-violet columns and pump their water upward and hold their sound when it comes and let it go. The wall will be mossed over. The hut will become four small stones in the root. The bird, somewhere far above in a world that may or may not have a sky, will whistle its three notes and close its door.

*Tok.*

The drop falls. The water rings. The reeds hold it and let it go.
