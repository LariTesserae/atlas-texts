The people of the Long Plateau are a small community — perhaps eight thousand in the winter census, swelling to ten or eleven in the brief summer when the contract miners come up to work the upper seams. They are not, by the standards of the lowland cities, a particularly varied lot. They are the descendants of the surveyors, the engineers, the foundrymen, the stonecutters, and the sappers who came up the cog rail in the first and second waves, and the people who came after to feed and clothe and house and teach and bury them. They have, over five generations, made a kind of regional face. They are broad across the shoulders and narrow in the wrist. Their lungs are larger than the lowland norm. Their skin, even in the children, carries a faint grey undertone that the local physicians have named, with the small professional pride of men who are cataloguing a new place, the *ashen complexion of altitude*, and which the local mothers simply call *cloud-tan*. They squint more than lowlanders. The sodium light flattens their expressions, and the cloud flattens their expressions further, and so the people of the town have a certain reserve to the face that visitors sometimes mistake for unfriendliness but is in fact only the habit of not having much to react to.

They live in the houses on the grid. The grid is, as has been noted, a lowland grid, a confident grid, a grid that does not know about the mountain, and the houses on it have accordingly had to be retrofitted, over the decades, to the realities of the slope. The houses on the upslope side of every street have a story that the houses on the downslope side do not have, a story cut into the stone of the foundation, a story the locals call the *root level* and which serves as a kind of cellar and a kind of retaining wall and a kind of quiet concession to the fact that the ground is not, in fact, level. The root levels are damp. They smell of stone and iron. The children play in them, when the weather is too foul even for the foul weather of the town to permit outdoor play, and the parents tolerate this because the children have to play somewhere. The root levels are also where the older members of the household sometimes sleep, in the summers, because the root levels are cooler. The oldest woman in the town, who is ninety-three and was born in the town and has never left it, sleeps in her root level year-round and says she will die there and not before, and the family has accepted this.

The houses are built of the local stone, which is a pale, banded limestone that weathers to an orange-grey and rings, when struck, with a bell-like note. The stone is good stone. The masons of the first three generations developed a vocabulary of cuts and joints for it that allowed them to build walls four and five stories tall without the use of internal timber framing, which was fortunate because the timber had to come up the cog rail and was ruinously expensive. The houses are roofed in slate from the upper quarries, and the slate is of a quality that has begun, in the last thirty years, to be exported down the rail to the cities below, where the architects pay astonishing prices for it, because the slate of the Long Plateau rings, too, and the architects down below cannot explain why this should be so, but they like the sound of it. The houses have chimneys, although the chimneys seldom smoke in the conventional way, because the furnaces of the town burn a low, almost smokeless coke, and what comes out of the chimneys is mostly steam. On the rare days when the cloud breaks, the chimneys of the town, seen from the observation deck, look like a forest of small, pale, vertical fountains, and the steam plumes go straight up into the deep blue and are gone.

The food of the town is a problem that has been largely solved, but only largely. The root vegetables — turnips, parsnips, a particularly tenacious variety of potato that was bred up on the plateau itself over four generations — do well in the thin soil of the upper gullies, where the townspeople maintain a strange, scrappy, terraced agriculture on plots that are, in some cases, only a few feet wide and a few feet long. There are small herds of goats, kept in pens at the edge of the town, that produce a strong, goaty milk and a strong, goaty cheese. There is a flour that is milled from a dwarf barley grown in the more sheltered folds above the second ridge. The flour is grey. The bread made from it is grey. The bread is dense and keeps for a long time, which is its primary virtue. The townspeople eat it in slices, fried in the rendered fat of the goats, and they consider it a normal food and a good food. Visitors do not always consider it a good food. The townspeople do not care what visitors consider.

The meat of the town is, in the main, goat. The goats are slaughtered at the foundry, where the waste heat of the furnaces is used to scald the carcasses, and the carcasses are then butchered in a long, low, stone building that smells, even in winter, of blood and of the strange, mineral tang of the local water. The meat is hung in the root levels of the houses to cure, and the cured meat is dark and dense and has a flavour that the townspeople describe as *full* and that visitors describe, with varying degrees of diplomatic restraint, as *acquired*. There is fish on certain days. The fish comes down the cog rail on ice, in boxes, from a fishery somewhere in the middle elevations, and it is a thing the townspeople look forward to and eat quickly, because the fish does not keep. There is, once a year, in the late summer, a brief, ritual, communal harvest of the red berries of the upper slopes, and from these berries the women of the town — and it is, by long custom, the women — make the sour jam. The jam is eaten on the grey bread. The jam is considered, by the people of the town, to be the best thing they make. They are not wrong.

The drink of the town is the water. The water comes down from the upper slopes in iron pipes and emerges, at the public fountains on every third street, in a stream that is clear and cold and tastes, faintly, of limestone. The water is the best water the people of the town have ever had, and they are grateful for it, and they drink it in quantities that astonish the visitors, who come from the lowland cities where the water is, by comparison, warm and flat and faintly sick. The water of the Long Plateau is the reason the town exists. The foundry was built where it was built because of the water. The miners drink it. The foundrymen drink it. The schoolchildren drink it at the long stone trough in the courtyard of the schoolhouse, and the trough has, on its lip, a worn inscription in the older language that no one in the town can read anymore, because the older language is not a written language, and the inscription is a kind of mistake, and the schoolchildren have made a game of trying to sound it out, and the game has been going on for so long that the inscription is the most worn feature of the trough.

The other drink of the town is the beer. The beer is brewed in a half-dozen small breweries in the lower town, from the grey barley, and it is a dark, thick, almost-black beer with a head that lasts and a bitterness that lingers. The beer is drunk in the beer halls, which are the social centers of the town, and which are open, in principle, from the end of the afternoon shift until the small hours, and in practice from the time the foundrymen finish their meal until the time the last of them is carried out. The beer halls are warm. The beer halls are loud in a way that competes with the loudness of the foundry, and that is, in fact, the reason for them. The foundrymen come off shift with the sound of the mountain still in their ears, and they need a louder sound, and the beer hall provides it. The beer halls are where the men of the town tell the stories about the mountain. The beer halls are where the herders will not take their animals above the second ridge. The beer halls are where the miners will say, in a low voice, that the mountain is not finished being made. The beer halls are where the foundry foremen, who are sober men by trade, come at the end of their shift and drink a single beer and listen and say nothing. The brewery workers, who are a guild of their own, look at the foundry foremen with a particular expression, because the brewery workers know that the foundry foremen know things, and the brewery workers would like to know them too, and the foundry foremen do not tell.

The work of the town is the work of extraction. The town is a mining town. The town is a foundry town. The town exists because there is, in the mountain, a particular ore that is not found, in the same form, anywhere else in the surveyed world, and that is, when smelted in the particular way that the foundry of the town has developed, a metal of unusual properties, and that the cities below will pay a great deal of money for. The miners go into the mountain. The miners come out of the mountain. The miners are paid in scrip that can be spent in the town and, with a small loss of face, in the cities below. The miners are the largest single occupational group in the town. The miners are also the shortest-lived occupational group in the town. The lungs of the miners are large, and the lungs of the miners are dark, and the miners die, in the main, of the lungs, in their fifties and sixties, in a way that the local physicians have named, with a certain grimness, *the long cough*, and that the local widows recognize, and that the local children are told about in a careful, matter-of-fact way, so that the children understand that the work of the mine is the work of the town and that the cost of the work is the cost of the work.

The miners are organized into shifts that correspond, roughly, to the canonical hours of the temple, and the shifts are worked by teams of four, and the teams are hereditary, in the sense that the sons of miners are expected to become miners, and the daughters of miners are expected to become the wives of miners, in a pattern that has held for five generations and that is, in the last ten years, beginning to fray, because some of the daughters of miners have begun to go down the cog rail to the cities below and have begun to send letters back, and the letters have begun to be read aloud in the beer halls, and the contents of the letters have begun to make some of the younger miners think. The foundry foremen do not like the letters. The foundry foremen do not like the thinking. The foundry foremen, who are appointed by the consortium that owns the mine and that owns the foundry and that owns, effectively, the town, have a quiet conversation about this once a quarter, and the conversation is the same conversation every quarter, and the conclusion of the conversation is always the same, which is that the thinking is not, at present, at a level that requires intervention.

The other work of the town is the work of keeping the town. The masons. The slaters. The plumbers, who are a small and overworked guild, because the iron pipes of the town freeze in the winter and burst in the spring and the plumbers are kept busy year-round. The carters, who move the ore from the minehead to the foundry and the slag from the foundry to the tailings. The bakers, who bake the grey bread. The butchers, who butcher the goats. The schoolteachers, who teach the children, in a schoolhouse that is, by the standards of the lowland cities, badly equipped, but that has, on the wall of the upper classroom, a large, carefully drawn map of the mountain, and a smaller, less carefully drawn map of the world below the cloud, and a still smaller, almost-lost map of the way the world is thought to look from the upper slopes, which is to say a map of the cloud, seen from above, which is a thing almost no one in the town has seen, and that the schoolteacher points to with a certain quiet pride.

The schoolteacher is one of the most respected people in the town. The schoolteacher is paid, by the consortium, in a way that suggests the consortium considers the school a necessary expense rather than an investment, and the schoolteacher, who is aware of this, has, over the years, evolved a curriculum that emphasizes the practical and the local. The schoolteacher teaches reading and arithmetic and the keeping of accounts, because these are necessary. The schoolteacher teaches the history of the mountain, which is a short history, and the history of the town, which is shorter. The schoolteacher teaches a kind of basic geology, drawn from the books of the geologists who came with the second wave and who left, some of them, a small library in the schoolhouse. The schoolteacher teaches the children to read the older language, or to try, because the schoolteacher considers it important that the children understand that there was a time before the town, and that the time before the town is still, in some sense, present, in the cracks of the cliffs and in the inscriptions on the trough and in the names of the gullies that the herders use and that are not the names the surveyors put on the maps.

The schoolteacher also teaches the children, in a way that is not in the curriculum, to look up. The schoolteacher does this on the days when the cloud breaks. The schoolteacher takes the children out of the schoolhouse and into the street and tells them to look up, and the children look up, and the children are quiet, and the schoolteacher is quiet, and the town is quiet, and the sky is the deep, bruised blue, and the sun is bright, and the shadows are sharp, and the small pale orchid on the iron fire escape is, for a moment, the most vivid thing in the world. The schoolteacher does not explain this. The schoolteacher does not need to explain this. The children will understand it later, or they will not, and in either case the schoolteacher has done what the schoolteacher can do, which is to make sure that the children have seen it, once, with their own eyes, and that the seeing is theirs.

The people of the town have a religion, or several. The census mentions three or four, depending on the year and the political climate, but in practice there is one temple that is always open, and the temple has a bell, and the bell rings at the canonical hours, and the canonical hours are the rhythm of the town. The temple is dedicated to a god whose name, in the older language, is a word that means *the one who waits*, and who is, according to the temple priests, patient, and according to the temple priests, present, and according to the temple priests, not yet finished. The temple priests are an import. The temple priests came up the cog rail with the third wave, sent by a lowland church that considered the Long Plateau a mission field. The temple priests have been here for four generations now, and the temple priests have, in that time, become something the lowland church did not entirely intend, which is to say, local. The temple priests speak the local dialect. The temple priests drink the local beer. The temple priests have, in the slow and unannounced way that institutions adapt, begun to incorporate, into the canonical hours, observances that are not in the lowland ritual, including a small, quiet ceremony at the second canonical hour of the morning, in which the priests go to the foundry gate and bless, in silence, the first shift of the miners as they go in. The lowland church, when it hears of this, is uncertain what to make of it. The lowland church sends, every few years, a visitor of higher rank, who attends the ceremony, and who writes a careful report, and who goes back down the cog rail, and who does not, in the end, forbid the ceremony, because the lowland church has, in its slow and considered way, decided that the Long Plateau is a place where the rules are negotiable, and that the negotiation is, perhaps, best left to the people who live there.

The temple priests are the only people in the town, apart from the foundry foremen, who are regularly in contact with the consortium. The temple priests write a quarterly letter to the consortium, and the consortium writes back, and the letters are, in the main, about money, because the temple, like the schoolhouse and the bathhouse and the root levels of the houses, costs money, and the money has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is, in the end, the mine. The temple priests do not write about the mountain. The foundry foremen do not write about the mountain. The schoolteacher does not write about the mountain. The miners, in the beer hall, will say that the mountain is not finished being made, and the foundry foremen, who are present, will not contradict them, and the brewery workers will pour another beer, and the silence that follows is the silence of a town that knows something it does not say.

The people of the town are, on the whole, a quiet people. This is not a comment on their temperament so much as on the noise. The noise of the town is such that a person has to speak loudly to be heard, and a person who speaks loudly all the time becomes, over the years, a person who does not speak unless speaking loudly will serve. The people of the town have, accordingly, a number of small, quiet habits. They communicate, in the streets, with a vocabulary of small gestures. They tip their hats, or they do not tip their hats, and the not-tipping is a message as clear as the tipping. They have a way, in the beer halls, of lowering the voice to a near-whisper that carries further than a shout, because the beer halls are so loud that the only way to be heard is to find a frequency the noise will not mask, and the near-whisper is that frequency, and the near-whisper is, over generations, becoming the natural register of intimate conversation in the town. Visitors find this unnerving. The people of the town do not understand why.

The people of the town are also, on the whole, a long-lived people, with the exception of the miners. The non-mining population of the town lives, on average, into the late seventies, and the women of the town, in particular, live into the eighties and beyond, and the oldest woman in the town, as has been noted, is ninety-three. The reason for this longevity is not clear. The local physicians attribute it to the water, and the water is, indeed, unusually pure. The lowland physicians, when consulted, attribute it to the absence of certain lowland diseases, which is also true. The older people of the town attribute it, in a low voice, to the air, and the air of the town is, indeed, a thing apart, being thin and damp and mineral, and being, according to the older people, *good for the blood*, and the older people have been saying this for five generations, and the older people may be right, and the older people may be wrong, and in either case the older people are old, and the older people are the people who remember the things that the foundry foremen prefer not to be remembered.

The things the older people remember include the first survey, which arrived before the town existed, when the mountain was just a mountain, and the surveyors came up the cog rail in a single car and walked the upper slopes with instruments and wrote down what they saw. The things the older people remember include the first pour of the foundation, which was a ceremony attended by the consortium and the priests and the lowland dignitaries, and which the older people will describe, if pressed, in a detail that suggests the older people were not, at the time, supposed to be there, and were, in fact, children, and were watching from a distance, and have, over the decades, polished the memory to a high gloss. The things the older people remember include the first time the mountain groaned, which was, according to the older people, in the winter of the second year, and which was, according to the older people, *a different sound from the sounds that came after*, and which the older people will not, in the beer hall, describe further.

The children of the town are, on the whole, a pale and wide-eyed and somewhat solemn lot. The children of the town grow up quickly, because the town is a place where children grow up quickly. The children of the town learn, before they learn to read, the sound of the foundry and the sound of the cog rail and the sound of the temple bell, and the children learn, before they learn to read, that the cloud is, on most days, a ceiling and not a sky, and the children learn, before they learn to read, that the upper slopes are a place the herders will not go, and that the cliffs above the foundry are a place the foundry foremen look at, sometimes, in a particular way, and that the small pale orchid on the iron fire escape is not to be picked. The children of the town learn, in the schoolhouse, the names of the lowland cities, and the children of the town learn, in the schoolhouse, the names of the gully plants, and the children of the town learn, in the schoolhouse, the alphabet of the older language, and the children of the town practice the alphabet on the worn lip of the trough in the courtyard, and the children of the town make a game of it, and the game has been going on for a long time.

The children of the town go into the mine, or they go down the cog rail to the cities below. The children of the town who go into the mine become miners. The children of the town who go down the cog rail become, mostly, small things in the lowland cities, clerks and shopkeepers and the wives and husbands of clerks and shopkeepers, and they send letters back, and the letters are read in the beer halls, and the letters are a kind of slow, low-grade pressure on the town, and the foundry foremen do not like the pressure, and the pressure continues. The children of the town who stay in the town but do not go into the mine become the other things, the masons and the slaters and the plumbers and the bakers and the butchers and the schoolteachers and the brewery workers and, occasionally, the temple priests. The children of the town who do none of these things become, in time, the people who stand in the streets on the days the cloud breaks and look up and are quiet, and who then go back into the cloud, and who do not, in the main, talk about it.

The foundry foremen are a small, separate, slightly apart class in the town. There are, at any given time, perhaps a dozen of them. They are appointed by the consortium. They live in the same houses on the grid. They send their children to the same schoolhouse. They drink in the same beer halls, although they drink less and later. They are, in most respects, indistinguishable from the other men of the town, except that they are paid in a different scrip, and that they have, on the inside of their jackets, a small brass badge that marks them as foundry foremen, and that the foundry foremen wear, at all times, in a way that the foundry foremen consider a matter of professional pride and that the foundry men consider a matter of professional resentment. The foundry foremen are the only people in the town who have been, in any number, down into the deep seams. The foundry foremen are the only people in the town who have heard, at close range, the low, intermittent groan that comes from inside the mountain, and that the miners have a name for but do not say where the foremen can hear. The foundry foremen are the only people in the town who have felt, at close range, the walls of the shaft shift by fractions of an inch, and the floor of the shaft lift by fractions of an inch, and who have, in the slow and private way of men who keep the things they know, made an accommodation with the knowing.

The foundry foremen do not, as a rule, talk about the accommodation. The foundry foremen do not, as a rule, talk about the mine, except in the technical language of their reports to the consortium. The foundry foremen do not, as a rule, talk to the miners about anything but the work. The foundry foremen do, in the beer halls, at the end of the shift, drink a single beer, and listen to the miners talk, and say nothing, and the not-saying is, in the slow and considered way of men who know things, the loudest thing in the room.

The miners, for their part, do not, as a rule, talk to the foundry foremen about the mountain. The miners talk to each other, in the beer halls, in the near-whisper that is the only register the noise will permit, and the miners tell the stories that are not in the official surveys, and the stories are stories about the upper gullies, and the cliffs above the foundry, and the lights in the cloud on certain nights, and the patterns the lights make, and the patterns the lights make are, in the slow and patient way of stories that are told because they are true, or because they are necessary, or because the telling is itself a kind of work. The miners are not, on the whole, a superstitious lot. The miners are, on the whole, a practical lot. The miners are, on the whole, a lot that has learned to step lightly on the upper platforms, and not to drop things from a great height, and to make the small adjustment in the shift schedules that the foundry foremen have made, and to build the gutters in the upper streets so that the water that runs uphill will run somewhere, and to do all of this without discussing it in front of visitors, because the miners have a sense, in the slow and unexamined way of people who have always lived in a place, that the place is theirs, and that the rules of the place are theirs, and that the rules of the place are not, in the end, for export.

The herders are the smallest occupational group in the town, and the oldest in a different sense, because the herders are the people who, by long custom, take the goats up onto the upper slopes, and the herders are the people who, by long custom, know the upper slopes better than anyone except the geologists, and the geologists are mostly dead or down the cog rail. The herders are the people who will not take their animals above the second ridge anymore, and who will not say why, except that the herders have a look in their eyes, and the look in the herders' eyes is a look that the people of the town recognize, because the look in the herders' eyes is the look of people who have seen something that the rest of the town has not seen, and who have decided, in the slow and private way of people who keep the things they know, that what they have seen is not, in the end, for export. The herders are the people who, in the late summer, lead the communal harvest of the red berries, and who are, during the harvest, the only people in the town who go above the second ridge, and who go, during the harvest, in a group, and who come back, after the harvest, with the look in their eyes somewhat lighter, and with the sour jam somewhat more plentiful, and with the silence of the harvest, which is a particular silence, still on them.

The animals of the region are not, in the main, the animals of the town. The animals of the town are the goats, and the dogs that herd the goats, and the cats that live in the root levels and catch the small things that rustle, and the pigeons that live in the eaves of the temple and that the temple priests feed, on the canonical days, in a ritual that is not in the lowland book. The animals of the region are the mountain goats, or the things very like mountain goats, that pick their way along the cliffs and look down at the town with the casual contempt of creatures who understand the terrain better than the people do. The animals of the region are the foxes, smaller and thinner than lowland foxes, with fur the color of wet stone, that hunt, in the evenings, along the tailings, and that the children of the town have learned to leave alone, because the foxes have a way, when cornered, of looking at the children with a particular steadiness that the children do not like. The animals of the region are the large, pale, almost-luminous birds of prey that ride the updrafts along the cliff faces and whose shadows, when they pass over the town, cause people to look up. The animals of the region are the small things that rustle in the gullies and are not often seen.

The animals of the region are also the others.

The others are what the townspeople mean when they say *the fauna* in a careful, neutral voice. The others are the large cat, perhaps, that has been glimpsed on the cliffs above the foundry, except that the cat has too many legs, or its legs are in the wrong places, or its shadow does not match its body, and the glimpsing is, in the slow and considered way of things that are glimpsed and not spoken of, a thing the foundry foremen prefer not to investigate, and a thing the miners, in the beer hall, will tell you about, in the near-whisper, and a thing the children, in the schoolhouse, will not be told about, except that the schoolteacher, who is a quiet subversive, has been known, on the days when the cloud breaks, to point, very briefly, to a shadow on the upper cliff, and to say, very quietly, *that one*, and the children have looked, and the children have seen, and the schoolteacher has not explained, and the children have not asked, and the seeing has been, in the slow and unexamined way of things that are seen because they are there, enough.

The others are the things in the upper gullies that move like goats and sound like goats and may be goats, except that the herders will not take their animals above the second ridge anymore. The others are the lights in the cloud on certain nights, and the patterns the lights make, and the patterns are slow, and the patterns are patient, and the patterns, when watched long enough, begin to look like a kind of writing, or a kind of weather, or a kind of attention. The others are not, in the strict sense, animals. The others are not, in the strict sense, not animals. The others are a problem, in the sense that the herders will not take their animals above the second ridge, and in the sense that the foundry foremen, who are sober men, will not, in the beer hall, contradict the miners when the miners say that the mountain is not finished being made.

The others are a problem in the sense that the rules of the place are, very gradually, becoming more local. The others are a problem in the sense that the mountain is rising, and the rising is not, in the end, a thing that can be exported, or explained, or contained. The others are a problem in the sense that the consortium, down below the cloud, would like to think that the Long Plateau is a mining town with some local colour, and the foundry foremen, who are the consortium's men, would like to think that the Long Plateau is a mining town with some local colour, and the miners, in the near-whisper, in the beer hall, after the shift, would like to think that the Long Plateau is a place where the rules are negotiable, and that the negotiation is theirs, and that the mountain, which is not finished being made, will, in its own time, finish being made, and that the finishing will be, in its own way, a kind of making, and that the making will be, in its own way, a kind of presence, and that the presence will be, in its own way, a kind of patience, and that the patience will, in the end, be the patience of the one who waits, and that the one who waits is, in the slow and unexamined way of a god who has been here longer than the town, present.

The people of the town are patient. The people of the town endure. The people of the town pour their smoke into the cloud, and grind up and down the cog rail, and ring the bells of the temple at the canonical hours, and bake the grey bread, and brew the dark beer, and make the sour jam, and send the children into the mine, and read the letters from the cities below, and look up, on the days the cloud breaks, and are quiet. The people of the town have, in the slow and considered way of a people who have made an accommodation with the place they live, a kind of dignity that the lowland cities do not understand, and that the lowland cities, in their slow and considered way, are beginning to be afraid of, and the fear is, in its own way, a kind of recognition, and the recognition is, in its own way, a kind of attention, and the attention is, in its own way, a kind of presence, and the presence is, in the end, the presence of a place that is becoming more itself, in a direction that no one, in the end, is entirely sure of, and that the people of the town, in the slow and unexamined way of a people who have always lived in the place, are willing, in the end, to wait for.

The lichen spreads, degree by degree, across the raw stone. The mountain rises, degree by degree, into its own weather. The animals watch, from the cliffs, with their too-many eyes or their correctly-numbered eyes, and they wait, because they have always waited, because the mountain is young and the rules are new and there is time.
