The chamber settles around you like a held breath.

First there is only the light, which is less light than a suggestion of light — a thin gray wash that arrives from somewhere above and diffuses into the water until you cannot tell whether you are seeing or remembering. The cold is the next thing. Not sharp, not biting, but total, the kind of cold that has been here longer than anything living and intends to stay. It enters you through the thin places — the wrists, the throat, the soft skin behind the ears — and it does not leave.

Then, slowly, the shapes resolve.

The ceiling is low here, lower than you would have guessed from the fissure's height. It is not stone, though it was once. A pale rind has crept over the rock, an encrustation of white and bone-gray that hangs in slow scallops and ridged drapery, like fabric that has been left to stiffen in mid-fall. From it descend the first of the structures.

They look like ribs at first — long, curving members, slightly tapered, that arch down from the ceiling and root themselves into the floor. But ribs imply a creature they belonged to, and there is no creature here large enough, no body vast enough to have worn this architecture. So they are not ribs. They are something built, though no one built them. They have the regularity of intention and the irregularity of growth, and you cannot quite tell, looking at any one of them, which it is. Each is a few inches thick, a few feet long, and they are spaced just far enough apart that the water between them moves in a different texture, a slightly softer current you can feel against your closed eyes if you hold still long enough.

The ribs multiply as you drift further in. They become a forest, then a thicket, then something that is no longer ribs at all but a kind of close-pressed scaffolding, a lacework of curving members that have fused where they meet and branched where they couldn't, and the spaces between them — which were the main feature at the entrance — have become the exception. You have to turn sideways. You have to exhale.

The floor is not visible. Neither is the far wall, or the other side of wherever you are. The structures press in from every direction, and in the dim, evenly distributed light, the architecture has no clear scale. A member that looked inches thick at the entrance might, in the interior, be the size of your arm, your leg, the trunk of a tree you once sat beneath in a place where trees grew. You cannot tell. The place has no perspective.

And it is growing.

You notice this because something brushes your ankle. Not a fish. Not a tentacle. A tendril — a single pale thread, no thicker than a hair, trailing from the base of one of the larger members into the open water. It is warm. It is the only warm thing here. Where it touched you, a small bright point of contact lingers, a memory in the skin, and when you look down you see that the member it trailed from is slightly fatter at its base than it was a moment ago, slightly paler, slightly less mineral and more something else.

It is laying itself down. Incrementally, imperceptibly, the architecture is being added to. The scaffold is thickening, members are joining, new ribs are descending from the ceiling in pale buds that have not yet hardened. The place is being built by being lived in, and the builders do not pause for you.

You do not see them clearly. They are the same color as what they make, and they move only when you are not looking, and you suspect, sometimes, that the pale threads you have been following are not threads at all but the edges of very small, very patient bodies, laying down the next fraction of a millimeter. There is no sound from them. There is no sound at all.

The silence here is not the silence of an empty room. It is the silence of a room so full that sound has no room to be. There is breathing, somewhere, you think — a slow communal inhale that barely displaces the water, an exhale that you feel as the faintest change in pressure against your eardrums. There is the click of something small against something hard, very far away, too soft to be sure you heard it. There is, once, a low vibration, felt rather than heard, that travels through the nearest member and into your shoulder and then is gone.

The cold tightens.

The light does not change.

You understand, floating here in the close architecture, that you are inside a thing that does not know it is a thing. It is a process. It is a process that has been going for longer than you have a number for, and it will go for longer than that, and the place you are in is not a place but a moment in that process — a moment, a fold, a soft layer of it, briefly occupied. The pale members are the ridges of its slow growth. The cold is the temperature of the long time it has been at work. The silence is the sound of a thing that does not need to make sound to continue.

You are very small here. You are a temporary warmth in a place that has been cold for a very long time.

You begin, very carefully, to look for the way out.
