# Who Lives in the Underline

They are called, in the fragments of municipal record that still exist in a clerk's filing cabinet in a building that no longer has a clerk, the *subcurials*. The name is a joke that was not meant as a joke, coined by an inspector in 1971 who never came back to verify it, and it has stuck in the way that names do among people who must refer to one another only in documents.

The people themselves have other names for themselves. The names are not spoken. They are not, in fact, sounds at all, but a pressure-change in the throat and chest that conveys meaning in a register slightly above the human hearing threshold. Visitors sometimes report a feeling of being addressed. Visitors are usually correct.

They are small. This is the first and most persistent thing that confuses the surveys. The infrastructure they maintain — the way the pipes are kept, the way the current is balanced, the way the upper platforms remain inhabitable against the inward creep of the damp — suggests a body roughly the scale of a competent adult human. The bodies themselves are roughly the scale of a competent adult squirrel. The discrepancy is the subject of an internal report that has been in draft for eleven years and is, the inspectors have learned to their various frustrations, unlikely ever to be completed. The subcurials do not consent to measurement. The subcurials do not consent to being looked at, in the direct sense; what a survey team sees is always a subcurial in the act of not-being-seen, which is a creature moving in the corner of the eye at a rate of speed that does not violate any laws but does strain the witness's willingness to file an affidavit.

They are, as best as the surveys can establish, *both*. Furred, in the way that the rats who share the platform edge are furred — a fine, dense pelt, gray-brown, that lies flat in dry air and lifts, visibly, in damp. Scaled, in the way that the salamanders in the drainage sumps are scaled — a fine, dark integument along the spine, the forearms, the backs of the hands, that glistens when the light is right and disappears into the fur when it is not. They have hands. The hands have, depending on the individual and the angle of observation, four or five or six fingers, and they use all of them, and they do not appear to consider the matter worth discussing. They have faces. The faces are not faces. They are the place where a face would be if the head were not, as it so often is, in motion. The eyes, when caught, are dark and very round and do not blink. The mouth, when caught, is a small downturn that may or may not be a mouth.

They are warm. This is the second most persistent thing that confuses the surveys. The station is cool, the way underground things are cool; the concrete is at the temperature of the earth around it, which is the temperature of the water moving through the earth around it, which is the temperature of the year. The subcurials are warmer than this. They are warmer by several degrees. They radiate. When a survey team stands near a wall-stack at the right moment, they can feel it on the skin of the forearms, a faint pulse of animal heat that does not belong to the geometry of the place. The thermal cameras show concentrations of warmth along the pipe runs, at the elbows of the conduit, behind the dwelling-stacks where the light does not reach. The warmth is not uniform. It migrates. It moves, slowly, through the wall the way the water moves through the wall, and the survey teams have learned to read the migration as a kind of traffic, a body heat traffic that tells them where the subcurials are going, which is to say, everywhere, on a schedule that is not the survey team's to read.

They live in the walls the way bees live in a hive, if the hive were a small city and the bees were also the city and the honey were a continuous negotiation with the damp. They maintain the pipes. This is not a metaphor, or it is not entirely a metaphor. The original six-inch main that runs the length of the hall has not corroded through in eighty years, which is a hundred and fifty years longer than the same pipe in any comparable aboveground installation, and the explanation that the inspectors prefer is that the mineral content of the water has formed a protective scale on the interior, and this is true, and it is also true that the scale is being applied, in a continuous and patient process, by small hands working in the dark where the pipe narrows enough to admit them. The smaller lines — the electrical conduit, the copper that someone ran in some unguarded decade — are similarly tended. The subcurials run along them. They pause at the joints. They make small sounds, ultrasonic, in the backs of the survey team's teeth, and the joints continue to function. No one has ever seen a subcurial repair a joint. No one has ever seen a joint in need of repair.

They keep the warmth. This is the strangest thing, and the surveys have, in the privacy of their internal reports, allowed themselves to be confused by it. The warmth is not a by-product of their bodies, or it is not only a by-product of their bodies; the place is too vast, the subcurials are too few, the math does not work. The warmth comes from somewhere in the strata, from somewhere in the bedrock and the structure and the patient negotiation between the two, and the subcurials are its stewards. They are the *medium* of the warmth. They move it through the wall. They release it, in the long dark hours, into the places where it is needed most, which are the upper stories of the dwelling-stacks, where the youngest of them sleep. The light that pulses in the upper windows is, the survey team has come to suspect, the warmth made visible — a faint orange in the very old bulbs, a faint orange in the chimneys, a faint orange in the bodies of the subcurials themselves when they are seen in the right light, which is almost never.

They do not speak. Or they do not speak to the survey teams. They speak, the subcurials, in the *sound*. The place is not silent. The place is full of sound, in registers the human ear was not built to receive; there is a high, thin, almost-broadcast quality to the air of the lower hall, a shimmer at the upper end of hearing, that the survey teams have, in the privacy of their after-hours conversations, described as *the chatter*. It is not the sound of animals in the walls, although it includes that. It is the sound of *the wall*. It is the dwelling-stacks in conversation with the pipes in conversation with the conduit in conversation with the subcurials in conversation with one another, a continuous low murmuring that does not aggregate into words but does, in a way the survey teams find difficult to articulate, *mean*. The meaning is not for the survey teams. The survey teams have learned, in the long years of their intermittent visits, to receive it the way a stone in a riverbed receives the river.

They are, the survey teams have come to understand, *old*. Not old as the place is old, the centuries-long patience of the structure; old as a people, as a continuous culture, as an unbroken thread of being that has run through this place since before the place was a station, since before the place was a station the place was a quarry, and before the quarry a worked seam of the earth, and before the worked seam an unworked seam, and at every stage the subcurials were there in the strata, in the cracks, in the places where the rock meets the water meets the air. They have been here in some form for as long as the place has been a place. They will be here in some form when the place is, in some future age, no longer a place. The dwellings on the wall are recent. The chimneys are recent. The cabbage-rose curtains, faded toward the dusk of pink, are very recent, scavenged from the demolished rooms of a building above that was demolished in a year the subcurials remember, the way the subcurials remember all the demolitions, with a small ceremonial adjustment to the upper stories of the wall.

They are not afraid of the survey teams. The survey teams have spent considerable effort, over the years, in establishing a protocol for this, in determining whether the subcurials' indifference is the indifference of unawareness or the indifference of knowledge. The latter, they have concluded. The subcurials know the survey teams are there. The subcurials have always known the survey teams are there. The subcurials were there before the first survey team, and will be there after the last, and the survey teams are, in the long arithmetic of the subcurials' tenure, a brief and unremarkable visitation, like the visitation of the water in the spring or the visitation of the cold in the fall. The survey teams are welcome. The survey teams are not waited for. The subcurials continue, in the survey teams' presence, to do what the subcurials do.

What the subcurials do is, in the surveys' best understanding, *maintain*. They maintain the pipes. They maintain the warmth. They maintain the dwellings, in the patient, ongoing way that one maintains a thing one has built and intends to inhabit for a long time. They maintain the lights, in the upper windows, which pulse with the respiration of whatever the subcurials have hooked into the conduit. They maintain the switch, the great iron lever with the porcelain handle, which is in the *on* position and has been for some time, and which the subcurials, on the nights when the survey teams are not there, attend with small sounds and small adjustments. The switch controls something. The something is, the survey teams have never been able to determine, *the place*. The place itself, its hum, its patience, its dim and ongoing *is-ness*. The subcurials are the ones who keep the switch in the *on* position. The subcurials are the ones who would, the survey teams have come to believe, know what to do if the switch were ever thrown the other way. The switch has never been thrown the other way. The survey teams have been told, in the private memoranda that travel up the chain to the people who must be told these things, that the switch is not to be thrown the other way. The survey teams have, in the long years of their work, come to agree.

They are, the surveys conclude, with the careful and slightly wistful precision of a long-running bureaucratic project, *a public*. They are a public that the public, above, does not know about. They are a public that the public, above, has chosen, in its wisdom, to bury and forget. They are a public that has, in the long interval since the burying and the forgetting, continued to be a public, in the patient and uninterrupted way that publics are publics, with their councils and their conventions and their slow traffic of small bodies through the conduits of a small city in a small place. They have a switch. They have, the survey teams suspect, an *agenda*, in the sense that any continuous people have an agenda, which is to continue. They have a place. The place is, in some quiet and uninterruptible way, *theirs*. The place is the way it is because they are the way they are. The place will be the way it is, long after the survey teams have stopped coming, long after the survey teams' offices have been closed and their clerks dispersed to other clerkships, long after the buildings above have been built and unbuilt and built again. The subcurials will be here. The switch will be in the *on* position. The lights will pulse. The chimneys will send up their thin smoke, which will rise a foot and stand and then, slowly, disperse.

The water will be here. The water will always be here.

The subcurials are very good at waiting. They have been waiting for a long time. They are, the surveys conclude, in the careful and slightly awed language of a long-running bureaucratic project that has, in the privacy of its internal reports, allowed itself to be *moved*, *not* in a hurry.
