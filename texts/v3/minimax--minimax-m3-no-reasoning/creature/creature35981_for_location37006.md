# The Inhabitants

They are not named, in any language that has survived long enough to matter. The surveyors who came and did not return left a single descriptive phrase — *articulata lithophila*, "the jointed stone-lovers" — and the name did not stick to anyone but themselves. So they are the inhabitants. So they are the small brown ones. So they are the click and the polish and the slow inward growth of a city that is, in places, beginning to become their city without their noticing.

An adult at full extension reaches roughly the length of a forearm. They rarely extend fully. The carapace is articulated into seven or eight visible segments, each plate fitted to its neighbors with the same microscopic precision the long-gone builders brought to their stonework, though this precision is biological and unconscious — the soft click one hears from a passing individual is the carapace's own version of fitted joints, a sound the inhabitants themselves seem not to register, the way a human does not register the creak of their own spine. The limbs — and there are many, more than the eye can comfortably count in a single passing individual, more than the early observers ever agreed on a number for — articulate beneath and beside the central mass in a way that suggests a body designed to flow along surfaces rather than across them. They move low. They move pressed to the stone. They move, when they move at all, with the unhurried intention of something that knows where it is going, even if no observer can tell.

The color is brown the way the basin is brown: not one brown but a layered geology of browns, a sediment of generations. A newly moulted individual, fresh from the ecdysis, is pale as bone, almost cream, and over the days of its new carapace the dust and the trace minerals of the place bind to the chitin and darken it. A juvenile is the color of damp sandstone. A working adult, in the thick of its colony's season, is the color of the pavement itself. An elder — and they have elders, though no one has ever identified the mechanism by which an elder is recognized — is nearly black, the carapace so dense with mineral accretion that the segments barely articulate, and the creature moves with a creaking, slow deliberation that sounds, from a distance, like wind over gravel.

They are numerous. They are numerous in the way water is numerous when it fills a basin: not because any single one is large, but because the basin is large, and they fill it. A plaza in the upper city, the kind that opens onto two other plazas that open onto two others, may hold in its various chambers and side-passages many thousands of them at any given moment — not crowded, not piled, but distributed through the available space with the patient efficiency of a fluid finding its level. They occupy the human-scale architecture without complaint, and without comprehension. A doorway is a doorway to them only because it has a width they can pass through in their preferred formation; a staircase is a staircase only because its risers are short enough that their limbs can find purchase on the edge of each tread. The proportions of the long-vanished builders were not theirs, but the proportions of the long-vanished builders happened, by chance or by design, to be close enough that the buildings could be inhabited by another species without significant modification. The buildings were not built for them. The buildings were not built against them. The buildings were simply left, and the small brown ones came, and the small brown ones stayed.

They stay because of the water.

In a place where the light gives nothing and the soil will not grow anything and the wind comes howling down off the plateau rim with a regularity that has shaped the dust into low crescent dunes against every westward-facing wall, water is the only currency. The condensation that gathers on the stone each evening, as the temperature drops and the air surrenders what it has been holding in suspension all day, is the difference between a habitable chamber and an empty one. The inhabitants know this. They know it in their bodies, in the chemo-receptors that line the underside of the central mass, in the way a colony will relocate — every individual, every brood, every scrap of stored material — over the course of a single night, if a chamber's humidity falls below some unverbalizable threshold. They do not understand the mechanism. They do not need to. They understand the gradient, and the gradient is enough.

A working colony arranges itself around the moisture the way a plant arranges itself around the light: not by choice, but by the slow accumulation of small movements, each individual orienting itself along the gradient, each generation born into an arrangement that has been refined by the previous generation's dying. The chambers closest to the surface, where the night's condensation pools deepest and lasts longest into the morning, are the favored chambers. They are the brood-chambers, the nurseries, the great pulsing centers of activity, where in the warm months the air is thick with the sound of soft clicking and the faint grinding of many bodies in transit. As the year moves toward its dry pole — and the year here has a wet pole and a dry pole, though both are arid by any other standard — the brood-chambers empty. The young disperse, following older individuals down the shallow spiraling ramps into the deeper excavations, where the water does not evaporate quite as quickly and the stone stays damp until nearly midday. An abandoned upper chamber does not stay abandoned. Within a few seasons the moisture returns — the deep pulses, perhaps, shifting the aquifers, or some longer geological cycle that the inhabitants cannot perceive — and the chamber fills again, and the colony flows back up the ramps like a slow tide, and the brood-chambers flare again with the sound of soft clicking and the faint grinding of many bodies in transit.

They have, it must be said, no language that any observer has been able to detect. No calls. No songs. No ceremonial postures. The sharp percussive sound one occasionally hears from a colony in dispute — a sharp tap, a crack, a single percussive note that carries down the avenues and echoes off the fitted stone — is not speech but announcement, the way a thrown stone is not speech but a thrown stone. It means *I am here*. It means *do not approach*. It means, possibly, *I am willing to be approached*. The inhabitants respond to these percussive notes with changes in gait, with small shifts in the orientation of their many limbs, with a kind of distributed attention that is difficult to describe because it has no analogue in any social species the observer is likely to have known. They are aware of each other. They are aware of each other in ways that do not require communication. The colony is not a conversation. The colony is a body, distributed across many thousands of small brown individuals, and each individual is a cell in that body, and the body feels what its cells feel, and acts on what its body feels, without any cell needing to know what the body is doing.

Their diet, insofar as the term applies, is mineral. They scrape the stone. They scrape the stone with small appendages near the mouth that are harder than the carapace and that wear, in the course of a working life, down to nothing and are replaced in the next moult. What they ingest is not, primarily, the stone itself, but the thin film of biological matter that lives in the pores of the stone — a slow-growing community of lithophilic organisms, bacteria and fungi and the occasional lichen, that takes years to colonize a fresh surface and that the inhabitants cultivate, without knowing they are cultivating it, by the simple act of leaving a polished floor alone for a generation. The polished floors of the deeper plazas, where centuries of passage have worn the stone to a mirror gloss, are not barren; they are cropland, and the crop grows in a cycle that no inhabitant understands and that the inhabitants nonetheless participate in, generation after generation, by the simple act of walking.

They do not maintain the structures. They cannot maintain the structures; their appendages are not the right shape for the work, and their intelligence — to the extent they have intelligence — is not the right kind of intelligence for the work. But they maintain the floors, in their way. They maintain the floors the way a river maintains its bed: by passing over them, by wearing them smooth, by finding the paths of least resistance and following them until the paths are no longer the paths of least resistance, at which point they find new paths. A plaza that has been heavily trafficked for a long time develops a topography of shallow grooves, each groove worn by a particular line of passage, each line of passage corresponding to a particular route between two chambers that the colony has found useful. In the oldest inhabited plazas, the grooves have worn themselves into something that resembles, at a glance, a written script — curving lines that branch and rejoin and sometimes terminate in small circular depressions that are the resting-places of generations of elders, gone to dust indistinguishable from the dust in the cracks. The resemblance to writing is illusory. The grooves mean nothing. The grooves are the bones of the colony, and the colony is the flesh that wears them.

They die. They die in the middens, in the designated chambers at the edges of the inhabited districts where the dying go to die, where the carapace stops clicking and the limbs stop articulating and the body slowly, over the course of many days, releases its accumulated mineral load back into the dust. The middens are not cemeteries. The middens are not revered. They are simply where the dying go, the way water goes downhill, the way a worn groove deepens. The dust in a midden, after a long habitation, is darker than the dust elsewhere, and slightly damper, and richer in trace organics, and the brood-chambers of the next generation are sometimes built, by the patient unconscious logic of the colony, on the middens of the previous one. The young are born into a soil that is partly their own ancestors. This is not remembered. This is not honored. It simply is.

And then there is the pulse.

In the deepest excavations, in the chambers no living thing enters — the chambers of the older red stone, the stone that catches light and throws it back in wavelengths the eye cannot quite process — something moves. Something pulses. The pulse is slow. The pulse is regular. The pulse passes through the rock the way the condensation passes through the air, and by the time it reaches the inhabited upper layers it has attenuated to a low vibration, a subsonic tremor that the small brown ones feel in the chemo-receptors of their undersides, in the way a deep swimmer feels the pressure of a depth they have not themselves descended to. When the pulse comes, the colony goes quiet. The clicking stops. The grinding stops. The percussive disputes are suspended. Every individual in the inhabited districts presses itself flat against the stone, carapace to pavement, limbs tucked close, and waits. The pulse passes. The pulse has been passing for as long as any inhabitant has been alive, and as long as any of their ancestors were alive before them, and as long as the buildings have stood over the older stone. The pulse is older than the buildings. The pulse is older than the basin. The inhabitants do not know what the pulse is. They do not ask what the pulse is. They press themselves flat, and they wait, and the pulse passes, and they rise, and they resume the business of being the small brown ones in the upper chambers of a city built by hands they have never seen.

What lives in the red stone chambers, in the too-dense water, in the deep places where the pulse originates — that is a different question, and the inhabitants are not equipped to answer it. The shadows that move in the deep water are too large for the spaces they occupy. The light that illuminates them is not the sodium white of the sky. Whatever is down there is not a neighbor to the small brown ones. Whatever is down there is a thing the small brown ones have never seen and will never see, the way a tide has never seen the moon. The pulse is the only communication that passes between them, and it is not communication. It is the older stone making its presence known to the younger stone, and the younger stone translating that presence, as best it can, into a vibration that the small brown ones can feel in the undersides of their bodies and respond to by going quiet and pressing themselves flat.

They are, in the long view, a small species. They are a small species in a small niche, in a small part of a basin that is itself a small feature of a plateau that is itself a small feature of a world. They will not outlast the city. The city will not outlast the basin. The basin will not outlast the plateau. The pulse will outlast all of them. But the small brown ones, in the meantime, are numerous, and they click, and they scrape, and they polish the floors of a civilization they will never understand into shapes that suit them, and in the evenings when the condensation comes they gather at the shallow concavities at the centers of the plazas and drink the water that falls from the bright white sky, drop by drop, and they are, for as long as the wet pole of the year lasts, sufficient.

The basin holds its breath. The small brown ones do not hold theirs. They go on breathing, in the upper chambers, in the polished plazas, in the middens, in the brood-chambers, in the damp and the dust and the long patient turning of the year. They will be here when the next survey comes, if a next survey comes. They will click at the surveyors' feet. They will press themselves flat when the deep pulse passes. They will go on, in their small way, being the inhabitants of a place that does not know their name, and that they have never bothered to learn the name of, because the place is not a place to them. The place is a body. The place is a body they live inside, the way a body lives inside its skin, and they do not need to know the name of the skin to feel the wind against it.
