Who lives there

The first thing to say is that they live *in* the water more than on the stone. A person born on the shelf spends the hours of the early part of life almost continuously wet, from the knees down, with the water finding its way up the calves and the inner thighs whenever the alleys slope, which is most of them. Their feet are broad and splayed and the toes are prehensile in a way no one on dry land would recognize as useful — they grip the wet basalt the way the crabs grip, and the calluses on their soles are a pale yellowish horn, thick as boot-leather, ridged in patterns that are as individual as fingerprints and are read the way fingerprints are read elsewhere, by the mothers and the aunties who share the alleys. The skin between the toes is pale and soft and perpetually macerated, and a person who has been off the shelf for any length of time loses the grip of the toes and has to relearn it, the way a fish-man ashore loses the ability to hold his breath. They do not, in fact, ever leave the shelf, and so the grip never has to be relearned. The feet are made for the stone and the stone is made for the feet and neither of them has changed since the grandmothers of grandmothers.

The people are small. This is the first thing an outsider would notice, and the second thing an outsider would notice is that they are all small in the same way, which is to say that the men and the women are of a height, within a hand's breadth, and the children are simply smaller versions of the adults in the same proportions, with the same long fingers and the same broad shoulders and the same slightly recessed eyes set deep against the glare of the wet stone. The bones are dense. The musculature is short and hard, the kind of muscle a person develops from wading and hauling and dragging and twisting ropes of gut, and it is the same muscle in everyone, because everyone does the same work. A girl of eight can haul a driftwood log across an alley; a boy of ten can crack a crab the size of a dinner plate against the basalt with a single blow of the heel of the hand, the way their mothers taught them, the way their mothers were taught. There is no word in their language for a person who cannot do these things, because no such person has ever lived among them.

Their skin is very dark, a brown so deep it is nearly black in the dim light, and it has a permanent sheen to it, an oiliness, partly the kelp-oil they rub themselves with and partly the water itself and partly something the skin does of its own accord in air that never dries. The skin does not wrinkle in the way that dry-air skin wrinkles; instead it thickens in places and stays smooth in others, and the faces of the old women have a particular polished look, as if they had been rubbed for a long time with a soft stone. Their hair is black and very coarse and it mats without much resistance into long damp ropes that hang down the back, and it is usually thick with salt. They do not cut it. They do not comb it. They bind it at the nape with a length of kelp-fiber, when it gets in the way, and otherwise leave it alone. There is no word in their language for *beauty*, and so no one on the shelf is beautiful or ugly, and no one knows which they are.

They wear almost nothing. A strip of kelp-cloth, oiled, wound once around the hips and tucked, falling to mid-thigh, is the standard garment. Children below a certain age wear nothing at all and are indistinguishable by sex to an outsider, and barely so to an insider; the differences are marked, when they are marked at all, by a particular shell hung on a fiber string, and the shells have meanings no outsider has ever decoded. A man or woman on a working day in the alleys wears the kelp-cloth and nothing else. On a day when they go out to the shelf-edge to fish in the deeper channels, they oil the skin of the chest and shoulders until it gleams, and the oil keeps the chill of the deeper water off. On a day when something is being celebrated — and there are celebrations, though they are not celebrations an outsider would recognize as such, and they happen at intervals no one can predict because no one counts the days — they wear strings of pierced shells at the throat and the wrist and the waist, and the shells clack together with a small dry sound that can be heard over the papery thunder of the fish, and is in fact heard as part of it.

Their faces are the part of them that an outsider would find hardest to read, because the expressions are small and few. They have the basic set: the wide-eyed stillness of attention, the narrowed eyes of suspicion, the open mouth of the shout (and they are almost always shouting, or about to). They do not have the small, dry, indoor expressions of dry-land people — the pursed lips, the raised brow, the half-smile. Those expressions require a face that can be seen from across a room by lamplight, and there is no lamplight on the shelf, and no rooms. Their faces are read at shouting distance, in the dim milk-light, across water, and the expressions that matter at shouting distance are the ones they have. A woman who is pleased shows it by a long exhalation through the nose and a slight lift of the chin. A man who is angry shows it by a flattening of the mouth and a forward set of the shoulders. A child who is afraid shows it by a quick pulling-in of the limbs and a stillness. There is no smile. There is no frown. There is no word for either.

They shout. They shout all day, and the shouting is not the shouting of anger; it is the only way a voice can be heard over the sound, and so the voice has been trained from birth to carry. Their voices are rough and high and slightly hoarse, and a woman shouting to her sister across three alleys and a channel has a particular note that can be heard at the back of the skull a long time after the words are gone. Their language is made for shouting. It has very few vowels and an enormous number of hard consonants, and the consonants are mostly the percussive ones — *k*, *t*, *p*, *ch*, the clicks of the tongue against the palate — and the words are short and end hard. A sentence in their language, spoken at a normal pace, sounds like a fist hitting a wooden board in a pattern: *tak-tak ta-tak-tak*. They have no grammatical tense. They have no grammatical number. A noun is a noun; it stands for the thing, and the thing is the same thing whether there is one of it or a thousand, and whether it is doing now what it did before or will do after. The verb is just a louder noun. They have, as has been said, no word for *yesterday* and no word for *tomorrow*. They have a word for *now*, and the word is the same as the word for *always*, and the word is the same as the word for *the same*.

The children are everywhere. They are in the alleys and the channels and the shallow pools and the high rocks, and they are watched but not hovered over. A child of three is expected to be able to catch a thumb-sized crab with the bare hand, and to know which pools have the stinging jellyfish and which do not, and to be able to identify, by the sound alone, the particular bird whose droppings are white from the particular bird whose droppings are black. A child of five is expected to be able to mend a section of wall with the basalt blocks and the mud-and-shell mortar, and to know the difference between the twelve kinds of crab by the sound they make when they click. A child of seven is expected to be able to set a snare and clear a channel and haul a driftwood log and shout a message accurately down three turns of alley. A child of ten is expected to be able to do everything an adult can do, and from that point on, the child *is* an adult, and is paired, and the pairing produces another child, and the new child begins at three, and the cycle is the cycle.

The pairing is not, as an outsider would understand it, a marriage. It is the simple fact that a man and a woman have chosen to share a sleeping-mat, which is a length of kelp-matting laid on the slightly higher basalt of a corner of a house, and the sharing produces, with no particular ceremony, children, and the children are raised by the mother and the aunties and the older sisters and, when they are old enough, by the fathers. The fathers do not, as a rule, sleep in the same house as the mothers and the children; the houses are small, and the sleeping-mats are few, and the men sleep in a separate building, a long low communal structure at the edge of the town, and they come to the women's houses in the hours when the work is not being done. There is no shame in this and no particular joy in it; it is one of the things that happens, like the hauling of a log or the clearing of a channel, and it produces a child, and the child is raised, and the raising is the same raising that has been done since the grandmothers of grandmothers, and no one thinks to remark on it.

They do not, as far as an outsider can tell, think about their lives. They do not sit in the dim milk-light of an evening and consider the nature of existence or the meaning of their days, because the days are all the same and there is nothing to consider. A woman mends a wall. The wall is undermined in the night. The woman mends it again. The woman does not think *why do I mend a wall that will be undermined again*. The question does not occur. The question is not in the language. The wall is mended because the wall is mended. The crab is caught because the crab is caught. The child is raised because the child is raised. The sea sounds because the sea sounds. A person born on the shelf does not wonder at the shelf, the way a fish does not wonder at the water.

And yet — and this is the strange thing, and the thing the people themselves would not be able to say, because they have no words for it — there is something in them that is not nothing. There is a look that comes into the face of the oldest women sometimes, in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, when the wind has dropped and the sound has thinned to just the pulse of the mud and the great sighs out past the shelf-edge, and the woman sits on the higher basalt with her hands on her knees and looks out at the mist as if she could see through it. The look is not the look of a person thinking a thought. It is the look of a person who has been for a very long time in a place that does not change, and who has begun, without knowing it, to feel the weight of the not-changing. The weight is not grief. The weight is not boredom. The weight is something that has no name in the language, and so it cannot be spoken of, and so it sits in the body like the water sits in the alleys, and it is carried, and it is not put down.

The oldest women live a long time. No one on the shelf can say how long, because no one counts, but the women with the polished faces and the ropes of white-dropping-stained hair are women who have been doing the same work in the same alleys for a span of time that would be remarked upon elsewhere. Their hands are gnarled and their fingers are bent from the gripping of basalt and the twisting of rope and the cracking of crab, and they move more slowly than the younger women, and they are given, without being asked, the easier tasks — the sorting of the catch, the drying of the kelp, the watching of the children in the shallow pools. They sit on the higher basalt in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, and they say the longest word in the language, which is the name for the sound the sea makes at the shelf-edge, and the word is not a word but a sequence of clicks and a long exhalation and a small downward turn of the head at the end, and the younger women do not say the word because the younger women do not yet have the weight.

The men are the men. They are darker in the rain and lighter in the dry, which is to say their skin changes shade very slightly with the amount of mist on it, and an outsider would not notice the change but a wife would. They do the heavier work — the hauling of the driftwood, the clearing of the larger channels, the going out to the shelf-edge in the small skin-covered boats that are paddled, not sailed, because there is no wind worth the name — and they are broad in the shoulders and narrow in the hips and they have a particular rolling gait from years of wading, and they shout as much as the women but with a slightly lower note, and the lower note carries better across water, and so the long messages down the alleys are usually given by men, and the short messages across a single alley are usually given by women.

The children are children. They play in the shallow pools, and their play is the work of the adults done in miniature — the catching of the thumb-sized crabs, the dragging of small driftwood, the mending of small walls with small stones. They shout at one another across the alleys in the hoarse voices they have learned from their mothers, and they laugh, and the laugh is a high barking sound, like the cry of the wheeling birds, and it can be heard over the papery thunder of the fish and the click of the crabs. They are not afraid of the dark, because there is no darker dark than the dim. They are not afraid of the water, because the water is the floor. They are not afraid of the mist, because the mist is the ceiling. They are afraid, briefly, of the great long-backed things out past the shelf-edge, when the great things surface and send a swell across the channels that slaps the walls, but the fear passes, because the great things do not come in, and the swell is just the swell.

The people are not unhappy. This is the important thing, and the thing an outsider would have to be careful not to assume. They are not unhappy the way a prisoner is unhappy, because they do not know they are imprisoned. They are not unhappy the way a person in grief is unhappy, because they have no grief. They are not unhappy the way a bored person is unhappy, because boredom requires the expectation of something else, and they have no expectation of anything else. They are what they are, and they do what they do, and the doing is the same doing, and the being is the same being, and the shelf is the shelf, and the shelf is all of it, and the all of it is forever.

There is one more thing, and it is the thing that the oldest women carry in their bodies and cannot put down, and it is this: that the shelf, which is all of it, has only just arrived. The rock is young. The rock is sharp. The world is being made, and is not yet finished, and yet will never be finished, because nothing changes here. The grandmothers of grandmothers fished these same channels and mended these same walls and shouted down these same alleys and caught these same crabs and laid down on these same sleeping-mats and raised these same children, and the basalt under their feet was the same basalt, and the mist was the same mist, and the sound was the same sound. And the women who sit on the higher basalt in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, and look out at the mist as if they could see through it, are the women who have begun, without knowing it, to feel the weight of the always. And the weight is not a thing they can speak of, and not a thing they can lay down, and not a thing they can pass on to the younger women, because the younger women do not yet have it.

And so the weight sits in them, and the shelf goes on, and the sound goes on, and the people go on, and the world, which is barely finished being made, goes on not being finished, forever, in the warm wet deafening dim unchanging dark.
