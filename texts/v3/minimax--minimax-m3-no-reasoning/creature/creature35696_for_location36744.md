The people of Saeren are the colour of the basin. This is the first thing, and it is the most accurate thing. The brown-grey of wet bark, the black-grey of the dark rock, the paler grey of skin that has not seen hard sun in many generations. Their hair is dark, mostly, and they wear it bound back against the wind. The wind comes down the mountain in sudden squalls, and the people of Saeren have always minded it.

There are perhaps a hundred of them. You will not see most of them in a day. You will see two or three, the way you see two or three stones in a scree slope, and then, if you keep still, three more. They move alone, mostly. Not in any studied way, not as if they have chosen solitude over company, but because the basin is large and the houses are apart and there is no reason to walk together when the path will take each of them past their own door anyway. A woman carries a wooden bucket of water uphill along the flagged path. The bucket is heavy, and she has carried this bucket before and will carry it again. She is not hurried. She is not burdened. She is a person doing a thing, and the thing is the thing. On a warm day only one chimney in the basin has smoke coming from it, and the smoke is gone before it has climbed ten feet, taken back into the cloud.

When a person goes through a doorway in a squall they bow their head a little, the way you would bow under a low branch. The doorways are low. The wind is the wind. They have always done this.

The houses are dark stone. The people are dark stone. This is not a saying, this is the truth of how they look. A house seen from the path, with someone standing in its doorway, is a thing with two openings — the door, and the figure inside it. From a little distance you cannot always tell which is the figure. The stonecutter who built the wall and the figure in the doorway are the same colour. They are made of the same place.

Their hands are the hands of people who work the stone and the wet. Only some of them cut stone. The others tend the alders in some quiet way, or watch the streams for the moment a channel shifts, or cut the ferns back from a doorway, or do the things that have no name because they are done so often that a name would be excessive. The hands are wide at the palm, with the fingers a little flattened at the tips and the knuckles a little swollen. The damp does that. A hand that is always wet changes its shape in slow ways, the way a stone changes its shape in slow ways, and the people of Saeren are patient with the changing. The calluses on the fingers are pale and hard, and the skin across the back of the hand is soft from the air, and the hands are not beautiful and are not meant to be.

The boy on the wall is perhaps twelve. He is working at a piece of stone with a smaller piece of stone, and the chips are falling into his lap and onto the wall. He has been there a long time. He will be there a long time still. He is not, when you look at him, a boy learning a craft. He is a boy being the craft. The piece he is working has a particular shape in his mind — a shape that is not decorative, a shape that fits — and he is taking the stone toward that shape one chip at a time. When the stone is the shape, he will set it down, and another piece of stone will be given to him, and he will begin again. There is an older man in the basin who is his teacher, though teacher is not quite the word. The older man has done this for many years. He is the one who knows the wall. The boy is the one who will know the wall, in time, when the wall has taught him.

The stonecutters do not carve. They fit. A woman — a different woman, or the same woman later, it does not matter — can be seen at the meeting house, fitting a small piece of slate back into the place where a piece of slate came loose in the last high wind. She is not repairing the meeting house. She is fitting the slate. The difference matters. A repair is a thing put back to how it was. A fit is a thing made to sit where it sits, so that the wall is again a wall and the rain is again outside it. She does not mark the slate. When she has done the work, the slate will be in the roof, and the roof will be the roof, and no one walking past will be able to tell which slate was the new one. This is the point. The point is the fit.

The clearing around each house is the only sign that the people of Saeren are there. The houses, the path, the people themselves — all of these are so close to the colour of the basin that you have to know what to look for, and you have to look slowly, and you have to be quiet while you do it. The clearing is held open. Nothing taller than moss is allowed in it. This is not a rule. It is the way the people are in the basin. If you asked one of them why the clearing is held open, they would not say because the clearing should be a clearing. They would say something simpler. They would say the moss is enough, or the fern belongs on the slope, or nothing needs to be tall there.

They eat. They sleep. They lie with each other. They have children, and the children grow up, and the older people go quietly into the damp. None of this is dramatic. A woman dies in her house, and the house is closed for a while, and the clearing is allowed to grow back a little, and then a younger sister or a younger cousin or no one in particular comes and lives in the house and holds the clearing open again. A man dies at the wall with the mallet in his hand, and the wall is not finished where he left it, and another man finishes it, and the wall is the wall. They do not mark the graves with anything you would recognize as a marker. The dead are in the basin, and the basin is the basin, and the lichen will come.

In the meeting house, when there is a meeting, the people sit on the stone benches and the timbers overhead are silvered, and the air moves underneath the floor through the space the pillars make, and the people speak when they need to, and the rest of the time the water is the sound. A decision is made when a decision is made, and it is not made again until it is made again, which may be a long time. They are not a people of many decisions. The basin has been decided. The houses are where the houses are. The path goes where the path goes. The streams are where the streams are. What is there to decide?

There is, somewhere in the basin, a small brown animal that lives under the alders. It is not a kept thing. They know it by sight, and it knows them by sight, and the relationship is the relationship of neighbours who do not speak. There are birds. The birds are the colour of the cloud. When a bird calls from a holly, the call is just there, like the drip from the meeting house eaves — countable, but no one is counting.

The light is the colour of wet slate, and they have grown into the colour of wet slate, and so when the sun does not show itself, which is most days, they are not hard to find but they are not easy to find either, and this is, if you ask them, fine. They are not lost. They are where they are. The light falls on them, and the light does not make shadows, and so a person standing by a wall is a softer version of the wall, and a person walking on a path is a slightly darker part of the path, and a person sitting on a stone is the stone with a thought on it.

The boy on the wall will be a man on the wall, in time. The woman with the bucket will carry a heavier bucket, in time, or a lighter one. The stonecutter at the meeting house will fit a different piece, in a different year, in a different wind. The clearing will be held open. The path will be the path. The water will fall in the same places. The clouds will sit in the basin, and the basin will hold them, and the people will move through the clouds at the speed of people, and the clouds will move through the people at the speed of clouds, and neither of them will be in any kind of hurry about it.

That is who lives in Saeren.
