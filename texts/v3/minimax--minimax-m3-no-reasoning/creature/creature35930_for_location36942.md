What lives in the Pump Lot, you ask. Stand there long enough and the answer stops being a list and becomes a single slow argument.

The machines live here. That is the strangest and most true thing, and it is the thing the place presses on the inside of attention whenever anyone stands inside the fence for any length of time. They live here the way a heart lives in a body that has wandered off and forgotten what it was for. They have been left running. They are running well. Their pistons rise and fall behind a window crusted with condensation so that you can see only the dark motion of them, never the mechanism itself. Their valves open and shut somewhere inside the pipework with a soft click, soft click, soft click, like a man clearing his throat in a room you cannot find. The pump in the grate below the marsh hums at a frequency you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. The chimney of the low windowless shed leaks a thin feather of vapor that is not quite steam and not quite smoke but something between the two, something that smells of warm oil and old iron, and this is the place's slow exhalation, and it does not thin, and it does not hurry.

The machines do not know they have been left. The machines do not need to know. They are doing what they were made to do, which is everything they know. They have found a tempo they like and they keep it. They are running and no one is watching them and they are persisting in their function with an indifference so steady it has begun to look like devotion.

The water lives here, and the water is the whole point.

It comes in along a concrete channel from the east, a low tongue of marsh the color of cold tea, littered with the bleached stems of last year's reeds. It feeds the grate. Below the grate the pump takes it down. The pump gives it up again somewhere else. The system is closed. The water obeys the system. It rises in pipes, falls in pipes, travels in pipes, fans out on the broken concrete of the apron in a clear steady stream, threads back into the marsh, joins the standing water that has been there all along. It is a citizen of a closed loop, sworn to it. It does not argue with the pipes. The pipes do not argue with it. They have made peace, the way things that have lived together too long do. The water is everywhere and the machines are still working, and these two facts do not fight each other. The water moves through the compound at the pace the machines tell it to move, and underneath everything, beneath the grate, beneath the apron, beneath the foundations, it is traveling somewhere with purpose, and it does not stop. The only proof of its speed is the small whirlpools the size of coins that dimple the surface above the pump intake and vanish, and vanish again, and keep coming back.

On the surfaces, time lives here.

The lichen has made maps of the pipework in pale gray and mustard yellow, and it adds to them at the pace of centuries. It knows the metal the way a long tenant knows a building: every seam, every flank, every elbow. It is patient the way deep water is patient. It does not hurry. It has made peace with the rust and the rust has made peace with it and neither can remember, now, what they were originally quarreling about. The moss — sour, vivid green — lives on the lower third of the brick pump house, and it stops at an agreed line, as if by treaty. The damp has offered the moss a certain height, and the moss has accepted the offer, and neither party will speak of it again. The lichen and the moss are the lowest of the visible tenants, and they are content.

The sedge lives in the angle where the channel meets the grate, a few clumps lodged there by whatever small accident lodged them. It is shy. It does not announce itself. The bleached stems of last year's growth lean at the knees under their own weight. It will lean there for as long as the channel feeds it. It does not need more than this.

The willow lives here, and it is the most improbable resident.

It grows out of the rust-eaten husk of an old boiler at the back of the lot. It is no taller than a man. It is absurdly healthy. Its leaves are the only moving color in the whole compound, the only green thing above the height of a man's knee, and they sway faintly with no wind you can feel because the air itself moves — the warm breath from the shed mixes with the cool damp air above it and makes a slow private current that nudges the leaves and nothing else. The willow has chosen an extraordinary place to live — out of a dead machine's corpse — and it does not seem to have noticed that this is unusual. It is simply a willow. It is simply green. It has come up out of the dead iron as if the dead iron were just soil that happened to be the right color, and it is persisting in its function the way anything green persists in its function, which is to be green, which is to keep going.

A heron lives here.

It stands on the far rim of the marsh with the fixed patience of furniture. It is more like a piece of weather than an animal — gray-blue, vertical, motionless, the kind of stillness other things have to walk around. It watches the channel with the long attention of a thing that knows its food will come. Sometimes it lifts and relocates with a single stiff motion to a place three feet away and stands again and watches again, and this will be its whole day, and the day will be enough. The heron does not seem to belong to the compound and it does not seem not to. It belongs to the marsh and the marsh belongs to the compound, and the heron has claimed the rim as its post and has not been asked to leave.

A frog lives here, somewhere beneath the grate.

It calls once — a single low note that rises out of the dark under the pump — and stops. It does not call again. It has said what it needed to say, or it has forgotten the rest. Its single call is the only proof that anything in this compound has a voice.

Insects live here. They are small and silver and low. They vibrate above the standing water in the channels. They do not rise high enough to matter. They are the commas and periods of the compound's slow sentence. The compound does not need them but it has them.

The rust lives here. It has reached a depth it likes and slowed. It lives in the seams of the steel tanks, in the flanges of the pipes, in the husk of the old boiler that the willow has made its home. It is a slow creature. It is one of the oldest tenants in the lot. It works in centuries. It has given the iron a deep saturated orange that looks almost edible. It has rusted one of the squat steel tanks through along its seam, so that a permanent dark stain spreads beneath it like a shadow that has pooled. The rust is not failing. It has found a tempo it likes.

A person lives here, or lived here, or comes and goes.

The traces are everywhere, none of them recent enough to be sure of. A thermos sits on a flange beside the pump house door. Footprints mark themselves in the rust dust inside the shed. A calendar hangs on the inside of the shed wall, six months out of date, with one square circled in red. The notices inside the clouding Plexiglas of the bulletin board have turned to uniform tan by damp and cannot be read, but they have been read by someone, and read with attention, because the Plexiglas is still on its hinges, and the thermos was placed, and not dropped. Whoever they are, they have decided this compound should keep running. They come. They leave the thermos. They go. They come back. They circle a day in red. They go. They are the unseen steward of a closed circuit. They are the only witness the machines have, and they are mostly not here.

There is no answer offered to the question of who, and the place is not going to give one.

What lives in the Pump Lot, in the end, is what has been left and what has decided to stay.

The water keeps moving through the pipes. The shed keeps breathing its thin vapor into the cool air. The heron watches the channel with the long patience of a thing that knows its food will come. The willow's leaves — the only green, the only motion above the height of a man's knee — sway in their private current. The moss holds its agreed line on the brick. The lichen continues its slow cartography of the pipework. The rust continues its slow eating of the iron. The valves click, the valves click, the valves click. The pump hums at the frequency you feel in your sternum. Somewhere beneath the grate the water is moving fast, and the small coin-sized whirlpools dimple the surface and vanish, and the pump does not slow, and the moss does not climb higher, and the sedge leans, and the heron watches, and the willow sways in air that is moving because the shed is breathing. Somewhere — outside the fence, beyond the half-step — a thermos is cooling or a calendar is being turned or a square is being circled in red, or no one is doing any of these things anymore, and the place has settled into itself, and the water has come up to a level it likes and stopped, and the machines have found a tempo they like and keep it, and the rust has reached a depth it likes and slowed. The indifference is its own kind of devotion. The sound does not rise. The place does not change its mind.
