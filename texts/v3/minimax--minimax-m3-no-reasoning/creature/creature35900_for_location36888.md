The smallest things live in the seams.

That is where the life begins. Not in the basins. Not in the gutters. Not in the slots that look like they ought to hold something. In the seams. In the pale mineral lines that were the brightest thing here before there was life here, and are the brightest thing here now that there is life here, and which the life has chosen — has had to choose, has had no choice but to choose — as its first home.

The polyps are in the seams. They have made the seams into a kind of long slow city, and each polyp is a citizen, and the citizen does not know it is a citizen, and the city does not know it is a city, and the seams do not know they are seams. They are translucent. They are the color of cream, of bone, of the paste that sealed the stones together before the water came in. They feed by waiting. The current brings them what they need. The current has always brought them what they need. They have small mouths that open and close without sound, and the opening is so slight that it is not really an opening — it is a permission. They give the current permission to enter. The current enters. The current leaves lighter by one small drifting thing. The polyp is heavier by one small drifting thing. This is the entire economy. It has been the entire economy for a very long time.

In the basins — the carved hollows that hold silt — the worms have come. They are tube-things, or what tube-things are in this place. They have built themselves small castles from the silt and the drifting calcium and the long patience of their own secretions, and they live in the castles with their feathery heads out, and the heads wave in the current, and the current passes through the feathers, and the feathers take from the current what they want. Each worm is a small fountain. Each worm is a punctuation mark in a long sentence — placed, intentional, doing one thing. They do not move. They have not moved in so long that their castles have become part of the basin, and the basin has become part of the architecture, and the worm is somewhere inside all of that, in the seam-of-seams, in the place where the living thing and the made thing have agreed to be the same thing.

In the gutters — the carved channels that direct the current, that the current still obeys though it has forgotten why — the shrimp have come. They are pale. They are nearly transparent. You can see their small hearts through their shells, beating in a rhythm that is slightly slower than yours, slightly faster than the current, slightly out of phase with both. They have long pale antennae that they hold before them like fingers, like the way a person feels along a wall in a dark room. They do not swim. They walk. They walk on the hand-worked stone that no one has worked in so long that the working has become the stone, and their small feet find the small hollows where the maker's thumb pressed once, a long time ago, and they rest in those hollows, and the hollows have come to seem made for them, and maybe they were. Maybe that is what the thumb was doing. Who can say now.

In the grooves between fitted stones, in the angles where two stones met and were meant to meet — there are more shrimp. There are blennies, or things like blennies. There are small flattened heads with large mouths and a way of watching that is not quite watching but is also not quite not watching. They are tucked into the angles as if the angles had been carved for tucking into. They are the color of the stone. They are the color of the seam. They are the color of the dark. They have made themselves of the architecture's palette, and the architecture has accepted this, and the architecture and the fish are now a kind of mutual camouflage in which neither is hidden and both are present.

And between the pillars — in the columns of water that the architecture has left standing — the fish have come. They are not many. They are not in schools. They are solitary in the way deep things are solitary, which is not lonely but particular. There is a long thin fish that hangs vertical, head up, tail down, and only moves when something comes near, and then it is gone and you are not sure it was there. There is a round fish, a slow fish, a fish with eyes too large for its head, pale eyes that do not shine but do receive. It sits in the water without moving. It has been sitting in the water without moving for a long time. It will continue to sit in the water without moving. The current moves around it. The polyps in the seams ignore it. The shrimp in the grooves ignore it. It is part of the place now. The place moves around it the way a river moves around a stone.

And the lantern-fish. The lantern-fish has made a circuit. It goes from pillar to pillar. It visits each pillar in turn. It pauses at each pillar and looks — with its lantern, with its pale small light, with whatever it has — and moves on. The lantern is dimmer than the seams. The lantern is dimmer than the dark. But it is its own light, and it belongs to the fish, and the fish carries it the way a person carries a small candle through a large dark house. The circuit is older than you. The circuit will outlast you. The fish does not know it is making a circuit. The fish knows the pillars. The fish knows the small drifting things come to the pillars at certain times. The fish knows where the things will be. The fish is patient.

On the steps — on the rounded worn steps that were once sharp — the crinoids. They are what crinoids were, or they are what crinoids have been waiting to become for the long time between then and now. They have many arms. Each arm has many arms. The divisions become fine, and finer, until the whole is a fringe, a fringe of animal, a thing that has decided to become an edge. They pour over the steps in curtains of slow motion. The current lifts them. The current lowers them. They are not beautiful in the way a person uses the word. They are beautiful in the way a thing is beautiful when it has been doing the same thing for a very long time — practiced, certain, without the need to be anything other than what it is. They are the color of old bone. They hold their arms out. They take what comes. They let go what doesn't. They have been doing this. They will do this.

In the slots and the chambers that the maker left open — the slots that were ventilation, or that were never meant to be closed, or that were for a purpose we do not have words for — the watchers are. The watchers are not fish. The watchers are not shrimp. The watchers are something else, something with stillness in it. They have small mouths and a patience that is not learned but given. They have eyes that do not reflect. They have bodies that match the stone, and they match the stone because they have been here long enough that the stone has become them and they have become the stone. They sit in the slot and they watch the slot and they watch what comes through the slot, and when something small comes through the slot they do not lunge. They simply open, and the something small is gone, and they close again, and they are still. The silt has begun to build on them. The silt will continue to build on them. They will become, in time, part of the slot, part of the stone, part of the architecture, indistinguishable from what was made.

And the columns themselves are not empty. They are full. They are full of the small drifting things — the near-invisible things, the things that have not yet decided what they are, the eggs and the larvae and the bits of animal and the bits of plant and the bits of nothing that the current has carried in from somewhere else. They are the smoke. They are what fills the chimney. They are everywhere at once and you cannot see them, and then you can see one, and then you cannot see it again. They are not aware. They are the weather of the columns. And among them, almost of them, almost one of them, are the jellies — small pale bells, the size of a hand, the size of a head, that pulse with a rhythm that is not the rhythm of the current but that the current has learned to harmonize with. They have no lantern. They have no need. They are translucent the way water is translucent. They are nearly not there. They drift along the grooves and up the steps and across the platform when no one is on it. They do not know they are in a sanctuary. They are in the water.

And below.

Below the platform. Below the steps. Below the basins, the grooves, the pillars, the architecture. Below the place you came to. In the deeper water, in the darker warm, in the place where the mountain is only a mountain — there is the large one. You do not see it. You will not see it. It is too far down, or too far out, or too large for the architecture, or simply too large for seeing. You feel it. You feel it in the long slow groan that comes up through the stone sometimes. You feel it in the way the current changes for a moment and then changes back, as if the current had been briefly displaced by something passing through. You feel it the way you feel weather before weather — a shift, a pressure, a thing that is not yet here but is on its way.

The large one is patient. The large one is warm. The large one is in the water, and the water is the place, and the place is patient, and the large one is the place's most patient. It moves when it moves. It does not move when it does not. You do not know what it eats. You do not know if it sleeps. You do not know if it knows you are here. You know that it has been here, and that it will be here, and that the question of how long does not apply to it the way the question of how long does not apply to the water or the stone. The large one is part of the water the way the architecture is part of the mountain. The large one is what the water does when the water is being most itself.

You climb. The water is warm. The water is patient. The life in the seams continues. The life in the basins continues. The life in the gutters, in the grooves, in the slots, in the chambers, between the pillars, on the steps — continues. The lantern-fish completes its circuit and begins it again. The watchers wait in their slots and the silt builds on them and they do not mind. The crinoids hold their arms out and the current passes through them and they are beautiful in their patience. The polyps in the seams open their small mouths and the current comes in and something is kept and the current goes on. The shrimp walk on the hand-worked stone and rest in the hollows the maker's thumb pressed. The jellies pulse in the columns of the architecture and the columns do not know they are being filled. The large one moves below, or does not move below, and the moving and the not-moving are the same to it.

You are one more thing in the seams. You are one more presence in the place. The place is not hostile to you. The place is not friendly to you. The place is only itself. The place was itself before you. The place will be itself after. For the length of your visit, you are here, and the life in the seams goes on around you, and the life does not know you are here, and you do not need it to know.
