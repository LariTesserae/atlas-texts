# The Inhabitants

To live here is to be a part of the long argument between cold and water. Almost everything that has a body in this place has made a settlement with that argument, and the things that have not are wind, sound, and the people on the platforms, who argue with it by other means. What follows is a kind of census — not a complete one, because a complete census of this place would require a hundred years and a slow boat, but a census of the kinds of bodies one is likely to find, ordered roughly by size and approximately by the patience they seem to embody.

## The fingernail-things

The smallest things here are smaller than a fingernail. They are the most numerous. If you could weigh the living tissue of this place by category — and the data would be ambiguous, because most of the water is water — these would outweigh all the others by an order of magnitude, perhaps three. They are the foundation of every other body in the sea. Without them, nothing else eats.

You see them first as a stain. From the deck of a boat, miles out from the glass shore, you can see a half-kilometer bloom on the water, a dull rose discoloration of the surface, like a single drop of ink turning a litre of water grey. From above, if you were a place above, the bloom would look like a slow bruise on the surface of the sea, pinkish at the periphery and a denser pink toward the centre. The bloom moves. The wind pushes it; the swell turns it; the creatures inside it push it too, gently, by their own slow vertical migrations. Inside the bloom the water is more viscous than the water outside it. The bloom is, in part, a thickening of the water.

Closer, if you take a sample in a clear glass jar and hold it up against the flat grey sky, you see them as individuals. They are mostly transparent. They have a soft pearlescent outline, a faint pink inside the gut where they are digesting, a barely visible dark dot that is the only solid thing about them — a small dense kernel of chemistry, the organ that does the most of the work, that nobody would mistake for a brain. They drift. They do not really swim. They pulse, slowly, with a kind of internal rhythm, and the pulse moves them forward and back and around, the way a heartbeat moves blood. They have a slow vertical migration — down by day, up by night — inverted from many of their distant analogues elsewhere, because here the sun does not warm the surface but the cold does sink, and the food is more concentrated lower where the vents leak their chemistry into the water. They have been doing this vertical migration for as long as they have existed here. They will not stop.

If you look at one very carefully — if you take a single individual into a glass and let it sit for an hour while the boat rocks — you can see the inside. There is a feeding current running through it: cilia along a fine groove pull water in at one end, past a filtering membrane finer than dust, and push it out at the other. The water carries bacteria. The bacteria get caught. The bacteria get digested. The digested product — the small molecules, the chemistry of life — diffuses into the body cavity. Most of the body is water with a difference. Most of the body is not really body at all; it is seawater, with a faint salt of itself added. You could not point to where the body ends and the sea begins. The boundary is a convention.

They have no real gut. They have a kind of distributed digestion. They have a slow internal current, slower than the feeding current, that moves the chemistry around. They have no skin, only a soft pellicle. They have no real skeleton, only a faint internal turgor. They have no brain. They have a small cluster of sensory cells at one end that responds to light and to pressure changes, and that is enough to tell them where the surface is and where the bottom is, in a rough way, and where the swarm is, in a finer way. They find the swarm by smell — they release small molecules into the water that other members of the swarm respond to, and they swim up the gradient of those molecules, and so the swarm swarms.

That is how the swarm is organized. Not by vision. Not by sound. By chemistry, by the soft cloud of self-smell each individual puts out and each individual reads. The swarm is a kind of autocatalytic cloud — wherever one of them is, the water is a little more inviting to others, and so the others come, and so the water becomes more inviting still, until you have a million of them in a single cubic metre of water. Inside that cloud the chemistry is a kind of language, a slow syntax of release and response. The cloud is not a single organism. It is something else, something more like a city than a body — a place where many small processes are doing roughly the same thing at roughly the same time, and finding each other by the faint perfumes of being alive.

They reproduce, mostly, by simply dividing. One becomes two. The two become four. They do not, most of the time, mate. They bud. They are, in the long view, more or less continuous with each other, like the branches of a single very large and very divided thing. Occasionally, very rarely, something goes wrong, or right, and two of them exchange a piece of their internal chemistry, and the result is a new individual that is not a clone of either parent, and that individual carries some new variation, and most of that variation is not useful and the individual dies, but a few of the variations are useful, and the few are the ones that, over the long stretches, become the swarm.

The swarm is the parent. The individuals are not. They are temporary, in the sense that an afternoon is temporary. They live for weeks. They feed for most of those weeks. They die for any of several reasons — disease, age, the long jaw of one of the larger things — and their bodies, when they die, fall apart quickly into the water and are eaten by others of their own kind, and the chemistry of them rejoins the chemistry of the cloud, and the cloud persists.

You cannot really hear them. They are quieter than quiet. The only sound they make is the faint sound of water passing cilia, and that is below the threshold of the wind and the waves. They are not silent. They are inaudible. They are present in the way that air is present: not as a sound but as a medium through which other sounds move.

## The shapes in the middle

The mid-sized things are bigger than a finger and smaller than an arm. They come in a few kinds, and the kinds overlap. They are, in the language of an older ocean, vaguely fish-shaped, except where they are not. They have a long soft body, a flattened head, a mouth that opens in a way that is not quite a fish mouth — wider, more like a soft hinged trap than a hinged jaw — and a long fin running down the spine that they use for slow propulsion. They have no scales. They have a fine silvery covering, almost metallic, that catches the flat light when they turn. They are, individually, the colour of the deep water. They are, in a swarm, almost invisible.

They eat the fingernail-things. That is most of what they do. They move, slowly, with the edge of the swarm, and they open their mouths, and the water and the creatures in it rush in, and they close their mouths and press the water out through the fine filters on their gills, and the creatures stay inside. They feed almost continuously when they are near a swarm. They feed almost not at all when they are not.

They have senses. They have eyes — soft, dark, the size of peas, set into the sides of the face — that work in the dim water and work better in the dark, because their pupils open very wide and the lens is soft and gathers what little light there is. They have a lateral line, a fine canal running down the side of the body, that feels the pressure changes of moving water, and so they feel the swarm as a moving pressure, a soft disturbance that travels. They have, perhaps, something like electroreception, a faint sense of the small bioelectric fields of the creatures they eat. They do not hear. They do not need to hear. There is nothing to hear.

They are not fast. They are not built for speed. They are built for patience. They drift through the water and let the water bring them what it brings. When a faster thing needs them, they are sometimes killed; when a faster thing is not near, they live for a long time, perhaps a season, perhaps longer.

They have no parental behaviour. A female will, at some internal prompting, release a few thousand small eggs into the water near a swarm, and the eggs hatch into something smaller than the fingernail-things, and the larvae feed on the bacteria and on each other, and most of them die, and a few grow into the adults. The adults do not know their young. The adults do not know anything. They drift. They feed. They drift.

There are several species of these mid-sized things. The species differ in size, in shape of mouth, in the colour of the silver on the sides, in the pattern of the long dorsal fin. They do not seem to compete with each other. They occupy slightly different parts of the water column — one near the surface, one a little deeper, one further out toward the deeper black water. They meet at the swarms. They pass each other without recognition.

You can see them from a boat if you are patient. You can see them as a slow dark shape beneath the surface, the size of a forearm, that parts the swarm and is then closed over by it. You can see them as the absence of so much pink in a place. They are, individually, the same color as the deep water.

## The deep shadow

There is a shadow that moves under the boat a year from now, or maybe not, depending on where you put the boat. It moves under the boat sometimes. You do not see it clearly. You never see it clearly. You see the swarm part around a large absence. You see the mid-sized shapes turn, suddenly, and move away in a soft scatter. You see, for a moment, a dark shape moving at a depth you cannot see, and then you see nothing.

It is bigger than the mid-sized shapes. It is bigger than you can easily estimate. It moves slowly. It moves with a soft lateral undulation of a long body, or with a soft vertical motion of long fins, or with something else; you cannot tell. It does not surface. It does not come into the lit water. It stays in the dark water below the chop, and it drifts.

It eats the mid-sized shapes. It probably eats other things. It may eat the fingernail-things directly, in vast slow mouthfuls. It may eat things you have not seen. It is the largest thing in the place that you are likely to know about. There may be larger things, deeper, that you do not know about at all. There probably are.

You cannot tell what species it is. You cannot tell if it is one species or several. You cannot tell if it is the adult form of the mid-sized shapes, or a different lineage, or several different lineages. You can only tell that it is there, sometimes, in the deep water, by the way everything else behaves around it.

The mid-sized shapes know it is there. They react to it before you can see it. They part. They go deeper. They go still. They adopt a posture you have not seen in them, a long slow hang in the water with the fins folded, that makes them look, for a moment, like a piece of water. They do this in unison. They have, evidently, a sense of the deep shadow that is older than any sense you have.

The deep shadow does not seem to react to you. You are not in its food. You are not in its interest. You are a soft warmth in the cold water and a small disturbance of pressure, and you are not part of its argument with anything. You are not its enemy. You are not its food. You are simply not there, in the way it counts there. This is, perhaps, the most unsettling thing about it: the indifference.

## The things near the vents

The vents are scarce. They are the few places where the young geology below the glass still leaks heat, and the heat leaks chemistry, and the chemistry is the foundation of the food web. A vent is a small warm column of water, a few metres across, that smells of sulphur and metals and that is slightly less cold than the surrounding water. Around a vent, the water is more alive. Around a vent, there is colour.

The colour is the bacteria. They live in the mats. The mats are pink, yellow, orange — the pigments of the bacteria, which are different from the pigments of the fingernail-things because they are doing different chemistry. The bacterial mats are thick, in places, a few centimetres deep, layered over the cracks in the volcanic rock where the warm water seeps out. They grow in the warm water. They grow fast. They get eaten fast. They are the pasture.

The things that eat the mats are small. They live close to the vents. They are specialists. They have soft bodies and fine teeth, or fine rasps, or fine hairs, and they graze the mats carefully, and they do not venture far from the vent because the vent is the food and the food is the vent. They are, individually, smaller than a thumb. There are many of them, around each vent, in a soft cloud of small grazers.

Some of them are pinkish themselves, from the bacteria they eat. Some are paler. Some are almost colourless. A few have a faint hard shell, a single piece, that covers them like a fingernail when they fold. They do not move much. They sit, they fold, they extend, they graze. They are the most rooted things in the place, though they are not rooted at all.

Above the grazers, in the warm column, there are a few small predators that eat the grazers. They are like the mid-sized shapes from the open water, only smaller and more delicate, and they stay near the vents because that is where the grazers are. They are, perhaps, the same species as the mid-sized shapes from the open water, or they are different. The question is open. The vents are not well studied. The vents are a small fraction of the place, and the people on the platforms have other things to do.

Below the vents, in the warm cracks in the volcanic glass, there are things you cannot see. They are small. They live in the cracks. They feed on the chemistry directly, the way the bacteria do. They have no light. They have no eyes. They have a slow patience.

The vents are the foundation. Everything that lives here, lives here because the vents are here. The wind brings a little, but not much. The rain brings a little, but not much. The vents bring everything else. The vents are the only true source of life in the place. The rest of the food web is built from the vents, with the wind that rebuilds it as the wind takes it.

## The people in the structures

There are people. There are not many. A structure might have a watch-stander, a mechanic, a cook, a foreman, a researcher or two. There might be six people. There might be twelve. The number depends on the structure. The structures are scattered, a dozen in a hundred kilometres, and the people are scattered with the structures, and the people are a smaller fraction of the place than the swarms are.

The people are loud. The people are louder than the swarms, louder than the mid-sized shapes, louder than anything in the place that is not the wind or the waves. The people shout at each other across the platforms. The people run engines. The people weld. The people run pumps. The sound of the structures is small in the great roar of the place, but it is the loudest thing that has ever lived here that is not the wind or the waves.

The people have routines. The watch-stander watches the dials. The mechanic keeps the pumps running. The cook cooks. The foreman foremanages. The researcher researches. The routines are largely the same, day to day, because the place is largely the same, day to day. The place is a long argument with a single set of conditions, and the conditions do not change, and the people adjust.

The watch-stander learns the rhythm of the pumps. The pumps have a rhythm — a slow steady throb that rises and falls in a small cycle, the way a heart rises and falls, and the watch-stander can feel when the throb is off by a beat. The watch-stander can feel it in the floor, in the chair, in the inner ear. The watch-stander knows the pumps the way you know your own breathing. The watch-stander is the most adapted of the people. They are not adapted to the place; they are adapted to the work.

The mechanic has grease under the fingernails. The mechanic opens things and closes things. The mechanic replaces parts that wear out. The mechanic knows the cold the way a slow room knows the cold — by the way the metal contracts, by the way the seals leak, by the way the pipes creak. The mechanic wears layers. The mechanic does not stay outside long.

The cook has a small piece of the world inside the galley. The galley is a small room inside the structure, and it is warm, and it has a window, and the window has a small shelf, and on the shelf there is a single plant in a pot. The plant is small. The plant is green. The plant is the only green thing for a hundred kilometres in any direction, and the cook waters it, and the cook talks to it, and the cook is the only person who talks to anything that does not talk. They are very fond of their plants. They do not tell the others how fond. They would not know how to explain. The plant is the only argument the cook has with the place.

The foreman has been here a long time. The foreman is the longest-tenured person on the platform. The foreman does not say much. The foreman knows the wind. The foreman knows when the wind is about to change by the way the clouds lean, by the way the smoke from the stacks trails, by the way the boats pitch in the harbour. The foreman has a small vocabulary. The foreman uses it carefully. The foreman does not want to go back. The foreman does not say so. The foreman knows, perhaps, that the back is a place they know to be other, and this place is a place they know to be here, and they prefer here, and they do not have a word for that preference.

The researcher studies the swarms. The researcher is obsessed with the swarms. The researcher takes samples. The researcher measures the chemistry. The researcher is writing a long paper, or a short book, or a long letter to no one, about the swarms. The researcher thinks the swarms are the most interesting thing in the place. The researcher does not think about the deep shadow. The researcher does not want to think about the deep shadow. The researcher has, perhaps, seen it once, in the dark water, from the deck of a boat at night, and the researcher has put that seeing in a different part of the mind.

The people have very little for the place. The place has very little for the people. The people are here because the work is here, and the work is here because the minerals are here, or the fauna is here, or the heat is here. The reasons are not, from the outside, obvious. The reasons may not, from the inside, be obvious. The people are here. The people make small claims against the place. The place largely ignores the claims.

## The wind

The wind is the largest inhabitant, in a sense. The wind is the most patient. The wind is the most indifferent. The wind has a voice — a long, steady, broadband voice, without edges or beginning or end — and the voice has been here as long as the plateau has been here, and the plateau has been here as long as the wind has been here, and the two are part of the same argument with each other, and the argument has settled into a kind of agreement, and the agreement is the place.

The wind is not a body. The wind is a process. The wind is the differential between the cold air above the water and the colder air above the snowfields, channelled by the geography, falling downhill, always downhill, always toward the lower air. The wind is a slow pour of cold. The wind pushes the swarms. The wind pushes the mid-sized shapes. The wind pushes the people. The wind pushes the structures, gently, for decades, and the structures lean into the push, and the lean is in the design, and the design is in the place.

The wind has no intentions. The wind has no preferences. The wind has no argument with anything except the desire to equalize, which is not really an argument at all but a kind of physics, and the physics is not personal. The wind is the closest thing to a presence the place has, but the wind is not a presence. The wind is a process. The wind is what happens when cold air is heavier than cold air above snow.

You will, if you stand here long enough, begin to attribute intentions to the wind. You will begin to think the wind has moods. You will begin to think the wind is angry, or tired, or patient, or bored. You will begin to think the wind is listening. The wind is not listening. The wind is moving. The wind will continue to move.

## The whole

If you stand on the glass and look out at the water and try to count the inhabitants, you will not succeed. The inhabitants are too small, too numerous, too distributed, too much a part of the water to be counted. The inhabitants are, in a different accounting, the water itself. The water is mostly water. The water is partly alive. The living part of the water is mostly the fingernail-things. The fingernail-things are mostly the foundation of the place. The foundation of the place is mostly chemistry. The chemistry is mostly the chemistry of the vents.

The place is, in the end, a place where chemistry has found a way to persist. The place is a place where a slow argument between heat and cold, between rock and water, between the deep and the surface, has settled into a long stasis, and the stasis has given chemistry time to become biology, and biology has given the place its small warm bloom of life. The life is not many. The life is not large. The life is mostly small, mostly soft, mostly transparent or silver, mostly patient. It eats the chemistry. It becomes the chemistry. It does not leave much behind.

The people leave the most behind, in a sense, because the people have built things and the things are made of iron and concrete and will, perhaps, outlast the people. But the things are small. The things are a few dozen in a hundred kilometres. The things will, eventually, fall. The things will be claimed by the glass, the way everything is eventually claimed by the glass. The places are not, in the end, the most numerous inhabitants. The places are the most recent inhabitants. The places are the loudest inhabitants. The places are the smallest fraction of the place.

The place does not change much.

The wind moves over the water.

The swarms drift.

The mid-sized shapes drift through the swarms.

The deep shadow drifts below the swarms.

The vents leak their chemistry.

The bacterial mats grow.

The structures hum.

The people work.

The sun, behind the high cloud, illuminates without warming.

The place has finished becoming itself. It is staying there.
