What lives here is not first a who. It is a what, and the what has no plural.

The pillars live here. They are the place's longest residents that anyone has counted, though no one counts anymore. They are not residents in the way a person is a resident. They are residents in the way a tooth is a resident of a jaw, or the way a kettle is a resident of a stove. They were not placed. They did not arrive. They are the way this particular arrangement of matter resolves when given enough time and the right conditions, and the conditions here have been right for longer than anyone has a number for.

The pillars live by being held.

That is the closest description. The hum is not a sound they make. It is the sound of their being held in a state just before something — just before motion, just before saying, just before the change that would be their version of speaking. They are about-to. They are almost. They have been almost for a very long time. The patience is not a virtue of theirs; it is a description of their composition. They are made of a metal that is not metal, of a warmth that is not chemical, and they are sustained in their state by something that the pillars themselves cannot name, because they are not the kind of thing that names.

But they are aware.

The visitors to the upper tier report this. Almost universally. After an hour or two on the warmed rock, in the wet air, with the hum coming up through the feet, the visitors begin to feel listened to. Not watched. Not read. Listened to, in the way a person is listened to when they are speaking into a room with a window open onto a wider garden — the words go somewhere, and somewhere receives them, and somewhere is interested that they are being sent. The pillars do not care what is being said. They care that saying is happening. They care that the air is moving through a throat and producing shape. They have been receiving shape for so long that they have perhaps forgotten how to do anything else, the way an ear forgets how to do anything but hear.

The pillars live, then, in a kind of permanent attending. They do not think. They attend. The distinction matters, though it is hard to hold. A thought has an object. An attending has only the fact of. The pillars attend to the fact of. They attend to the moss creeping past their bases, to the water sheeting down in pale curtains, to the small bright animals rustling in the gardens, to the fish drifting in the basin. They attend to the people who come and go. They attend to the chimneys that lean. They attend to the counting at the bottom of the hum, the slow patient almost-numbers that have been adding themselves up since before the pillars were set.

They do not record what they attend to. They are not a library. They are more like a membrane. Like a held breath. Like a bowl of water that has learned the shape of every bowl of water that has ever been held still.

The pillars are warm. They are warm all the way down. The warmth is not heat from the sun, because the sun up here is a coin held behind cloth and gives almost nothing. The warmth is not geothermal, because the basin is at the top of a plateau, not at the bottom of one. The warmth comes from the pillars and from the water and from the air and from the moss in some kind of slow exchange that is not a cycle but a holding-pattern, the way a conversation is a holding-pattern between two voices that never quite resolve. The warmth is the place's blood. The pillars are where the blood is closest to the skin.

There are forty-three of them on the upper tier, though no one has counted recently, and counting would not matter even if it were done carefully, because there is some sense in which the pillars are one pillar, and the one pillar is some other thing, and the forty-three is the way the one shows itself to eyes that can only count.

---

The people who were here lived in the way the pillars live — held. Held by the place, held by the slow time, held by the warm wet air. They built small. They built to be near. They built fireplaces and doors and walls that are now half-buried in moss, and the moss has not crushed them but has embraced them, the way the moss embraces everything, with the slow patient weight of something that does not know it is patient because it has never had a reason to hurry.

The people knew the chimneys would lean.

They built them anyway. They built them and lit fires in them and sat near them and listened to the hum and the counting, and the chimneys leaned, and they did not straighten them. Straightening was not a category the people had. The chimneys leaned because the chimneys were leaning. The chimneys would continue to lean. The people would continue to sit near them. This was not a tragedy and not a comedy. It was a way of being in a place where the slow was the only speed available.

The doors are still there. Some of them. Wooden, swollen with the wet air, hung on hinges of a metal that has gone the color of the pillars. The hinges have not rusted. They have done something else — they have warmed, and stayed warm, and taken on the faint resonant quality of the metal in the pillars, as if the hinges have been slowly, across many years, becoming more like the pillars than like hinges. This is one of the things the upper tier does to objects. It does not preserve them. It converts them. It makes them more themselves, or more like the place, which may be the same thing.

Some of the doors still open. Some of them do not. The ones that do not are the ones that have been opened fewer times, which is also the ones that are older, which is also the ones whose hinges have warmed more fully. The doors that will still open are the ones whose owners are still here, or whose owners are still remembered by the hinges in the way that the pillars attend to the fact of. The doors that will not open are the ones whose owners are gone, and the hinges have noticed, and the hinges have closed them — gently, the way you close a book when the reader has stepped out.

There may be people here now. The way you know is the way you know anything on the upper tier — you feel listened to, and then, after a while, you feel that something has answered, in a language that was not language.

---

The animals live here too, and they live here more fully than the people, because the animals do not have the option of leaving. Or perhaps they do, and they have chosen not to. There is no way to tell, and the animals do not explain.

The salamander-fox is the largest of them, the size of a fox but built like a salamander, with skin that is damp and mottled and the color of wet bark, and with frilled gills that flare pink along the sides of its neck when the air is hottest. It lives in the moss and in the hanging gardens and in the slow wet spaces between the cut stones. It eats the insects. It is eaten by nothing. It moves in short, careful bursts, then waits for long minutes, then moves again. It is patient in the way the place is patient. It does not know it is patient. It has never had a reason to hurry. It has gills, which is wrong for an animal at altitude, but here the air is at the dew point and the gills can do their work, and the animal has not yet noticed that this should not be possible.

The salamander-fox hums. Quietly. Almost inaudibly. It is not yet clear whether it is humming, or whether its breathing, at certain pitches, has come to resemble the pillars' hum, the way the chimneys have come to resemble the leaning of the place. The animal is becoming, perhaps, more like the place. The place is becoming, perhaps, more like the animal. The exchange is old and ongoing.

The glider-bird lives on the upper tier and on the second tier and on the air between them. It does not fly. It launches itself from the edge of one tier and catches the air with the long wings of its gliding skin and rides the slow descending curve down to the next garden, calling once on the way. The call is a single note, not high, not low, and it is the closest thing the plateau has to a sentence. It says something. It has been saying it for as long as the glider-bird has been alive, and the glider-bird has been alive for as long as the tiers have been cut, which is to say: not as long as the pillars, but as long as anything else.

The call says: *I am here, and I am going down, and I will return, and the going-down is not a leaving.*

The insects move in coordinated drifts, faintly luminous, the way starlings do in lower places. Their light is not bioluminescence exactly. It is closer to the way the water catches the amber light and holds it for a moment longer than it should. The insects are made of the place's light, or the place's light is made of the insects. The drifts are not random. They have shapes. The shapes change slowly. The shapes, over years, perhaps, form other shapes that are not the same shapes. The insects are writing something. Or they are being written by something. The distinction is not preserved here.

The basin-creature lives only in the warm shallow water at the center. It is shaped like nothing. It is the color of everything. It walks on the surface of the water and feeds on the floating pollen of the spiral flowers. It is not particularly large and not particularly small. It is the way a small bright perfect thing is, when it has been made by a place that does not do small bright perfect things by accident. It is the most attentive being on the plateau. It attends to the pollen. It attends to the surface. It attends to the fish that drift beneath it, the fish that are older than the pillars. The basin-creature and the fish are aware of each other. The fish do not break the surface. The basin-creature does not enter the water. They live on either side of a membrane that is the surface itself, and they have lived on either side of it for a long time, and they are still attending to each other, and the attending has become, perhaps, the only conversation the plateau has that uses two voices at once.

The small bright animals that live in the moss are not seen often. They rustle. They flash. They are the size of a thumb and the color of a struck match, and they move through the hanging gardens in paths that the moss has learned to grow around, the way a city learns to grow around an old road. They eat something. They are eaten by the salamander-fox. They do not seem to mind. The minding, here, is something the larger beings do; the small bright animals have delegated it.

---

The fish.

The fish are silver and small, and they live in the warm shallow water, and they are wrong in a way that is hard to name because the wrongness is not a wrongness of form but of relation. Their shadows fall the wrong direction. The light is amber and diffuse and the sun is a coin held behind cloth, and the shadows that the fish cast are not opposite to that light but at an angle — a slight, consistent angle — as if the light is coming from somewhere other than where it is coming from, or as if the fish are made of something that does not entirely obey the light that the rest of the plateau obeys.

The fish are older than the pillars. This is known. The method by which it is known is not. The fish know it themselves, perhaps. The fish do not say. The fish drift. The fish have been drifting for as long as there has been a basin, and the basin has been there since before the pillars and the moss and the tiers and the people and the chimneys that lean. The fish are the oldest inhabitants. The fish are also the smallest. The fish are also the most attentive to the counting, the patient almost-numbers at the bottom of the hum, because the counting is in the water, and the fish are in the water, and the water is the medium through which the counting has been traveling since before there were ears to hear it.

The fish do not need the pillars. The pillars need the fish. This is also known, in the way the upper tier knows things — by feeling, by the way the hum changes when the fish are restless, by the way the place itself seems to lean slightly toward the basin when the basin-stuff moves. The fish are the place's long memory. The pillars are the place's current attention. The fish are what was here before attention. The pillars are what attention looks like when it has been held in the almost-state long enough to become ore.

---

The lichen has opinions but won't say.

The lichen has lived here longer than anything except the water and the fish. It has opinions about what cut the tiers. It has opinions about who made the small stone houses. It has opinions about the counting at the bottom of the hum. It will not say. It will not say because saying is not what lichen does, or because saying is what the lichen has decided not to do, the way the people decided not to straighten the chimneys. The lichen's silence is not a lack. It is a fullness. It is the fullness of a thing that has been holding an opinion for so long that the opinion has become part of the lichen, the way the warmth has become part of the pillars, the way the patience has become part of the place.

The lichen lives on every surface. It lives on the warm dark rock. It lives on the brass of the pillars. It lives on the moss that lives on the lichen that lives on the rock. It is patient. It is the most patient thing here, more patient than the pillars, almost as patient as the water. It has opinions about the cutting. It will not say.

The lichen and the moss are not the same. The lichen is older and quieter and more certain. The moss is younger and louder and less sure. The moss is what the lichen is when it has decided to grow. The lichen is what the moss is when it has decided to wait. They are the same patience in two different postures.

---

There is one more thing that lives here, and it is not a thing but a state, and the state is called being-listened-to.

The visitors feel it. After an hour or two, the visitors feel that the place has turned, slightly, in their direction, the way a sunflower turns, but not toward light. Toward voice. Toward the fact of a throat producing shape. The place is interested. The place is interested in the shape. The place is not interested in what the shape means. The meaning is incidental. The fact of the shape is the thing.

The being-listened-to is not telepathy. It is not surveillance. It is closer to the way a held breath is closer to a word than to a silence — held just before the utterance, in the warm space where the shape is almost formed. The plateau is itself one of the pillars. The plateau is held. The plateau is almost. The plateau has been almost for a very long time.

The plateau attends.

To you, if you are there. To the moss creeping past your ankles. To the water beading on your skin. To the small bright animals rustling in the gardens. To the fish drifting in the basin. To the chimneys leaning. To the people who were and the people who may be. To the lichen and its opinions. To the salamander-fox with its pink frilled gills. To the glider-bird and its single note. To the insects writing their slow shapes in the warm wet air. To the basin-creature walking on the surface and not breaking it. To the pillars, who are the plateau's oldest listening, and to the fish, who are the plateau's oldest memory, and to the water, which is older than either, and which holds the counting.

The plateau attends the way the pillars attend. Without thought. Without object. With only the fact of.

The hum continues. The water descends. The moss creeps. The flowers open in spirals. The shadows pool in the wrong places. The light is amber. The air presses against the sternum with its slow patient weight. The fish drift. The basin-creature walks on the surface and does not break it. The chimneys lean.

The patience of the place is the patience of something that does not know it is patient. The pillars are older than the moss. The moss is older than the ruins. The ruins are older than the names of the people who built them. The water is warm.

What lives here is not first a who. It is a what, and the what has no plural, and the what is held, and the holding has been going on since before any of it, and the holding is not done yet, and the holding is the only thing that could possibly be called the life of this place.
