# What Lives Here

A place like this does not have inhabitants. It has *participants*. The distinction matters, because an inhabitant implies a place that existed first and was then filled; a participant implies a place that is being made, continuously, by everything in it, and which would cease to exist if any of them stopped. So I will not list the residents. I will describe, as best I can, who is in the room.

## The Air Itself

Start with what you cannot see, because it is the most numerous and the most consequential. The airspace from the herb layer up through the main canopy is not empty, and is not, properly speaking, air. It is a thick, living medium, a kind of broth. A cubic meter of it, at any given moment, contains a staggering density of small trajectories: gnats so small they exist mostly as a slight shimmer in the peripheral vision; midges that will rise at dusk in columns so dense they look like smoke reversed, falling upward; flies of a hundred species, some of them metallic, some of them mimics of wasps, some of them robbers, dressed in the colors of things that sting because the arrangement works. Mosquitoes are here, in numbers that depend on the season and the water level, and they are not malicious. They are simply thorough. The females need blood to lay their eggs, and a warm body passing through is a resource, the way a fallen fruit is a resource, the way a dead leaf is a resource. The air has a population density, and you are now part of it.

Beetles are everywhere, doing the work that no one sees. There are click beetles the length of a finger, clicking themselves upright when they fall onto their backs with a sound like a fingernail flicked against a thumbnail. There are weevils with their improbable snouts. There are bark beetles so small they live their whole lives inside a single layer of phloem, in a tunnel the width of a pencil line. There are ground beetles that run at night over the forest floor in quick silver arcs, hunting the things that hunt the things that hunt the leaf litter. There are scarabs, broad and patient, that roll their balls of dung along the paths they have made, which are the same paths their great-grandparents made, because the geometry of a good ball-rolling path does not change much in a few generations. The beetles are the cleanup crew, the demolition crew, the recyclers. Without them, in perhaps a decade, the forest would be hip-deep in its own dead leaves, and after a century it would be buried, and after a thousand years it would be a peat formation that future geologists would puzzle over.

Wasps are here in great variety, and they are, if you can resist the panic reflex, among the most beautiful things in the forest. The paper wasps have made their nests in the lower branches, those gray, papery, melon-sized globes with their hexagonal geometry, and they are, individually, surprisingly delicate—they will not sting unless you mishandle them, and even then, the message is mostly informational. The spider wasps hunt the spiders, and the spiders hunt the other wasps, and so on, in a tangle of predation that has no particular top. The yellowjackets nest in the ground, in paper cities under root systems, and they are less forgiving. The forest accepts them. Ants, of course, are everywhere, and they are not so much animals as they are a substrate. They have laid their chemical highways along the roots so long that the roots themselves have been shaped by the traffic—worn smooth, grooved, in some places hollowed into tubes. Leaf-cutter ants, if they are present, are running their assembly lines: a worker cuts a precise arc from a leaf, hoists it above her head like a sail, and walks it back to a subterranean garden where it will be used to cultivate a particular fungus, which is the only thing the colony actually eats. The leaf is not food. The fungus is food. The leaf is a substrate for the food. This is a level of indirection that the forest treats as ordinary.

Spiders hold the air in webs, and the webs are everywhere, in the geometries you would expect and in geometries you would not. Orb weavers have strung their great round nets between trunks at face height, and they sit in the centers, heads down, patient as axioms, waiting for a vibration that will mean something has hit the silk. The forest gives them a great deal of business. There are also the tangle-web weavers, the cobweb spiders, the jumping spiders—the last of which will, if you are very still, turn and look at you with the particular intensity of a thing that has eight eyes and an opinion. There is a species, somewhere in this kind of forest, that is the size of your hand, and it lives in a burrow with a trapdoor, and it does not come out unless something heavy walks past, at which point it explodes upward and pulls the thing down. The forest has its ambush predators at every scale.

## The Chorus

Above the invertebrates, woven through the canopy, is the sound. The sound does not stop. The sound is not, in any meaningful sense, a thing made by individuals. It is the aggregate output of thousands of small voices, and at any given moment the air is carrying perhaps a dozen distinct calls at once, layered, braided, overlapping in such density that the ear gives up trying to separate them and hears instead a single, long, modulated tone—the voice of the place, rising and falling with the hour and the light and the weather, but never breaking.

In the morning, before the heat becomes oppressive, the insect layer is still loud and the bird layer is building. There are flycatchers sitting on exposed perches, sallying out, returning. There are antbirds following the army ant swarms below them, waiting for the things flushed by the ants. There are toucans—large, absurd, with bills that look like a practical joke but are in fact extremely sophisticated heat exchangers—calling to each other in yelps that carry for half a kilometer. There are parrots, in pairs, in the higher canopy, gnawing at things. There are the small brown things, the understory specialists, the antwrens, the woodcreepers, the foliage-gleaners, each of them doing one job with extreme competence and a song that is theirs alone, identifiable to anyone who has spent enough mornings in the canopy to learn.

And below them, in the shrub layer, the wood-quail move in coveys, calling to each other in soft whistles that you will hear before you see them, which is if anything worse, because the sound seems to come from several directions at once and you cannot tell if you are inside the covey or outside it. There are tinamous, drab, almost ground-dwelling, whose song is a single long, clear, trembling note that can hold for so long you begin to suspect the singer is not real. There are ground-cuckoos, if you are lucky, which you probably are not, because they are ghosts.

At dusk the register changes. The day birds fall silent, and the night birds take over, and the insects modulate downward into a lower, more continuous hum, and the frogs begin. The frogs are everywhere, in a riot of species, and the males of each species are calling to claim a small bit of stream or puddle, and the combined sound is so loud and so layered that it has its own weather. Some of them call from the canopy, in the water that has collected in bromeliads, those small green tanks that hold an entire miniature ecosystem in their cups. Some call from the ground. Some call from the surface of the slow brown rivers, sitting on floating leaves. Their calls are how the forest knows itself is still here, the way a heartbeat tells a body it is alive.

## The Quiet Ones

Beneath the noise, and around it, and through it, are the animals you do not hear. The forest floor is dense with them, and most of them have made an art of being overlooked.

There are mice, of several species, and rats, and small opossums, all of them running their errands in the dark spaces between the roots, in tunnels they have used so often the leaf litter has compressed into highways. There are armadillos, if the range is right, snuffling through the litter with their particular patient intensity, leaving little conical pits where they have dug. There are pacas and agoutis, large rodents with the particular poise of animals that know they are on someone's menu and have arranged their entire nervous system around the question of not being there when the something arrives. The agouti, in particular, is a serious animal. It can sit up on its haunches and hold a Brazil nut in its paws and crack it with the same competence you would use to crack a sunflower seed, and it caches the nuts in scattered places, and forgets where some of them are, and this is the principal way the Brazil nut tree propagates itself. The agouti and the Brazil nut tree are in a mutualism, and the agouti does not know this, and the tree does not know this either, and the forest knows it only as a fact.

There are anteaters. If you are very lucky, you will see one. The giant anteater is a strange and serious animal, the size of a small bear, with a tail like a flag and a snout like a tube, and it walks through the forest with its nose low and its tongue flicking in and out at a rate you cannot consciously track, lapping up the ants and termites that live in the soil and the rotting wood. It has no teeth. It does not need teeth. It has a stomach that grinds. It moves slowly because it does not need to move quickly. Nothing it eats can outrun it, and nothing eats it, because it has claws the length of your fingers, and it will stand up and embrace you, and this is not an embrace you will survive. The forest tolerates the anteater the way it tolerates the rivers: as a force.

There are sloths, in the canopy, moving at a rate that the rest of the forest has accepted as a fact of life. They hang upside down from particular branches, and the green algae that grows in their fur is, in some sense, their camouflage, and the moths that live in the fur are a small ecosystem, and the sloth moves between trees on a schedule so slow that the moths have time to complete a generation between moves. They are not lazy. They are running a very slow program. The forest is patient with them, the way the forest is patient with everything.

There are coatis, raccoon relatives, moving through the understory in long, vocal bands, their tails held up like flags, their noses working. There are tayras, weasel-like, bounding through with an arrogance that the forest permits. There are kinkajous at night, in the canopy, with their enormous eyes and their prehensile tails, drinking nectar from flowers and being, in their small way, pollinators. There are ocelots, small wildcats, the ghosts of the understory, spotted and silent, and if you see one it will be for a moment and then it will be gone, and you will not be certain it was there.

## The Heavy Things

And then there are the ones you hear but rarely see, and when you see what they have done, you understand the scale.

Tapirs are here. The tapir is a strange animal, a holdover from another age, looking like a small horse assembled from a kit of parts that did not quite match—pig-like, rhino-related, with a short prehensile trunk that it uses, with great delicacy, to pick specific leaves off specific stems. It is the largest land animal in this kind of forest, and it moves through the understory in the way an iceberg moves through water, leaving a wake of disturbed vegetation and certain knowledge, in the small animals, that something large has passed. The tapir drops its dung in the water, and the dung contains seeds, and the seeds germinate in the nutrient-rich muck at the river's edge, and so the riverside is a tapir garden, and has been for a very long time.

Peccaries are here, in sounders of a dozen or more, and they are the forest's most social large mammals. They travel in tight groups, grunting, sniffing, their small sharp teeth busy with everything, and when they are alarmed they click their teeth together in a sound like a rattlesnake, which is not a coincidence. They are not dangerous unless cornered, and even then, the danger is mostly the math: there are a lot of them, and they are committed, and they smell strongly of a particular musk that the forest has grown accustomed to. They root in the soft ground, looking for tubers, and they shape the forest floor in their turn, and the forest floor is shaped by many hands.

There may be deer here, small brocket deer, the size of a dog, browsing in the understory with the delicate precision of animals that know exactly which leaves they want. There may be capybaras, the largest rodents in the world, sitting in the shallows like small hippos, chewing, watching, unbothered, the most relaxed of all the large mammals, masters of the art of being left alone.

And there are the cats. The jaguar. The puma. The jaguarundi. The margay, which is the size of a house cat and lives its entire life in the branches, hunting the things that live in the canopy with a prehensile tail and an understanding of three-dimensional space that the other cats find admirable. The jaguar is the apex. The jaguar is what the forest is, in a particular sense, arranged around. The jaguar does not tolerate disturbance. The jaguar will not be approached. The jaguar hunts the tapir, and the peccary, and the caiman, and it will also, if it must, eat the smaller things, and it carries in its jaw a force sufficient to crush the skull of a caiman, and the forest has arranged itself so that there is room for it. The jaguar requires a huge territory, and the forest provides one, and in exchange the jaguar keeps the populations of the large herbivores in a particular balance, and the balance is, in the long run, what allows the forest to keep being a forest.

## The Water-Dwellers

The rivers are not empty. They are full, in a quiet way, of things that have given themselves over to the water.

There are caimans, relatives of the alligator, lying in the shallows with only their eyes and nostrils above the surface, and they are patient in the way the forest is patient. A caiman can hold its breath for a long time. A caiman can wait. A caiman can be mistaken for a log, and the mistake is fatal in a way that the log is not. They hunt the fish and the capybaras and, sometimes, the things that come down to drink. They are cold. They are slow. They are perfectly designed.

There are otters, giant otters, the size of a small person, living in family groups along the rivers, fishing cooperatively, chattering to each other in a vocabulary of squeaks and grunts, and they are, by general consensus, the most social of the mustelids, and the most noisy, and the most fun to watch, which is not a scientific observation but is also not wrong.

There are turtles, side-necked turtles, on the logs, basking in whatever thin sun the canopy allows. There are fish, in the brown water, in a riot of species, most of them small, most of them drab, most of them too busy being fish to be anything else. There are electric knifefish, navigating their dark water with electric fields, sensing the world in a way the rest of the forest does not. There are the small things you do not see, the larvae, the nymphs, the eggs, the entire parallel civilization that lives in the water and never comes out.

## The Floor

And the floor itself is alive. Not metaphorically. The spongy loam is a single, interwoven organism in a meaningful sense, because the fungi have threaded themselves through every cubic centimeter of it in a network so dense that the forest is, in some readings, a single superorganism with the trees as its visible organs and the fungi as its nervous system.

Mycorrhizal fungi wrap around the roots of the trees in a fine white felt, and the trees feed them sugars from photosynthesis, and the fungi feed the trees phosphorus and nitrogen and water from places the roots cannot reach. The trees of one species, through the fungi, are connected to the trees of another species, and nutrients flow along the network from where they are abundant to where they are scarce, and a tree that is dying will, before it dies, dump its remaining resources into the network, and the network will distribute them, and so the forest is, in some real sense, in conversation with itself, in a language we are only beginning to learn to read.

The fungi are the things that make the slow work possible. They are the ones breaking down the lignin, the tough structural compound in wood that nothing else can digest. They are the ones turning the fallen trunks, over decades and centuries, back into soil. Without them, the carbon would stay locked in dead wood. Without them, the forest would not be a forest. It would be a museum of itself.

The bacteria are here too, in uncountable numbers, doing the other half of the work, fixing nitrogen, cycling sulfur, mediating the chemistry of the soil in ways that we are still, frankly, only beginning to understand. The forest floor is the most complex chemical environment on the continent, and it is being run by things that do not have nervous systems, and it has been running for longer than anything with a nervous system has been around to watch it.

## The Whole

What lives here, then, is a system. Not a collection, not an assemblage, not a list, but a system, in the sense that any part removed will cause the rest to change, and some removals will cause the whole to collapse. The trees cannot exist without the fungi. The fungi cannot exist without the trees. The trees cannot reproduce without the agoutis (in the case of the Brazil nut) or the toucans (in the case of many canopy species) or the bats (in the case of others). The insects cannot exist without the plants. The birds cannot exist without the insects. The jaguars cannot exist without the peccaries and the caimans. The caimans cannot exist without the fish. The fish cannot exist without the insects whose larvae live in the water. The rivers cannot exist without the forest that slows the rain and binds the soil. And the forest cannot exist without the rivers that bring the sediment and the nutrients.

It is a closed system, mostly. It takes in sunlight and rain and carbon dioxide, and it gives out oxygen and transpiration and the slow brown rivers, and it runs, and it has been running, and it will run for as long as the climate allows and longer than the engines will.

What lives here is a thing that has learned, over a very long time, to be itself. It is not optimized. It is not efficient in the industrial sense. It is, in some real sense, extravagant—an excess of species, an excess of relationships, an excess of mutualisms, an excess of voices. It is a thing that is, in its entirety, more than the sum of its parts, and the parts are themselves parts of parts of parts, and the whole is the only one of its kind, and it is in this particular place, and it is not anywhere else.

You stand in it. You are small. You are temporary. The forest, in its patient and dripping and calling and immense way, is not aware of you. It is not indifferent to you—indifference implies a posture, and the forest has no posture. It is simply *running*, the way a heart is running, the way an engine is running, and you are standing in it, and it is letting you stand in it, the way it lets the rain stand in it, the way it lets the rivers stand in it, the way it lets everything that is small and temporary pass through it and become part of it and, eventually, become part of the slow brown water that is leaving, always, for the sea.
