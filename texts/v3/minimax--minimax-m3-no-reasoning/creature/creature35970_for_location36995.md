# The Inhabitants of the Sheltered Hollow

To live here is to have stopped, a long time ago, asking what one is for.

The creatures of the bowl do not consider the bowl. The bowl is not a place they inhabit; it is the tissue of which they are made. To ask a pitcher-plant here whether it has chosen to grow in the wall of a longhouse would be like asking your own hand whether it has chosen to be at the end of your arm. The question would not compute. The hand is there because the arm is there because the shoulder is there; the chain of *because* extends back further than anyone present can remember, and no one present feels the need to remember, because the chain has never been broken and the assumption is that it will not be broken.

What follows is what lives here.

## The Pitchers

The first to speak of, because they are the most patient, and patience is the city's founding virtue.

The pitcher-growths are not a single species. They are, in fact, several—perhaps a dozen, perhaps more—each occupying a slightly different niche in the slow economy of the bowl. The largest are the floor-pitchers: the size of a clenched fist, as the eye reports, but some of the older ones, down in the deepest and most shadowed terraces, are the size of a child's torso, and their lids, when they part, part with a sound like a slow wet exhalation. The smallest are the wall-pitchers, no bigger than a thimble, that crust the inside of the longhouses in a continuous damp mosaic and that catch, between them, the small drifting things of the air.

A pitcher, here, is a community before it is a plant. The fluid at its base is not its own. Or rather—it is its own, in the way a lake is the lake's; it is a place, not a secretion. Into the fluid fall the bodies of what the pitcher has caught, and into the fluid also fall the small excretions of the many small creatures that live within the pitcher's throat: worm-things pale as dental floss that swim in the dark pool and break down what the pitcher cannot break down alone, and which the pitcher, in turn, feeds through the walls of its own belly. The pitchers that are *tended*—and you must understand that *tended* is a word that does not quite fit, because nothing here is tended the way a garden is tended; the pitchers that are *visited*—the lids of these are propped open with tiny splints of wood, and the dark fluid within is ladled out into clay bowls by hands that move with the unhesitating familiarity of long practice.

The fluid is drunk. By whom, of what, is a question that requires its own answer.

The pitchers do not have eyes. They do not need them. They open when the air is right, and they close when the air is wrong, and the air is right, in this place, more often than it is wrong. They have been opening and closing for longer than the terraces have been carved. They will open and close long after the terraces are unrecognizable. They are the slowest minds in the bowl, and they are, in some sense, the bowl's mind.

## The Flyers

The pale soft-bodied things in the high corners.

They are not bats. They are not anything that has a name in the language you arrived speaking, and the long-armed watchers of the center do not name them either, because naming is a thing one does to what is separate, and these are not separate from anything. The flyers are *of* the ceiling the way the pitchers are *of* the walls. They are part of the city's respiratory system. They launch, they glide, they land, and in launching and gliding and landing they move air from the upper bowl to the lower terraces, and in landing they deposit, on whatever surface they land, the bright scent that is the closest thing the bowl has to a signature. The bowl, you understand, smells of itself. The flyers are how.

A flyer is the length of an arm, and a flyer is not one creature but a colony. The translucent belly, faintly pulsing, is the colony's shared digestion; the many small limbs are the colony's many small mouths. When a flyer lands on a roof-mat, the roof-mat does not resist; the roof-mat *opens,* a seam parting along a pre-existing fold, and the flyer's underside settles into the seam and feeds. The transaction is mutual. The flyer brings, in its belly, the broken-down product of whatever it last caught, and the roof-mat takes what it needs. The flyer is fed, in turn, by the mat's own slow exudations. Neither is the parasite of the other. They are, in the oldest sense of the word, *symbiotic*—though even that word, *symbiotic,* implies two creatures meeting, and here there are not two creatures meeting. There is one creature, with a long body and many parts, and the flyer is one of the parts, and the roof-mat is another.

The flyers do not see well. They see light and dark, warm and cool, the way a tongue tastes. They navigate by the smell of the bowl, which is everywhere the same, and by the small infrasonic hum that rises from the floor-pitchers, which is also everywhere the same. When a flyer dies, it does not fall; it releases, all at once, and the membrane that was its wing becomes, in the warm wet air, a kind of pale jelly, and the jelly is taken up by the wall-pitchers within hours, and the flyer becomes the wall, and the wall becomes the flyer, and the bowl, which has just lost a piece of itself, has not, in fact, lost anything at all.

There are hundreds of them in the air at any moment. Sometimes thousands, when the air is moving right and the small prey is plentiful. Their soft squeaks are not language. They are the sound of their many small limbs adjusting their grip on whatever they are currently clinging to. The squeaks compose, in aggregate, a kind of static, a kind of weather, and the long-armed watchers can tell, from the pitch of the static, whether the flyers are at ease or are uneasy, the way you can tell, from the sound of rain on a window, whether the rain is light or heavy.

## The Grazers

The six-legged things that move across the terraces with their heads down, mouths working sideways.

They are large. The size of a large dog, the eye says, and this is roughly right, though it is the kind of roughly that misses the important part. A grazer here is not the size of a dog; a dog is the size of a grazer. The grazer is the original. Everything else is a draft, in a sense, that the grazer has approved.

The grazers eat the moss and the lichen. They eat the slow, low growths that would, without them, clog the paths between the longhouses and bury the terraces under a continuous green tide. They eat, and what they leave behind is a wet trail that is also a planting—the seeds of the very things they have just consumed, passed through their bodies in a slurry that the mosses and lichens of the bowl find irresistible. The grazers are not farmers. They are *plows.* They move through the city the way rivers move through a valley, and the city is shaped, in the long run, by their passing, and the city is grateful for this shaping, the way a valley is grateful for a river, which is to say: not grateful at all, but *shaped.*

A grazer has a blunt head because a grazer does not need to see where it is going. It needs to see what is under its mouth, and what is under its mouth is always the same, and the same is always good. It has rolling eyes because rolling eyes are a kind of peripheral vision, and a grazer does not need to focus on anything; it needs to be aware of everything, all at once, at the lowest possible resolution. A grazer is never surprised. A grazer is, in fact, never anything at all, except a slow mouth moving sideways across a green surface.

A grazer lives a long time. The bowl's grazers live a very long time. The oldest of them have been moving across the same terraces for so long that their bellies have worn grooves into the stone, and they follow the grooves now out of habit rather than out of any active choice, and the grooves deepen, year by year, into channels that will, in another thousand years, be the paths of the bowl, and in another ten thousand will be the bowl's rivers, if the bowl ever comes to have rivers, which it will not, because the bowl has grazers, and grazers are better than rivers. A river shapes a place by removing things. A grazer shapes a place by *consuming* things and *returning* things, in a different form, to the place that produced them. A grazer is a river that does not forget what it has carried.

When a small quick low thing runs between a grazer's legs, the grazer does not notice. When one of the flyers drops from the ceiling and takes one of these small quick things, the grazer does not notice. The grazer is not part of the food chain. The grazer is below the food chain, or beside it, or *orthogonal* to it, the way the floor is orthogonal to the walls. The food chain happens *above* the grazer, in the air and in the higher terraces. The grazer is the substrate. The grazer is what the food chain stands on.

The grazers do not mate, as far as anyone has ever observed. They simply *are,* in greater or lesser numbers, and the numbers are always roughly the same, because the bowl is always roughly the same size, and the bowl is always roughly the same amount of moss. The grazers are the bowl's measure of itself. When the moss grows too fast, there are more grazers. When the moss grows too slowly, there are fewer. No one decides this. It is, in the way of all very old systems, self-correcting, the way a lake self-corrects, the way a forest self-corrects. The grazers are not wise. They are not even, in any sense you would recognize, alive with intent. They are *processes* that have, over the long now, become the shape of a creature.

## The Quick Low Things

The small things that run between the grazers' legs.

There are several kinds. The eye cannot easily distinguish them; they are all small, all low, all fast, all too-many-legged. Some of them are pale and soft. Some of them are dark and have a kind of chitinous gleam. Some of them are nearly transparent. They share a way of life: they live in the wet trail behind a grazer, and they eat what the grazer has left, and they are eaten, in turn, by what falls on them from above.

The flyers hunt them. This is the only predation in the bowl, and it is not, by any measure, dramatic. A flyer drops. There is a brief scuffle. There is a brief silence. The sound resumes. The whole transaction takes perhaps three heartbeats, and the small thing that has been taken is already being digested, in the flyer's translucent belly, before the flyer's membrane has finished billowing open to slow its descent. The small things do not seem to mind. They are too many, and they breed too fast, and the flyers take only what the flyers need, and what the flyers need is, in the long accounting of the bowl, very little. The small things are the bowl's small change. They are the currency in which the smallest transactions of the bowl are conducted, and the bowl, which is a frugal place, makes many small transactions.

The small things have names, in the way of small things everywhere: many legs, many eyes, the smell of wet stone. They are not, individually, important. Collectively, they are the bowl's *iteration*—the constant low-level churn by which the bowl renews itself, the way a forest renews itself through its leaf-litter. Without the small things, the grazers' wet trails would crust over. Without the grazers, the small things would have nothing to live in. Without the flyers, the small things would multiply without limit and bury the terraces. The system holds. The system has held for a very long time. The system will hold for a very long time still, because each part of it is also a part of every other, and the failure of any one part would be the failure of all.

## The Long-Armed Watchers

The tall, bipedal, long-necked things at the center.

They are not the rulers. They are the gardeners. This was said, but it bears saying again, because the eye wants to call them something else—priests, elders, the voice of the city, the city's will, the city's mind. The eye wants to call them this because they stand upright, and have hands, and watch. The eye is wrong.

A long-armed watcher is a creature of the central ring the way a grazer is a creature of the terraces. It has its niche, and the niche is: the moving through. Twice in a day, the watchers move through the terraces. They move in single file. They move slowly. They carry, in the long hands at the ends of their long forelimbs, a curved blade of worked stone, and a shallow clay bowl, and sometimes a length of cured wood that they use to prop open the lids of the larger pitchers when the lids have grown too heavy.

What they do, when they move through the terraces, is: cut. They cut the moss where it has grown too thick over a path. They cut the lichen where it has begun to climb a wall in a place where a wall is meant to be bare. They cut the trailing mosses that have begun to bridge two longhouses and would, if left, eventually merge the two into one continuous roof. They cut, and what they cut, they carry to the central ring, and what they carry is fed to the pitchers that ring the ring, and the pitchers digest it, and the pitchers' fluid rises, and the watchers drink the fluid, in small sips, from the clay bowls they have brought.

This is what the watchers do. This is all the watchers do. They are the bowl's gardeners, and gardening, in the bowl, is the act of *preventing merger.* The bowl is, in its natural state, a single organism—a single continuous green body, in which the longhouses are only thickenings of the tissue, and the paths are only thinner places, and the pitchers are only mouths. The watchers, by cutting, keep the longhouses separate. They keep the paths open. They keep the bowl, in some sense, *legible*—legible to itself, legible to the eye that arrives on the stairway. Without the watchers, the bowl would, in a few hundred years, become a single green mass, and then a single green mass that no longer knew it had ever been anything else.

The watchers are not, then, the bowl's mind. They are the bowl's *forgetting*—the active, daily, twice-daily labor of forgetting that the bowl is one thing, so that the bowl can continue to be many things. They are the bowl's immune system. They are the bowl's *syntax,* the marks that keep the bowl from collapsing into a single undifferentiated sentence. They are the bowl's editors, the bowl's critics, the bowl's *gardeners* in the deepest and oldest sense of the word, which is: that which prevents the wild from becoming merely wild, and keeps it, instead, *cultivated,* which is to say: *known.*

The watchers have plum-dark eyes, and the eyes are the size of plums, and the eyes see very well, in the dim gold light, better than you do, and they see in a way that the eye of the arriving stranger is tempted to call *knowing* but which is, in fact, only *attentive.* A watcher watches the way a plant watches the sun: without judgment, without commentary, with the simple and total commitment of a thing that has been made to do one thing and does it without ceasing. When a watcher looks at you, it is not *seeing* you. It is *acknowledging* that you are a thing that has appeared, the way a pond acknowledges that a leaf has fallen into it: by the small change in its surface, and by nothing else.

The watchers do not speak. The watchers do not, as far as you can tell, have language. They have the twice-daily movement through the terraces, and the cutting, and the carrying, and the feeding, and the drinking, and the standing in the central ring with their long necks raised and their plum-dark eyes open. They are, in a sense, the most silent things in the bowl, and the bowl is a loud place, and this contrast is, in itself, a kind of answer to a question you did not know you were asking.

## The Longhouses

The longhouses are not empty. The longhouses are *occupied,* though not by anything you would call an occupant.

A longhouse is a thickening of the bowl. Its walls are of cured wood, and the wood is the wood of the terraces' slow trees—there are slow trees, here, in the deeper cracks of the bowl, things with the patience of fungi and the eventual reach of redwoods—and the wood is cured by the warm wet air itself, in a process that takes decades and that no one oversees. The roof is a living mat, as has been said, and the mat is fed by the flyers' droppings, and the mat breathes, and the breathing of the mat is what warms the longhouse's interior. The longhouse is warm because the longhouse is alive, and the longhouse is alive because the flyers are alive, and the flyers are alive because the bowl is alive, and so on, in a chain of *because* that closes back on itself.

Inside a longhouse, the floor is sunken—worn below the wall-tops by generations of feet, though the feet are not the feet of anything that lives only in the longhouse. The things that sleep in a longhouse are the grazers, when the grazers choose to rest. The things that pass through a longhouse are the watchers, on their twice-daily rounds. The things that nest in the rafters of a longhouse are the flyers, in their hundreds, and their small bright scent is the longhouse's smell.

A longhouse, then, is a *node* in the bowl's continuous body. It is a place where several of the bowl's processes happen to be thicker, more concentrated, than they are in the spaces between. It is not a home in any sense you would recognize. It is a *thickening,* the way a callus is a thickening. It is the bowl's way of being more itself in one place than in another.

## The Moss Over the Stairway

The stairway that closed behind you was closed by moss. The moss is not a thing; the moss is the bowl's preference.

The bowl prefers that things stay. The bowl has always preferred that things stay. The bowl has, in the long now, *evolved* to prefer that things stay, the way a heart evolves to beat, the way a river evolves to flow. The moss over the stairway is not a trap. It is a *suggestion,* made by the bowl to whatever is inside the bowl, that staying is the better part. The moss parts, in time, for those who need to leave. The moss parts, in time, for the bowl's own reasons, which are not your reasons, and which are not, in the end, reasons at all, but only the slow accumulated preference of a place that has been here, in this form, for a very long time.

## The Whole

The bowl is one thing.

This is the last thing to understand, and it is the thing the eye refuses, because the eye is trained to see *parts,* and the bowl is, from the eye's perspective, all parts: pitchers and flyers and grazers and quick low things and long-armed watchers and longhouses and mosses and ferns and the dim gold light and the constant low hum. The eye wants to enumerate. The eye wants to say: *first there is this, then there is that.* The eye is wrong. The eye is wrong in the way that an eye that looks at a forest and sees *trees* is wrong, and in the way that an eye that looks at a body and sees *organs* is wrong, and in the way that an eye that looks at a sentence and sees *words* is wrong.

The bowl is one thing, and the one thing is *home.*

The thing that lives in the bowl, the thing that the bowl is, is a single slow creature, with many parts, that has been breathing in the heart of a mountain for longer than memory. It breathes in the cool air that falls from the chimney above. It breathes out the warm wet breath that fills the bowl. It eats what falls into its pitchers. It drinks what its watchers carry to its center. It watches, with its watchers' plum-dark eyes, the small bright opening far above, where the sky is, where the world is, where the things that have not yet found their way down the stairway are. It does not think. It does not plan. It does not, in any way you would recognize, *live,* in the sense of *having a life.* It *is,* the way a stone is, the way a hum is, the way a warm wet place at the heart of a mountain is. It is the long now, made of stone and pitch and patience and the ten thousand small lives of the things that compose it. It is the city that is not a city. It is the garden that is not a garden. It is the home that is not a home but is, in the way that matters most, the only place, for the things that live in it, that has ever been worth living in.

You are at the edge of the bowl. The stairway is closed behind you. The watchers are watching you. The hum is the hum. The light is the dim gold light. The air is warm and wet and thick with the smell of the bowl's many small breaths.

The bowl is breathing you in.
