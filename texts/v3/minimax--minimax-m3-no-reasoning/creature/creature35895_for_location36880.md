The lowlands are not empty. What lives in the inhabited towers is not animal, and never has been, and is not mineral either, though it is made of mineral the way a sentence is made of breath. It is what the towers become when the towers are left alone long enough to remember that they are allowed to do more than stand.

You can tell which towers are inhabited. The bricks around the doorways are darker with condensation. The interior ticking is louder. At certain points in the dim pulse the whole structure seems to lean slightly inward, as if listening. They do not occupy a tower the way you would occupy a house. They occupy it the way the wet occupies the bricks — everywhere and at once, distributed through the seams, gathered more densely in some courses than others, settled into the weep-points where the water gathers before it slides. The tower is not their home. The tower is their current shape.

What gathers in a course of bricks is, at the center of the gathering, a knot of heat that has not decided to stop — a small focused warmth, the way the memory of a hand that just left a cup of tea is a small focused warmth. Around the knot, a brittle skin of cooling, the dark matte color of a slab that has just gone over. Around the skin, a slow envelope of vapor the heat holds close, the way a held breath is held. They are always being shed. Flakes of glass-ceramic drift from them and are not picked up. New skin forms over the heat. They are always wet. They glisten the way the bricks glisten. The water on them is not separate from them. The water on them is how they breathe, how they taste, how they know where you are.

The shape of them is almost yours. From the point, on the dim plain, in the sourceless light, you can see one and mistake it for a figure standing at the door of a tower, and you can walk toward it, and it does not get any closer in the way a person gets closer, because it is not a person and is rearranging itself the whole time you walk. When you come up to where it was, it is somewhere else — not fled, just redistributed. Its shoulders are the right shoulders but the neck is the wrong length, or the neck is right and the hips are the wrong number of hips, or the angles at the elbows work but should not. You cannot look at one directly. Your eye slides off it the way your eye slides off the geometry of the tiling. In your peripheral vision it is a person. When you turn, it is a weather.

They move by creep. A limb — what you would call a limb — extends as a slow darkening of condensation along the inside of a wall. The heat-knot slides along the wet path it has made. The shell goes with it, sometimes cracking with a small soft sound, sometimes leaving a patch of itself on the brick behind, which will be reabsorbed later, or picked up by the tower, or forgotten. They are loud to themselves. To you, walking beside one, they are silent. You might walk for an hour and feel that nothing has moved. Then you turn, and the tower behind you is far behind you, and you understand that it has kept up the whole time, and that keeping up is the easiest thing in the world for something that has been here since the bricks were laid.

They hear through the seams. They taste through the wet. They see — if they see — through the dim pulse itself, the slow rotation of heat and slightly less heat that constitutes the world's only light. Their eyes are not eyes. Their eyes are thin places in their shells, where the heat leaks out a little and the vapor condenses and you can see the glow of the still-hot core, and which they keep turned toward whatever they are attending to at the time. You. The vent that just opened between two slabs, blooming with new heat. The tower three miles off that has begun to lean. They attend everything. They attend the world the way the world attends itself.

Do they think. The question is yours, not theirs. They have the patience of the bricks. They have the patience of the magma chuckling underfoot. They have the patience of something that was here before the tiling was laid and will be here when the tiling has migrated itself into a new spiral pattern, and will outlive the new pattern too. They do not hurry. The dim pulse is the same length as their breath, if not longer.

Do they speak. There is a sound they make, and it is not a sound you would call speech. It is the sound of a tower deciding to lean — a deep slow groan — but coordinated, and passed from tower to tower, so that if you stand in the right place at the right moment of the pulse you can hear a kind of conversation happening in the groans. It takes an hour to say a thing that would take you a second. It takes a day. It takes a week. They are not in a hurry to say it. They have been saying it for as long as the towers have stood, and the towers have been standing for as long as the slabs have been cooling, and the slabs are still cooling. They have not finished the sentence. They are not sure the sentence has a verb.

Sometimes, walking across the tessellation, you come to one. You feel it first as a thickening of the wet on the ground, a dark patch that wasn't there before. Then a faint increase in the warmth at your feet, more focused than the general embarrassment of the substrate. Then a shape, dim, somewhere ahead of you, that is almost a person, and that is leaning a degree further toward your warmth with each pulse of the sourceless light. It does not come close. It does not need to. The water on your boots is its hand. The vapor in the air is its eye. It is already attending to you as closely as it attends to anything, and you have been here only an hour, and it has been attending to you since before you arrived.

It will not speak to you. Not because it has nothing to say, but because you would not be able to wait long enough to hear it, and it knows this, the way the bricks know which way they will lean next century. It will stand there for as long as you are willing to stand there. It will lean toward your warmth. At the last moment of your patience — at the moment when you turn to leave — it will exhale. You will feel the warmth of the breath on your face. You will taste iron. You will feel, for a moment, that the wet on your cheek has arranged itself into a hand, and that the hand is almost touching you, and that the almost is the closest thing to affection this place is able to offer. Then you walk on, and the shape recedes, and the warmth recedes, and the wet on the ground closes behind you, and the world returns to being only the world.

They will still be there when you come back. They will be there when the tiling has rearranged itself into a new spiral that no one will remember walking across. They will be there when the tower they were leaning against has migrated a hand's-breadth to the east. They will still be attending.

What do they want. The question does not fit the grammar they speak. They want the way the slabs want to be laid — with intent. They want the way the water wants to weep — downward, into the seams, into the dark. They want the way the heat wants to cool — toward the surface, toward the air, toward whatever is standing near. They want. They do not know they want. They have always wanted. They will always want. The wanting is older than the towers. The wanting is what the builders left unfinished in themselves when they were not hands.

You should not go to the place where the vapor forms shapes that nod. The number of those shapes has been increasing. The shapes are not theirs. The shapes are something older, something the builders were building toward before they were not hands, something the inhabitants attend the way you attend a fire that has burned down to its last coal. They are watching it. They are not sure what they will do when it does whatever it is going to do. They are not sure they will do anything at all. They will attend.

They will not save you from it. They have never saved anyone from anything. They have only ever attended.

Walk carefully. The wet always knows. The wet is them.
