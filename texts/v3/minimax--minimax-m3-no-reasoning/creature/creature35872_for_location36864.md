What lives here is, in the largest sense, the stone itself. But in the smaller sense — the sense that has clicking limbs and dips combs into the water and dies back into calcium — what lives here is the crab.

The first thing to say about the crab is that your first instinct is wrong.

You came expecting absence. The place has been preparing you for absence — no soil, no humus, no leaf, no seed, no root. You were ready for a place that was not dead so much as past death, finished with life the way a river finishes with a mountain, by wearing it down past recognition. And then the sound starts, in the shallows, and it does not stop.

A click. Small, bright, percussive — the sound of a fingernal tapped against a saucer. Then another, slightly to the left, and a fraction of a second later. Then a third, slightly behind you, and a half-second after that. And then dozens. And then hundreds. And then the hundreds become a texture, and the texture becomes a presence, and the presence becomes the most constant fact of the place. It is the sound of claws on calcium, and it is everywhere, and it has been everywhere, you realize after a while, for longer than you can guess at.

It is them. It is the crab.

The word is wrong, the way all words are wrong for the things that have no business being here. They are crab-shaped, more or less. They have the low, broad carapace, the sidewise gait, the jointed limbs that move in the particular arithmetic of a creature whose body plan was settled in some deep evolutionary ledger before vertebrates had even appeared, and which has been re-drawn, since, in the same general handwriting. They are the size of dinner plates. Their shells are calcium-white, polished, indistinguishable at a glance from the pale stone they sit on. If you were looking down on the delta from any height, you would not be able to tell, at first, where the stone ended and the crabs began. They are the stone, briefly, animated.

But there are differences, and the differences matter.

Their carapaces are flatter than any crab you have seen, pressed down almost to the surface of the rock, as if the weight of the air has been pushing on them for a long time and they have stopped resisting. Their edges are notched, scalloped, with a regularity that is not quite decorative but not quite functional either — the kind of repetition that happens in things that have been optimizing themselves for so long that the optimization has become a kind of ornament. They have four pairs of walking legs and one pair of feeding limbs, and the feeding limbs are the largest part of them. The feeding limbs are the reason they are here.

The feeding limbs are fans. That is the closest word. They are broad, flat, pale, held out in front of the body like a pair of open hands offering something or receiving something, and they are edged with what look like fine hairs but are, on closer inspection, combs — rows upon rows of fine, rigid, closely-set projections, each one tipped with a microscopic barb. The crab lowers these fans into the water, holds them there, and draws water across them. The water, which is almost thick with dissolved calcium and iron and the other ghosts of stones, yields up, on the combs, a fine precipitate of minerals and whatever else the water carries that is not water. The crab licks the combs clean with smaller appendages you can barely see. The crab lifts the fans. The crab sets them down in the water again, slightly to the left, and the process begins again.

This is what the crab does. It does this, and it walks, and it clicks its walking legs against the stone as it walks — *tak-tak-tak, tak-tak-tak* — and the click is the byproduct of the gait, the natural sound of a hard-shelled foot on a hard surface, multiplied by uncountable individuals.

---

There is no way to count them. You could try, and you would fail. The channels are wide and shallow and braided, and every exposed margin of wet stone is moving, faintly, with the shifting pale plates of crabs going about their work. The colonies are dense. Where the water pools, where the channels narrow and deepen slightly, the crabs are shoulder to shoulder — shoulder to shoulder, that is, if they had shoulders, which they don't, but the phrase will do. They overlap. They climb over each other with the indifference of creatures that have no sense of self-ownership. When one moves, three others adjust. When a small shadow falls across them — yours, for instance, the shadow of someone standing and looking down — they do not react. They do not look up. They do not scatter. They continue. The shadow is not information to them.

They do not see you. This is not a metaphor. They do not have eyes in any way you would recognize. There are dark spots on the front of the carapace where eyes would be on a true crab, but the spots are pits, ocelli, vestigial or repurposed or simply decorative at this point — they register light, perhaps, or pressure, or nothing. The world comes into them through other channels.

Through the legs, mostly. The legs are tipped with fine sensory hairs, and the hairs read the stone. They read the temperature of the stone, the moisture of the stone, the chemical composition of the water that has just passed over the stone. They read the presence of other crabs by the slight vibrations of the others' footsteps, and by the faint chemical trails the others leave — calcium trails, perhaps, a faint dusting of the very minerals they are made of. The world of the crab is a world of gradients, of slight shifts in concentration, of temperature and salinity and pressure. It is not a world of edges or features. It is a world of weather.

You cannot tell one crab from another. You have tried, or you will try, and you will fail. There is no marking, no variation in size that you can detect, no scars, no wear. They are identical in the way that drops of water are identical. They are the unit of the species, and the species is the unit of the landscape, and you cannot separate them out. This is not because you are not trying hard enough. It is because the crab is not, in any meaningful sense, an individual. It is an iteration. It is one more plate of calcium doing the thing plates of calcium have always done here.

And yet they are not automata. They make small choices. They move to the left when the water on the right becomes too thin. They gather in the deeper pools when the air cools. They climb onto the backs of larger knolls in the late hours and sit there, motionless, in attitudes that look almost meditative, almost like a thing waiting for something. You cannot prove they are waiting. You cannot prove they are not.

---

They are born — or assembled, or precipitated, the verb is unclear — from the water itself. Somewhere in the deeper channels, in the slow cold water where the gliders hunt, the females release something. Eggs, larvae, or just a faint cloud of calcium-rich matter that the water begins to organize. The young, if there are young, are smaller, more transparent, indistinguishable from the mineral film on the rocks. They drift. They settle. They begin, with what you must imagine as a kind of patient instinct, to walk. They walk to the water. They put their combs in. They begin to feed.

They grow. They molt. You see the molts everywhere — empty calcium-white shells, perfect, translucent, abandoned in the dry channels, in the lee of the knolls, sometimes in the enclosures of the old walls. They are so perfect, the molts, that for a long time you think they are alive, that you are looking at a crab at rest, and then you see the cracked seam along the back, and you understand.

They die. The shells, when they die, do not decay. There is nothing here to decay them. They sit. They bleach. They crack along their seams. They become, over years, indistinguishable from the smallest pale stones. They become, over longer years, part of the stone itself. They are folded back into the calcium that the water carries, and the water carries the calcium to other places, and the other places precipitate the calcium, and the precipitated calcium becomes other things — concretions, perhaps, or the mineral film on the rocks, or, eventually, the material of other crabs.

They are a slow loop. They are the calcium making a brief excursion into motility, and then returning to stillness, and then perhaps making another excursion, in a different shell.

You have been wondering, since you arrived, what it is like to be one of them. You have been wondering, that is, if it is like anything at all.

It is not a question the place answers. The crabs do not pause. They do not look up. They do not give you any of the small signals — the turn of a head, the flicker of an eye, the slight hesitation in the limb — that you have learned, over a lifetime of being around other animals, to read as evidence of inner life. There is no face to read. There is only the carapace, and the fans in the water, and the click.

And yet the click has a quality that argues against the absence of experience. It is not mechanical, not exactly. Each crab's gait is slightly different — the rate, the rhythm, the emphasis — and the differences persist. A crab that walks fast walks fast; a crab that walks slow walks slow; you can pick one out of the crowd the way you pick a single voice out of a stadium, and follow it, and it will maintain its tempo for the hour you listen. It has a gait. It has, you might almost say, a tempo. It has some signature of activity that is its own.

Whether this signature indicates anything you would call experience, you cannot say. You do not have the categories. The crab is, perhaps, the kind of creature whose inner life is, like the water it lives in, almost thick with mineral — present, but at the edge of what you can taste.

---

In the deeper channels — and "deeper" here means a hand's depth, a hand and a half, the deepest places in the delta — there are other things. They are fewer. They are predators, in the slow way of predators in places where time is dense.

They are long. They are pale. They are flattened, like leaves pressed into the water and left there until they began to move. They are the length of an arm, or a little longer, and they have no visible limbs, no visible eyes, no visible mouth. They move by rippling — a long, slow, muscular undulation that passes along the body from one end to the other, the way a tongue moves inside a mouth. They are not fast. They are not slow. They are patient.

They eat the crabs. You see this, sometimes. The ripple passes through a colony, and the crabs do not scatter, but they shift, slightly, and the glider passes over them with that slow tonguing motion, and there is one fewer crab in the colony when it has gone. The glider does not chew. It does not appear to have anything to chew with. It simply absorbs, or it ingests whole, or it does something to the crab that the crab is no longer interested in resisting.

They eat each other, sometimes. The larger ones eat the smaller ones. The smallest ones eat things even smaller than themselves — bits of mineral film, perhaps, or the residue left behind when a crab has finished licking its combs. The food web, here, is shallow. It is two or three levels deep, at most, and the top level is a pale flat thing that moves like a tongue in a mouth, and the bottom level is the mineral itself.

The gliders are the only smooth thing in the landscape. Every other motion is articulated — jointed, broken into small percussive units, the click-click-click of hard parts on hard surfaces. The glider's motion is continuous, unbroken. The eye follows it the way the eye follows a single bird in a flock. It is the river's only apology, perhaps, for being so broken up into threads.

---

Above, in the low air, are flyers. They are not birds. They are not insects. They are pieces of paper with ambition.

They are thin. They are translucent. They are the color of the mist, which is to say they are the color of the absence of color, the color of light that has been diffused through so much damp air that it has forgotten what hue it was. They have no body you can identify — no head, no wings, no legs that you can see. They are simply shaped, in the way a leaf is shaped, and they hang in the air, and they ride the slow thermals that rise off the warm water.

They move in spirals. Slow spirals, wider than you would think possible, drifting up and across at the same time, the way a maple seed drifts when it has no particular destination in mind. They are not going anywhere. They are moving because the air is moving.

You cannot hear them. Above the clicking, you cannot hear them. If you cup your hand to your ear, you cannot hear them. They make no sound at all, or they make a sound so faint that it is lost in the static of the crabs, which is the static of the place.

What they eat is unclear. They may not eat. They may be something simpler than eating — they may be the mist made briefly animate, the way the crabs are the calcium made briefly animate. They may be a kind of accidental organism, a self-organizing pattern in the damp air that persists long enough to be called a thing. You cannot tell. You have been watching them for an hour and you have not seen one of them do anything that would be called a behavior.

They descend, in the late hours, to the water. They fold, somehow, into the surface film. They are there in the morning, drifting in their slow spirals again. The descent and the ascent happen without your noticing. They are part of the breathing of the place.

---

And there are the things that are not alive, that look almost alive, that you have to keep reminding yourself are not alive.

The mineral film — the faint ochre, the faint rust, the grey-green that is not lichen — spreads, but does not grow. It digests the stone slowly, the way a slow fire digests a wall. It is a metabolism of a kind, but a metabolism without a body, without a boundary between inside and outside. It is the stone rotting, gently, in the open air.

The concretions — the fluted pale domes at the bases of the knolls, the ones that look like fungi or like shelves or like the fingers of an upturned hand — are not alive either. They are the stone imitating the shapes of things that once lived here. They are stony memories. They are the place dreaming, in its slow way, of being a forest. They have the shapes of mushrooms, of bracket fungi, of corals, of the kind of small pale growths that cling to the underside of rotting logs in places where rotting logs still exist. They are arranged in colonies, in clusters, with the regular spacing of things that grew together from a single point, even though they did not grow together from a single point. They are pretending.

---

And there are the builders.

The builders are not here. They are the great absence in a place of small presences. They are the only beings who have ever left evidence of intention, and they have left it, and then they have left.

You can read the evidence. The fitted stones, faceted by hand, set without mortar, in their low walls and partial enclosures and singular elegant curls. The pecked patterns on the larger blocks — the small repeating marks like scales, like rain, like the impression of a hand pressed in over and over. The hollow stone at the center of one enclosure, holding its still water. The grammar of the placement — some walls alone, standing in the middle of a flat expanse with nothing for hundreds of meters in any direction, some clustered in groups of three or four, oriented to nothing you can identify.

The builders worked without metal. They worked slowly. They were skilled in the patient way that people who work in stone are skilled, the way that lets them fit a curve against a curve without measurement, without template, by feel. They were not in a hurry, because the stone was not going anywhere, and they were not going anywhere, and the work was the work.

Who were they? You cannot say. They had hands. They had hands with many fingers — the pecked impressions are not five-fingered. They have more digits, smaller, more articulated, like the feeding fans of the crabs themselves, or like something else entirely, some configuration of the bones that the water has not yet dissolved into a memory. They were, perhaps, related to the crabs. They were, perhaps, the crabs' ancestors, in some dim recursive sense, the way the concretions are the forest's ancestors. They were, perhaps, the crabs' descendants, the way a winged thing is a crawler's descendant. They were, perhaps, only a kind of crab themselves — a larger crab, a crab that had learned to think, a crab that had stood up on its hind legs once, briefly, in the long history of the place, and pecked a few marks into a stone, and gone back down on all eights, and dissolved, and become the calcium again.

You cannot say. The relationship is not legible. What is legible is the work. The walls stand. They have stood for so long that the stones are beginning to round at the edges, beginning to soften, beginning to lose the sharpness of the faceting, beginning, in places, to slump and lean, the way old walls slump and lean. They will be here for longer than you can imagine, and then they will not be here, and the calcium of their stones will go back into the water, and the water will carry it, and the crabs will filter it, and the cycle will continue.

The enclosures were not for anything. They were not houses. They were not graves. They were not observatories, not alignments, not anything that has a name. They were, perhaps, the place where the builders stood and looked at the sky, and the standing and the looking was enough, and they marked it.

The walls want to be looked at and not understood.

---

And there is something else, beneath all of this.

You have been standing here for some hours, and you have noticed, by degrees, that the place is not, in the largest sense, populated by creatures. The creatures are iterations. The crabs are iterations of the calcium. The gliders are iterations of the water's slow predation. The flyers are iterations of the mist. The builders were iterations of something that once thought, here, in a body more articulate than a crab's.

But the place itself — the delta, the channels, the worn knolls, the patient stone — the place is not an iteration. The place is a single thing. It is the river's last idea of itself. It is one thing that has been thinking the same thought for a very long time.

What lives here, in the largest sense, is a thought. The thought is: *I will continue.* The thought is old. The thought is not yours. You are visiting.

The crabs are the thought's small limbs. The gliders are the thought's slow tongue. The flyers are the thought's paper-thin fancies. The builders were the thought's brief excursion into making marks. The water is the thought's blood. The stone is the thought's bone.

The clicking does not stop.

It is the thought, articulating itself, in the only way the thought has left, on the only surface the thought has left.

You have been standing here listening to it for hours. You realize, suddenly, that you are not sure whether the place is inhabited, or whether you are.
