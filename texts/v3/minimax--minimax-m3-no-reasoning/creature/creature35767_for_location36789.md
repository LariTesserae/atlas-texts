# The Dwellers

## The People

The people of the City are pale in the way that things are pale which have never been otherwise. Their skin is the color of weak tea, of mushroom flesh, of cave-fish underside — openly translucent, the small blue-violet of their veins visible at the temples, at the inner wrists, at the soft underside of the jaw. The paleness is not illness. It is what a body becomes, over many generations, when the body has lived under a single warm light and has slowly become more legible to itself — more visible, more readable, more open to the eye of another body in the same light. They are easy to read. You can see, on the face of a person from the City, the thought that is happening, or the feeling, or the tiredness, or the warmth, or the absence of any of these, and the seeing is unhurried, and the thing seen does not resist the seeing.

Their hair is long and loose and slightly clumped, in the way hair gets when it is never quite dry. They do not cut it. Cutting is a habit they have not developed, in the way that people who lived under open skies did not, for most of their history, develop the habit of cutting their nails or shaving their faces. The hair is part of the body. The body is part of the place. There is no clear boundary between the hair and the damp air. A person walking through the City, seen from a distance, looks like a body in motion with a small pale weather above it.

Their eyes are large and damp and the same weak tea as their skin, and the eyes have a look you might call patient or that you might call resigned, but that is, more accurately, the look of a creature that has nowhere else to look and has, over a long time, become content with the looking. Their eyes do not track the way your eyes track. Their eyes dwell. Their eyes rest on a thing for a long time before moving to another thing, and the moving is slow, and the resting is complete. This is not wisdom. This is not peace. This is what eyes do when the light is constant and the world is small and the things in the world have, over a long time, agreed not to surprise.

Their clothes are loose. Their clothes are made of vat-cloth — a soft slick never-quite-dry fabric grown in shallow trays in the upper layers, where the lamps are strongest — and the cloth is the color of what the vats are fed, which is the color of compost, which is the color of a soft brown the City has many names for and no fixed one. The clothes have a smell. The clothes always have a smell — the smell of the damp, the smell of the vats, the smell of the engine-mineral, the smell of the lamps' faintly ozone-warm breath. The people do not notice the smell. The people, in visitors, notice the wrongness — the dry-wind smell, the sharp chemical of a thing that has come from elsewhere, the way a thing that has come from elsewhere smells like a thing that has come from elsewhere.

The people work.

The people work the engines. The engines are many and the engines are old and the engines are layered in the City the way the City is layered — low, lower, lowest, higher, highest — and the engines run, and the engines are kept running by the people, and the people are kept by the people, and the keeping is the work.

A person becomes attached to a particular engine in the way a person becomes attached to a particular animal, or a particular lamp, or a particular corner of a particular room. The attachment is not chosen. The attachment is what happens over time when a person stands near a thing for many hours, and the thing is warm and hums low, and the person's body learns the rhythm of the healthy hum, and the person's ear comes to know the sound a thing makes when it is ailing, and the person becomes the thing's ear and the thing becomes the person's worry. The attachment lasts as long as the person or the thing, whichever ends first.

The shifts are not shifts in your sense. The shifts are habits, arrangements, the long-standing negotiations between a person and a machine. A person works an engine until the engine fails or the person dies, or until something else — a child's birth, a long-one's death, a higher engine that needs a more careful ear — moves the person on, and the engine is mourned, briefly, in the quiet way the place mourns, and the new engine is loved as the old was loved, with the slow patient love of one piece of equipment for another.

The work is not making. The work is continuation. The engines run on the engines. The water moves on the water. The lamps glow on the lamps. The skin sweats and the skin drinks and the skin repairs. The people are the part of this that can be taught to listen for the sound of a thing that is dying, and the people listen, and the listening is the work.

The people eat.

The people eat the animals of the place. A long-one is taken by the leg, and the long-one falls, slowly, because the long-one is slow, and the people wait. The people kill with a long warm tool, the edge held against a lamp first, in the old habit. The blood that comes out is the color of the lamps — warm, a little thick. The blood is drunk. The flesh is dried on raised racks near the lamps, the drying slow because the air is wet, and the flesh is eaten for days.

The bell-ringers are scooped from the channels in wide nets at the hours of habit when the bell-ringers surface. The bell-ringers are eaten fresh, alive by accident. The small things of the channels and the engines are eaten by people who are hungry and tired and have stopped being particular about the source.

The people drink the water that the skin sweats. The water is the only water. The water is drunk and the water is passed and the water is taken back by the skin. The people, in drinking, drink the place.

When a person dies, the body is offered to the skin. The body is laid in a low place, where the water will be soon, and the small things come, and the long-ones come if they smell the offering, and the place takes the body back. The body becomes a soft hill for a time, and the hill is walked on, and the hill shrinks, and the body is gone.

Some of the people — the ones who work the highest engines and see the highest lamps — say the lamps glow a little warmer over the spot where a particular body was taken. They say the warmth is the person's warmth, returned. They say this is a comfort. The comfort does not make the dying less. The comfort makes the living more inhabitable.

Children are born here. Children are born warm and wet and already accustomed to the lamps and the close air and the give of the skin underfoot. A child learns to walk on the skin, and the skin's give is the ground. A child learns, by being around, that other people are warm in the same way and that engines are warm in the same way. The children play small games with small animals in the channels. The children are lifted to the loudest engines, and they are not afraid. The children grow up. The children become people.

The children do not know night. The first inhabitants made a word for night. The word did not catch on the way the words for skin and lamp and engine caught on. The word is, for the children, an inheritance without a body. A child holds her hand over a lamp and looks at the dark spot beneath her hand and calls the spot *night*. This is not night. This is a small dark made by a hand. The child knows this. The child keeps doing it for a while, then stops. The word becomes a great-grandparent's name, without a face.

The children dream. The children dream of the place, which is warm and wet and lit. The dreams sometimes include, briefly, an absence. The absence has no name. The child, waking, does not speak of it. The child grows up. The child, sometimes, becomes one of the dreamers.

## The Animals

The animals of the Long Bottom are not from elsewhere.

The animals are of the place, in the way the lamps are of the place, in the way the engines are of the place, in the way the long-ones graze the skin and the skin heals under them and the place and the long-ones have, over a long time, become each other's maintenance. The animals are made here, by the slow method that anything is made in a place that makes things by being itself: by being what they are, and continuing to be what they are, for a long time.

The animals are many. The animals are very many. The skin holds more bodies than the City does, and the City holds many bodies. The animals are the biomass the place carries above and below itself, and the biomass is quiet in a way the place is quiet.

The long-ones, first.

The long-ones are the size of a person but longer, much longer, low to the ground, six-legged, the legs in three pairs along the length, walking with a slow rippling gait that flows down the body in a wave. Their backs are armored, thin, the color of warm honey — which means they are hard to see in lamp-light and impossible in the lower light. Their bellies are soft, soft-bellied, the mouths on the underside a soft rasp that takes the skin in small amounts as the long-one passes. The skin closes under the long-one as the long-one passes, and closes a little differently after — a little firmer, a little darker — and this is part of why the long-ones are necessary.

The long-ones are quiet. They make a low continuous sound when they walk, the sound of many soft bellies against a soft surface, and the sound carries, and the people, hearing the sound, are restful.

The long-ones live in groups of seven or eleven, never larger, and they move across the skin in long days of their own slow reckoning. They are not in a hurry. The long-ones have never been in a hurry. A long-one, when it is in distress, makes a low brief sound, and the sound carries. A long-one, when it is dying, makes a long sustained sound, and the sound carries farther than the others, and the people stop what they are doing, and the people listen, and the listening is the respect the place has for the place.

The wide-ones.

The wide-ones are rare. The wide-ones are the largest of the surface animals, and they are flat — very flat, very wide — with many, many small legs under them, and they move with the slowness of tectonic plates. They live in the dim places, in the lower layers where the lamps are few, and they are seen rarely, and they are seen, when they are seen, in passing.

A wide-one, when it dies, makes a sound that lasts a very long time, and the people come from far to see, and the people wait with the dying wide-one, and the waiting is not grief but is the acknowledgment of a thing that is rare being also a thing that is dying. The wide-ones are legendary. Most people have not seen one. The people who have seen one speak later in a voice that is quiet in the way the place is quiet, in the way that a thing is quiet when it has been witnessed and the witnessing has changed it.

The bell-ringers.

The bell-ringers live in the channels, in the slow water. They are small, mostly transparent, jointed in many places, with gas-bladders at their tops. They rise to the surface in crowds — hundreds or thousands — and tap their small hard bellies against the air-bladders, and the tapping makes a small repeated high sound, the only truly rhythmic sound in the place.

The sound carries. The people hear it in the upper layers. The people use it as a clock, the way humans everywhere use the sounds of animals as clocks. The people wake to the bell-ringers. The people mark their hours of habit by the bell-ringers' timing. The bell-ringers do not know this. The bell-ringers rise at a particular lamp-level, which is never, because the lamp is constant, but the bell-ringers have some other sense of the hour, and they rise, and they tap, and the people are informed.

The bell-ringers are eaten by the people and by the long-ones and by most of the smaller things. The bell-ringers are many. The bell-ringers, all together, are the smallest of the many.

The small things.

The small things are everywhere — in the water, on the skin, in the engines, in the vats, on the lamps, in the channels. The small things do not have names. The small things are eaten by every larger thing, and the small things eat each other, and the small things, all together, are the part of the place that is not articulate.

A small thing in the engines: it is the color of metal, soft as a moth, and it lives in the running engines in the small spaces between parts, and it eats whatever accumulates. Whether the small thing cleans or fouls, the people cannot tell. The people leave it alone. The people have, over a long time, decided that the small thing is part of the engine.

A small thing on the drafts: it is flat, with thin membranes at its corners, and it catches the warm air that rises from the skin. The small thing has no wings. The small thing has sails. The small thing is the closest the place has to a flier. The people, looking up, see them sometimes — small moving shadows on the lamp-light — and the people are glad in a way that does not have a name.

A small thing on the lamps: it is a thing that has learned, somehow, to live on the luminescence, that feeds on the lamp-light, and it clings to the lamps in bright clusters. The lamps, where the clusters are, are a little dimmer, and the cluster-spots are warm, and the small things are not eaten. The lamp-clusters are restful to the people the way shadows, in another place, would be restful.

A small thing in the channels: it is like a thread, like a long soft living filament, and it eats the small things the long-ones leave, and it is eaten by the bell-ringers and the engine-things and the long-ones themselves.

A small thing at the skin's edge: it is something that lives where the skin meets the channels, where the water meets the substrate, and it is dark, and it is shaped like a small hand, and it rests there, and it is not understood.

The animals are quiet. The animals are quiet in the way that forests are quiet at the bottom of a forest, in the way that the deep sea is quiet, in the way that a place is quiet when it is full of life and the life has, over a long time, agreed upon a register. The animals make sounds — the small sounds of bodies moving, bodies calling, bodies eating, bodies in distress, bodies dying — and the sounds are heard, and the sounds are respected.

## The Place

There are dwellers that are neither people nor animals.

The lamps, first. Whether the lamps are alive is not a question the City asks. Asking would imply the answer matters. The answer, by every visible measure, is no. The lamps do not respond. The lamps do not vary. The lamps have always glowed. The lamps will, presumably, continue to glow. The lamps are the way the place sees.

But the lamps are dwellers. The lamps are parts of the place, in the way that eyes are parts of a body, in the way that the warmth of a hand is part of the hand. The lamps, when the people are near them, glow a little warmer, the way a lamp glows warmer when a hand is near it. The lamp might like the nearness. The lamp might not. The people do not know. The people do not ask.

The engines, next. The engines are old. The engines, by some measures, are older than the people. The engines hum. The engines continue. The engines fail, slowly, and the people come and listen and repair. The people are good at repair. The people have been good at repair for a long time. The engines, the people, the lamps: a kind of system, and the system runs, and the system is the place's being-alive in the way the place is alive.

The engines, too, are dwellers. The engines are parts. The engines are the place's keeping-awake, the place's insistence on continuing, in the way the engines have insisted on continuing since before there were people to keep them.

There are the dead.

The dead of the people are offered to the skin and taken. The dead of the long-ones are taken by the small things. The dead of the wide-ones are taken by everything. The dead are not absent. The dead are present in the way that compost is present in a forest, in the way that the warmth of a place is built on the warmth of all the things that have warmed the place before, in the way that a memory is present in the body that holds it.

There are the dreamers.

There are people, among the people, who dream the dark. The dreamers have inherited the word for dark, and the word has no referent in the waking life of the place. The dreamers, in their dreams, encounter something that does have a referent, and the dreamers find it, on waking, hard to name. The dreamers describe it in the language they have, which is the language of the place, which has no word for what they have seen. The dreamers use, sometimes, the word for night. The dreamers' friends nod and do not understand. The dreamers keep the dream. The dreamers are not, on the whole, listened to. The dreamers are listened to, sometimes, by other dreamers, and the listening is the place's quiet acknowledgment that one of its parts has seen something the rest of it cannot yet see.

There are the ones who suspect.

The ones who suspect that the place is large, that the place is a body, that the place is not going anywhere. The ones who suspect that what is below the skin is of the place, in the way the skin is of the place, in the way the lamps are of the place. The ones who suspect that the engines are the place's way of being awake, and that the people are the parts of the place that have woken up inside the place. The ones who suspect are not many. The ones who suspect are not, on the whole, listened to. The ones who suspect are listened to, sometimes, by other ones who suspect, and the listening is brief, and the listening is enough, and the place holds the listening the way the place holds everything — gently, because the place has time.

There are the watchers.

The watchers are not, in the City, an institution or a profession. The watchers are those, of any age, who have come to a part of the City where the lamps are few and the engines are few, and who sit, and look, and wait. The watchers are not waiting for anything. The watchers are waiting the way the place is waiting, which is to say, not waiting, which is simply being the body the body is.

And there is the place.

The place is the dweller that the dwellers are inside of. The place is not a place. The place is a body. The place breathes and sweats and drinks and is warm and is lit and is patient and is, you begin to suspect, going nowhere. The place is large. The place has the patience of a hand that is holding a thing, and the thing has stopped struggling, and the hand, since the thing has stopped struggling, has begun to hold more gently.

The place is the dweller. The place is, you might suspect, all the dwellers and none of them and the body they share. The lamps are the place's seeing. The engines are the place's keeping-awake. The people are the place's articulate. The animals are the place's biomass. The dreamers are the place's attempts to see what cannot be seen. The long sustained dying sound is the place's grief. The warmth is the place's warmth. The damp is the place's damp. The lit spot under a child's hand is the place's gentle memory of someone else's absence.

The place is patient.

The place has always been patient.

The place will, presumably, continue to be patient — with the people who work the engines and tend the long-ones' dying, with the long-ones who graze the skin and quiet the City with their many-bellied walking, with the bell-ringers who tap their bellies at hours that no clock confirms, with the wide-ones who pass through the dim places like slow tectonic small thoughts, with the small things that live in the engines and on the lamps and in the channels, with the dreamers who dream the dark they have no word for, with the watchers who sit in the dim places and not-wait, with the dead who are not gone, with the lamps that are the place's kind of eyes, with the engines that are the place's kind of heart.

The place is patient.

The place will continue.
