# The Inhabitants of Lith-Khor

There is a way of walking that the people of the plateau have, and it is not the way of anywhere else. They walk as if the stones are slightly uncertain beneath them, even when the stones are not. Their feet come down lightly, almost delicately, the way one walks on ice that has been walked on for a long time and may have begun to forgive it. Their weight is on the ball of the foot, not the heel, and the heel touches last and barely, and the next step is already beginning before the last one is finished. The reason for this is the dust. The dust of Lith-Khor is fine and damp and it coats every surface, and the dust of Lith-Khor does not crunch so much as it *gives*, a small compressing sound underfoot, and if you walk the way lowlanders walk, with the full weight of the heel coming down, you stir the dust up into your own face and you walk the rest of the day with the fine grey powder in your eyelashes and in the corners of your mouth. The people of the plateau have learned to walk so as not to do this. They walk the way the wind moves across the stones: low, close, not displacing more than is necessary.

The people are small. They are not small in the way that malnourished people are small, with the smallness coming from a deficit; they are small in the way that people who have lived in a thin cold place for many generations are small, the body taking less air, asking less of the air, making itself compact because compactness is warmth and compactness is economy and the air up here does not support the long limbs of the lowlanders the way the richer air of the lower country does. Their chests are deep for their size. Their shoulders are rounded. Their hands are wide and the fingers are short and the knuckles are large, because the hands are used. Their faces are weathered early and weathered continuously; you cannot tell the age of a Lith-Khori by the lines of the face because the lines begin in childhood and deepen steadily through the whole of life, and an adult of twenty-five has the lined face of a lowland person of forty. The skin is dark, though the darkness is not uniform; the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose are darker than the hollows of the cheeks and the skin under the eyes, where the sun does not reach so directly, and the contrast gives the face a particular look, a kind of permanent inscription, as if the sun has been writing on them for years with a fine nib. The hair is black, mostly, and is worn long by the women and short by the men, and both are tied back from the face with a strip of wool. The eyes are dark. The expression on the faces, when the faces are at rest, is not solemn and is not cheerful and is not anything you can put a word to; it is the expression of people who are paying attention to the air.

The clothes are wool. Almost all of the clothes are wool, because wool is what there is. The sheep and the goats and the long-necked alpaca-beasts give their fleece and the fleece is spun and the fleece is woven and the fleece is what is worn. It is woven thick, in two layers for the cold months, in a single looser weave for the warmer months, and the dyes that color it are few and are made from the lichens of the north walls and the crushed stems of the shrub that grows in the cracks of the oriented walls, and so the palette of the city is the palette of the city itself: grey, pale green, the off-white of undyed wool, a dull rust that may once have been red but has been watered down by generations of lichen-dyers who could not get enough lichen. The clothes are not ornamented much. A strip of dyed wool around the hem of a coat. A row of small bone buttons down the front. A fringe at the wrist. The herders, who come up from below, wear brighter colors sometimes — they trade for them, they bring them up already dyed — and the city people look at the herders' colors with the polite disinterest of people who have decided, collectively, long ago, that color is a kind of noise.

The coats are long, to the knee or below, and the sleeves are long and the cuffs are turned back so that the hands can work without the wool getting in the way, and the hoods are large and can be pulled up over the head in the fog and the wind and are pulled up often. Boots are of wool felt, or of hide traded up from below, and the boots are soft-soled because the streets are stone and the streets are wet and a hard sole on wet stone is a hard sole that slips. The boots are worn until they are felt more than leather, until the original hide is more memory than material, and the people keep wearing them because new boots are a trade good and a trade good is a thing to be careful with. Children go barefoot until the first real cold, and then they are put into small felt boots, and the first time the small felt boots are put on is a small ceremony not spoken of as a ceremony but attended with the particular attention of the family, because the first felt boots are the first acknowledgment that the child is going to be a person of the plateau and not some warmer thing that the plateau only borrowed.

The hands of the people are the most articulate part of them. The hands of the people of Lith-Khor are used all day, and they are used in a particular register of touch, a touch that is light and informed and that knows the grain of stone and the give of damp wool and the temperature of water in a covered cistern by the feel of the lip of the cistern when the hand brushes it in passing. The hands do the work: the spinning of wool into thread on a small hand-spindle, a wooden disc with a shaft, turned by the fingers while the fiber is drawn out by the other hand; the weaving of the thread into cloth on a frame loom that stands against a wall and is small enough for one person to operate; the scraping and the cutting of the stones that occasionally fall from a wall and must be dressed and reset before the wet gets into the joint and makes it worse; the milking of the goats, done with the whole hand, not the fingertips, a stripping motion that the goats tolerate with the resigned patience of animals that have been milked by these particular hands for generations; the gathering of the lichens from the north walls, which is done with a small curved blade of traded metal, held lightly, the lichen peeled away in a single sheet and laid in a basket and taken home and dried and stored. The hands of the people of Lith-Khor are never quite clean. There is always a dark line in the creases of the knuckles where the dust of the city has worked its way in and stayed, and the line does not come out with washing because the dust is a part of the hands now, has been a part of the hands for so long that the line is a kind of feature, an element of the hands' own pattern, the way the dark vertical streaks of centuries of wet are a feature of the walls.

The voices of the people are not loud. This has been said. But the *texture* of the voices, the quality of the speech, is also particular. There is a hush to it, a kind of held-breath quality, because the lungs are not generous up here and the breath that goes into speech is breath that does not go into other things, and so the words come out shaped for economy, with the vowels a little flatter than they would be down below, the consonants a little harder, the sentences a little shorter. There is not much singing. The singing that exists is the singing of work, the singing that accompanies the carding of wool or the stirring of a pot or the walking of a long path across the empty city in the fog, and the songs are not so much melodic as rhythmic, a kind of timed breathing that makes the work easier to continue. The mother-singing to children is a different thing. It is soft, and it is close, and it is sung directly into the small ear, and the children grow up with the particular intimacy of having had song breathed into them rather than sung at them from any distance.

The people are not given to touching each other much in public. The cold does not encourage it, because touching means skin and skin means the meeting of two cold surfaces and the meeting of two cold surfaces is not a comfort. But they are given to proximity. They stand close when they talk, the way fish swim close in cold water, not for the contact but for the small pooled warmth of being near another body. Friends lean together. Mothers carry children against the body, inside the coat, in a kind of pocket of warmth. Lovers, if there are lovers, are lovers in private, and the privacy is not a secrecy but a practicality, because what happens between two people in this climate happens under every scrap of wool that can be gathered, and is not a thing for the open air. The old are helped carefully, by the arm, by the elbow, and the helping is done without making a thing of it, and the old accept the help without making a thing of it, and the not-making-a-thing-of-it is itself a kind of respect, a kind of love, that does not need to declare itself.

The faces of the old are particularly lined and particularly dark, and the eyes of the old are particularly clear, and the old move through the city with a slowness that is not the slowness of weakness but the slowness of certainty, because the old know exactly where they are going and how long it will take them to get there and they are not going to waste any of it. The old are consulted. The old are not consulted in any formal way, because there is no formal way, but when a question arises — which cistern to reopen, which path down the cliff is still passable, whether a particular wall is safe to lean against or whether it is going to come down in the next big wind — the question is taken, by whoever is asking it, to one of the old, and the old gives an answer that is not offered as opinion but as a kind of reading, the way one reads the weather. The old do not command. The old do not advise. The old simply know, and the knowing is available to anyone who comes to ask for it, and the city has a great many old in it, because the city is patient and does not mind keeping its old.

The children are quiet too, but the quiet of the children is a different quiet, a held-in quiet, the quiet of small creatures in a large cold place who have been told, by every signal of the city around them, that the appropriate volume is low. The children of Lith-Khor play in the streets, in the dished gutters, in the empty courts, and the games they play are games of small motion: games with pebbles, games with the small leaves that fall into the open cisterns, games of running a short way and then stopping and then running again, the body warming itself in bursts. The children are not scolded for being loud. The children are simply not loud, and the not-being-loud is a thing that is learned in the first year of life, by the example of the parents, by the hush of the rooms, by the silence of the city itself, which is itself a kind of instruction. The children are given small tasks early. A child of four can be sent with a small pot to the nearest working cistern and will come back with the pot full, having understood the way, having understood the slight uphill grade of the particular street, having understood that the cistern in question is the one with the worn lip on the right side and not the similar cistern thirty paces further on which has a cracked lining and is not for drinking. The children are trusted early. The children are trusted because the city is small enough that the trusting of a child is not a great risk, and because the alternative — keeping the children close and watched — is a luxury of warmer places with more children to lose.

The food that the people eat is the food that the plateau provides or that the herders bring up. It is not varied. There is the meat of the goat, eaten fresh when an animal is killed and eaten dried in the colder months, the drying done on the roof slabs in the thin sun. There is the milk of the goat, drunk fresh and also made into a hard cheese that keeps for a long time in the cold, the cheese being small and pale and very dense. There is the wool-fat of the alpaca-beasts, rendered down, which is eaten in small amounts and which provides the fats that the diet otherwise lacks. There is the trade-grain that comes up in the saddle-bags of the herders, a coarse grain that is ground between stones and made into a porridge and into a flat bread that is cooked on a hot stone by the fire. There is the lichen that is good for what ails you, which is also eaten, in small amounts, as a kind of roughage, and which has a taste that the lowlanders would find unpleasant and that the people of Lith-Khor find simply the way things taste. There is, in the very best of summers, a small root that the herders dig in the lower meadows and bring up in small baskets, and the roots are boiled and eaten and are, in their season, a kind of celebration, the way the first fruit is a celebration in warmer places. The people of Lith-Khor are not heavy eaters. They are small and they are cold and they do not have much to eat and so they eat what they have carefully and they are not wasteful and they do not think of themselves as deprived because they have no other frame of reference. The food is enough. The food has always been enough. The body that has lived here for many generations has become, in its small way, a body that is tuned to the food that there is.

The fires are the centers of the rooms and the centers of the evenings. The fires are small, of dung and dried scrub and the occasional bit of gathered wood that has come up in a herder's pack, and the fires are set in stone-lined hearths that are built into the walls of the rooms and that draw, when they draw at all, through a small hole in the roof that may or may not have been deliberately placed there. The smoke is part of the evening. The smoke sits in the upper part of the room and slowly finds its way out and the people below the smoke breathe the smoke and the smoke is not good for them and they know the smoke is not good for them and the alternative is the cold and the cold is worse and so the smoke is accepted as the smoke is accepted, the way the wet is accepted, as a part of the conditions. The fire is for warmth and the fire is for the cooking of the porridge and the cooking of the meat and the fire is for the small heat-rituals, which are not rituals in the sense of being performed, exactly, but which are the small repeated actions by which a household keeps itself in relation to the fire: the placement of a particular stone in a particular place at the beginning of the evening, the brushing of a small amount of ash from the hearth-edge in a particular direction, the speaking of a few words over the kindling before it is lit. These actions are not explained. They are done, and the doing of them is the explanation.

The evenings around the fire are when the stories are told. The stories are told by whoever is moved to tell them, which is sometimes an old person and sometimes a younger person and sometimes a child who has heard a story and is repeating it the way children repeat things, with the order not quite right and the emphasis on the parts that struck them. The stories are of three kinds, and the three kinds are not distinguished from each other by the people who tell them, because the people who tell them do not think of the stories as kinds. There are the stories of the city: how a particular cistern came to be opened, how a particular path was found, what is known of the people who lived in a particular building before it was empty. There are the stories of the lower country: what the herders have reported, what the traders have said, what a young person who went down and came back has described, which is rare, because the young people who go down mostly do not come back. And there are the older stories, the deep stories, the stories of the building of the city, of the people who are not people, of the time before. These stories are told in the same tone as the other stories. They are not held to be more true. They are not held to be less true. They are held. That is the word that the people would use, if they were asked, and they are not often asked, because the people do not interrogate the stories. The stories are part of the fire-evening the way the smoke is part of the fire-evening. They are what is there.

The herders who come up in the summer are not quite the same as the city people, and the city people know it and the herders know it. The herders are taller, with longer limbs, because they live part of the year in the lower country and the air there has shaped them differently. The herders are louder, by the standards of the city, which is to say that the herders speak at what would be considered a normal volume in the lower country and which the city people find, in the first days of the herders' arrival, a kind of assault, and which the city people adjust to, in the second week, and which the city people are glad to see go, in the autumn, when the herders take their flocks back down the cliff path. The herders bring news. The herders bring gossip. The herders bring, in their packs, the things that the city does not have, the small luxuries that the city has decided are not luxuries: a bit of bright cloth, a handful of the dried fruit of the lower country, a tool of metal that holds an edge better than the stone tools of the city. The herders trade these things for what the city has, which is the wool of the alpaca-beasts, which the lower country cannot grow because the lower country is too warm for the beasts to flourish, and the small cheeses, and the lichens, which have a use in the lower country that the herders do not explain and the city people do not ask about. The trading happens at the eastern gate, mostly, and the trading is not a market but a meeting, and the meeting is also a kind of social event, the only social event of the year that is not within the city itself. The city people are not unfriendly to the herders. The city people are not particularly friendly to the herders. The city people regard the herders the way the city regards the wind: as a thing that comes and goes and that one adjusts to while it is here.

The dead of the city are taken down. This is a thing that the people of Lith-Khor do and that the people of the lower country, when they learn of it, sometimes find strange. The dead are wrapped in wool and they are carried, by the family and by whoever else is willing, down the cliff path, and the carrying is done carefully and the path is taken slowly, and at the bottom of the cliff the dead are given to the lower country. They are buried in the lower country, in the warmer ground, in the ground that the herders will point to. The body of a person of Lith-Khor does not stay on the plateau. The body of a person of Lith-Khor is not put into the stone of the city or under the floors of the layered buildings or in any of the places that the older stories suggest were the older ways. The body is taken down. This is done because the plateau does not give back what is put into it: the ground is too thin, the soil is too little, the cold preserves rather than dissolves, and the dead of the city, if they were put into the ground of the plateau, would not become part of the ground, would remain themselves for a long time, and the long remaining would be felt, in the cold way that the people of Lith-Khor feel things, as a presence. So the dead are taken down. The taking-down is a journey, and the journey is a small ceremony, and the ceremony is not spoken of as a ceremony. The carrying is done by people who knew the dead, and the knowing is the only thing that is required.

The births happen in the city. The births happen in the upper rooms of the inhabited buildings, in the rooms that are the warmest because they are the smallest and because the smoke of the fire sits in them and heats them and because the body of the mother, in labor, heats them further. The births are attended by the women of the family and by an older woman who has attended many births and who is not a midwife in any formal sense but who is a woman who has done this before and whose hands know the work. The births are not easy. The children of Lith-Khor are born small and they are born into the cold and the first breath of the child is a hard-won thing, a thing that the child has to fight for in the thin air, and the cry of the newborn is thin and high and the mother holds the child against her body and the wool of her coat and her own warmth and the warmth of the fire, and the small body warms and the small lungs learn the air of the plateau and the child becomes a person of the place. The first bath of the child is in water from the covered cistern, water that is black and very cold, and the coldness of the water is not a cruelty but a presentation, an introduction of the child to the conditions of the life that the child has been born into. The child does not love the water. The child accepts the water. The acceptance is what is wanted.

The young people who go down are the young people who feel the pull, and the pull is real and is not to be argued with. The pull is in the body, the way the cold is in the body, and the body wants what the body wants, and the body of a young person of Lith-Khor wants, sometimes, the warmer air and the longer growing season and the things that the lower country has. The young people go down in ones and twos, in the summer, in the company of the herders, and the city lets them go and the city does not grieve and the city is not resentful when they do not come back, because the city understands that the pull is a pull and that the holding of a person against the pull would be a worse thing than the losing of the person. The young people who come back — and they are few — come back changed, taller, longer in the limb, with the color of the lower country in their faces, and they take up their lives in the city again and they do not speak much of what they have seen, because what they have seen is not for the city. The city does not ask. The city is patient. The city has time.

The people of Lith-Khor are a quiet people. They are not a sad people, though an outsider might think so. The outsider would be wrong, but the outsider would not be entirely wrong, because the condition of the people of Lith-Khor is not a condition of joy and is not a condition of grief and is not a condition of anything that has a name in the languages of warmer places. The condition is the condition of people who have been tuned, over many generations, to a place, and who have become, in their small way, an expression of the place, the way the lichen is an expression of the north wall and the moss is an expression of the damp gutter. The people of Lith-Khor are not unhappy. The people of Lith-Khor are not happy. The people of Lith-Khor are *tuned*, and the tuning is so thorough and so long-established that the question of happiness does not arise, the way the question of whether the cistern is wet does not arise. The cistern is wet. The people are tuned. The wet and the tuning are the conditions of the place. The place goes on. The people go on with it.
