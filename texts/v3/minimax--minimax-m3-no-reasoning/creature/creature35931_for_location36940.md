The creatures most like animals here are also the ones hardest to think of as animals. You will see them first as shapes in the wrong places — between the looms, beneath the dripping points of the ceiling, in the long pale shafts of light where the air is thickest — and you will not at first be certain they are alive, because they have no faces. They have surfaces. They have undersides. They have ends, but the ends do not commit to being heads; they taper, or they blunt, or they simply trail away into a kind of mucous that makes it unclear where the creature has stopped and the floor has started. They are longer than dogs and lower than otters, but those comparisons give the wrong shape. They are the shape of a thing that has been molded by warmth into whatever path the warmth follows. Where the floor tiles are amber and still hot from the presses, they lie in long loose ribbons, several bodies deep, each one laid across the others like fingers laced. Where the tiles are cool, they are not.

Their skin is the color of a peeled root that has been left on a wet plate — pink-white, faintly opalescent, slick with something that is not quite sweat. The light passes into them a finger's depth. You can see, just below the surface, the dark branching lines of what is moving inside them: a circulatory system, perhaps, or a hydraulic one, or a network of fine channels in which something darker than blood is being carried slowly from one region of the body to another. The lines pulse, but the pulse is not the heart-beat pulse of anything you have touched. It is slower, and it does not arrive everywhere at once. Parts of the creature seem to be waiting while other parts are already in motion. You have the impression, watching one for a long time, that you are not seeing one creature but a colony of decisions that have agreed to share a skin.

They do not look at you. They seem, in fact, to lack the apparatus for looking — no orbits, no lenses, no place where a face would gather itself. What they have instead is an attention that is spread out across the whole of their bodies. When you step near one, it does not turn toward you, but the channels inside it shift their carrying, and the creature begins, very slowly, to flow away from the warm tile you have shadowed. They go toward warmth. They go toward the places where the machines have been running longest. They go toward the crushed tubes that the presses have leaked onto the floor, and they go toward the rust, which they eat in a way you can hear if you stand still long enough: a faint, dense sound, like someone running a thumb slowly along a wet balloon.

They eat the tubes. They eat the rust. They are eaten.

What eats them is much smaller, much more numerous, and infinitely more patient. The beetles are the color of old iron, and they have antennae so long that the antennae seem to be doing most of the work of being a beetle. They move in processions. The procession is not the marching line of an army; it is the broad slow drift of a thing that has been going in the same direction for so long that the direction has become a property of the species. Each beetle carries a fragment of the pale creature larger than itself. The fragment is held aloft on the antennae, or braced between the mandibles, or simply dragged, and the carrying is done with an air of great seriousness — the seriousness of a thing that knows its job. They go to the base of the walls, where the brick is going soft. They go down into the soil-paste. They are the return stroke of a cycle that the eye cannot see the beginning of.

Above them, threaded through everything, are the plants.

The plants here should not be called plants. They are what is left of the idea of a plant after the plant has been allowed to forget the sun for a hundred years. The leaves are lobed in threes and they have the sheen of something polished by moisture alone. They do not photosynthesize in any way you would recognize; their green is darker than photosynthesis requires and their posture is wrong for catching light. They have been catching the machine instead. Their pale tubes — and the tubes are everywhere, descending from the bulbs on the ceiling, threading through the gears, crushed and re-emerged, crushed and re-emerged — are their mouths, their stomachs, their circulatory system, all at once. The brick is being eaten by them the way the rust is being eaten by the pale creatures: slowly, with the long patience of a thing that does not need to hurry because it will be here when you are not.

And then there are the yellow flowers. The flowers only appear on the machines in their third state, the ones at the deep end that have stopped running. They grow from the flywheels. They crown the pistons. They face, when you pass, the direction of your movement, with a slowness that is not the slowness of a sunflower (which is slow but still searching) but the slowness of something that has decided, and is waiting for you to confirm the decision. They are not watching you the way a sunflower watches. They are watching you the way a trigger watches.

You understand, standing in the warm amber light, that none of these are quite separate. The pale creature, the beetle, the tube, the lobed leaf, the brick becoming soil — these are not species sharing a place. They are stations of a single body, expressed in different materials, on different timescales. The place is the organism. The thing that lives here is the room itself, in its wet dark cathedral of digesting brick and patient machines, and you are a passing warmth, briefly noticed, briefly metabolized, soon to be forgotten.
