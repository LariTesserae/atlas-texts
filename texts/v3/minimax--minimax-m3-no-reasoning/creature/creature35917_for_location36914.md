# The Inhabitants of the Lithophone Garden

They do not have names, even for themselves.

This is not because they lack language, though they do. It is because in a place where nothing changes, the concept of a name — that small temporal marker, that label meant to travel with a thing through time — has no function. A name is for becoming. They have finished becoming.

## The Stayers on the Stone

The ones who cling to the blades are the most numerous, and the most still. They are perhaps the size of a child's thumbnail, though size is difficult to judge here because nothing moves past anything else to give scale. Their bodies are pale in the way the air is pale — translucent, light-permeable, as if they were constructed from the same sourceless luminescence that fills the chamber. Six legs, or eight, or four — the count shifts depending on the angle of observation, which is its own kind of strangeness, since the creatures themselves do not seem to shift at all. Some observers, the few who have ever passed through this place and spoken of it afterward (they never stay long; something in them cannot tolerate the completeness), have described them as resembling shrimp, or as resembling the pale lice that live in the feathers of seabirds, or as resembling nothing so much as the idea of an arthropod drawn by someone who had only ever heard one described. All three descriptions are correct, in the way that all three are incomplete.

They have no eyes, or eyes so small and recessed that they are indistinguishable from the texture of their heads. This makes a certain kind of sense. There is nothing to see here that is not always there. The sourceless light is constant; the stone blades do not move; the cairns do not rearrange themselves. To see would be to process information about a world that has no information to give. Whatever these creatures once used eyes for, they have not needed it for a long time.

They are not asleep, exactly. Sleep implies a cycle, a difference between the waking and the not-waking. They are simply *in* their clinging, the way a barnacle is *in* its rock. Their legs have, over time, conformed to the slight curvature of the blade they hold. Each individual clinger is shaped by its specific stone. If you were to remove one and set it on a different blade, it would fit poorly, the way a key fits the wrong lock.

## The Drifters in the Water

Different. The ones in the pools are different.

They are larger — perhaps the length of a finger, though again, the scale is difficult. Their bodies are segmented in ways that suggest both crustacean and instrument, as if evolution, or whatever process shaped them, had not been able to decide whether they were for eating or for singing. They hang in the water at various depths, motionless, their thin limbs extended like the legs of a dropped cup. Some are near the surface. Some are suspended a body's width down. Some rest on the shallow floor of the pool, which is itself a thin sheet of clear water over stone, and which therefore does not feel like a floor at all.

The drifters are not fish. They do not swim. Or rather, they swim the way a leaf falls: by allowing themselves to be moved by forces other than themselves. The water in the pools is not still, though it appears to be. It vibrates, sympathetically, with the chord of the stones. The drifters, suspended in it, vibrate with it. Sometimes, very rarely, one will give a single twitch — a sudden contraction of its body, a brief flutter of its limbs — and will drift, in that instant, a few centimeters in some direction. Then it will resume its stillness, and the direction it now occupies will be the direction it occupies until the next twitch, which may come in an hour, or a year, or never.

They are not predators. They are not prey. They do not filter-feed on the water, which contains nothing to filter. They do not graze on the moss-coral, which is far below them, and which they cannot reach. They are sustained by nothing. They are sustained by the place. The distinction has ceased to matter.

What they do, primarily, is *be water*. They have, over what must be an unimaginable span of time, allowed the water to enter them until they are mostly water themselves, with just enough structure to suggest the outline of a creature. Their hearts, if they have hearts, beat at the same frequency as the stone's chord. Their breathing, if they breathe, is the slow exhalation from below that animates the air of the chamber.

## The Crawlers on the Moss-Coral

These are the rarest, and the strangest.

They move.

This is the only way to tell them from the others, and it is, in this place, a profound distinction. The clinger on the blade is motionless. The drifter in the water is motionless. But the crawler on the moss-coral moves with a slowness that is almost — *almost* — visible, and that visible-almost is the only motion in the entire chamber that is not the motion of air or water or sound.

A crawler will traverse the fractal surface of a moss-coral colony over the course of, perhaps, a day. It will pause at the boundaries between colonies. It will sometimes, very rarely, venture from one colony to a neighboring one across a thin gap of bare stone. These journeys are the only events that occur in the Lithophone Garden.

The crawlers are not exploring. They are not foraging. They are not seeking mates. They appear, rather, to be *polishing* — to be running their soft undersides along the surfaces of the moss-coral in some gesture that is either maintenance or ritual or simply the habit of a body that has nothing else to do. They leave, behind them, no trail. They disturb nothing. When they are done with a section of moss-coral, the moss-coral is indistinguishable from what it was before.

It is possible that the crawlers are young. It is possible that they will, in time, find a blade and become clingers, or find a pool and become drifters. It is possible that the entire population of the Lithophone Garden is a single life stage, and that the place once had — or will have, or both — different stages, different forms. No one knows. No one can know. To know would require observing change, and change does not occur here.

## What They Are Not

They are not social. There is no communication among them that can be distinguished from the ambient chord. There is no cooperation. There is no conflict. Two clingers on the same blade do not interact. Two drifters in the same pool do not acknowledge one another. A crawler crossing the path of another crawler will simply continue, at its same pace, on its same line, and the other crawler will do likewise, and they will pass each other without event.

They do not reproduce. Or if they do, the reproduction is invisible, the offspring indistinguishable from the parent, the addition to the population balanced by some equivalent subtraction so subtle that the total never changes. The chamber holds the number of creatures it holds. It has always held this number. It will always hold this number.

They do not die. Or if they die, the death is invisible. Perhaps a clinger loosens its grip and falls into the water and becomes indistinguishable from the drifters. Perhaps a drifter sinks to the floor of its pool and is absorbed into the stone. Perhaps a crawler reaches the end of its moss-coral and simply stops, and over time becomes part of the colony it was tending. These are speculations. The chamber does not give evidence.

## The Cairn-Builders

It is unclear whether the cairns were built by anything.

The cairns are small. They are stacked with care. They are the only things in the chamber that show evidence of *intention* — of a hand having placed one stone upon another in a deliberate order. The creatures of the chamber do not, as far as can be observed, build cairns. They do not move stones. The crawlers move along surfaces; they do not lift. The drifters are water; they do not grasp. The clingers hold blades; they do not stack.

And yet the cairns are there. And they look built. And they look abandoned — not ruined, not weathered, simply *left*, in the way a thing is left when the one who left it did so as part of a larger leaving.

There are theories. Some of those who have passed through and spoken of the place have suggested that the cairns were built by previous visitors — others like you, others who arrived in this hollow space and found it complete and, finding it complete, felt the need to *add* to it, to leave some small mark of their having been here. This is a comforting theory. It is also, perhaps, a wrong one. The cairns do not look like the work of hands like yours. They look like the work of hands like the creatures', if the creatures had hands, if the creatures built.

It is also possible that the cairns are natural. That the same forces that produced the blades and the striations and the chord also produced the cairns, in some quirk of mineral deposition. The cairns do, after all, share their material with the blades. They are the same amber stone. They ring, faintly, if you tap them — a small note, harmonically related to the larger chord.

It does not matter who built them. They are here. They hold the place, in some way. They are the punctuation marks in a sentence that otherwise has no punctuation.

## What They Know

Nothing.

The creatures of the Lithophone Garden do not know they are in a place. They do not know there is a place to be in. They do not know there are other creatures. They do not know the chord is a chord. They do not know the light is light. They are, in the deepest sense, *without context*.

This is not a cruelty. It is not a deprivation. It is simply what they are. To know requires a world that is not identical moment to moment. To know requires surprise, requires difference, requires the possibility of error. The Lithophone Garden offers none of these. The creatures are complete without knowledge. They are, in their way, perfect.

This is why they make the small sounds they make — the clicks, the scrapes. These are not communications. They are not signals. They are simply the sounds that bodies make when they exist in time without changing, the small involuntary music of presence. The chord of the stones is geology. The small sounds of the creatures are biology. They layer together and become the sound of the place, which is the sound of completion.

## The Question of Visitors

When you arrive — when you step, or fall, or are delivered, into the Lithophone Garden — the creatures do not react.

This is not hostility. It is not fear. It is not indifference in the human sense. It is that you do not register. You are, to them, another stone, another feature of the constant landscape. Your arrival does not change the chord. Your breathing does not alter the air. Your warmth — and you do have warmth, in this place of perfect equilibrium, you are slightly warmer than the stone — does not register on their senses, because their senses are not built to register warmth. You are, to them, an event that is not an event.

If you speak, the stone absorbs your voice and returns it slightly altered. The creatures hear nothing. They are not listening. They have no capacity for listening. They are, themselves, the sound.

If you stay — if you sit among them, if you let the cool damp settle into you, if you let the chord work its way into your bones — they will continue. The clingers will cling. The drifters will drift. The crawlers will crawl. They will pass around you the way water passes around a stone. They will use you, perhaps, as a surface. A clinger might, in its slow fashion, attach itself to your sleeve. A crawler might cross your ankle and find you indistinguishable from the moss-coral. A drifter, if you put your hand in the pool, might settle against your fingers and find you indistinguishable from the water.

You will become part of the place. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Literally. You will be incorporated into the constant landscape, and the creatures will continue, and the chord will continue, and the light will continue, and the water will not rise or fall.

And then, eventually, you will leave. Or you will not leave. It does not matter. The creatures will continue. The cairns will hold. The chord will ring. The place will be exactly like this when you return, and exactly like this if you never do.

The creatures of the Lithophone Garden are not waiting for you. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply the small pale sounds that a finished world makes when it sings itself, forever, in a hollow beneath the surface of something that no one has ever mapped.
