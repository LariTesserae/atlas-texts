They are the smallest complete animals anyone has ever described, and they are also, in a sense that becomes clearer the longer you look, the largest — a single body, fourteen meters across, with no head and no boundary and no nervous system that anyone would recognize, only ten thousand jointed ovals running in a slow perpetual drift over a tilted slab of andesite, processing the breath of the rock into more of themselves.

Each one is built on the same plan. A central capsule, hard, segmented, faceted like a cut and poorly polished gemstone — a teardrop of chitin laid on its side, with the pointed end forward and the broad end trailing. Six legs, three to a side, hinged at the upper third of the body and folded flat against the carapace when the creature is still, splayed out in a slow rotary sweep when it is feeding or making way. The legs have no feet. They end in blunt, slightly hooked pads, and the hooks catch in the microscopic pits of the stone and pull. They walk, if that is the word, by reaching one leg forward at a time, in the alternating tripod gait of an insect — but so slowly that the gait is not visible as a gait. It is a continuous rotation, like a wheel turning in thick honey. When you watch one for a full minute, it has not appeared to move. When you mark its position against a sulfur crystal and look again after an hour, it is somewhere else, having traveled perhaps the length of your thumb, and the crystal is behind it, and the vent mouth it was beside is behind that.

There is no head. The forward end of the capsule tapers and rounds and is slightly darker than the rest, and if you look at it with a glass — and someone, somewhere, has looked at it with a glass — you can see a constellation of pores there, finer than the vent holes themselves, each one ringed with a faint brown stain. These are the chemoreceptors. They taste the air the way your nose tastes it, only slower, and with a vocabulary that has no word for anything you would call a smell. They taste the gradient. They know the vent is to the left because the concentration of hydrogen sulfide is greater to the left. They know the slab is tilting because the temperature is rising. They know you are standing near them because the water vapor in your breath is a sharp foreign note in the air they have spent their whole life breathing, and they do not know what to do with it, so they do nothing. After a minute or two the water vapor has dispersed and you are part of the background again.

Underneath, where the legs attach, the capsule softens into a series of overlapping ventral plates. These are what the creature opens to feed. When it settles on a vent, the plates part — not all at once, but in sequence, like the petals of a flower opening in time-lapse — and the soft tissue between them is exposed. It is golden. It is the only color in the creature that is not bronze or black. It is alive in a way the carapace is not, and it glistens, and out of it, as the plates reach their fullest spread, the cilia emerge.

The cilia are the heart of the business. Each one is a single bristle, no thicker than a spider's silk, golden at the base and darkening to almost black at the tip, and there are thousands of them on each ventral plate, packed so close that the tissue beneath them is invisible. They are wet. They are not wet with water — there is no water — but with a dense, slightly oily fluid that the creature's body manufactures continuously from the sulfide it has been processing. The fluid beads on the bristles. It trembles. It does not run, because it is too heavy; it hangs in tiny silver-gold droplets at the bristle tips, and in the dim red light of the slab they catch the sulfur-yellow of the vents and look, from a distance of a few feet, like a halo of pale fire around each feeding creature. The fluid is the lung. It is the stomach. It is the entire interface between the animal and its food. The vent gas, rising through the stone, passes through the cilia and dissolves into the fluid, and the fluid is doing chemistry the way your blood does chemistry — binding oxygen from the trace atmospheric breath that seeps down from above, binding sulfide from below, and running a redox reaction between them that yields energy and a little water and a small quantity of sulfate waste. The energy is what the creature lives on. The water is precious — the pocket is bone-dry, and the creature's interior tissues are the only place in the entire system where water exists in liquid form. The sulfate is excreted, mixed with the fine powder ground from old carapaces, and spread behind the creature as it crawls: the pale gray paste that paints the slab, and that the next creature to pass over will pause to feed on, briefly, before continuing.

The cilia, when they are fully fanned, do not move. The earlier description of a slow rotation is the legs. The cilia are still. The fluid on them moves — a slow circulation, driven by some internal current in the tissue — and as the gas dissolves into it the creature's body processes it, and you can hear the processing. It is the wet grinding. It is the chitin of the internal mandibles, two curved plates deep in the ventral tissue, working against each other in a slow figure-eight that breaks the sulfide compounds down further and pumps the resulting slurry through the creature's body. The plates are faced with something harder than chitin — something mineral, something deposited from the vent gas itself, and over the life of the creature they wear down and are rebuilt, the way your tooth enamel wears and is not rebuilt, except that in this creature it is. The plates darken with use. The oldest creatures, the small black ones, have plates that are almost metallic, and their grinding is the slowest of all.

The three size classes are not different species. They are the same animal at different ages, or at different diets, or in different parts of their life history — the boundary between those three readings is not clear, and may not be meaningful. The smallest are about the size of a grain of rice. The mid-sized ones are about the size of a chickpea. The largest are about the size of the first joint of your thumb. They share the same anatomy, the same faceting, the same six legs, the same golden underbelly. What differs is the chemistry they have been running.

The small black ones, near the eastern lip, are the oldest. They are nearly dead, and have been nearly dead for a long time. The vents they were born to are sealed; the gases they were built to process no longer rise to the place they now occupy. They have been pushed, by the slow outward tide of the colony, to the margins, where the slab is coolest and the air is thinnest and the only food is the trace atmospheric breath that comes down through the fissures above — a thin, cold, oxygen-poor trickle of outside world, mostly nitrogen, with just enough oxygen to keep the small black ones' chemistry barely turning over. They are, in effect, starving in slow motion, and they have been starving for years, and they will continue to starve for years more before they finally stop. Their carapaces are black because the chitin has oxidized past the point of iridescence. Their grinding is almost inaudible because their internal mandibles are turning once every several seconds. They are still. They are patient. They are the memory of the pocket. If the chemistry of the slab were to change back — if a new vent were to open along the eastern lip, bringing fresh sulfide up to the margins — they would, in theory, begin feeding again, and their grinding would speed up, and their color would lighten, and they would rejoin the general mass as mid-sized bronze creatures indistinguishable from any of the others. But the vents do not open on the eastern lip. The vents open on the western tilt, where the slab is hottest, and so the small black ones wait, and slow, and are slowly ground by the larger ones that occasionally wander past them into the same pale gray paste as everything else that has stopped.

The mid-sized bronze ones are the bulk of the colony. They are the ones you see when you stand at the lip. They coat the slab. They are the visible animal, the way a coral reef is the visible animal of a coral. They are the ones that swarm to a new vent within minutes of its opening — how they know, no one has determined; the most careful theory is that the new vent's heat pulse travels through the stone faster than the gas itself, and the chemoreceptors on the creatures nearest the spot register the rising temperature before the gas arrives, and the colony begins to move before there is anything to feed on, as if by anticipation. By the time the gas begins to hiss through the fresh fracture, the creatures are already settling into position, plates opening, cilia fanning, the wet grinding rising in pitch. They are loud. Ten thousand of them, running slightly out of phase, produce a continuous mid-frequency drone that you feel in the bones of your jaw and in the soles of your feet, and the sound has a slow shifting texture, like a fabric being rubbed, because the phases drift, because the creatures are not synchronized, because there is no central clock. Each one is its own clock. Each one is its own mill.

The large dark red ones, near the western tilt, are something different again. They are not older. They may be younger. They are heavier. Their carapaces are thicker, almost half the depth of the body, and the chitin is impregnated with iron compounds that give it the color of cooling lava. They feed on the heavier gases that rise from the deeper vents — gases richer in metal chlorides, in silicates, in compounds that would poison a smaller creature. The large ones have evolved, if that is the word for the slow chemical drift of a population, to process these dense exhalations, and the energy yield is greater, and the creatures grow larger on it, and their mandibles turn more slowly because each rotation is doing more work. Their grinding is a subsonic rumble. You feel it in your feet before you hear it with your ears. When you approach them they do not scatter the way the mid-sized ones do. They close their ventral slit — a single, slow, deliberate motion, the plates drawing together like a purse being drawn shut — and they sit. They are not afraid. They are not aggressive. They are simply heavier than you, and slower, and the disturbance you make in their preferred atmosphere is so slight that it does not register as anything to be addressed. After a moment you are part of the stone to them too, and they open again, and resume.

What they are, in any language the surface world would recognize, is this: a colony of chemosynthetic arthropods, three millimeters to fifteen millimeters in length, feeding on the volatile exhalations of a young volcanic fumarole field. They are not the only kind of life that could live here. They are the only kind that does. They have no predators — there is nothing in the pocket larger than a mid-sized creature, and the only predator-sized thing is the colony itself, which grinds its dead into paste and feeds on the paste and is, in a literal sense, cannibals of its own past. They have no parasites. They have no competitors. They are alone in the pocket the way a single species of tube worm is alone on a hydrothermal vent six thousand feet down, except that they are not on a vent — they are the vent, in animal form, a mobile rind of living chemistry spread across a slab of stone, processing the breath of the magma below into the slow noise and slow heat of their own perpetual feeding.

They do not see you. They do not hear you. They taste you, briefly, as a sharp local excess of water vapor and organic carbon, and then you diffuse into the background. If you stand very still they will treat you as they treat a sulfur-crusted knob of stone: an obstacle, climbed over. Their legs on your ankles are a faint dry tickle. Their weight on the top of your boot is almost nothing. If you kneel and put your hand flat on the slab they will cross your palm in a slow uneven column, and the underside of their bodies against your skin is the only warm thing in the pocket that is not stone, and you will feel the faint vibration of their grinding through your fingertips, a purr at the threshold of sensation, and you will understand, suddenly and without being able to explain it, that the slab is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The slab is a lung. The slab is a stomach. The slab is a single animal, fourteen meters across, with ten thousand mouths and ten thousand grinding chambers and a chemistry that has been running since the crack first opened in the cooling rock, and will run until the heat is finally gone, and then it will slow, and slow, and the ten thousand mouths will close one by one, and the pale gray paste will harden on the stone, and the vents will seal, and the pocket will go quiet for the first time in its life, and the mountain above will not notice.

When you climb back up through the fissure, your clothes carry a faint dusting of the pale gray paste, and a few of the smallest creatures have come with you, lodged in the seams of your jacket, still grinding, slowly drying, slowly dying in the cold outside air. You brush them off. They fall into the scoria. They stop. The wind takes the dust of them and the dust of the paste and spreads it across the flank of the mountain, and the mountain does not know, and you, standing up there in the thin silver light, can feel the faint vibration in the soles of your feet for a few minutes more, as if the rock itself is still remembering the sound. Then even that fades, and there is only the wind, and the cold, and the dark line of the fissure breathing its thin yellow breath into the high air.
