The Vent at Traben is not inhabited. It is *tenanted*, in the older sense of the word—a place held, occupied, but not by anyone who has agreed to be there. What lives in the Vent are the things that have outlived their reasons for being where they are, and the things that have never had reasons at all.

The moss lives there. It is the principal citizen, if citizenship can be measured in area. It has been on the valley floor for a long time, and on the lower walls of the buildings for nearly as long, and on the pipework it is still arriving. The moss is not one moss. The deep cushions on the flat ground are one kind—sphagnum, probably, or something like it, the kind that holds water the way a sponge holds it, so that when you kneel on it your knee comes up wet through to the skin. The crusty stuff on the concrete is another kind, drier, tighter, the color of bone that has been in the sun. And the creeping, almost-black moss that grows in the shadow side of the pipes, where the metal weeps, is a third. None of them are fast. None of them announce themselves. They are the slow green blanket that the valley pulls over itself, night after night, and they do not mind the light, and they do not mind the damp, and they have made their peace with the metal in a way the metal has not yet made its peace with them.

The lichens live there, and they are older than the mosses and prouder of it. There are three families of them, at least, and each has chosen its territory with the deliberation of an old tenant. The white ones—*Lecanora*, the crustose kind, the kind that paints itself in patches on the high rock—have been there longest. They are the slowest. They grow a millimeter a year, and you can see, on the largest of the boulders, the rings of their progress, each one a slightly different shade of grey, the way the lines on a tree trunk are a slightly different shade of brown. They will outlive the buildings. The orange ones live on the iron. They prefer the pipework, and the rivets, and the bolts at the joints, and the long curving rails of the conveyor. They are the ones eating the place. They are doing it slowly, but they are not going to stop. The black ones live underneath. They are on the underside of every horizontal surface, on the north faces of every wall, in the lintels of the doorways. They stain what they touch. They are the lichens of the damp, of the shadow, of the morning before the light has found its way in. The botanists who came, when they came, did not write much about them, but they are the most numerous of the three, and the most patient.

The creeping willow lives there, and it has forgotten, if it ever knew, that it was supposed to be a tree. It runs along the ground. It makes low mounds you can sit on. It has put its roots into the rail of the conveyor, into the cracks between the concrete blocks, into the soft places where a foundation has shifted by less than a finger's width. Its leaves are small and round and pale on the underside, and they turn up in the wind, and in the still air they lie flat. It does not flower often. When it does, the catkins are yellow and brief, and the valley is, for a few days in late spring, the color of weak tea.

The ferns live there. They are not the tall ferns of the lower forests; they are the kind that have made a treaty with the altitude, and the treaty is that they will stay small. They grow in rosettes. Each frond uncurls from a tight coil at the center, slowly, over weeks, and when it is out it does not move, except in the wind, and there is not much wind. The botanists called them *Cystopteris*, the bladder ferns, the kind that like the mouths of mines. They have colonized the cracks in the smallest foundations, the gap between a door and its frame, the inside lip of the open tanks. They are the most visible proof that the valley is not stopped, because they are always in the middle of doing something. A frond is uncurling. A spore case is ripening. A fiddlehead is pushing up between two stones. There is no season when the ferns are quiet.

The tall pale stalks live there. The ones that come up through the moss here and there, alone, like fingers held up to test the wind. They are *Narthecium*, bog asphodel, and they are not a fern and not a grass and not a flower, exactly, though they flower in late summer with a small yellow star that no one sees unless they are looking. They have a rhizome that runs under the moss for a long way before it sends a stalk up, and so what looks like a single plant is often a single plant, and what looks like a scattered colony is often a scattered colony, and the two are sometimes the same organism. They are the tallest thing in the valley, in some places, and they are not very tall. They are the way the valley marks its own height.

The white moth lives there. It is not one moth. It is a species, a small white moth with a faint dusting of grey on the forewings, and it comes out in the damp of the evening, and it is drawn to the vapor that comes from the stacks. The vapor is warm. The moth is not warm. The arrangement suits them both. The moth lays its eggs somewhere—on the pipework, on the underside of the eaves, on the moss near the open tanks—and the caterpillars are small and green and eat the lichen, and the lichen does not seem to mind, and the moth is not numerous but is not rare. It is the most visible animal in the valley, and the only one in the air.

The pale frog lives there. It is in the runoff from one of the lower tanks, the one that has a crack in its side and a slow seep that keeps a small pool wet through the driest part of the year. The frog is the color of wet stone, except for a single dark stripe that runs from its eye to its shoulder, and it sits in the water with only its eyes above the surface, and it does not move when you approach, and it does not move when you leave. It eats the moths, when it can catch them, and the small things that live in the moss, and the things that live in the things that live in the moss. It is the highest predator in the valley. It is also, as far as anyone knows, the only one.

The small things that live in the moss live there. They are not seen. They are felt, sometimes, as a small movement underfoot, and sometimes as a faint dry sound, and sometimes not at all. They are springtails, mostly, and mites, and the larvae of things that will fly or not fly depending on the year. They are white, or pale, or transparent, because they have no pigment, because they have never needed pigment, because the moss is dark and they live in it and no one has ever looked at them. They are numerous. They are the true citizens of the valley, in the sense that they have never left. They were there before the buildings. They will be there after the buildings are ground.

The bacterium lives there. It is not one bacterium; it is many, and they have made their home in the iron. The orange lichen is only the visible part. Underneath, in the pitted metal of the pipes and the rails and the rivets, there is a community of microbes that have learned to eat iron, and they are eating it, and the iron is becoming, by their digestion, the orange crust that the lichen then colonizes. They are slow. They are not the speed of the valley; they are slower than the valley. But they are at work, and the work is the same work the valley is doing, which is the work of becoming the ground it stands on.

The thing that moves through the pipes lives there, or something does. You can hear it if you put your ear to the metal: a hush, a pulse, a low far-off throb, the way a heart sounds in a pillow. The pipes are not empty. The buildings are not all dark. The light that winks on in the windows is steady and the color of a flame seen through fog. The conveyor moves, sometimes, so slowly you think the wind is doing it. Something is being carried, somewhere, into a building, and not back out. It is warm inside some of the buildings, warmer than the rock, and the moss leans toward the walls of those buildings as if it knew. What is in there is not known. The records stopped. The people left, or the people who were there stopped being people, which is also a kind of leaving. What is in there now is a process, or a presence, or a remnant. It hums. It pulses. It does not speak.

The river lives there. It is thin and clear and almost too cold to feel like a river. It has cut its bed into the stones of the valley floor and is still cutting, and the stones it cuts are the same stones the scree is made of, and the scree is the same stone the cliffs are made of, and the cliffs are the same rock the whole valley is made of, and the river is older than the rock, or no—the river is younger than the rock, but the water in the river is older than either, and the cold it carries is the cold of the high passes, and the sound it makes, when it makes a sound, is the sound of something that has been moving for a long time and is in no hurry.

The valley itself lives there. That is, the valley is a thing that is happening, slowly, and the happening is the living. The rocks are settling. The pipes are sagging. The lichen is in the iron. The moss is on the pipes. The ferns are in the cracks. The buildings are becoming the ground. The ground is becoming the valley. The valley is becoming whatever it will be when no one is watching, which is what it was becoming before anyone was watching, and which it will go on becoming, in the same unhurried way, in the same thin and violent light, in the same damp, in the same held-breath silence, for as long as the rocks hold together, which is longer than the pipes will, which is longer than the buildings will, which is longer than anyone now living will be there to see.
