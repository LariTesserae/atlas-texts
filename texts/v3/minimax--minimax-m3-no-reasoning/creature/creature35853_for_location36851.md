# The Inheritors of the Cane Yard

There is no single creature that lives here. There is an arrangement.

The bamboo is the oldest voice in the room, and it does not think of itself as a tenant. It thinks of itself — if it thinks of itself — as the patient. It is sixty feet tall at the perimeter and climbing, its culms fused into a single vertical organism, and it has forgotten that it was ever planted. It has forgotten that there was an experiment. It remembers only the geometry of the light and the slow negotiation with the concrete. It does not eat the machines. It does not eat the pigeons. It eats the carbon that falls from the high canopy, the nitrogen that arrives on the feet of the birds, the water it pulls from its own breath. It is a thing that has stopped competing with its surroundings and has begun to compose them. Where a culm dies, it is not mourned; it is incorporated, the way a river incorporates a fallen tree. The bamboo is not a forest. The bamboo is a single organism that has chosen to look like a forest for reasons of its own.

The machines are the second voice, and they are the strangest. They were looms, or presses, or something for which the word has been eaten along with the middle of the painted loading-bay letters. They are forty feet long and bolted down, and they have not moved in living memory. They hum. This is the central fact of the yard. The hum is a single note, somewhere below the range of conversation, and it is not electrical — there is no current in this place, has not been for longer than anyone can say. The hum is residual. The hum is what happens to metal that has been vibrating in the same way for so long that the vibration has become a property of the metal itself, like the rust, like the weight. The loom, the press, the unnamed thing, they have become instruments. They are played by no one. They play themselves. The bamboo has grown around their hum the way moss grows around a standing stone: not in opposition to it, but in accompaniment. The two sounds — the deep hum of the metal, the higher creak of the growing wood — have come into a kind of interval, a held chord, and the yard is that chord made architectural.

The pigeons are the third voice, and they are the heirs. They came in through the gaps when the gaps were still gaps, or they were here from the beginning, brought in with the machines the way starlings were brought to America. They are the fat gray pigeons with iridescent necks, the pigeons of machine sheds and railway stations, and there are hundreds of them, and they own the floor. They walk the cracked concrete in the mornings between the slender new shoots, bobbing their heads, cooing in their private register, and their droppings fall and feed the bamboo that grows the leaves that make the condensation that waters the bamboo that feeds the pigeons. The pigeons do not know they are part of a cycle. They know only that the floor is theirs and that the seeds fall in late summer and the water is sweet. They nest in the eaves of the machine bays. They roost in the crooks of the high culms. They are the visible democracy of the yard — many bodies, many small decisions, a soft constant murmur of opinion.

The lizards are the fourth voice, and they are the ones you do not see. They live in the upper canopy, where the culms lean together into a vault. They are small, and they are green, and they move in quick corrections among the wheel-shaped webs that span the gaps. The spiders are the fifth voice, and their webs are everywhere in the canopy, layered, the new ones built across the old ones, sagging with their small catches. The flies are the sixth voice, and they are the food, and they are also the weather. The flies hatch from the still water that gathers at the nodes. The spiders eat the flies. The lizards eat the spiders. The lizards live a long time because nothing comes next. The food chain here is a closed parenthesis.

The condensation is the seventh voice, and it is the most constant. It gathers on the undersides of the high leaves and runs down the culms and pools at the swollen nodes and falls again, a soft internal rain that never reaches the floor. It is the yard's own hydrology. It is the proof that the yard has become its own climate — a cellar in late autumn, a greenhouse in winter, a place that has seceded from the weather outside. There is no wind here. There is no sun. There is only the rising of warm wet air from the concrete and the falling of cool dry air from the vault, and the small percussion of water letting go, sixty feet up, tick, tick, tick, the heartbeat of a place that has otherwise forgotten how to count.

The shoots are the eighth voice, and they are the youngest. They come up through every crack in the concrete, each one as thick as a wrist, some of them twenty feet tall now, still slender, still the bright green of new wood, leaning at the angles their cracks taught them. They are the bamboo's revenge on the floor. They are also the floor's revenge on the bamboo — the concrete will not give, and so the shoots must lean, and the leaning makes them beautiful in the way that all forced things are beautiful. They make the floor look like a dry seabed. They make the floor look like the page of a book that has been left open too long and has begun to sprout its own marginalia.

And then there is the gate.

The gate is a gap on the north side where two culms died at the same time and the gap has not yet been filled. It is narrow — the width of a person sideways, the way cattle gaps are — and beyond it is another yard, and beyond that another bamboo fence, and beyond that another yard, and the whole of the old industrial estate given over to the cane. The gate is not a door. The gate is a sentence that has not yet been finished. Through it, in time, the whole city will become a single organism, a single grove, a single slow thought. But that is not yet. That is the future the bamboo is growing toward, one culm at a time.

Inside the gate, inside this yard, the inheritors continue.

The bamboo thickens at the base, patient and absolute. The machines hum their single note, residual in the metal. The pigeons walk the concrete and drop their fertilizer. The lizards correct themselves in the canopy. The spiders rebuild their webs across the old ones. The flies hatch at the nodes. The condensation falls and falls and falls. The shoots lean at the angles they were taught.

No one comes.

No one needs to.

The arrangement holds. The chord is held. The bamboo is always growing.
