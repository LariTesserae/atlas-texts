What lives here has forgotten it was ever anything else.

The ferns were the first to arrive, or the first to stay. They came as spores no larger than the period at the end of this sentence, slipped through the two-centimeter gap in the west wall on a current of air that doesn't move anymore, or carried in the gut of a bird that nested once in the fissure and never came back. They settled in the cracks because the cracks were where the moisture was, and the moisture was where the heat leaked up through the floor, and the heat was where the spores could wake up and do the only thing a spore knows how to do, which is become a thing that wants to keep becoming. There are perhaps nine ferns now of meaningful size, and perhaps forty of smaller size, and uncounted thousands in the moss that has spread between them, and the moss is itself a city, and within the moss there are smaller citizens — thread-fine filaments that have no name in any language you speak, that branch and anastomose in the dark, that trade sugars for minerals across membranes no wider than a wavelength of light.

The ferns are untroubled. They have never been dry. They have never been cold. They do not know that the room they live in is the inside of a machine, because no one has ever told them, and they would not understand the telling. A fern reads the air for water vapor and reads the soil for calcium and reads the light for the particular wavelength that tells it the year is turning, and none of these signals are missing here except the light, and the light they have replaced — the ferns in the yellow trapezoid grow toward the fissure with the slow urgency of compass needles, and the ferns elsewhere grow toward the red glow of the running pump, which is enough to count as a sunrise in the lexicon of a fern.

The vines are older. They were here when the room was built, or very nearly. A root, perhaps, found its way down through the conduit while the conduit still carried wires, and waited in the dark for the wire to fail, and when the wire was removed the root thickened and became a stem and sent out a leaf and then another and then a runner that dropped thirty centimeters to the floor and re-rooted and sent out another runner, and so on, patiently, for the decades it has taken for the vines to hang now in long motionless ropes from the ceiling and climb the pumps and find the seams. The vines are fig, or something very like fig, and they have the indifferent strength of fig, which is the strength of a plant that has decided, in its slow wooden mind, that this cast-iron housing is also a thing it will live in. They do not care about the pumps. They do not know the pumps are pumps. They care about the seam, because the seam is where the water collects, and the water is where they will root next, and rooting next is the only grammar they speak.

The liverworts in the rosettes are newer than the moss and older than the ferns in this place. They are a species that should not, by rights, live below grade, but the heat has confused them into thinking they are at home in a cloud forest, and the constant wet has given them what they needed to believe it. They are dark, almost black, because dark is the color of a liverwort that is not worried about being eaten, and nothing here eats them. Their rosettes lie flat against the concrete and against the lower trunks of the ferns and against the rust of the oil drums, and they are slowly, grain by grain, dissolving the concrete and the rust into something they can drink. They have been at it for years. They will be at it for years more. They are in no rush. They are not capable of being in a rush. A liverwort is a thing that has decided, in its small flat way, that this particular patch of calcium carbonate is now part of its body, and it will incorporate the rest in due time.

In the empty impeller housing of the third pump, the large fern has anchored its rhizome into the steel shaft and spread its fronds up and out through the access plate, and it is, in its quiet way, the king of the chamber — the largest single organism, the most visible, the one that has made the most radical compromise with the architecture. It does not know it is living inside a pump. It knows that there is a dark cavity behind it where the moisture pools, and that the metal shaft conducts heat up from somewhere below, and that this is, by the standards of a fern, a very fine place to be a fern. Its fronds are three meters long. They brush the ceiling. They have begun to push against the corrugated steel decking with the slow insistence of something that wants to be bigger than the room allows.

The moss has colonized the workbench, the wooden stool, the wire spool. It has climbed the legs of the locker. It is patiently digesting the cardboard shim under the short leg of the stool, and in a few years the stool will lean, and in a few more it will fall, and in a few more the moss will have eaten most of it, and no one will know there was ever a stool. The rubber gloves in the locker have gone brittle and the moss has not touched them, because rubber is not food for moss, but the placard beside them has softened at the edges and the moss is feasting on the paper, and the ink, which was gray, is now the brown-gray of a thing that is being eaten, and the words on it are disappearing into the moss in the order in which the moss reached them, which is not the order in which they were written.

The fourth pump is the only running thing that is not a plant.

It is not alive. It is not aware. It is a mechanism that was wound into motion decades ago by a hand that is no longer attached to a person that is no longer attached to a community that is no longer attached to this place, and the mechanism has been turning ever since, drawing current from somewhere — a wire that runs down through the column, or a circuit that was laid into the floor, or something stranger, something that the original engineers would not have drawn on a schematic. The motor hums. The impeller turns. Water, or something with the properties of water, moves through passages that no one has traced. The pump does not know what it is pumping. The pump does not know there is an orchard above. The pump does not know that the vines are prying at its seam, or that the fern has colonized its neighbor, or that the moss is eating the stool, or that you are standing in the room. The pump is doing the only thing it has ever known how to do, and it will do that thing until the current fails, or the impeller seizes, or the vines finally work the seam open and the housing cracks and the shaft bends and the machine stops, and after the machine stops the room will continue, and the moss will continue, and the vines will continue, and the pump will rust, and in a thousand years no one will know it was ever a pump.

The glow under the central column is the strangest thing in the room, and the room has not decided what it is.

It is not a fungus. It is not a bioluminescent bacterium. It is not a chemical reaction, or if it is, it is a chemical reaction that someone has maintained for a very long time. It pulses on a cycle of about four minutes — a slow brightening and a slower dimming — and the cycle has no relationship to the pump's cycle of eleven seconds, and no relationship to the breathing of any animal in the room, because there are no animals in the room. The glow comes up through the seam between the column and the floor and pools on the concrete in a thin wash of blue-white light, and the light does not warm anything, and the light does not cast a shadow, and the light has no apparatus that anyone has been able to identify. The column itself is concrete, poured, and the glow appears to come from within it, somewhere near the base, somewhere below the floor. When you press your ear to the column you hear nothing. When you put your hand over the seam you feel nothing. The glow is simply a fact. It is the slowest fact in the room. It will be here when the pump has rusted to nothing. It will be here when the moss has eaten the last of the wood. It will be here when the vines have pulled the last of the cast iron apart. It will be here when the concrete has softened and fallen and the chamber has filled with green and the orchard above has sent down a thousand roots to claim the space. The glow is older than the room, perhaps. Or the glow is what the room was built around, and the room was built around it the way a cathedral is built around a relic, and the relic has no name and the relic will not be moved.

The slowness of the room is the thing that lives in it most truly. Everything else — the ferns, the vines, the moss, the pump, the glow — is a passenger on the slowness. The slowness is the medium. The slowness is the water these things swim in, the air these things breathe. A frond lifts a millimeter in an hour. A vine extends a centimeter in a week. The rust advances a micron in a day. The moss dissolves a grain of concrete in a season. The glow brightens and dims on a four-minute clock. The pump turns and turns and turns.

There is no hurry here. There has been no hurry for a long time. There will be no hurry for a long time still.
