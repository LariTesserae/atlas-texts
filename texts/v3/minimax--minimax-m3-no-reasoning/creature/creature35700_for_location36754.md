# A Patient World

You are not the first thing to arrive here. You are barely a thing at all, in the country’s accounting. The country counts in centuries and the things in it count in minutes, and you, you are a minute kind of creature — bright and brief, with a heat behind your sternum and a small knot of purpose you carry like a stone in your pocket. The country allows you the way deep water allows a leaf: without interest, without objection.

You will meet, eventually, those who live here.

---

**The water is the first citizen.**

It is in the cupped leaves of the great herbs, lying flat as a mirror, giving back the weak tea-light in a smaller, colder version of itself. It is in the air, which is to say it is everywhere, which is to say it is the country. It is in the moss that runs from you hanging from every horizontal surface in long beards, dripping so slowly that the drips have names you do not know. It is in the root-flesh under your feet, which is not so much ground as a wet felt of a million threads, all of them drinking.

The water does not hurry. It lives in the gutters of bark; it lives in the slow rivers that move through the marsh-edges at the pace of a walking child; it lives in the permanent mist, which is not falling and not rising but suspended, which is to say patient. The water has been here longer than the trees. The water will be here longer than the trees. The water has forgotten that it was ever anything but what it is.

Sometimes the water flows upward. You will see it: a sheet of it climbing the underside of a leaning trunk, defying what you cannot say, and you will stand and the river will stand and neither of you will remark on it. The water does not believe in you. The water believes in gravity, mostly, but only when gravity agrees with the country.

---

**The trees are the second citizens, and they are not individuals.**

This is the first thing you must understand, and you will understand it slowly, the way you understand everything here. A single tree in the country is a sentence in a paragraph in a chapter in a book that has no author and no end. Its roots run into the mouths and the roots and the root-flesh of its neighbors, and its neighbors’ neighbors, and the whole forest is one root-system wearing many hats. You will walk for a day and be walking over the same body the whole time. A single tree may die as, say, a tree, but the trunk survives, dead, hollowed, leaning, and she is now a wall; the wall is now a clearing’s edge; the clearing’s edge is now a place where small bright things live; the small bright things sing, very softly, and the song goes up into the mist where the air has it.

The great trees are columnar. They are wider than you can put your arms around, and they rise straight up for fifty meters before the first branch, and the first branch is the size of a small tree itself. They are so old their bark has stopped peeling and become a kind of geology, a stratified thing of lichens and mosses and the trailing roots of long locks — roots of a host, roots of a guest, roots of a parasite that has forgotten it is a parasite. Their buttresses are as tall as your waist and as thick as your bed, and they are walls. Standing in the country you are always walking among walls, and the walls are always alive, and the alive is always slow.

The great trees do not move. The great trees move, but only by millimeters, in the way of a thing settling into its own weight. When one of them finally falls, she falls across a clearing as a single piece of weather, and where she lies she lies for a century, and a century is nothing to her. The country has a different accounting of centuries than you do. The country’s century is your week.

The strangest trees are the ones that have stopped being trees. You will see them: a hollow thing the size and shape of a tree, but green all through, and breathing, and slowly walking, by means of roots that grow ahead and roots that let go behind. You will see one only once, because they are rare and because they are slow. It will take you a day to walk past it, and when you have passed it, it will have moved a finger’s width, and you will not be sure you saw what you saw.

---

**The small bright things live in the cupped leaves of the great herbs.**

They are the size of your thumb, or your smallest finger, or the first joint of your smallest finger. They are bright in the manner of a thing that has decided to be bright, in colors that have no name in any language you still speak, colors that are not quite colors but suggestions of frequency. They live in the cupped leaves the way you live in a room: with a small door, and a small concern, and a small song.

The small bright things sing. The song is the country’s song. It is a low distributed hum, like the inside of a seashell, like the inside of a sea-shell — like the inside of a sea-shell held against the ear of a child who has never seen the sea. You feel the song more than you hear it. It is in your sternum and in your teeth. It is the sound of ten thousand small lives going on with their small concerns.

The small bright things do not know you. They do not know anything. They know their cupped leaf and their small song and the small thing they eat and the small thing that eats them. They are afraid of you, in the way that a small thing is afraid of a large thing. They will hide when you give them your shadow. They will emerge when you have gone. They will sing their small song, and the song will be the country’s song, and the country will not know you were ever there.

---

**The climbers live on the trunks of the great trees, in long spirals.**

They are many-legged, or few-legged, or their legs are in the wrong places. They are the length of your arm, or twice your arm, or as long as you are tall. They are the color of the bark they live on, which is to say they are the color of the country, which is to say they are the color of patience.

The climbers move in long spirals around the trunks, ascending by a hand’s width in an hour, descending by a hand’s width in a day. They are going somewhere. The always-purpose is not for you to know. The always-purpose may not be for them to know. The always-purpose may be only the spiral itself, and the spiral alone, and the spiral as a way of being in the country without being still.

The climbers eat the bark, or the things that live in the bark, or the air. The climbers do not eat you. The climbers do not see you. The climbers are too busy with their spiral, and you are too large, and the country has a different accounting of largeness than you do. The country’s large is your small. The country’s small is your invisible.

---

**The burrowers live in the root-flesh under your feet.**

You do not see them. You see their works: low trails through the moss, a yard wide, a hand deep, that close behind them as the country heals. The trails are the streets of fresh sleep of a thing that has been taking its small concern from one end of the clearing to the other, and back again, and again.

The burrowers are blind, or nearly so. They have many legs, or no legs, and they move by other means. They are the color of the root-flesh they live in, which is to say they are the color of the country, which is to say they are the color of patience. They have been here longer than the trees. The trees grew over the burrowers; the burrowers grew under the trees; the country is now equal to itself.

The burrowers do not know you. The burrowers know the root-flesh and the things that live in the root-flesh and the slow dark seep that comes through the root-flesh. The burrowers are afraid of the light, in the way that a small thing is afraid of a large thing, and the light is afraid of them, and the country allows them both.

---

**The high things live in the canopy, and only rarely descend.**

You will not see them. You will see their marks. A stripped patch of bark on a great trunk, twenty meters up, with the wound weeping in the manner of all the country’s wounds. A leaning tree, leaning in a way that suggests something heavy landed on it and rose again. A clearing made by something that lay down and was not the wind. A single tree that rests on a single tree, not you, not I, not the wind.

The high things are large. The high things are large in the country’s accounting, which is to say they are larger than you can put your arms around, and they move in the canopy as a slow weather. Their calls come down through the mist as a long, slow, descending thing, and you can listen to it for a quarter of an hour before it ends, and when it ends you will not be sure what the weather was.

The high things are rare. The high things are rare in the way that a sentence is rare in a long book: you must wait for them, and when you read them they are over. The high things do not know you. The high things know the canopy and the high wind and the slow storms that wander the canopy in spirals so wide you can walk under one for a week. The high things are afraid of nothing, in the country, and you are a thing to them, and the thing is small, and the small thing is not.

---

**The marks of the other ones are here too.**

The other ones are not here. The other ones were here, once, and they are not here now. You come across their marks rarely, because the country is so vast and the marks are so scattered, but you come across them.

A line of stones, half-swallowed, running through a clearing for no reason. A flat shelf of rock laid against a hillside in a way the geology cannot account for. A single standing pillar in a marsh, so eaten by lichen and root-hold that it has become a kind of tree itself. The marks are old — older than the oldest trees, you suspect, which is to say older than anything alive anywhere. Whatever made them made them with no tools you would recognize as such; whatever they were, they were made by hands and bodies and a patience as long as the country's own.

There is one place, if you find it, where several of these marks come together in a pattern that is not quite a pattern — a circle, perhaps, or the ghost of a circle, or the memory of a circle in a place that has forgotten how to be a circle. Nothing is built. Nothing is whole. But the marks are here, and they are quiet, and they belong to the country now the way everything belongs to the country.

The other ones did not leave. The other ones did not arrive and did not depart. The other ones were always here, and the country was always here, and the marks are what is left of the other ones, and the marks are the country, and the country is the marks, and the country has been here so long that it has stopped waiting for you, and it will be here so long after you that it will not notice you have gone.

---

**You walk on.**

You walk on, and the root-flesh gives a little under your weight, and somewhere a small thing sings, very softly, in a frequency you feel more than hear, and the song goes up into the mist where the air has it, and the mist goes up into the canopy where the canopy has it, and the canopy goes up into the slow weather that is the country’s weather, and the country has it, and the country has you, and the country has been here so long that it has stopped waiting for you, and the country allows you, and you walk on, and the walking is the country, and the country is the country, and you are a minute kind of creature in a century kind of place, and the minute and the century are both of it, and it is not waiting for you, and you walk on.
