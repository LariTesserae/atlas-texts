# Who Lives in Renkara

What lives in Renkara is not a people but a *practice* — a way of being vertical, a way of inheriting upward. The city has made its inhabitants in its own image: patient, accretive, damp in the seams, green where the water finds them. Below are some of them.

---

## The Wheelwrights of the First Wrap

They live closest to the river and to the source. Their grandmothers were midwives and their grandfathers were rope-makers, and they themselves are neither — they are keepers of the *lift*, the slow muscular labor that hauls water up the mountain's hollow spine.

A wheelwright apprentice begins at nine. She is small for it at first, but the work makes her shoulders wide by twelve and her hearing strange by fifteen, because the waterwheels never stop and she has learned to listen *through* them, the way a sailor learns to hear through wind. She carries a leather hood for sleeping, because even in the dark the wheels are turning somewhere and the sound gets into the teeth.

They are taciturn people, the wheelwrights. They have a saying — *"The river does not hurry"* — which they use to mean many things, most of them about other people's hurry. Their hands are webbed slightly between the second and third fingers, though this is not genetic; it is the work, the long hours in the wet channels where the buckets come down again and the splash has nowhere else to go. They wear oilskin aprons that never fully dry. Their children learn to swim before they learn to walk, in the warm brown outflow below the First Wrap where the river has already taken on the color of the dye-vats upstream.

They die young or they live forever. There seems to be no middle distance. The old wheelwrights are dry and pale and move like reeds, and the young ones are brown and broad and seem to be made of the same wood as their wheels.

## The Hammer-Singers

On the Second Wrap, in the smithing quarter. They are a guild of feature workers.

A hammer-singer's day is bounded by rhythm. The drop-hammers fall in patterns, and the smiths who tend them fall in with the patterns, and over the centuries the patterns have become — without anyone quite deciding this — a kind of music. Each smithy has its own. The big forge on the southern face keeps a slow five-beat, almost a heartbeat; the little armory on the eastern face keeps a faster seven, a gallop; the nailworks keep a relentless four, the sound of which, after an hour, stops being a sound and becomes a wall you live inside.

The hammer-singers do not call themselves singers. The name was given by the children of the Sixth Wrap, who can hear the rhythms faintly from above, through the stone, and who make up words to them.

They are scarred people, the smiths, and loud-voiced, and their children are born knowing to step aside when something is being carried. They drink a great deal, in the wet months especially, when the iron sweats and the work is dangerous and the only cure is the small sharp beer of the brewery next door, which the brewery keeps hot in case the smiths are coming in cold.

There is an old hammer-singer on the eastern face, the last in her line, who has worked the same forge for fifty-one years and whose husband was a wheelwright and whose son is a gardener on the Eighth. She says the rhythm gets into the spine eventually and you can feel weather in it — the hammers falling faster when a storm is coming, slower when the air is heavy, and once, famously, *stopping* for nine minutes in the summer of the great drought, which the upper city took as an omen and the lower city took as a sign that something had broken.

Nothing had broken. She was listening to her dead husband.

## The Cistern-Keepers

These are the city's politicians, though they would not use the word. They are the metrologists, the water-clerks, the quiet inheritors of a dry and exacting trade.

A cistern-keeper is apprenticed to the records — to the ledgers kept since before anyone can remember, in which the water-rations of every terrace, every block, every family with a registered cistern, are inscribed in a careful hand. The hand is the trade. To be a cistern-keeper is to write beautifully about water, and to be willing to walk, in any weather, to any terrace, to verify what one has written.

They are thin, dry people, with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of those who spend their days in cool stone rooms full of ledgers. They wear a small bronze disk on a chain at the throat — the symbol of their office — and the disk is pierced with a number of holes equal to the terrace they serve, plus one, which no one explains.

In the dry months they become important. In the wet months they are ignored, and they return to their ledgers with relief. They are respected but not liked, which is the usual condition of the trade. Their children are quiet and good at sums and tend to marry gardeners, who are patient and can wait out a long dry season.

The eldest cistern-keeper — a woman whose name I will not write, because her name is also the name of the office and to write it would be to give her a body she no longer has — has not left the records-hall in eleven years. She is said to be reviewing the accounts of the First Wrap, which have not balanced since the reign of the grandmother of the grandmother of the current chief wheelwright. The lower city has sent her food. She has accepted it.

## The Children of the Sixth Wrap

The Sixth is the narrowest terrace, and the children raised there are narrow too, in a way that has nothing to do with food. They learn to move sideways before they learn to walk forward, and they have a game called *equest of the Fifth*, which is played by holding one's breath the entire length of a certain alley that runs, somehow, all the way down to the Fifth, and the winner is the one who comes out still silent. They are cruel about this game. They are kind about everything else.

They wear small bells on their ankles when sent on errands. This is practical — the bells warn the carriers above and the workers below — but it is also the mark of the Wrap, and the children are proud of it, and they oil their bells carefully, and they grow up with a particular music in their stride which is, in time, the music of the Sixth.

The cats of the Sixth belong to them. Not to their families — to them collectively, to the children-currently-living, who inherit the cats from the children-previously-living, who named them after grandmothers and great-aunts and the occasional disgraced uncle. The cats are sleek and serious and seem to understand the bells.

## The Gardeners of the Upper Wraps

The Seventh, the Eighth, the Ninth. They are the inheritors of almost everything green.

A Seventh Terrace gardener is something like a vine herself — slow, opportunistic, patient, capable of holding on through long dry seasons by means that are not entirely visible from outside. She knows every cistern-mouth on her terrace by the feel of the stone around it. She can tell, by the slant of a leaf at four in the afternoon, whether the morning's watering will hold. She is unmarried in the way that the river is unmarried — there are attachments, there are seasons of attachment, but the work is the long marriage.

The Eighth gardeners keep bees. This is the city's chief export, and it is a serious business, and the beekeepers are a serious people. They wear veils of fine gauze and they speak to their hives in a low voice and the honey they produce is dark and peppery and is sold, in the lower markets, in small clay jars sealed with wax. The jars are stamped with the beekeeper's mark and the year, and a serious collector will pay a season's wage for an old jar. The beekeepers do not drink their own honey. This is considered correct.

The Ninth is legendary. Few have seen it. The gardeners of the Ninth, when they are mentioned at all, are mentioned as a guild of feature workers — feature in the strict sense, as in *featureless*. They are said to be silent. They are said to prune nothing. They are said to let the figs grow where they will, and to water only the Unfinished — the white terrace above — which is said to thirst.

## The Pigeon-Harvesters

They are the city's only true vertical people. They live on the Fourth, but they work hanging.

A pigeon-harvester rappels from the upper city in a swinging cradle, with a long curved blade and a sack, and works her way along the underside of a buttress-bridge where the droppings have whitened the stone. The work is dangerous and slow and it is the only work of its kind, and so the harvesters are given a festival once a year, in the dry months, when the bridges are driest and the droppings come cleanest.

They are slight, flexible people, with the particular calm of those who have made peace with falling. Their hands are always cold. They marry late or not at all. They are said, by the children of the Sixth, to be in love with the hawks.

---

## The Otter-Watchers

This is not a guild. It is a tradition, and a tradition does not require an office.

The otter-watchers are the children of the lowest terrace, mostly, though some are older and have not yet stopped. They sit at the outflows of the dye-works in the wet season, when the offal is rich and the otters are fat and reddish and unafraid, and they watch. They throw stones, sometimes, but they miss on purpose, and the missing is the point. To miss on purpose is to be seen by the otter. To be seen by the otter is to be counted, in some old accounting that no one any longer keeps the books for.

The otters themselves are not people, but they live in Renkara, and so they are part of what Renkara is, and the children's missing is part of what the children are.

---

## The Dying

The wind keeps moving. In the upper gardens, in the last hours, when the bells are loudest and the light is golden and the fruit-bats are beginning to pour out over the city in their dark ragged stream, the dying are carried up — if they can be carried, if there is time — to the Ninth, or to the Eighth, or to as high as the family can afford.

This is the unspoken custom. The wind of the upper gardens is the city's only true silence, and the silence is reserved for the dying, and so the dying are taken up to where the silence is.

The wheelwrights carry them sometimes, in their webbed hands, because the wheelwrights know the lift and are strong.

The hammer-singers do not attend. They keep the rhythm. The rhythm is the city's heartbeat and the heartbeat must not stop.

The cistern-keepers record the death. This is their work, in the end — to keep the books, to mark the passing of a name from the water-rolls, to inscribe the small precise character that means *no longer here, water-rations reassigned*. They do this in the morning, before the heat. They are brisk about it.

The children of the Sixth wear bells at the funeral, but only at the funeral, and the bells are muffled with cloth, and the sound is wrong and quiet and is the sound the Sixth makes when it is sad.

---

## The Unfinished

Above the Ninth, if the old stories are to be believed, there is a single white terrace where nothing grows and no one lives.

I cannot tell you who lives there. No one lives there. That is the point.

The gardeners of the Ninth are said to water it. The cistern-keswaters are said to meter it. The wind of the upper gardens is said to be reserved for it, in the end.

What lives in the Unfinished is the city itself — the part of the city that began before anyone now living, and that will be here after anyone now living, and that is patient, and damp, and vertical, and that hums.
