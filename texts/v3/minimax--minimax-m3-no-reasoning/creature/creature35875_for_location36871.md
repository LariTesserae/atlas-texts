The lichen is what came first, if you could put a first on it. The lichen arrived on the rocks before anything else did, and it is still arriving, in the slow way that lichen arrives — a greenish-grey suggestion that gathers in the micro-creases of stone that hasn't yet decided what shape it will be. It has no opinion about the buildings. It is beginning to consider them too, in the same unhurried way it considered the boulders that have been there since the rock cooled. There is a particular kind, pale and almost crusty, that likes the flat faces of the new construction; it spreads across the building walls without anyone minding, and the buildings don't seem to mind either, though how you would tell if they did is unclear.

The small grazing things live in the wet meadows between the building tracts. They are reddish, low to the ground, with the kind of thick pelt that says the climate has been negotiating with them for a long time and they have made concessions. They move in clusters of six or eight or twelve, never alone, and their clusters are tighter when the wind picks up and looser when the wind drops. They eat the new grasses that have begun to colonize the thin soils, and they drink from the shallow basins that fill and refill with rain. They have small ears and small eyes and small hooves that leave prints in the mud of the meadows. Their prints are everywhere, but especially in the places where the buildings aren't. They go around the buildings. They don't seem to know why. It is just that the paths between the structures don't have the right kind of ground for their small purposes. They find the wetter margins, where the runoff collects, and they graze there.

The predators are the color of the rock when the rock is dry, and the color of mist when the rock is wet. They are patient. They do not move in the way that the grazers move — in sudden rushes, in tight startled knots — but in the way that weather moves, in long considerations. One of them can be sitting on an outcrop a hundred meters from a cluster of grazing things for an entire afternoon, and the grazing things will not break formation. They know the math. The predators follow the herds at a distance that is more a relationship than a measurement, and they take what they take, and the herds remain the size that they remain. The predators are not interested in the buildings. They cross over them on their routes. They sleep on the warm stone of a wall when the sun has been on it. They leave their marks on outcrops beside the construction, and the marks do not change because the buildings are there.

The birds are the loudest non-weather thing. They come over the plateau in movements that have nothing to do with the city — seasonal passages, north and south, following pressures and prey that predate anything built. They wheel and cry and dive and do all the things that birds do in places where there are no buildings, and the buildings do not interrupt this. The birds nest in the lee of outcrops and on the small ledges that the fresh rock provides, and they also nest, without hesitation, on the ledges and recesses of the buildings, where the architecture offers something that the geology doesn't — shelter, maybe, or the right orientation, or just a place where the wind doesn't reach. They wake early. Their calls are the first sound of the morning, before the wind picks up, before the rain if rain is coming.

The people are spread thinly across all of this. You can walk for a long time between one inhabited structure and the next, and the walk will take you through raw rock and wet meadow and open air. The people are quiet in the way that the buildings are quiet — not by rule, but by the kind of absence that falls over a place where the loudness has been engineered away. They wear clothes the color of the stone, mostly. They speak at a volume that the wind would take if the wind were louder. They know the grazing things by their shapes and the predators by their patience and the birds by their calls, and they don't remark on these things, because to remark on them would be to remark on the climate. They go about their business in the tracts of construction that are scattered across the plateau, and the tracts shift over the years as the geology beneath them becomes more interesting and as the lichen completes its slow opinions.

There is, somewhere in the wet margins, a creature that is only a sound. It lives in the lowest part of the meadows, where the water sits longest, and it calls at dusk and at dawn in a voice that you can't place to a source. The grazing things ignore it. The predators ignore it. The people ignore it. It is just there, part of the ambient sound of a place that is still arriving at what it will be. It is one of the first things to have made a home here, in the sense that a sound can make a home, and it is one of the things that will remain, probably, long after the buildings have shifted to wherever they shift next, and long after the lichen has finished its first slow work on the fresh stone.

The plateau is not finished. Everything on it is, in its way, early. The lichen is early. The soils are early. The rivers are barely suggested in their basins. The animals are passing through. The people are the latest thing to have arrived, and they have arranged themselves across the surface in a way that is careful and quiet and almost not there, and the rocks and the weather and the living things go on with their slow business, and the city is part of the business, and is not remarked upon, and is part of what the plateau is becoming.
