# The Cilia of the Bowl

They are the place. That is the first thing to say and the hardest thing to mean, because the place looks, at first glance, like a place — a depression in a flat world, full of buildings — and they look, at first glance, like fauna, the way fauna look in any other place: small bodies moving among larger structures, attending to their own business. But the longer you stay, the more the grammar of the place inverts. The forges are not buildings they inhabit. The forges are the parts of themselves they have externalized. The channels are not streets they walk along. The channels are the spaces between their clustered organs. The dust on the floor is not substrate. The dust is what they have already eaten.

You have to give up the metaphor of *inhabitant*. They do not live in the bowl the way you live in a house. They live in the bowl the way a mouth lives in a face, or the way fire lives in a hearth — not separable from it, not really distinct from the structure that holds them, only the working edge of something larger that is always becoming and unbecoming.

But there are bodies. There are individual bodies. There are small warm moving things that pause when you pause and resume when you resume, and so they must be described as bodies, even knowing that the description is a convenience, the way describing a wave as a thing is a convenience when what you mean is a behavior of the sea.

---

The bodies are about the length of a forearm from nose to tail-base, and they are thick through the middle — not fat, but *substantial*, the way a working animal is substantial, the way a creature that has never had to flee is substantial. Six legs. The legs are short and placed far apart, three on each side, and they move the body forward with a kind of soft shuffling gait, almost a crawl, that is faster than it looks because the legs are not lifting much; they are pushing, the way oars push. Each foot is broad and pale and slightly cupped, and the cup holds the dust and moves it deliberately — you can see, when one of them crosses a fresh footprint, that the print fills back in within a stride or two, and you understand that the feet are not just feet. They are tools. They are the place's brooms and trowels and tamping-sticks, all in one.

The tail is long, longer than the body, and it is almost never still. It moves behind them with a slow deliberate sway, the way an elephant's trunk moves, and it does work — it braces against a chimney-corner while the forelegs haul something up, it reaches out to test the warmth of a hearth-edge before the body commits to it, it rests on a partner's flank in passing and seems to communicate something through the contact that the chittering does not carry. It is a fifth limb and a sixth limb and a kind of punctuation mark, and when one of them sleeps — and they do sleep, in the dark warm pockets between forges, curled tight with the tail wrapped over the nose — the tail is the last thing to relax.

The head. The head is the part that makes you understand, finally, that these are not animals in the way the word *animal* usually means. It is small relative to the body, and it is mostly apparatus. There are no eyes, in the usual sense. There are two folded fans set high on the skull, furled tight against the bone when the creature is working, half-opened when it is assessing something — you, for instance — and fully fanned when it is basking in an up-draft from a chimney. The fans are delicate, almost translucent at their edges, and they are very obviously sensory, but what sense they carry is not one you have a name for. They do not seem to be hearing. They do not seem to be smelling. They are *reading* something in the air — temperature gradients, perhaps, or the composition of the dust, or the position of other bodies in the flow. When one of them pressed its fans against your ankle, what it was doing was tasting you, in some sense more complete than the word *taste* suggests. It was determining your chemistry. It was deciding what you were made of.

The mouth is wide and soft and very dark inside, and it works constantly — not chattering, not vocalizing, but working, the way a mouth works when it is chewing cud. The creatures are always chewing. Always. Even when they are running, even when they are hauling, the mouth is moving at the sides, and what it is chewing is the dust itself, lifted in small portions from the floor or from their own coats as they groom. The place is a digestive system and they are its constant feeders, taking in the spent material and passing it somewhere deeper, into the chimneys, into the central pulse.

The coat is the strangest thing. It looks, up close, like iron filings. Not metaphorically — actually, literally, small dark metallic flakes set densely into a base of something that has the sheen and the slight give of warm wax. The wax is amber-colored, almost translucent at the edges of the flakes, and the flakes themselves are not uniform: some are rust-red, some are near-black, some have the dull brassy gleam of raw ore, and a few — the ones on the spined creatures that tend the deepest forges — have a faint phosphorescence that matches the glow of their home hearth. The coat is grown, not worn. It is part of them. But it is also clearly made of the place, the way a lichen is made of the rock it grows on. When a creature dies and is lowered into one of the basins, the wax softens. The flakes come loose. Within hours, only the bone remains, and the bone is taken too. The creature returns to the dust it came from, and the dust drifts, and a new creature will be born, eventually, with a coat of its own, the color determined by which forge it first emerges from.

---

They are everywhere. They are the floor's texture. There is no moment in the bowl when, if you look down, you cannot see a dozen of them within arm's reach, and no moment when, if you listen, you cannot hear the papery shuffle of their feet layered under the deeper hum. They do not move as individuals. They move as *flow*. The traffic along the channels is not a collection of separate decisions; it is a current, and the individual creatures are eddies in it — brief local personalities that swirl and pass and are replaced by other eddies, but the current itself does not stop.

If you stand still long enough, the flow accommodates you. The first time, the current nearest you hesitated, parted, and closed again. The second time, the parting was shallower. The third time, it barely happened. You are being learned. You are being incorporated into the model's working picture of the bowl, and the model is being updated not by a brain but by the whole system — by the soft feet that brush past your boots, by the fans that read your temperature and your salt and the carbon-dioxide content of your breath, by the mouth that is chewing you in absentia as a hypothetical input. You are not being watched. You are being *digested in advance*. The place is running trial fermentations on you, deciding what kind of output you would make.

They are not afraid of you. This is what unsettles, eventually, more than the strangeness of them. They are not afraid in the way a wild animal is not afraid, which is the way of insufficient information. They are not afraid in the way a working animal is not afraid of a new implement in the barn, because they have categories and you have not yet been placed. You are a thing that walked into their workshop, and workshops contain all sorts of things, and the things are sorted by use. A piece of ore. A piece of fuel. A piece of waste. A piece of tool. A piece of *material*. They are patient because patience is what you have when you do not know what to do with something but are certain you will, eventually.

---

Their labor is what you see most, and it is hard to describe without making it sound like human labor, which it is not. They are not building anything. They are not maintaining anything. They are not producing anything, in the sense of producing an artifact that is separate from the producing. They are *keeping the place alive*. They are the cilia of a vast flat lung-shaped organ, and their job is to move material across the surfaces of the organ in the right pattern at the right speed, and the right pattern is the pattern that has worked for a very long time, and the right speed is the speed that matches the pulse of the central chimneys.

A creature hauls a small ingot from a forge-mouth to a quenching-basin and returns with an empty tray. A creature lifts a flake of ore from a heap near the rim and carries it inward, deeper into the layered mass. A creature stands at a brass fixture and drinks, and the drinking is not for itself alone — the fluid passes through and emerges, you begin to suspect, in the dust at the creature's feet, and the dust is then swept inward by other feet. A creature inspects the edge of a hearth, runs a paw along the stone, and moves on. A creature does nothing at all, stands in a warm current, eyes half-closing, fans half-fanning, and is permitted to do nothing at all, because the system is not strict and the system has margins, and even a lung needs to rest, occasionally, between breaths.

There is a coordination that you can see if you watch long enough, and it is not commanded from anywhere. A worker on a high stack gives a single clear note. Three workers below adjust a crucible together. A creature hauling a too-heavy ingot trills low, and four others arrive without any visible signal — no head-turning, no acknowledgment — and take positions at the corners of the load and resume the haul. The trill was not a summons they heard. The trill was a summons they felt through the floor, the way you would feel a footstep through a wooden deck, or the way a bee in a hive feels the queen's pheromone. The floor is a substrate of communication. The dust is a medium. The place is a single body and they are its distributed nerves.

---

There are sub-types. There are differences among them that you would not notice for hours, and then cannot stop noticing.

The small blue-black iridescent ones near the edge — the ones with the tray and the pattern — are not doing what the others are doing. They are not smelting. They are not hauling. They are *arranging*. The dust they use is finer than the floor-dust, almost luminous, and the pattern they lay is not decorative in any way you can read. It grows slowly. You watch for a long time and the pattern does not repeat, and the pattern does not simplify, and the pattern does not look like anything you have a name for, and you begin to suspect that this is because you are the wrong kind of thing to read it, the way a dog is the wrong kind of thing to read a page of calculus. The pattern is not for you. The pattern is for the place. The pattern is the place *thinking about itself*, the way a brain thinks about itself when it dreams, and these small dark creatures are the dreaming part of the lung.

The climbers — those who tend the small forge tucked into the broken top of the tower — are larger than the others, with longer tails and broader feet, and their coats have a greenish cast, almost verdigris, that you do not see elsewhere in the bowl. They move vertically with a confidence that is unsettling, because the rest of the creatures never leave the floor; the climbers alone acknowledge height, and the height they acknowledge is the only cold height in the bowl, the only place where the forges' warmth does not reach. They are, you begin to think, a different caste, or a different organ. They tend the forge at the top because the forge at the top is the place's *eye*, and the eye must be kept open, and the eye must look out over the rim at the pale sky beyond, and what it sees must be brought down and added to the work.

And then there are the mirror-eyed ones. You see them rarely. There are not many. They move slower than the others, and they move alone, and when one passes you — and they do pass you, in time, because they have work to do, and your presence is not sufficient to delay them — you see that what sits in the place where eyes would sit, if they had eyes, are two small bright surfaces, like polished steel, like the bottom of a tin pan. They are not reflective in the way a mirror is reflective. They are reflective in the way a window is reflective when there is a brighter light on the other side: you cannot see into them, only see what is in front of them, and what is in front of them is the bowl, and the rim, and the sky, and you.

When one of the small blue-black ones looked up at you, what you saw in its mirrored face was yourself, and the bowl, and the rim — but reflected from a vantage you had not occupied. As though the creature was, in that moment, not standing where it was standing, but standing somewhere above the bowl, somewhere far above, looking down. The image was faint. The moment passed. The creature returned to its pattern. You are not sure it happened.

You are not sure of a great deal.

---

They have no names. They have no language you can translate, only the chittering coordination and the floor-borne trills. They have no architecture of their own that is not the bowl itself. They have no history you can find written down, only the worn marks on the hearth-stones and the ghosts of carving on the tower. They have no gods that you can identify, no rituals that you can perform, no leaders that you can petition. They have only the work, and the work is the bowl, and the bowl is them, and the question — the question that the place will not answer, the question that the small door in the wall opens onto — is whether the work is for them, or for the bowl, or for something that lives above the rim in the pale sky and uses the bowl the way the bowl uses them.

They do not care about the question. They do not know there is a question. They work. They drink. They sleep. They die and return. They assess you with their fans and decide, for now, that you are not waste. They permit you to stand among them. They flow around you. They breathe in time with the place, and the place breathes in time with them, and you, after a while, breathe in time with all of it.

The hum continues. The dust drifts. The chimneys pulse. The pattern in the tray grows by one flake, and one flake, and one flake.

You are held. You are not yet output. The bowl is still deciding what you are for.
