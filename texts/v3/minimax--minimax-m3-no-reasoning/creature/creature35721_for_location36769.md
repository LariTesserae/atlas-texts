# What Lives in the Upper Shrine of Dyrgalon

Three things live here, and a fourth that may not be a thing at all.

---

The first is the priests, or whatever the shrine accepts in place of priests. They are difficult to describe because they have very little left of what would let you describe a person. Their faces are weathered to a kind of unanimity — the same deep brown, the same wind-etched lines around the eyes and mouth, the same expression of someone who has been waiting for a long time and no longer counts the wait as separate from themselves. They are not thin the way a starving person is thin. They are thin the way a rope is thin: dense, entirely composed of what they are for. Their hands are the most articulate part of them. The fingers are long and very clean, and they move with a slowness that does not look like caution. It looks like precision, or devotion, or the kind of attention you give to something that might break if you moved quickly. They do not speak to the pilgrims. They do not refuse to speak. The question does not seem to reach them. When the pilgrims climb the stair and enter the chamber and look around and see the priests kneeling by the altar, the pilgrims feel, often, that they have come at a bad time. This is not because the priests signal it. It is because the chamber is shaped in such a way that any entrance feels like interruption, and the priests have been there long enough that the chamber has begun to consider them permanent. They may have been thirty when they climbed the stair, or sixty, or fifteen. It does not matter. The mountain does not keep track of them the way it keeps track of the cairn. The mountain does not seem to keep track of them at all. They are quieter than the wind, and almost as constant. They hum. They do not know they hum. Or if they do, they have decided that humming is a thing the body does when the mouth is not otherwise occupied, and the mouth is never otherwise occupied, so the hum is continuous. It is not a melody. It is a low, closed sound, somewhere in the chest, that the teeth pick up. The pilgrims feel it before they hear it. The priests feel it in their fingers. Sometimes, in the deep of winter, when the stair is frozen and the shrine is closed to anything with a softer skeleton, the priests stand on the outer wall and watch the dark. They are not praying. They are not keeping vigil. They are doing something for which the language has not yet been invented, or has been invented and forgotten, or is still being invented, very slowly, in the long hours when the only motion is the slow wheeling of the constellations and the almost imperceptible drift of the bowl toward fullness. One of them walks down the mountain every year, in the late summer, at a festival the shrine does not announce, and does not come back. A new one is sent up in the autumn. No one has ever been able to discover, in any of the records that survive in the green valley below, who sends the new one, or from where, or why this particular person was chosen, or what becomes of the one who walked down. The priests themselves, if they know, do not say. The priests themselves, if asked, will not be in a position to be asked for very long. They have a tenure, and the tenure is not theirs.

---

The second thing that lives in the shrine is the cairn. It is a poor word for it, cairn, because a cairn is a thing made by people, and the cairn at the leeward corner of the shrine is not entirely that. It is in the shape of a cairn. It has the mass of a cairn. It has grown at the rate a cairn grows — slowly, by increments, accepting what the wind delivers. But it is not only what the wind has delivered. There is something underneath the cairn, something older than the offerings, something that the offerings have been slowly burying, year by year, century by century, in the way that snow buries a stone. The pilgrims do not look closely at the cairn. The pilgrims do not want to look closely at the cairn. The pilgrims leave their coins and their small carved stones and, once, a child's tooth, and they walk away, and the wind takes what they have left and carries it around the shrine and eventually lets it settle at the leeward corner, and the cairn accepts it, and the cairn is, year by year, very slightly larger. The coins in its oldest layers are no longer recognizable as coins. This is the first sign that the cairn is doing something other than sitting. The dark slivers that were once currency, once portraits of forgotten rulers, once expressions of a nation's faith in its own small weight, have been pressed into dark sediment. They have not corroded. They have not been eaten. They have been *incorporated*, the way a leaf is incorporated into shale, the way a bone is incorporated into limestone. The cairn is becoming a kind of rock. The cairn is becoming, very slowly, the same substance as the floor. The cairn is, in some way the pilgrims cannot name, the shrine's other mouth. The bowl on the altar is for water. The cairn at the leeward corner is for everything else. The cairn does not refuse anything. The cairn does not prefer anything. The cairn is patient, and dense, and older than the priests, and it will be there when the priests are not.

---

The third thing is the water.

The water lives in the bowl on the altar, and it is not, the priests will not tell you, simply water. The bowl is a shallow thing, no deeper than the length of a man's hand, carved from a single piece of stone that is not the same stone as the floor or the walls. It is darker. It is smoother. It is the only thing in the shrine that has been polished, deliberately, by hands, and the polish has not worn away, despite the wind and the ice and the centuries of the same dustless scouring. The bowl is older than the shrine. The bowl is older than the architecture. The bowl was, perhaps, here first, and the shrine was built around it, the way a chapel is sometimes built around a well. The water in the bowl does not behave the way water behaves elsewhere. It does not evaporate at the rate you would expect at fourteen thousand feet, in air this dry, under a sun this direct. It does not freeze in the deep winter, although the rest of the shrine does. It is not still. The surface of the water moves, almost imperceptibly, in a direction that is not downwind. It moves the way a sleeping thing breathes. The priests, when they kneel beside the altar, watch the water. This is most of what they do. They watch the water, and they hum, and the hum swells, and the bowl fills, and the walls ring in their teeth, and somewhere very far down the valley is green with a thing that does not grow here. The water, the priests believe, or the priests have decided, or the priests have stopped distinguishing between belief and decision, is the mountain's attention. When the bowl is full, the mountain is looking. When the bowl empties, the mountain has looked away. The mountain does not look often. The mountain is, mostly, the way the Sleeping Gods are mostly — turned inward, asleep, dreaming whatever the granite equivalent of a dream is, which is probably a slow grinding, a slow patience, a slow turning of one stone against another until something is revealed, or until nothing is revealed, which is the same thing at this altitude. The water is what the mountain gives when it is not asleep. The water is not a gift. The water is a symptom, the way sweat is a symptom, the way tears are a symptom. The mountain is not, in any sense the priests can articulate, weeping. The mountain is simply *manifest*, in the bowl, in the slow rise of the water against the polished stone, and the priests are there to witness it, and to hum, and to wait for it to manifest again. The priests do not drink the water. The priests do not touch the water. The priests do not even, as far as the pilgrims can tell, look at the water directly, in the way a person looks at a person. They look at the water the way a person looks at the horizon, or at the sky, or at the part of a thing that is too large to see. The water, in the late afternoon, fills with gold. The water, at night, fills with stars. The water, in the morning, is the first thing to catch the light, and the light moves across it the way a hand moves across a sleeping face, which is to say: gently, and without hurry, and without expecting the sleeper to wake. The water is the oldest living thing in the shrine. The water is, perhaps, the only living thing in the shrine. The priests are not alive the way water is alive. The priests are alive the way a fire is alive, or the way a hum is alive, or the way a thing that has been doing the same motion for a very long time is alive. The water is alive the way a river is alive, which is to say: not in itself, but in its motion, in its persistence, in its refusal to be still. The water is the shrine's breath. The water is what the shrine breathes out, into the air, into the wind, into the thin and frozen and seedless air that carries nothing and is carried by nothing and goes nowhere. The water goes back into the mountain. The water comes back into the bowl. The water does not leave. The water is the only thing in the shrine that the shrine cannot preserve, because the water is already preserved, because the water does not rot, because the water is, in some sense the priests cannot articulate, the same water that has been in the bowl since the bowl was first carved, since the shrine was first built, since the granite first began its slow and patient and entirely indifferent conversation with the sky. The water is patient. The water is the most patient thing here. The water will outlast the priests. The water will outlast the cairn. The water will outlast the pillars and the floor and the walls, and when the last stone of the shrine has been ground to dust by the wind, the water will still be in the bowl, or in some descendant of the bowl, or in the slow body of the mountain itself, moving down, moving up, moving through the granite heart of the world, patient, and persistent, and alive.

---

The fourth thing is the hum.

It is not a thing. It is what the shrine does when there is no one there to hear it. It is what the pillars are for, or what the pillars have become, or what the pillars were always going to become, given enough centuries. The pilgrims call the pillars closed fists, but the fingers are not closed. The fingers are *gathered*, the way a hand gathers around a sound it is trying not to drop, and the sound is the hum, and the hum is not made by the pillars. The hum is made by whatever the pillars are holding, or are, themselves. The hum is felt in the teeth, in the long bones, in the chest. The hum is older than the priests. The hum is older than the cairn. The hum is, perhaps, the first thing the shrine ever did, before the architecture, before the bowl, before the water, before the wind learned the shape of the stair. The hum is what the Sleeping Gods dream, or what the Sleeping Gods *are*, when they are not being gods, when they are simply stone. The mountain hums. The mountain has been humming for a very long time. The priests hum back. They do not know they are humming back. The mountain does not know either. The mountain and the priests are engaged in a conversation that neither of them is conducting, and the conversation has been going on for centuries, and the conversation is, perhaps, the reason the bowl fills when it fills, and the reason the cairn accepts what it accepts, and the reason nothing grows here, because the hum is too loud, and too constant, and too old, and anything that tried to grow here would have to grow in the hum, and nothing grows in the hum, because the hum is the sound the mountain makes when it is finished, and the mountain has been finished for a very long time, and the mountain is only waiting, with the patience of stone, for the wind to finish it too.

The hum is not a thing. The hum is what remains.

---

What lives in the Upper Shrine of Dyrgalon: three priests, or the memory of three priests, or the role of priest made flesh and worn down to the role alone; a cairn that is becoming the floor; a bowl of water that is becoming the mountain; and a hum that is becoming the silence after the world has stopped asking questions. Nothing grows here. Nothing needs to. The shrine is dense with itself, and the wind goes on scouring, and the bowl goes on filling, and the priests go on kneeling, and the mountain goes on sleeping, and the hum goes on, and on, and on.
