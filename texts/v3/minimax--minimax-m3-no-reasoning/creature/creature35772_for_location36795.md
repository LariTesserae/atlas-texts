# The Inheritors of the Vault

They live here the way moss lives on a stone: not by conquest, but by simply *outlasting* the original tenants.

## The Persistent Ones

The pale lichen under the pipe-tray is the oldest. You can tell because it is the most indifferent to you. It has been drinking condensation for years — perhaps longer than the bats have been sleeping here, longer than the rust-rat has known these pipes as its highway system. You will not see it move. You will not see it do anything at all, except, if you crouch and wait long enough, *breathe*: a microscopic expansion and contraction that smells faintly of petrichor and something older, something almost like the inside of a sick lung. It is not alone. If you were to scrape a thumbnail across its crust, you would find it is layered — three, four, five growths stacked one atop another, each one a different shade of bone-white, each one deposited by a different epoch of dripping water. The oldest layer has begun to calcify. It is becoming stone. It is becoming the wall.

The ferns are younger and stupider. They rooted in the calcium towers because the towers were always wet, and the towers were always wet because the pipes above them have been leaking for forty years. They are the color of weak tea because there is almost no light. They are three inches tall because there is almost no light. They have a plan, which is to grow toward a patch of dim amber glow from one of the caged service lamps, and they are executing this plan with the blind optimism of all green things, and they will never reach it. They do not know this. They are ferns.

## The Warm Ones

The bats sleep in the manifold because the manifold is warm. Three of them, maybe four — you will never get an exact count, because they wedge themselves into the gaps between flanges and valve-wheels, upside down, furled like wet leaves, and only one snout or one folded ear-tip will ever be visible at a time. They are small. *Pipistrelles*, probably — the kind that live in the eaves of suburban houses and eat moths out of porch-light beams. They do not belong down here at all. They came down through a crack in some forgotten ventilation shaft one summer when a heat-wave made the surface unbearable, and they found this place, and they never left. Why would they? It is always sixty-two degrees. It is always humid. The insects that find their way in through the same cracks — earwigs, drain-flies, the occasional bewildered moth — never find their way out. The bats have a larder and a climate and a darkness they do not have to defend. They are the aristocracy of the vault.

In winter, one of them — the oldest, the one with a nick out of her left ear — will wake in the dark and fly a lazy circuit of the chamber, just to remember that she can. You will not see her unless you bring a light, and if you bring a light, she will be gone before you register her shape, leaving only the brief memory of leathery motion and the faint musk of her passage.

## The Swift One

You saw it in the corner of your eye, and you did not pursue it, and you were right not to. It is a rat. Or it is the *descendant* of rats. It lives in the wall cavities between the cable trays, in a warren of borrows chewed through the calcium deposits and the crumbling insulation of dead wiring. Its ancestors were ordinary brown rats who came down looking for warmth and stayed for the same reasons the bats did, but several generations of living in total darkness have changed it. It is smaller than a surface rat. Paler. Its eyes, when you might catch them in the amber lamp-light, are dull and half-formed and seem to look at nothing. Its whiskers are longer than they should be. It navigates by smell and by whisker and by the faint pressure-changes of air moving through pipes, and it knows every drip-pattern in the vault by heart. It can run the length of a pipe-tray without slipping. It can leap from a sagging cable to a corroded I-beam and judge the distance in absolute dark.

It is not dangerous to you. It is dangerous to a crumb you might drop. It is dangerous to the ferns, which it does not eat, because ferns have nothing in them the rat wants. It eats the insects. It eats the spiders. It eats, sometimes, a bat that has fallen from the manifold and hit the iron grating too hard to fly again. It is the only predator in the vault. It wears this distinction lightly.

It does not have a name you will ever learn.

## The Small and Smaller

If you sit very still on the valve housing for ten minutes, the vault will begin to reveal its smallest citizens.

A spider — a *Tegenaria*, probably, a cellar spider, the kind with the long trembling legs — has built a web in the angle between two service lamps. The web is not for catching what flies, because nothing flies in here except the bats on their winter circuits. The web is for catching the drip. Yes. The spider has learned to harvest condensation. The web is a net for water. The spider sits at its center and waits, and when a bead falls through, it drinks. It has been here for two years. Its predecessor built the web. Its predecessor's predecessor chose the corner.

The drain-flies dance in the columns of rising steam. They are the color of smoke and no bigger than a fleck of pepper, and they are innumerable — a haze of them in the air between the grates, doing whatever it is drain-flies do, which is to hover and to mate and to die on the iron walkway, where their tiny bodies pile in the corners like grey dust.

In the calcium crust on the walls, there are things you cannot see without a magnifier. Springtails. Mites. Something that might be a tardigrade, if tardigrades lived in heated pipe-vaults, which they might. A whole dark bestiary in the white deposit, eating whatever passes through — spores, pollen, dead flies, each other. They have been here longer than the ferns. They will be here longer than the ferns. They are the true inheritors. They do not care about the heat exchanger. They do not care about the maintenance schedule in the distant control room. They will be here when the last lamp finally fails and the amber light goes out of the world for good.

## What It Adds Up To

The vault is not an ecosystem in the way a forest is an ecosystem. There is no photosynthesis. There is no season. There is only the endless cycle of water falling, water evaporating, water rising, water falling again, and the small lives that have learned to live on the surface of that cycle the way barnacles live on the hull of a ship — not in the sea, but on the skin of something moving through it.

The heat exchanger will run for as long as someone remembers to tick the box. When the ticking stops — when the last duty engineer retires or is reassigned or forgets — the heat will fail. The condensation will fail. The water will stop weeping down the pipes. And in the year that follows, the lichen will dry. The ferns will brown. The spiders will leave, or die. The drain-flies will hatch one last generation and find no more food and perish in the steam. The rats will go first, climbing back up through whatever crack their ancestors came down, and finding the surface changed beyond recognition.

The bats will go last. They will wheel once around the dark dome, confused, and they will leave through the ventilation shaft, and the vault will go quiet for the first time in a century.

And the springtails, in the calcium crust on the walls, will not notice for a very long time.
