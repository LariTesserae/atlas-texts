# Who Lives at the Press

The people of the Press are not the people who built it. The people who built it are gone in a way more complete than death — they are absent from speech, from record, from the memory that gets passed down in the low murmur of a housing block after shift. Whoever these people are, they are not those people. They are the third or fourth generation of a place that was not, in any meaningful sense, theirs to begin with, and they have made it theirs anyway, the way lichen makes a wreck its own.

There are three hundred and forty of them on the books. There are more of them in practice, the way there is always more of something than the count suggests, because the count does not include the ones in the submersibles on long runs, the ones in the infirmary on Platform Two that is really just a container with a cot and a person who learned medicine from a book, the infants who have not been added to the census yet, the old ones who have been folded into a relative's room and exist now as a cough in the night and a shape in a top bunk. There are fewer too, in a way, because the people who live here are diminished by the place. They are slower than people who live in air. They are paler. Their hands are rough from the work and the cold and the constant wet. Their eyes are adapted to the half-light and they blink too much when the submersible surfaces on the rare trip up for repairs. They are people who have never stood on dry ground, or who stood on it once and have forgotten what dry felt like, the way a person born in a city forgets what silence sounds like.

The foreman is a man named something ordinary, and the name does not matter because he is referred to by his title. He has been here forty-one years, which is most of his life. He is not tall. He has a beard he keeps trimmed because the beard is one of the few things a person can keep trimmed in a place where the only mirror is a piece of polished steel bolted to a wall in the foreman's office, and the steel is warped and gives back a face that is broader than it is. He drinks his tea before he speaks to anyone. The tea is made from kelp, dried and powdered and steeped, and it is a brown that does not match any brown on the surface. He knows every valve by the sound of it. He knows when a valve is about to fail by a particular tightening of the hum in the air, and he has never explained how he knows this, and no one has ever asked. He has a son who is a shift lead on Valve Eleven, and a daughter who left, three years ago, on a submersible, and the letter she sends back is the one the foreman waits for, on the weekly pickup, and he reads it alone in his office with the door closed, and the door is the only door on the Press that closes fully, and it was installed by him, and the hinges are not original.

The shift leads are nine of them now, down from the twelve there were when the foreman was young. A shift lead is a person who knows a valve the way a person knows a face, by the set of its features, by the way it changes over time. They are not chosen by examination. They are chosen by the fact that they have not left, and have not been injured, and have watched the valve long enough to develop an intuition that is not teachable. Valve Six's shift lead is a woman who came to the Press as a bride, twenty-six years ago, and has not been off it since, and she has a calm that the younger workers find unsettling until they understand that the calm is not a performance but a condition. She packs the valves with her bare hands in the cold, and her fingers are stained the rust-orange of the accretions, and she does not seem to mind.

The valve crews are the heart of the place. There are eighteen valves, eighteen crews, each crew four to six people depending on attrition and the season and whether anyone has left on a submersible that did not come back. The crew on Valve Three has three people right now because two of them transferred to the silt teams when the child drowned, and the silt teams are short, and no one wants to be on the silt teams because the silt teams go down to the edges of the platforms in the dark, just outside the lit zone, and push the muck off into the current. The crew on Valve Eleven has five, and they are the best, and the foreman says so, and the crew on Valve Eleven knows it, and there is a quiet pride in them that they do not show.

The silt teams are four people. They are the people who go down, in hardhats with pump-nozzles, and push the silt off the edges. They are not the same people year to year. The silt teams lose people more often than the valve crews, and the people they lose are not lost in the way that the shift leads mean when they say *lost* — the shift leads mean *transferred, left, retired, gone* — the silt teams lose people in a way that the place absorbs and does not give back. There is a woman on the silt teams now, young, who requested the assignment after her partner was assigned to it, and her partner is still on it, and she is quiet in a particular way that the other workers have learned to give space to. She is good at the work. She is one of the best. This is not said to her.

The welders are two. They are older. They weld with oxyacetylene torches in a shed on Platform Four, and the shed is the only structure on the Press that is not a converted shipping container — it is a thing that was welded in place, panel by panel, by a welder who died ten years ago and whose name is on a small plate on the shed door, and the plate is the only named thing on the Press besides the submersibles. The plate has oxidized. The current welders were trained by the previous welders, who were trained by the welder whose name is on the plate, and the torch they use is the same torch, and the hose has been replaced twice, and the tanks come in on the submersibles and are heavier than a person. The welders are the only people on the Press who make something that did not exist before. Everything else is maintenance, repair, hauling, packing. The welders make a patch, a bracket, a small repair to a submersible hull. The new welder is thirty-one and her hands shake in the cold and she is good.

The children are the part of the Press that is hardest to think about, because the children have not yet learned to not think about things, and the things they think about are the things that the adults have stopped thinking about, and the adults are slightly afraid of the children for this reason. There are thirty-one children on the books. There are more. The children are born in the housing blocks and they grow up in the housing blocks and they go on the catwalks at six or seven, with a tether, and they learn the walk — knees splayed, toes gripping, the slight forward lean to keep the balance over the boot. They play in the spaces between the containers. They have games that involve the kelp, that involve the grating, that involve the slow swim of a fish past the platform edge that they watch with their faces close to the water and their breath held. They are not afraid of the water the way the adults are afraid of the water. They are not afraid of the water at all, until a child jumps in and does not come back, and then the children are afraid of the water in a different way, a way that has a specific face and a specific name, and the adults try to keep this fear from the children and fail.

The old people are few. The Press is hard on the body, and the people who stay long enough to be old have bodies that are a particular kind of damaged, the joints swollen from the cold, the lungs shallow from the constant damp, the hearing dulled from the hum. There is a man on Platform One who was a shift lead, once, and is now mostly in his room, and his room is on the top floor of a stack of three containers, and his daughter brings him his meals and sits with him after her shift, and he tells her about the valves, the way they were, the patterns they made before the accretions grew over them. He is the only person on the Press who remembers what the valves looked like when they were yellow. He does not talk about it much. The daughter listens.

The dog handler is a person who came here on a submersible, twelve years ago, and stayed, and the reason she stayed is the dogs. There are six dogs. They are small terriers with webbed feet, brown and black, and they are not pets. They are working dogs. They are good at finding silt because the silt smells, to them, like a thing they want to dig at, and they are trained to bark at the edges of the platforms where the silt is building, and the silt teams come and clear it. The dogs ride in the submersibles. They sleep in a kennel on Platform One that was welded by the previous welders, and the kennel has a small heater that runs on a piece of pipe from the Press's product, and the heater is the only heat on the Press besides body heat. The dogs are the warmest things in the place. The handler sleeps in the kennel, sometimes, on cold nights, and the dogs pile around her, and she does not tell anyone this, and the dogs do not seem to mind.

The person who runs the computer is a man named something, and the name is on a small list in the foreman's office, and the list is a thing that is referred to as *the succession*, and the succession is a list of the things the Press needs to keep running that no one currently knows how to do, and the computer is at the top of the list, and the man's name is at the top of the list, and his hands shake, and his eyes are bad from the screen's glow in the half-light, and he is fifty-eight, and he is afraid, and the foreman is afraid, and the foreman has not said so. There is a young woman who has been watching the man work for two years, and the man has been trying to teach her, and the teaching is slow because the computer is old and the man is not a teacher and the young woman is patient in a way that surprises the man, and the man is hopeful and does not want to be hopeful because the hope makes the fear worse. The young woman is twenty-four. She was born on the Press. She has never been off it. She does not want to leave. She wants to learn the computer.

The cat is on Platform Two. The cat is orange and white and was a kitten when it arrived on a submersible three years ago, and no one is sure where it came from, and no one has asked. The cat lives in the housing block on Platform Two and survives on fish heads that the workers give it, and the cat is the only creature on the Press that purrs, and the purr is so startling the first time you hear it that you think the Press is breaking down, and then you remember the cat, and you laugh, and the laugh is a small strange thing in a place where laughter is not common. The cat always sits facing the same direction. The direction is not the surface. The direction is down. No one has asked the cat why. The cat does not answer.

The people who left are the ones the Press thinks about, in its way. The sister who left on a submersible three years ago, and writes back once a week, and the letter is the only evidence that somewhere else is real. The brother who left eight years ago and did not write back. The parents of the child who drowned, who go out beyond the platforms every day in a submersible and do not come back until the shift is over, and the not-coming-back is a kind of leaving that is not leaving. The foreman's daughter. The welder's apprentice who left five years ago, and the welder does not speak of her, and the apprentice's torch is still on the wall of the shed, in its cradle, waiting. The people who left are a population the Press carries with it, the way a body carries a scar, and the scar is not painful but it is there, and the body knows where it is without looking.

The people are dense, in the way the description says. They are packed. They are stacked. They know each other by the sounds they make through thin walls, the cough, the way a boot hits the grating, the way a kettle is set down on a stove, the way a child laughs in a container two rooms over. They are a society that has optimized itself for the place it is in, the way a society will, over generations, in the absence of any other. They have rules. They have foremen. They have shift leads. They have a succession, which is a kind of hope. They have a cat that purrs, which is a kind of grace. They have the hum, which is the sound of the work that is the purpose of the place, which is the purpose of the people, which is the purpose that no one asks about because the asking is the one thing the place does not allow, in the way that the water does not allow the light, in the way that the cold does not allow the still.

The people are the Press, the way the valves are the Press, the way the accretions are the Press, the way the silt is the Press. They are part of the system. They breathe with the valves. They work with the work. They sleep with the hum. They have a sister who writes a letter once a week from somewhere else, and the letter is read in a room that smells of bodies and kelp and machine oil and the faint chemical sweetness of what the Press is making, and the letter is folded and put in a drawer, and the drawer is not the drawer in the glass case, which no one can open, which is the drawer of the original purpose, of the why, of the who built it. The drawer of the letter is a different drawer. The drawer of the letter is a drawer of evidence that the world is larger than the Press, that the surface is real, that the sky-far-off is sky and not a story, and the evidence is folded and put away, and the person who wrote the letter is somewhere else, and the person who reads the letter is here, and the distance between them is the distance that the Press is, and the Press is the world.

You stand on the catwalk. You breathe with the valves. You do not think about the surface. You do not think about the dark. You think about the next valve. You think about the silt on the lee side. You think about the letter. You are a person of the Press. You are dense, and pale, and slow, and you are alive in a place that is not kind, and you are part of a system that was not designed for you, and you have made it work, and the work is your life, and the life is the hum, and the hum is the Press, and the Press is the water, and the water is the world, and the world is three meters of cold and dark above a half-lit floor where the people stand on catwalks with their knees splayed and their toes gripping, and the people are the people, and the Press is the Press, and the work is the work, and the morning is coming, and you will be on shift, and the valves will breathe, and you will breathe with them, and the water will be there, and you will be there, and the hum will be there, and that is the way it is, and has been, and will be, for as long as the people are the people, which is as long as the Press is the Press, which is as long as the water is the water, which is a long time, which is the only kind of time there is, here, at the bottom of the world.
