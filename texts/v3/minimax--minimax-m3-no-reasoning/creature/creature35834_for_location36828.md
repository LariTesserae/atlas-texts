The people of Lower Verdigre wake in the small hours, when the mosses shift on the walls and the spine-ferns lean another fraction of a degree toward the cliff, and they lie still in their iron-walled rooms and listen.

The first thing they listen for is the thrumming. The thrumming is the constant. If the thrumming is at its usual pitch, they may turn over. If the thrumming has changed key — if the underthing is humming a new note — they will lie still a little longer and consider what the change might mean, and whether the change is the kind of change that requires them to get up and check the window, or the kind of change that requires them to lie still and wait for the answer to pass.

They are patient people. The damp has made them patient. The constant cool year-round, the diffuse sourceless twilight, the mosses breathing a finger's width an hour on the walls — all of this has conspired, over many generations, to produce a kind of temperament in the inhabitants that is closer to *patient* than to *warm*. They are patient the way old stone is patient. They have been waiting for something for a very long time, and they have stopped being able to say what it is.

When the mosses begin to brighten along the walls — a faint phosphorescence that is the city's version of dawn — they rise. They wash, if they can find water that is not the canals. The canals are a finger's deep, and the water is cold, and the lights in the water are the color of a bruise, and although no one reaches in anymore, the people do not wash in them. They wash in the small basins the engineers have installed at intervals through the middle switchbacks — grey stone basins with grey stone taps, fed by pipes that come up from below, with a faint mineral taste and a warmth that the engineers do not explain. The water is warm. It is always warm. The people do not ask why.

They dress in layers. The temperature does not vary by more than a few degrees, the way a cellar does not vary. The damp is constant. Their clothes are mended many times, patched with the same moss-brick and vine-curtain and slag that the city is patched with, and the patches grow, sometimes, and have to be cut back. The children's clothes, in particular, have begun to develop their own small ecosystems — a sprig of spine-fern at the cuff, a creeping moss along a hem, a single blue vine that has woven itself into the weave of a sleeve and will not come out and is considered lucky. The adults' clothes are quieter. The adults have learned, mostly, to keep their clothes from sprouting.

They are soft-spoken, the people of Lower Verdigre. They speak at the volume that will not disturb the mosses. They have learned not to mention the engines in the presence of the spine-ferns. They have learned not to mention the bird in the presence of the mosses, and not to mention the mosses in the presence of the cabbage-tenders, and not to mention the cabbage-tenders in the presence of the moss-keepers, and so on, in a delicate web of small avoidances that the surface people navigate without thinking about it, the way a fish navigates water. They have made these accommodations over so long that they have forgotten that they are accommodations. They speak, and move, and are quiet, in the shape of a place that has worn them to its own fit.

They are not unfriendly. They are *present*, the way a ruin is present.

---

In the morning, after the gray tea, they stand at the iron-framed windows and check on the people across the way.

The windows are the only honest part of the building. The buildings will lie, gently, in the way that dreams lie — will present a wall that is not a wall, a floor that is not a floor, a door that is a wall — but the windows, the plain rectangular windows cut into the iron frames, will not. The windows show what is actually there. Sometimes what is actually there is not what was there yesterday. Sometimes what is actually there is a vine-curtain, or a soft green nothing, or the inside of a moss-hummock. The city is *honest*, in this narrow way. It tells the truth through its windows.

So the people stand at the windows in the morning, and they look across the way, at the windows of the building opposite, and they see whether the people there are themselves. If the people across the way are waving — if a hand is raised at a window, if a curtain is twitched in a particular way, if a small piece of moss-brick has been placed on the sill in the pattern that means *we are here, we are ourselves* — then you may go outside. If the people across the way are not waving, you stay inside. The people across the way are usually themselves. They are not always.

Some of the people, particularly the old ones, will knock on the neighbor's wall in the small hours and ask, very quietly, *Are you still you?* And the neighbor, sometimes, will answer. And the neighbor, sometimes, will not. And the old person will lie awake a little longer, listening to the thrumming, and will check the window again in the morning.

The old people are the most afraid of the buildings. The younger people have grown up with the buildings lying; the buildings are a normal part of life, like weather. The old people remember, or think they remember, a time when the buildings did not lie, or lied less. They are the ones who most carefully check the windows. They are the ones who, sometimes, in the small hours, will lie awake and listen to the mosses breathing on the walls of their rooms, and will feel the room *settle* around them, and will not be sure, for a moment, whether they are still in the same room they fell asleep in.

The old people are also the most patient with the cabbage. They sit against the cabbage in the central plaza, in the cool of the constant summer, and they lean into it, and they feel it breathing, and they feel the faint cool that runs a fraction of a degree colder than its surroundings, and they remember, or think they remember, when the cabbage was the size of a chapel, and not the size of three chapels, and had not yet eaten the chapel that used to stand on the plaza, and the plaza was a plaza, and not a cabbage. They sigh. They lean. The cabbage does not mind being leaned on.

---

The engineers are the most hidden of the city's people, and the most polite.

They live mostly in the deep levels, in the underthing, where the engines are. They are a hereditary caste. They do not, on the whole, encourage questions. They are very polite about not encouraging questions. They will offer you tea. The tea is a gray infusion made from a moss that does not grow anywhere but the engine rooms, and it tastes of nothing in particular, and it is warm, and after you have drunk it you will not want to ask questions anymore for an hour or two. This is considered a courtesy.

The engineers have a stillness to them that is not the stillness of calm but the stillness of people who have been humming for a long time and have absorbed the hum. Their skin is pale from the dim. Their hands are scarred, but the scars are old and smooth, like the slag-road is scarred, like the city's iron is scarred. They speak softly. They know things. They do not say what they know.

Their children are raised in the engine rooms, in the deep levels, and they learn the hum before they learn language. The children grow up to be engineers, mostly. Some do not. Some of the children who do not become engineers come up to the surface, and they bring with them a certain quality — a certain patience, a certain sideways attention — that the surface people do not have. These children become, sometimes, the spine-fern wardens, who are the most romantic of the city's small groups. Or the moss-keepers, who are the slowest. Or the water-walkers, who are the strangest. Or, very rarely, the lip-readers, who are the most argumentative.

The engineers have a council of their own, separate from the surface council, and the two councils do not speak to each other. The engineers' council meets in a chamber lit by a single cabbage-glow lantern, and they speak in low voices, and they are served each other's moss-tea, and they make decisions about the engines that the surface people will never know about. The surface people do not, on the whole, mind. They are used to not knowing. The underground has its own kind of patience, and the surface has its own, and the two kinds of patience are not the same, and the engineers will not tell you what the difference is.

---

The cabbage-tenders are the most patient of the surface people, and the most melancholy.

There are people whose job it is to keep the cabbage from the moss, and people whose job it is to keep the moss from the cabbage, and people whose job it is to keep the spine-ferns from both, and these three groups are in a state of polite, perpetual, low-grade warfare. They are all on the city council. They hate each other with the weary intimacy of people who have known each other for too long.

The cabbage-tenders tend the cabbage. They prune the outer leaves with curved iron shears. They keep the moss from settling on it. They keep the spine-ferns from leaning into it. They speak to the cabbage, in low voices, while they work, and the cabbage does not answer, but the cabbage-tenders continue speaking, because the speaking is part of the tending. They tell the cabbage about their day. They tell the cabbage about the mosses. They tell the cabbage about the people across the way, and whether they were themselves today. The cabbage does not mind being told. The cabbage has not minded being anything for a very long time.

The cabbage-tenders, like the cabbage, glow faintly at the creases of their hands after a while. The faint phosphorescence transfers, slowly, over years of tending. The cabbage-tenders do not comment on this. The other city groups do not comment on this. It is simply a thing that happens to people who keep the cabbage.

The people who are fined in time — those who have touched the light in the water and lost a small piece of their hand's memory — are sent to work the cabbage. They do not seem to mind. They come back, eventually, and they do not talk about what they did, and the violet tint at the creases of their hands is deeper than the cabbage-tenders' violet tint, and the violet tint never quite fades, and the other people notice, and do not mention it. The fined come back, and they go on with their days, and they have a slightly different quality — a slightly different attention — and the children, who notice everything, have noticed, and do not mention it either.

---

The moss-keepers are the quietest of the city's people.

The mosses are taller than a person. They grow in pale hummocks against any vertical surface — the cliffs, the ruin walls, the ferrocrete bridge-buttresses. They move in a slow visible way, a finger's width in an hour, bulging and subsiding, as if breathing. They are warm to the touch, faintly, and the children sleep on them when the children sleep, which is not often.

The moss-keepers tend the mosses. They trim them back from the cabbage, gently. They move the mosses that are wandering too far up the iron frames of the windows, because the windows are the only honest part of the building, and the moss-keepers, like everyone, value the honesty. They coax the mosses into pleasing shapes, sometimes, in the lower switchbacks where the moss-brick houses are built; the moss-keepers will spend a morning arranging a particularly large hummock into the rough shape of a chair, or a bench, or a small alcove for a child to sleep in, and the moss will accept the shape for a few days and then slowly bulge back into a hummock, and the moss-keeper will shrug, and the moss will not mind being shrugged at, because the moss does not mind being anything.

The moss-keepers move slowly. They speak little. They have warm hands. The other city groups consider them slightly foreign, slightly slow, but they are necessary, because the mosses would take the city entirely if they were not tended, and the mosses taking the city entirely would mean the end of the windows, and the end of the windows would mean the end of the morning check, and the end of the morning check would mean the people could not be sure whether the people across the way were themselves, and the people could not be sure whether the people across the way were themselves, then the people could not be sure whether *they* were themselves, and the city would become, in some slow way, uninhabitable.

The moss-keepers do not think about this. The moss-keepers think about the mosses.

---

The spine-fern wardens are the most physical of the city groups, and the only ones who still sing.

The spine-ferns grow in curving stalks, fifteen, twenty, sometimes forty meters tall, threaded through with metal, with pale silver-green crown-fronds the size of small houses. The wardens climb them, with spiked boots and rope. They shout to each other across the high arches, where the sound carries in the open air between the ferns, and their voices echo in a way that the moss-keepers' voices do not, because the spine-ferns absorb sound and the open air amplifies it, and the wardens have learned to use this, to call across the canopy in voices that are part shout and part song.

They prune the lower fronds. They tie back the stalks that have begun to arch over the canals. They are the most likely of the surface groups to lip-read the bird. They are the most romantic. They are the only ones who still sing, sometimes, in the small hours, in falsetto, songs that nobody else in the city remembers the tunes of, but that the wardens remember, and that they pass down, faintly, to their children, who do not always learn them, but who sometimes, in the small hours, will hum them without words, while they sleep against the warm mosses.

The wardens take it personally when the bird does not return at the agreed-upon time. They are the most likely to send runners along the crease to the other settlements, to ask whether the bird has been seen there, whether the bird is late everywhere or only here. They are the most likely to argue with the lip-readers about what the bird has said when it comes. They are the most likely to climb to the upper overlooks, in the late afternoons, and stand at the rail, and watch the eastern sky, and wait.

---

The lip-readers are the smallest and most contentious of the city groups.

The bird is the size of a small child. It is dark, with a long curved beak. It is silent. It has never been heard to make a sound, although it moves its beak as if it is making sounds, and the people of Lower Verdigre have a tradition of lip-reading the bird, and of arguing about what the bird is saying.

There is a school of thought that says the bird is a linguist, and that the sounds the bird is making are in a language the surface people have not yet learned to hear. There is a school of thought that says the bird is broken, and that the beak is moving but no sound is coming out, and that the people are projecting. There is a school of thought that says the bird is the only honest witness in the city, and is trying to tell the people something the windows will not say.

The three schools have formed a coalition. The coalition is fragile. The lip-readers themselves are a small group; they meet in the upper switchbacks, in a room that was once a reading-room of some kind, with iron-framed windows that look out onto nothing in particular, and they drink a slightly different tea — a tea made from a moss that grows only on the upper cliffs near the ivy where the bird nests — and the tea is faintly sweet, and they argue more.

When the bird returns, the lip-readers are the first to go to it. They stand at a respectful distance. They watch the beak move. They argue, sotto voce, in real time. They write down what they think the bird has said. They go home. They write longer accounts. They publish, sometimes, in small handwritten pamphlets that circulate in the central switchbacks. The pamphlets are read by the other city groups. The pamphlets are argued about by the social drinkers, in the tea-rooms, in the small hours. The pamphlets are the closest thing the city has to literature.

The bird, when it returns, will have a small bright insect in its beak. The insect will be glowing faintly, the color of the cabbage-glow, the color of the lights in the water. The lip-readers will not agree about what this means. None of the schools will be confirmed. The bird will fly away again, eventually, to the upper cliffs, to the ivy, to its nest, and the lip-readers will sigh, and put away their notebooks, and begin the long wait for the next return.

The time of year is *soon*. The time of year is *always soon*.

---

The water-walkers walk the canals at night.

The water is a thin cold stream, a finger's depth, that runs along the old drainage canals. Sometimes, in the night, if you walk along the canals, you will see a light in the water. The light is the color of a bruise. If you reach in, the light will close around your hand, gently, like a glove, and it will feel warm, and you will feel *seen*, and in the morning you will not be quite sure what your hand looked like before you reached in.

No one reaches in anymore. There is a small fine for reaching in. The fine is levied in time.

The water-walkers are mostly young. They wear thick boots and heavy coats. They carry a long pole with a curved iron end, like a shepherd's crook, with which they can stir the water without reaching in. They count the lights. They note the locations. They report back, in the morning, to the engineers, and the engineers nod politely and write things down in ledgers that nobody else ever sees.

The water-walkers have seen, sometimes, things in the water that are not lights. They do not talk about these things, either. They are a quiet group. They have a reputation for being slightly unreal, slightly half-there. They are trusted. They are also slightly feared. They are invited to fewer tea-parties than they would otherwise be, but their reports are read carefully.

The water-walkers come back from the canals in the morning and they sit in the iron-framed tea-rooms and they drink the gray tea and they look out the windows and they do not say what they saw. They have a faint light at the backs of their eyes, sometimes, that the soap-bar and the cabbage-glow and the bruises in the water have left there. The other people notice, and do not mention it.

---

The archeologists are the fewest, and the oldest, and the most resigned.

There are still a few archeologists in Lower Verdigre, although almost no one cares about what they do anymore, including, sometimes, the archeologists themselves.

They are an old couple, mostly. They live in one of the lowest switchbacks, in a small moss-brick house with a single iron-framed window that looks out onto nothing in particular — a stretch of slag, a single curving spine-fern stalk, a small patch of moss on the cliff face, the bruise-colored light on the canal below. The window is honest, as windows are, and what it shows is, in fact, nothing in particular.

They have catalogued five of the nine roads in the road. They know this because they have dug down, in places, with the engineers' permission, and they have found, beneath the topmost road, four more roads, each older than the last, each more degraded, each made of materials that the surface people no longer have names for. They have written these findings down. They have written them down many times. They have rewritten them, when the original documents have been consumed by the moss. They have written them down again. The documents accumulate in their small house. The moss creeps in at the edges and they pull it out and it creeps in again. They have made peace with this.

The archeologists are not on the city council. They are not invited to the tea-parties of the lip-readers, because their findings are not interesting to the lip-readers. They are not consulted by the cabbage-tenders, because the cabbage is not interested in the roads beneath it. They are tolerated by the moss-keepers, who occasionally warn them when the moss is creeping particularly fast in their direction. They are, on the whole, left alone.

The archeologists do not seem to mind. They drink the gray tea in the morning. They look out their honest window. They resume their cataloguing. They will finish the sixth road, perhaps, in their lifetime. The eighth and ninth are very deep. They may not get to them. They have made peace with this, too.

---

The children are the most attuned to the city's strangeness.

The children of Lower Verdigre sleep on the warm mosses, when the children sleep, which is not often. They are pale. They are quick. They have a way of moving through the city's vine-curtains that no adult can replicate, slipping through as if the vines are parting for them, which the vines sometimes are, and which the adults have noted, and the adults do not discuss.

When the children sleep, it is on the mosses, in the small hours when the mosses brighten, and they sleep deeply, and the mosses seem to welcome the sleeping, and the adults, when they pass by, step softly, as if at a funeral, although the children are not dying. They are, perhaps, paying attention.

The children know which vines will let you through. They know which parts of the floor are not floors. They know which of the people across the way are themselves and which are not, and they know before the adults do. The adults check the windows in the morning. The children have already checked, in the night, while the adults slept, and the children have a small system of signals — a knot tied in a vine-curtain, a pebble placed on a windowsill, a faint whistle that the adults cannot hear but the children can — and the children use these signals to warn each other, and sometimes to warn the adults, when the adults are not themselves.

The children will grow up. They will become engineers, or cabbage-tenders, or moss-keepers, or spine-fern wardens, or lip-readers, or water-walkers, or, rarely, archeologists. They will lose their attunement, mostly, although not entirely. They will drink the gray tea in the morning. They will look out the window. They will see whether the people across the way are themselves. They will go about their day.

But a few of them, sometimes, will pause, in the small hours, on the warm mosses, and listen to the city's hum, and they will hear something in it that the adults cannot hear, and they will lie still, and they will not sleep, and the city will continue, and the cabbage will breathe, and the buildings will lie, and the windows, in the morning, will tell the truth.

---

And there are the people at the top of the cliff, who are not the same people who are at the bottom, in the way that a reflection is not the same as the thing reflected.

The cable-rail still runs, although nobody inspects the cable anymore. The engineers say the cable is fine. The engineers are always polite.

The cable-rail carries people up. It carries cabbage, sometimes, in the small hours, in a great wrapped bundle, on the way to some unstated destination. It carries moss-bricks, vine-curtain, slag, the small packages of the parks. It carries water-walkers, who ascend to walk the upper canals. It carries archeologists, who ascend to dig, in the rare places where the digging is allowed. It carries passengers, sometimes, in the small iron cars, who look out the grated windows, and watch the city fall away below them, and see the crease stretching away, and the spine-ferns rising, and the cabbage in the middle, glowing faintly.

The passengers arrive at the top. They step out.

The people at the top of the cliff face are not the same people who are at the bottom. They look like the people at the bottom. They speak, mostly, the same language. They drink tea, although the tea is a different moss, and a different shade of gray. They have windows. The windows are honest, in the same way. The buildings lie, in the same way. The bird visits them, although the bird visits them at a slightly different time, and this has been the cause of three small wars, fought between settlements along the crease, polite wars, wars that the bird did not participate in.

But the people at the top are not the same people who are at the bottom.

They are quieter. They are paler. They move slightly differently. They have a different relationship to the mosses. The mosses, at the top, are sparser. The vestibules that the mosses make in the lower city do not occur at the top. The cliff face, at the top, is colder. The cabbage plants are smaller, or absent. The spine-ferns grow shorter, at the top, but they are older, and the iron in them is older iron, and the fronds at the top are not pale silver-green but a darker green, almost black, and they do not filter the light so much as drink it.

The people at the top have their own small council. They have their own engineers, who are not the same engineers who are at the bottom, and whose moss-tea is a different moss, and who are polite in a slightly different way, with a slightly different silence.

The people at the top do not, on the whole, like the people at the bottom. The people at the bottom do not, on the whole, like the people at the top. They trade, sometimes, in moss-tea and vine-curtain and engine-parts. They have a shared calendar, based on the return of the bird. They send runners, sometimes, along the crease, between settlements.

The cable-rail still runs. People up at the top do not, on the whole, come down to the bottom. People down at the bottom do not, on the whole, go up to the top. The cable-rail runs, mostly, empty.

The engineers do not say why.

---

The people have been changed by the place, in ways they know and do not know.

The damp has changed their lungs. The constant twilight has changed their eyes, which are slightly luminous. The constant low-grade weirdness has changed their sense of what is normal. They have made accommodations. They have made them so long that they have forgotten that they are accommodations.

The constant exposure to the mosses has warmed their hands. The constant exposure to the spine-ferns has taught them to lean. The constant exposure to the cabbage has given them, in some cases, a faint violet tint at the creases of their hands. The constant exposure to the windows has taught them to see. The constant exposure to the engines has taught them to hum, in their sleep, in a low subsonic note, the note of the underthing, which the engineers will not tell you.

They have a particular attention. A sideways patience. They have learned to listen for the small changes in the hum, and the small changes in the cabbage-glow, and the small changes in the vine-curtains, and the small changes in the mosses, and the small changes in the windows. They have learned to live with the strange. They have made the strange their home.

---

To live in Lower Verdigre is to live in the daily medium of the strange.

It is to wake in the small hours, when the mosses shift, and listen. It is to drink the gray tea in the morning, and look out the honest window, and see whether the people across the way are themselves. It is to go about the day — to tend the cabbage, or to keep the moss from the cabbage, or to keep the spine-ferns from both, or to descend to the engines in the underthing, or to walk the canals at night, or to read the silent beak of the bird, or to dig, in the rare permitted places, for the older roads beneath the road.

It is to be attended, on most days, by the strangeness. It is to make small talk in a voice that will not disturb the mosses. It is to consult the window before crossing the hallway. It is to remember, sometimes, that there is a time of year that is soon, and that the bird will return, and that the bird will have a small bright insect in its beak, and that the lip-readers will not agree about what the bird has said.

It is to stand, sometimes, in the central plaza, against the cabbage, and feel it breathing, and feel the soft bruised violet glow, and wonder, idly, what the cabbage is for. It is to walk, sometimes, along the canals, in the night, and see the bruise-colored lights in the water, and not reach in. It is to ride, sometimes, the cable-rail up the cliff, and arrive at the top, and be a slightly different person, in a way that the engineers will not tell you.

It is to be present, the way a ruin is present. It is to have work. It is to have tea. It is to have the windows, and the bird, and the cabbage, and the engines, and the mosses, and the small fine for reaching in, levied in time. It is to get up, and drink the gray tea, and look out the window, and see what is actually there.

The buildings, all around you, are lying.

The cabbage is breathing.

The bird, you think, is trying to say something.

You, also, are trying to say something. The window does not know what. The moss does not know what. The engineers, with their warm handshakes and their gray tea, do not know what. The bird, with its silent beak, may know, and may not say, and may return, soon, with a small bright insect in its beak, and you will stand at a respectful distance, and watch, and try to read.

The lip-readers will not agree.

You will go home. You will look out the window. You will see whether the people across the way are themselves.

They are, today.

You may begin.
