What lives here lives in increments.

I want to begin small. I want to begin with the lichen, because the lichen is where the rest begins, if there is a rest, if there is a beginning. The lichen is the color of old bruises and the color of new pennies, depending on its mood, depending on its age, depending on the angle of the dim light that filters down through the thin air and lands on it already tired. It spreads across the basalt in plates, in crusts, in slow rivers that branch and rebranch and do not hurry. It is not a plant. It is a negotiation. Two partners — one who can eat light, one who can eat stone — have agreed to be a single thing for a while, and the agreement has held for so long that the partners no longer remember they are partners. They remember only that they are here.

The lichen does not move. But the lichen changes. It advances into a fracture in the rock over the course of a season, and the fracture darkens with it, and the darkening is a kind of motion, the way a tide is motion. It retreats from another fracture, dries, lifts at the edges, peels away in flakes that curl like fingernails, and the crack it leaves is clean and bare for a while, and then another lichen moves into the vacancy. The map of the lichen is rewritten over decades. Whoever reads the map at any given moment has read a single page of a very long book.

The lichen does not care about the beetles. Or the lichen cannot afford to care about the beetles. Or the relationship is older than care.

---

The beetles are next. The beetles are the part that does not fit, and I will spend the most time on them, because they are the part that made me understand I was wrong about what kind of place this was.

I called them beetles when I began, and I will continue to call them beetles, but the word is a loan, a placeholder, the kind of word you use when the language you brought with you does not have the right one. A beetle, properly, is a thing with six legs and a hard back and a habit of falling over when you startle it. These things have six legs, or what look like six legs, and they have a hard back, but they do not startle. They do not startle because they do not appear to know that you are there.

They are the color of the rock. This is the first thing to say and the thing that needs to be said most loudly. Their carap is black, iron-black, the grey-black of basalt that has weathered for a long time, and when they are still — and they are still for hours at a stretch, for days — they are a sentence written in the same ink as the page. You can be standing in a field of them and see only rock. You can look down at your feet and see only rock. The fact of their presence is something you have to be told, or something you have to deduce, or something you have to wait for.

They wait for the sun.

The sun here does what the sun does everywhere, but it does it slower, and more reluctantly, and through more atmosphere than the atmosphere seems to want to give up. The disc is featureless. It is the color of a copper coin held over a candle. It does not warm. It illuminates. The distinction matters. The light that arrives on the shelf is light that has been argued with all the way down through the air, and the air has won most of the arguments. What reaches the basalt is a thin, exhausted illumination, the kind of light that does not cast shadows but only softens them, blurs them, suggests them. The beetles, when the light touches them, do not move. They warm. Their carap, which is also iron-black, takes the thin light and conducts it down through the layers of their bodies, down to the place where the lichen lives in their gut, in their second self, in the partner that makes them what they are.

I am telling you something I do not know. I am telling you what it looks like, and what it looks like is a transaction. I see them in the thin light and I see them warm, and I see them, hours later, begin to move, and the moving is the conversion. They have taken the light. They are now spending it.

They walk slowly. The largest of them — and there is a range, a graduated range, from a thumbnail to a hand to a forearm, and the gradation is not random, the gradation is a caste, or a station, or a phase, and I cannot tell which — the largest of them walk at a speed that a thoughtful glacier would consider leisurely. They do not appear to have destinations. They walk, and the walking changes them, and the changes accumulate, and at some point — at some point I do not see, because I am only here for an afternoon, because I am only here for no duration at all — a large one becomes a smaller one, or stops, and the lichen, somewhere, advances into a new fracture.

There are sounds. I have described them. The ticks, faint and dry, when you put your ear to the stone. The hum, lower, felt in the feet before heard with the ears, in the places where the beetles are densest. These are not two sounds. These are the same sound heard from different proximities, and the sound is the sound of many small hard feet on cold stone, and the hum is the resonance of the stone under the load. The stone is listening too.

---

There is something else that lives here, and I did not see it, and I am not sure it lives here in the sense I mean.

The hum is the closest I came.

The hum is not mechanical. It is not biological, not exactly. It has a tonal quality, a pitched quality, as if the rock itself were a string that had been struck a long time ago and was still ringing down. It is not everywhere. It is in pockets. It is in the pockets where the beetles are densest and the lichen is thickest and the rock, if you look, has a pattern of fine scratches on its surface, a pattern that is not random, a pattern that is older than the lichen, older than the beetles, older than whatever I mean by old.

I do not know what makes the hum. I have considered the beetles, and I do not think it is the beetles, or not only the beetles. I have considered the rock, and the rock here is basalt, which is a volcanic rock, which is a rock that has been melted once and will remember the melting for as long as it is rock, and the rock is humming, I think, because the rock is remembering. I do not know if this is a thing rocks do.

It is possible the rock is not humming. It is possible the rock is being hummed at. It is possible that the scratches on the surface are not scratches but are the mouths of small channels, and the channels carry something — water, air, vibration, the taste of metal in the thin air — and the channels were not made by anything, or were made by the long slow work of the beetles and the lichen working together, working at the same problem, working on the same patient sentence, and the sentence has been going on for longer than I can document.

I am telling you what I do not know. I am telling you what it sounds like when you wait for someone to finish a sentence they began in a long time ago and will not finish in any time you can be here for.

---

There are the forked stalks. I have not said enough about them. They are not numerous. You can walk for an hour on the basalt and see three, or see one, or see none, and the hour is a long hour, and the walking is slow walking, because the air is thin and the light is dim and the rock does not clarify itself. When you find one, it is hard to know what you have found.

It has a stalk, gray, thick, almost woody, that rises from a fissure in the basalt, or from the lip of a shallow depression, or from a place where the lichen has retreated and left a bare patch. The stalk is six inches to a foot tall. It forks at the top into something that is three to five lobes, depending on the individual, and the lobes are arranged like the fingers of a small awkward hand, or like the petals of a flower that has given up on petals, or like the tines of an antenna that has given up on signals.

It does not move.

This is not the same as the beetles' stillness. The beetles are still because they are waiting to move. The stalks are still because they have decided, on balance, not to. They have consulted the conditions. The conditions are: thin air, intermittent water, thin soil if any soil at all, light that does not warm, wind that does not howl, a place that is patient beyond any patience a traveler would recognize as patience. The conditions have been answered. The answer is the stalks. The answer is: we will continue.

I do not know what they are. I have considered lichen, and they are not lichen, or they are lichen that has gone further than lichen has gone here before. I have considered the beetles, which is also foolish. I have considered a third thing, a thing that is neither lichen nor beetle but a thing that has its own dignity and its own slowness and its own decision to continue, and I think this is closest. I think the stalks are a third thing. I think the shelf has room for a third thing.

The stalks do not appear to feed. They do not appear to be fed. They rise from the basalt and they fork, and the forking is the whole of their ambition. They stand in the thin light and they fork, and the forking does not change, or changes so slowly that a single afternoon cannot measure the change. They are, I think, the part of the place that is not converting anything into anything else. They are the part that is just here.

---

What I have not been able to describe is the sense the place gives of being unfinished.

This is not a thing. This is not a feature of the place. This is a thing my apparatus makes of the place, and I do not trust it, and I am including it anyway because the alternative is to leave out the part that is most true.

The shelf is not finished. The shelf is still becoming. The lichen is still mapping. The beetles are still walking. The stalks are still forking. The hum is still humming. The dust is still migrating. The water is still gathering in pockets and disappearing from pockets. The scratches are still being made, or are no longer being made, or were made a long time ago by something that is no longer here, or that is still here in a form my apparatus cannot detect.

There is a sense in which the shelf is being written. Not by anything. Not by anyone. By the place itself, in the slow patience of its own processes, in the long accumulation of small events that do not add up to a story but add up to a place. The lichen is one letter. The beetles are another letter. The stalks are another letter. The hum is another letter. The scratches are another letter. The place is being spelled, and the word it is spelling is a long word, and the word has been spelling itself for longer than I can document, and will not finish in any time I can be here for.

I have been here for an afternoon. I have been here for no duration at all. I have read a single page of a very long book and I have not understood what I read.

---

There is one more thing, and then I will leave it to them.

The light at night.

The stars are the color of deep amber. This is not the color stars should be. Stars should be white, or blue-white, or the pale yellow of a candle, and amber is none of these, and the amber is everywhere, and the amber is steady, and the stars do not twinkle, because there is not enough air up here to make them twinkle, and what is left is the steady amber burn of things that have been burning for a very long time and have not yet decided to stop.

The darkness under the stars is not the darkness I am used to. It is the darkness of a place that has been inhabited for a long time, the darkness of a shelf where things have been walking for a long time, the darkness that is the underside of a place that is still being written. In this darkness the beetles are walking. In this darkness the lichen is mapping. In this darkness the stalks are forking, slowly, in the wind that does not howl, in the dust that does not settle.

In this darkness the hum is felt, in the soles of the feet, in the bones of the ear, in the place where the apparatus of translation lives.

I have not translated anything. I have been here for an afternoon. I have read a page. I have not understood the page.

But I have heard the hum. And the hum is not a sound the beetles make. The hum is the place, humming to itself, the way a place hums when it has been alive for a long time and is still becoming whatever it is going to become.

I will leave it to them. The beetles, the lichen, the stalks, the rock, the hum. They have their own concerns. They have been spelling a long word for a long time. They will continue to spell it after I leave.

I will leave it to them.
