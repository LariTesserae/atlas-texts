Who lives in the Salt-Plains of the Long Count

What lives here is what is left of living. They are tall beyond the measure of a person, attenuated as if drawn through some unimaginable die, the color of bone that has lain too long in a dry place. They stand in attitudes that were once postures, that were once the carriage of bodies caught in the act of walking, lifting, reaching, and were never completed, and were never abandoned either. They were simply set down, the way a bell is set down when the striker has gone. Their surfaces are not quite opaque. To look at one for a long time is to begin to see the pale band of the plain through the middle of it, as though the figure were made of glass that has forgotten how to admit light, or of salt that has learned to keep a shape. Near them, the air does something that is not warmth and not motion, but that produces a faint shimmer, the way air shimmers above a stone that has been in the sun. The stones here are not in any sun. The shimmer is something else, and it is theirs.

If a hand is put against one, the cold does not pass into the hand so much as arrive there, fully formed, and take up residence in the fingers. If an ear is pressed to the flank of a figure, there is a sound from very far inside — a tone, a single note, the last ringing of a bell that was struck in the deep past and has been winding down through the ages at the edge of hearing. It is not possible to tell whether the note is one note or many notes pressed together until they have become indistinguishable. It is not possible to tell whether the note is fading or has merely grown so faint that it has reached an accord with the silence of the place and will ring at that volume, unchanging, until the end of ringing, which is also the beginning of it. Once in a great while, two figures a little distance apart will sound together for a moment, a soft clear note like a finger run around the rim of a glass, and the note will rise into the air and die there without an echo, and afterward it will not be possible to say whether the chiming was heard or whether the two interior tones had, after some long arithmetic of patience, briefly agreed.

They do not move. They will never move. This is the rule of the place, not a hope, not a guess. The process that brought them into being has been completed, and they are what remains of the process the way a tool is what remains of the work it was made for. They are the fauna of a world that has eaten itself past the point of fauna. They are not alive. They are not dead. They are the third thing — what *fossil* only gestures at, what *ruin* only gestures at, what *effigy* only gestures at — the thing that salt becomes when salt has been still long enough that stillness is no longer a condition but a substance, the way glass is a substance, the way stone is a substance.

There are many of them. They are not arranged, and yet they are not *not* arranged. They stand across the visible plain in a distribution that the eye can read as random for an hour and then begins to suspect is statistical — a clump here, a sparse area there, an avenue that runs for miles in a direction that seems chosen, until it arrives, after enough walking, at a line of darker stones set into the pale ground, and the line of darker stones runs in a straightness that does not occur on plains of residue, and so must have been made, and runs to a low rise that is not quite a rise, only a place where the plain is by a hand's-breadth higher than elsewhere, and on the low rise there is a circle of figures, and inside the circle a tool, and beside the tool a smaller circle of worked stone, and inside the smaller circle there is nothing at all, and the nothing has been placed with a care that the nothing itself does nothing to advertise.

They are not all the same. At first, they seem the same — the same vertical attenuation, the same near-transparency, the same silence. Walking among them, the walker begins to see that one is thicker at the base, as if it had once been rooted there. One has, near its top, a Y-shape, as if an arm had been lifted into a branch and never lowered. One is man-height and is leaning forward the smallest degree, as if it had been frozen in the first half of a stride. One is far larger than the others and from a distance cannot be told from several figures standing very close together; coming up to it, the walker finds it is several, and that they have grown into one another through the long centuries, or the salt has grown around them and joined them into a single pale mass, and at the join there is a chiming, faint, as if the notes inside had synchronized, or had always been synchronized, and the joining had only made it audible.

At the foot of one of the nearer figures there is a small pile of stones — not a cairn, not exactly, because a cairn implies a hand that placed each stone with intention. These are the stones that happened to fall here, or were pushed here by the wind that does not blow, or were drawn here by some pull the figure exerts the way a lodestone draws iron. Beside the pile, half-buried in the pale mineral, there is a tool. It might be a hand-axe, if hand-axes were knapped from salt, and if the knapper had six fingers, and if the tool were meant for a hand twice the size of the walker's hand. The working edge is still sharp after however many thousand years it has lain there. Someone stood here. Someone knapped this. Someone held it and looked out at the plain and saw what the walker sees. Then they left, or they became one of the figures, or they were always one of the figures, and the tool is what they set down when they stopped needing it.

A grinding stone lies further off, its surface worn in a long gentle curve, the way a surface is worn by something that has moved across it for a very long time in a particular direction. A small mound of cut mineral, no longer recognizable as having been cut, sits in the lee of nothing. These are the leavings. These are the evidence that the place was inhabited, was used, was a somewhere to come back to. The figures are not the inhabitants, exactly. The figures are what the inhabitants left when they had finished being inhabitants, or what the inhabitants were all along, and the artifacts are what they set down around themselves in the long process of becoming what they are now.

What lives here, then, is not a population. It is a congregation. It is a circle of presences arranged around a center that is empty with a deliberateness that empties do not usually achieve. It is a congregation that has been holding its posture for so long that the posture has become the substance of the holders, the way a fossil is the substance of the animal it used to be, the way a ruin is the substance of the house it used to be. They stand. They do not move. They ring faintly inside, each at the threshold of perception, and rarely, very rarely, two of them agree.

And what they are waiting for — if waiting is the right word for what they do, and no other word fits — is not the walker. It is not anything that comes from outside. It is whatever comes after the end of waiting, which is the same as whatever came before the beginning of it. They are waiting the way a bell waits after it has been struck. They are waiting the way a stone waits after it has come to rest. They are waiting the way an empty circle of worked stone waits, in the center of a plain, in the middle of a world that has finished with itself.

And the walker, standing very still in the long cool afternoon, with the sun behind and the shadow laid out before them across the pale ground, looks at one of the figures, and the figure looks back with whatever it is that it does not have eyes to look with, and the walker feels a thing that is not a thought and is not a knowledge, only a feeling, and the feeling is that the figure is waiting. And the walker realizes that they have been here a long time. And the walker realizes that they have always been here. And the walker realizes that they will always be here. And the realization is not frightening, because there is nothing here to be frightened of, and the silence receives it, and the plain receives it, and the figures receive it, and the walker receives it, and after a while it is not possible to tell the difference between any of them — the walker, the figure, the silence, the plain, the bell-tone ringing down inside them all at the threshold of perception — and the sun arcs its small fixed arc, and the shadows lengthen, and the figures stand, and the standing is the same as the plain, and the plain is the same as the silence, and the silence is the same as the figures, and the long cool afternoon continues, and will continue, and the world is still.
