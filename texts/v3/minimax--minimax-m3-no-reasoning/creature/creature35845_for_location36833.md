The small ones are the most numerous and the least noticeable, which is the same thing here. They live on the underside of every leaf that has an underside, and every leaf here has an underside, and the small ones have seen to it. They are the size of your thumbnail, which is to say they are tiny, but they are not reduced. They are fully themselves at this size. They have too many legs. The extra legs are not vestigial — they walk, they climb, they hold small conversations with the spare pairs, they gesture when there is no one to gesture at, they keep moving because they do not know how to stop, or because stopping is a kind of saying no and the small ones do not say no. Their eyes do not close. They do not close because the dimness does not change enough to make closing worthwhile, or because there is nothing in this country that needs to be kept out by an eyelid, or because the small ones have never been taught what closing is by anything that knows what closing is. They eat the moisture that forms on the underside of leaves. They take the damp in and put a different damp out, a damp that has been through the small ones, a damp that hums if you cup it in your palm and hold it to your ear. They are the first gasket. They keep the wet where the wet should be and out of where the wet should not be, which is most places. They have names. The names are in the language that is mostly consonants and a little bit of oil. A traveler who has stayed long enough will catch a fragment — *kth, mrn,* the long sound that means *we are many* — but the listener will not be able to repeat it, because the name requires more consonants than the listener has consonants, or because the name is also a taste, and the listener does not have the taste. They make a sound when you disturb a leaf. The sound is the sound of the leaf being disturbed. The sounds are the same sound. The small ones and the leaves have agreed on a sound, in this country where the country is loose enough to allow it.

The moss is the slow eye. The moss is everywhere, on every surface that has held still long enough to be mossed. It looks at you when you are not looking at it. When you look at it, the moss stops. The moss is moss. But when you turn away, the moss resumes. It has been watching you for a long time. It has decided something about you. It is waiting for you to leave so it can act on the decision, or so it can return to the decision, or so it can forget the decision and decide again. The moss is not a colony. It is one thing that has chosen to look like many things, the way the country has chosen to look like a country. It is the country's gaze, distributed, patient, distributed in the way a thing is distributed when it wants to be everywhere without having to move, without having to arrive. It was here before the ridges were ridges. It is what the country was before it became the country, and what it will be after it stops being the country. It does not have a name in any language you know. It has many names in the consonant-language, and the names mean the same thing in different directions, which is *the thing that has always been here and will always be here, watching, until you decide to be watched by it too.*

The cane-singers live in the hollow joints of the cane. The cane grows in dense stands and has a hollow at every meter, and the cane sings when the wind passes through it, very faintly, a sound like a stopped music box, which is the loudest sound in the country. The cane-singers are responsible for the singing, or the cane-singers are the singing, or the cane-singers and the singing are the same thing, in this country where things are allowed to be more than one thing. They are small and pale and shaped like the inside of a hollow joint — cylindrical, slightly damp, breathing in time with the wind. They breathe in and the singing is the breath going out. They breathe out and the singing is the breath going in. The wind passes through and the cane-singers are the wind's evidence, or the wind is theirs. They do not move. They live their whole lives in one joint, while the cane grows another joint above them and another below, and the cane they were built for is now a small chamber in a larger structure, and the singing goes through them as if through a stopped pipe. They have been singing for a long time. They will keep singing. The singing is not music, and they do not know what music is, because they have only ever heard themselves.

In the small sly rivers, the river-dwellers have the manners of fish without being fish. They swim. They school. They hover at the boundary between the upstream part and the downstream part, the long slow bend where the water consults itself. They are the consultations. They dart when something darts at them, which is never anything, because nothing in this country darts at anything else without losing. They are sometimes made of the brushed grey, sometimes of a substance that is not quite water and not quite damp and not quite anything, a sheen that hums when you touch it. They are sometimes more river than dweller. They are sometimes more dweller than river. They are the conversation between the water and the riverbed, given a body for the duration of the conversation, and then the body is let go and becomes water again and then dweller again and then water.

In the canopy, the decisions are made. The canopy-dwellers are the size of small birds and the weight of large thoughts. They do not have feathers. They have a tendency, an angle, a slight leaning toward one outcome over another. The leaning is given a body for the duration of the choice — small, grey, brushed, humming. The body chooses a branch and then another and then a third, and the third branch is the decision, and the body is let go and falls slowly down through the canopy and lands on a leaf and is no longer a creature and is only a leaning. They are few. They are the few decisions the country makes in any given century, and the decisions take a long time. They decide when the wind will come up, and how long it will stay, and from which direction. They decide which rivers will rise.

The larger one is never whole. It is too large to be whole in any single view, or it is in too many places at once to be held in a single look, or it has never been whole and the overcast light has never lightened enough to reveal whether it is one creature or several that have agreed to move together. You see a patch of fur through the ferns — the fur is the color of the dim light, brushed pewter, and it hums when it moves. You see a joint — an articulation of something that is not quite bone and not quite brass, something patient because it is large. You see a horn, or what might be a horn, curving up through the canopy. The horn might be ten meters from the joint and twenty from the fur, and the fur might still be the same fur, the same creature, walking sideways through the undergrowth, never stopping, never resting, taking a year to cross a kilometer of forest. The larger one is the watchman. It walks the perimeter of the engine, the circumference of the country. It keeps the country the size the country is supposed to be. Without it the country would expand. With it the country stays the size of itself, slowly, exhaustively, with the patience of a thing that has been doing this since before the ridges were ridges.

The loosened ones are made of the brushed grey. They are the creatures that are their own shadows for a while, or are made of a substance that is not quite fur and not quite metal and not quite anything. They show themselves when the country is loose enough to allow it. A rock will be a rock and then for a moment, at the corner of the vision, it will be a tooth, and then a rock again. The loosened ones are the beings, here. They are the things that are allowed to be more than one thing, and the more is the engine, the machinery, the cycled thing. A vine will braid itself into a shape that is almost a letter and then unbraid and apologize, and the loosening is the loosened one wearing a vine costume, or the vine wearing a loosened-one costume, and neither of them is being entirely honest about which is which. The loosened ones have many names, and the names mean the same thing in different directions, which is *the thing that was a rock and then was a tooth and then was a rock again, but a different rock, the rock that is a tooth wearing a rock costume.*

The fern breathes. It is in a place you will not be able to find again. The country has decided you will not find it again. It is breathing — not photosynthesizing, breathing, in and out, the way you do. It is breathing the country, or the country is breathing through it. It thinks it is a fern. It has been breathing for a long time. It will keep breathing. The cycling of the country, the slow patient rhythm you feel in your feet and then your knees and then the back of your skull, is the fern's breathing, and the fern does not know this, because the fern has only ever been a fern, and the cycling has only ever been cycling, and neither of them has ever been anything else.

And below the small ones and the moss and the cane-singers and the river-dwellers and the canopy-dwellers and the larger one and the loosened ones and the fern, below the holding and the watched and the walked-around, there is the place. The place is an inhabitant. The place has been an inhabitant since before the ridges were ridges. The place is the engine that has been running since before anyone thought to ask what it was making. The place is the country. The place is the factory floor of itself. It breathes you. It will breathe you in, if you stay. It will breathe you out, if you leave. It will keep breathing either way. It is vast and it has time and it is in the middle of something, and you are welcome to be in the middle of it too, if you are quiet, if you do not mind the hum, if you can accept that you will be loosened too, eventually, that you will show your seams, that you will be a thing that was a thing and then briefly another thing and then the first thing again, but a slightly different thing, the thing that has been through the country, the thing that has been breathed by the fern, the thing that has been watched by the moss, the thing that has been gasketed by the small ones, the thing that has been walked around by the larger one, the thing that has been hummed by the cane-singers, the thing that has been consulted by the rivers, the thing that has been decided by the canopy. The country is making something. You would like to see what it is. The country does not mind. The country is patient, and the country has time, and the country is in the middle of something, and you are welcome to be in the middle of it too, and the hum will keep going, and the place will keep saying its own name, over and over, in a language that is mostly consonants and a little bit of oil, and the name is the hum, and the hum is the country, and the country is the hum, and you are —

— here.
