# Who Lives in the Condensery

## The Distillers

The guild of distillers has not, properly speaking, existed as a discrete body for two hundred years. They dissolved themselves by slow consensus in the third century of the place, when it became clear that everyone was a distiller, that the work had leaked into the work, and that the category no longer meant anything except in the ledgers. But the ledgers persist, in the deep archive rooms on the seventh sub-level, and they are kept by clerks who call themselves distillers' clerks, and so the thing continues under the name of the thing.

A distiller today is a person of perhaps forty who has done the same work since they were fifteen, and who will do it until they cannot. Their hands are the brown of the rust-plaster, the cream of the boiled condensate, the green-black of the leaf-stain that never quite comes out. They wear canvas aprons that have been patched so many times the patches have patches, and the patches are the same color as the place, and so a working distiller is, in the half-light, almost invisible. You see the swing of the arm. You see the dip of the dipper. You do not quite see the person.

They are not unhappy. The question of happiness does not arise. They are *of* the place. They have been composted into it by repetition. To ask whether they would prefer another life is to ask whether the floor would prefer to be a ceiling. They are doing the thing, and the thing is them.

They do not, as a rule, leave the Condensery. There are stories of a few who have—distillers who climbed the eight thousand stairs to the surface and stood in actual sun and found it garish and harsh and full of animals, and who came back down within the hour, weeping for the dim. These stories are considered slightly embarrassing, the way any exile's homecoming is embarrassing.

## The Boiler-Keepers

The boilers are tended by a small caste of workers who, by tradition, marry late and have few children and die in the harness. This is not because the work is dangerous, though it can be—a pressure-valve in the wrong mood will take a hand. It is because the boilers have moods, and the moods must be known, and a boiler's mood is known only by long acquaintance, and a long acquaintance is broken if the keeper is replaced.

A boiler-keeper can tell you, by listening, whether a vessel is properly charged. They can tell you, by smelling the steam, whether the wood-flour in the feed has gone off. They can tell you, by putting a hand on the iron, where the rust has thinned to a danger. They are the closest thing the Condensery has to doctors, and they have a doctor's quiet authority and a doctor's habit of grave looks.

They are paid in distillate, which is to say they are paid in the thing itself, which is to say they are paid in the city. They drink from the vats in the morning and smell of the vats in the evening and at night they sleep in bunks in the boiler-rooms and dream, the older ones say, of pressures and temperatures and the proper weeping of iron.

## The Lighting Guild

In the early centuries, the mosses were wild bioluminescents that had wandered in from some deeper cave system and colonized the floors. Then a guild was formed to tend them, to seed them, to extend them. The Lighting Guild, they are called, though they are not a guild anymore in any formal sense. They are a handful of workers who go about with long-handled brushes and pots of spore-slurry, painting the moss into new corners, new interstices, new crevices where the dim needs deepening.

They are quiet people. They work alone, mostly. They know every passage of the Condensery, including the ones that are not on any map, including the ones that are not really passages but residual spaces left over from some older geometry. They are the only people who move through the city without seeming to be of it; they look, in the green-gold dim, like ghosts painting other ghosts into being.

They speak, when they speak, in a kind of murmur. They have learned not to startle the moss. They have learned, some of them, to sleep in the moss-light, and to find it sufficient.

## The Children

Children are born in the Condensery, in birthing rooms in the residential levels that smell of warm peat and boiling sugar-water. They are born small and red and squalling, and they are taken, by tradition, on a tour of the great vats within their first week, so that the vats will know them and they will know the vats, and this is considered a kind of baptism, though the guild that once performed baptisms is long gone.

The children grow up in the narrow ways. They play games that involve the boiler-pipes, that involve the drip-channels, that involve, in the upper levels where the ceiling is lowest, the leaves themselves, which they pull down and wear as crowns and which their parents later find, dried and brown, in the bunks. They are startlingly silent children. They learn early that the place has ears, and that the place does not like noise for its own sake, and that the proper noise is the noise of work. So they play at work. They play at boiling. They play at distilling. Their pretend is indistinguishable from the real, and this is considered a sign of a healthy upbringing.

By the time they are fifteen, most of them have decided what they will do. The decision is, more often than not, to do what their parents did. The Condensery is not a place for reinvention. It is a place for the patient continuation of things. A child who wanted to be something new would not know what to be new of, and so they do not want it, and so the thing continues.

## The Archivists

In the deep levels, below the boiler-rooms, below the vats, below the residential passages, there is a level where the floor is older and harder and the light is dimmer and the smell is older. This is where the archive is.

The archivists are an unsociable lot. They live in the archive. They sleep in the archive. They eat in the archive, brought food by runners who do not linger. They do not, as a rule, come up to the working city, and when they do, they wear veils over their faces because the new light is too much for them.

Their work is to copy the old ledgers, to repair the old ledgers, to add to the ledgers, to bind the new ledgers in the old style. The ledgers record every boiling since the founding—every charge, every release, every pressure, every distillate-yield. The archive is, in some sense, the real product of the Condensery. The drink that comes out of the vats is only the byproduct. The real product is the record.

The archivists are the only people in the Condensery who are, in any meaningful sense, alone. They have each other, of course. But they have chosen a solitude that the rest of the city has not chosen, and they are respected for it in the distant way that very old priests are respected by congregations that no longer believe.

## The Elders

There are always a few elders in the Condensery, though the place does not, as a rule, hold its old. The work is not kind to old bodies. The boilers do not slow down for anyone. The dim light plays havoc with eyes. The damp plays havoc with lungs. Most workers retire, in the sense that they stop working at sixty, and most die within five years of retiring. The body, no longer composted by labor, simply composts itself.

But a few last longer. A few last a long time. There is a woman on the fourth sub-level who is, by the ledgers, ninety-three, and who comes up every morning to the great vats and watches them, and who is consulted, sometimes, by the boiler-keepers, because she has seen more boilings than anyone alive and because her memory, the keepers say, is the memory of the vats themselves.

She does not speak much. When she does, it is in a voice that sounds, in the green-gold dim, like the place is saying it through her.

## The Visitors

There are always a few visitors in the Condensery, at any given time. Traders from the surface, come to barter for the distillate, which is prized above all other drinks because of its strange sweetness, its almost-food smell, its rumored restorative properties. Scholars from the surface universities, come to study the bioluminescent mosses, the symbiotic architecture, the patient rust. Curious travelers, come to see the place that is named for what falls from its own roof.

The visitors are tolerated. They are given narrow bunks and meals of boiled root and bean. They are shown the great vats and the boilers and, if they are well-behaved, the upper canopy where the trunks run horizontal and the leaf-rosettes hang in the dim. They are not, as a rule, shown the archive.

The visitors last a few weeks and then leave. They leave with the smell of the place still in their clothes, and they go up the eight thousand stairs and out into the sun, and they stand blinking in the harsh light, and they find that the world is full of animals and full of speed and full of new things, and they go home, and they remember the place as a kind of dream they had once, vivid and warm and slow.

They do not come back. Most of them. Some of them come back. The ones who come back do not, afterward, leave again.

## The Place Itself

The Condensery is not, strictly speaking, a place where people live. It is a place that lives, and that people are part of, and that would, if the people left, continue to live, in its own slow way, for a thousand years more.

The people are the condensate of the place. They are the slow dripping of the elms, given form and given work and given the long repetition of their own days. They are the rust that binds the joists. They are the moss that lights the floor. They are of the place, and the place is of them, and the two are not, and have never been, separable.
