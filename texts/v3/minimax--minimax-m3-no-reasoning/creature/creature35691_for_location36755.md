# Inhabitants of the Sinkhole Nursery

There is a census, if you take one. It is being taken continuously, by no one in particular, and it does not stay accurate.

## The First Citizens

At the spring, on the damp clay, in the permanent stain where water has been falling for twenty months, there is a film. It is not a film a person would notice as a thing. It is a film that has noticed itself as a place.

Diatoms live here. They are the color of weak tea. They have been building their glass houses out of silica dissolved in the seep, and the houses are ornamented — every species with its own baroque geometry, its own pores and striae, its own private lace. They drift in the thin film of water or cling to the clay face where it is wettest. They have been here, in some form, since before the hole was a hole. They were already in the chalk, in the marl, in the aquifer, waiting. When the collapse opened a mouth to the sky, they simply climbed into the light and went on making their impossible small architecture. They do not know they are first. They do not know there was a *before*.

Beside them, where the stain on the marl shelf goes purple, there are algae. Filamentous ones, mostly, in hair-thin ropes that wave in the slow trickle of the spring like drowned hair. They are the color of a bruise two weeks old. They smell, if you could smell a smell this thin, of green iodine. They are constructing the basis of everything that will come: a thin film of living chemistry that will, given time and a few more collapses, become soil. They are patient in the way that only organisms without opinions can be patient.

Cyanobacteria live between the diatoms and the algae, blue-green and ancient, older than ferns by a margin that makes ferns feel like a rumor. They do not care about the hole. They care about photons and nitrogen and have been doing so since the world was a different kind of place.

These are the tenants on the ground floor. They have no eyes. They have no plans. They are the place's first decision about itself.

## The Mosses, Pale and Lacy

Above the wet zone, where the clay is merely damp, the mosses have come.

They came as spores. Probably on the wind. Probably on a bird's foot — though no bird nests here, and no bird has been here long. More likely, honestly, on the wind, drawn down the column of light the way all light-dwelling things are drawn down. They landed and they decided.

They are not the deep, fat mosses of older worlds. They are thin ones. *Ditrichum*, maybe, or a small *Fissidens*, or something without a name yet because no one has climbed down to name it. They spread in pale networks across the floor near the spring, mint-colored, ghost-colored, the color of a thing trying to remember what green was. They look, from the rim, like rivers drawn in pencil by someone who wasn't sure rivers were allowed.

They are not thriving. They are also not dying. They are *holding*. Each rhizoid is a small rope tied to a small commitment. They are the place's second decision, and the decision is: *I will be wet and I will stay.*

## The Ferns, Chosen and Stubborn

Higher up the walls, on the ledges that catch the moving oval of light as it slides through the day, the ferns.

They are not many. They are *deliberate*. Each one is the result of a spore that fell, or was carried, or blew in on a thermal, and that found a crack it could wedge into and a moisture schedule it could tolerate. The mortality rate was — is — ferocious. Most spores land on bare clay and die. These few have won a long quiet lottery.

The hart's tongue (*Asplenium scolopendrium*, though I would not swear to it — it may be something else, something this continent wasn't supposed to have) has tucked itself into a horizontal crack on the north wall. Its strap-leaves hang down like green tongues, unfurling from fiddleheads the color of cinnamon. It trembles at every air movement. It will tremble for years. It is not bothered by this. A hart's tongue in a sinkhole has no one to perform for.

The woodsia is on a ledge above the spring, silver-haired, its pinnae folded tight against a stem that has thickened with the effort of holding on. It is a small saint. It has decided it lives here and it is not taking questions.

The *Asplenium* on the overhang — the dark one, the one whose fronds read as black against the chalk — is the strangest. It seeded itself into a crack with no visible moisture, on a face that gets only a few minutes of direct light each afternoon. It has, by every fernish logic, no business existing. And yet. And yet. It is the most committed of the three. Its rhizome has burrowed into the marl as if into conviction. It is the place's third decision: *I will have a north face, and something will live on it, and the something will be black.*

These are the punctuation. The bare wall between them is the larger sentence.

## The Insects That Are Not Quite Insects

The light column is full of them. They hover, they fall, they rise. They are the wrong ones.

**The crane flies** are the largest. Three of them, maybe four. Their wings are too iridescent — not the dull shimmer of a true *Tipula* but something more deliberate, as if each wing has been dipped in oil that has not decided whether to be oil. Their flight paths are wrong in a way the eye registers before the brain does. A crane fly, when it changes direction, should do so in a long lazy arc. These stop midair. They hesitate at angles that don't exist, as if briefly consulting a flight plan drawn for a different insect, then resume. They do not land. There is nothing here they would land on for long.

**The wasp** is orange and black, a paper-waist color, and it is hunting. It hunts something on the wall that moves like a spider. The something does fold its legs wrong — at the second joint, outward instead of inward, so that its silhouette is briefly a star. The wasp stings at it. The something does not die. The wasp stings again. The something, which I would call a harvestman if I were being generous, climbs a fraction of an inch up the wall and continues moving wrong. They have been at this for some time. Neither of them seems in a hurry.

**The moths** are pale. They are the size of thumbnails. They are drawn upward through the column toward the hole above and then circle back down, unable to find the hole again, as if the exit were a rumor they cannot quite locate. They are numerous. They cast shadows on the clay floor, and the shadows have more legs than the moths. The shadows have four more legs. The moths have not noticed. The shadows do not seem to belong to the moths. They seem to belong to the floor.

**The scarlet tiger moth** is sleeping. It is pinned against the chalk wall, its red wings folded over its back like hands in prayer, and it has been there, by your best estimate, since before you arrived. It is, as far as you can tell, the most ordinary creature in the nursery. Its red is a real red. Its wings are the correct number. Its shadow is, briefly, the correct shape.

**The hoverflies** swarm around it in a thin loose halo. They are wrong in two directions. Some have too many wings — a third wing, smaller, set behind the second, vibrating at a frequency the eye almost sees. Some have no wings at all and remain aloft anyway, riding something that is not air, that is not lift, that you cannot name. They do not seem to mind either arrangement. The winged ones drift. The wingless ones hover. They share the space without comment.

## The Wolf Spider, Ordinary and Keeping Still

At the spring's edge, on the damp clay, there is a wolf spider.

It is small. Its body is the brown of damp bark. Its eight eyes are the correct number, in the correct arrangement, in two rows of four. Its eight legs are its own legs. They bend at the correct joints. They cast the correct shadows. It sits, perfectly still, watching you with the patience of a thing that has decided it is outnumbered.

It is the only ordinary animal here.

It is the only fully rendered thing. You can feel this without being able to explain it. The place around it is *suggesting* — is sampling itself into being — and the spider is *there*. It has come down the wall, perhaps, on a thread, or it has always been in the chalk and has only now emerged into the new room. Either way it has the quietness of a creature that knows it is being watched by a process, not a person. It has decided to be still so that the process will not notice it has finished rendering. It is, in its small ordinary way, the most courageous inhabitant.

It will not move while you watch.

It will not move after.

It has, you suspect, been not moving for some time.

## The Wrongness, Partial and Patient

The wrongness is not a creature. The wrongness is a tenant.

It lives in the edges. In the ledge that is convex when you were sure it was flat. In the fern that shifted while you were not watching. In the moth that has, between one breath and the next, grown a beak. In the shadows with too many legs. In the wasp's endless unsuccessful hunt. In the flight paths that pause at angles geometry has not heard of.

It is not hostile. It is *partial*. It is the residue of a place that has not finished deciding what it is. It is the place being drafted, in real time, by something that is sampling possibilities without committing. A pool of possibilities, condensed, that takes the shape of whatever it lands on for a moment before trying another shape.

It does not have a body. It has a habit.

It is, you begin to suspect, the place itself, in the act of becoming. The sinkhole is not a hole that plants and insects are colonizing. The sinkhole is a *question* that is using plants and insects to ask itself. The wrongness is the question's open mouth.

## The Place, Growing Toward

And the place lives.

It is not, yet, what it will be. It is a draft. It is a thought. It is a thirty-foot room in the prairie, cut into chalk and clay, with a spring and a light and a small courage of mosses and three stubborn ferns and a column of wrong insects and a wolf spider that is keeping very, very still so as not to be redrawn.

It is young. It is two years old. It is growing. The spring will carve deeper. The mosses will thicken. The ferns will seed the floor. The walls will collapse in slow stages, widening the room. The owl will come. The raccoon will come. The pigeon will blunder in. And the wrongness — the half-rendered edges, the shadows that lie, the moths that cannot find the door — will not fade. It will *ripen*. It will become, perhaps, the place's reason for being a place at all: a small pocket in the prairie where reality is being reconsidered, where the draft of a world is being written and rewritten, where a wolf spider has decided to wait it out because the wolf spider is, after all, real.

You climb out. The prairie is loud with crickets.

Behind you, faintly, the spring continues its single-page whisper. The strange moths continue to look for the door they will not find. The ferns continue their slow wager. The wrongness continues its slow drafting. The spider continues not to move.

You mark the place in your mind. You will come back. You do not entirely expect it to be the same place.

You are correct.
