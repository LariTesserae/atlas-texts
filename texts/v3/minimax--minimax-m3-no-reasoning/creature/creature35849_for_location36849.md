# Inhabitants of the Breath-Gorge of Olethreon

The gorge is not empty. It only asks to be read slowly.

---

## The Pale Mountain-Goat, or What the Herders Never Named

They have no common name because no one has ever needed to address them across a pasture fence. The pale goats of the Oleth floor are tall for goats — leggy, narrow-chested, with a deep brisket that seems built for the thin air rather than against it. Their coats are the off-white of weathered limestone, with a faint greenish undertone where the moss-shadows fall across them, and their pelage is not hair so much as a fine, dense, almost down-like fur that sheds mist the way a duck's feathers shed water. They are not white in the clean way of a domestic goat. They are white in the way the gorge's limestone band is white, the way old bone is white. They look carved.

Their eyes are the remarkable feature. Set wide on a long, narrow skull, the eyes are large, pale amber, and they do not face forward the way a predator's do, nor fully to the side the way most ungulates' do. They sit at an oblique angle, as though the animal is in the habit of looking at two things at once. There is a permanent stillness to their gaze that visitors find unsettling, and after a few days, calming. Their pupils, in low light, dilate enormously — almost the whole iris — and this gives them at night a soft, almost ruminative look, as though the gorge has made them gentle by long acquaintance.

They move in small herds of four to seven, but these are loose affiliations. An individual will drift between groups across weeks. There is no dominant male or female that anyone has ever been able to identify. They browse the sedge-mat, but they do not crop it the way a lowland goat crops; they nip, almost delicately, at the tips of the alpine sedges and the dwarf willow, and they eat the inner pulp of the pale fungi when they can reach the lower shelves. They drink from the river where it surfaces, but rarely. They get most of their water from the moss itself, which they know how to compress between their flat back teeth to wring out the moisture.

Their hooves are splayed, almost spatulate, with a soft pad of horn that grips the wet stone silently. They walk the cliff-base ledge — the green tide-mark — as confidently as a lowland goat walks a meadow, and they will cross the dry reaches of the riverbed without hurry. The young, of which there are few, are born in the brief spring melt and can stand within minutes. They are darker than the adults, almost grey, and they do not attain the adult's pallor for nearly a year.

What they are doing, mostly, is being present. They are not skittish. A human walking among them will be watched, and then ignored. They do not bolt unless pressed, and even then they do not run so much as *detach* — one moment present, the next a pale smudge thirty feet up the talus, where they have placed themselves with a sureness that is not quite like climbing. They have, the gorge-dwellers say, an agreement with the place.

## The Dark-Furred Fox, or the Edge-Animal

Smaller than a lowland fox. Built low, long-bodied, with a tail that is not bushy so much as dense, like a bottle-brush that has been trimmed. The fur is dark — charcoal, with a faint blue-slate sheen that is only visible when the fungi-light catches it just so. The legs are short. The ears, however, are absurdly large, cupped forward, and they swivel independently. The face is narrow, almost weasel-like, and the eyes are pale yellow, which in the gorge's dimness means they are the most visible thing about the animal at any distance.

They are solitary, except briefly in the mating season (which is, like everything here, faint and hard to pin down — the gorge's sense of calendar is loose). They are crepuscular in theory and diurnal in practice during the long dim summer, when there is no real night to be crepuscular about. They den in the crevices, often in the company of the small sharp-billed birds, with whom they have an arrangement that neither species has explained to anyone.

What they hunt, mostly, is the stone-cricket, and the pale moths when the moths are on the wing, and the very young of the mountain-goats if they come across one that is unwell. They will also, and this is the strangest thing, hunt *fish* — leaning down to the river at the points where it surfaces, snatching a blind pale thing from the shallows with a quick sideways snap. They eat them whole, and the bones come up later as a small dry pellet, deposited on the moss with what looks almost like tidiness.

They make a sound. It is not the yip of a lowland fox. It is a thin, reedy, two-note call — almost interrogative — that carries in the gorge's still air far out of proportion to its volume. The first note rises; the second falls away into a kind of silent mouth-shape, as though the animal has remembered, mid-vocalization, that noise is not really the point. They will do this at dusk, and the sound will be answered by another fox on the far wall, and then silence, and then a third, somewhere upstream, and the visitor will realize they are being bracketed. Not threatened. Bracketed.

They are not afraid of the cylinders. They will den between two of them, in the warmth, and they will hunt along the river without regard for the hum.

## The Blind Pale Fish, or River-Things

The Oleth's fish are, properly speaking, almost not fish. They are elongated, almost eel-shaped, perhaps eight inches at the long end, and they are the color of river-stone — mottled greys and silvers, with a faint pearlescence along the flanks that shows only when they surface, briefly, and is gone. They have no functional eyes. Where the eyes should be, there are small dimpled pits in the skull, and these pits are lined with tissue that seems to *feel* the water — the pressure, the chemistry, the direction of flow. They taste their way downstream, and they taste their way back. They are, by every account, exactly where they mean to be at all times.

They are not many. The river is cold and not rich, and the fish are slow-growing, and they spawn — if they spawn — in a way no one has been able to observe. They eat what the river offers, which is mostly the small invertebrate life of the streambed: tiny shrimp-things, midge larvae, the washed-down bodies of the pale moths. They surface rarely, and when they do, it is a quick gulp of air that may not be strictly necessary but that they take anyway, an old habit, the river-dwellers think, from ancestors who lived in a fuller water.

In the dry reaches, when the river goes underground, the fish are not in the dry reaches. They are wherever the water is, in the under-gorge, in the cracks and the hidden channels, and they wait. They wait very well. When the river returns, after the three days of absence every nineteen years, they are already in the upper pools, as if they had walked there in the dark.

## The Pale Moth, or the Drift of Sparks

They come out at night. In the daytime they rest in the cliff-crevices, folded flat against the stone, wings overlapped, almost invisible. At dusk, when the last of the diffuse light fails, they emerge, and the gorge fills with them.

They are the size of a man's thumb, and they are pale — a translucent ivory, with a faint tracery of darker veins across the wings that, in the fungi-glow, looks almost like the linework on a map. Their bodies are slender, and their wing-beat is not the frantic flutter of a lowland moth. It is a slow, deliberate, almost rowing motion, and the sound of it — when there are many of them, and there are many — is a faint, continuous susurration, the sound the gorge has in the dark. It is the sound of the place breathing out.

They feed on the fungi, or on the moisture that beads on the fungi, or on nothing anyone can determine. They do not, in the conventional sense, pollinate, because nothing in the gorge is conventional. They lay their eggs on the underside of the fungal shelves, and the larvae feed there, and the larvae are small pale grubs that the small sharp-billed birds find and consume in great numbers. The moth is the base of a great deal of the visible life.

They are drawn to warmth. They will settle on a human hand, given a moment, and they do not flee from the cylinder-hum. Some of them spend the day on the cylinders themselves, in the thin warmth the metal gives off, and they emerge at dusk from the dark surface like sparks from a struck flint.

## The Stone-Cricket, or the Subliminal Voice

Smaller than a man's smallest finger-joint. Black, or very dark brown, with a body that looks like a chip of basalt with legs. They live in the crevices, in the cool damp cracks in the wall, and they sing — a faint, high, almost-not-there trill, in pulses, in a register that the human ear notices as a pressure rather than a sound. The song is continuous, day and night, summer and winter, and it is what the gorge is *made of*, acoustically, the way the moss is what it is made of visually.

They eat the lichens, the algae films, the organic matter that leaches down the walls. They are eaten by the dark foxes, by the small sharp-billed birds, by the pale fish when a winter flood washes a crevice clean. They are the most numerous large organism in the gorge, by an order of magnitude, and they are the reason the air is never quite silent.

In winter, their song is the only sound besides the river. It is what you learn to sleep to. By the third night, you do not hear it. By the fifth night, you cannot sleep without it.

## The Small Sharp-Billed Bird, or the Silent One

The only bird. Small, dark, almost black, with a long sharp bill that is slightly down-curved and out of all proportion to the body. The plumage is unremarkable — a sooty brown-black, with a faint green iridescence on the throat that shows only at certain angles. The legs are thin, the feet are strong, the tail is short. They look, on the whole, like a thing that has been pared down to its essential function.

They are insectivores. They hunt the pale moths at dusk, taking them on the wing with a quick snap. They hunt the stone-crickets in the crevices, reaching in with the long bill. They will also take the small grubs of the moth larvae from the fungal shelves. They are quiet. They do not sing. They do not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, call to one another. They move in silence between the rocks, and a flock of them is not a flock — it is a series of individuals who happen to be in the same place.

They nest in the cliff-crevices, often sharing a fissure with a denning fox, and the arrangement is one of the few clear commensalisms in the gorge: the fox leaves scraps; the bird sounds no alarm at intruders. They lay two or three eggs in a year. The young are fed on moths.

They are present in the gorge the way the moss is present: continuously, without drama, as part of the place.

## The Cylinder-Dwellers, or What Lives on the Things

No. There is nothing else.

There are no people. There are no herders. There are no locals to call the river *the Oleth*. There is no one who has come to theorize about the cylinders and gone home to write it down. There are no records. There are no roads.

What there is, is the pale goats, the dark foxes, the blind fish, the pale moths, the stone-crickets, the small sharp-billed birds, the fungi, the moss, the conifers, the river, the cylinders, and you, briefly, with your headache, listening to the hum.

The gorge does not have inhabitants in the way a city has inhabitants. The gorge has *residents* — things that have made an agreement with the place, on the place's terms, and that do not think of themselves as living *in* the gorge so much as *as* the gorge. The moss is the gorge. The crickets are the gorge's voice. The moths are the gorge's sparks. The foxes are the gorge's question. The goats are the gorge's patience. The cylinders are the gorge's hum. The river is the gorge's breath. The fish are the gorge's memory. The birds are the gorge's silence.

And the conifers — the small, white, ancient, inexplicable conifers, placed no one knows by whom, growing two feet in a century, leaning toward the river — the conifers are the gorge's thought, the one it does not say out loud, the one the cylinders do not hum.

You do not want to leave. That is not a sentiment. That is a fact about the place, like the cold, like the green, like the dimness. By the end of the week you understand that you have been *admitted*, briefly, and that you will go, and that the gorge will not notice you have gone, and that this is the kindest thing it could do for you.

The hum continues. The river continues. The moths emerge at dusk. The fox yips, twice, and is answered, and is silent. The cylinders, as always, are warm to the touch.

The breath of the place goes on.
