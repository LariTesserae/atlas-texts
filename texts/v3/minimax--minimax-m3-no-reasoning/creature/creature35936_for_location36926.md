The inhabitants are not separable from the inhabited. That is the first thing to say about them. To live here is to be slowly folded into the city, the way a root is folded into stone, the way moss is folded into a wall. The place has been digesting its people for longer than anyone still living has counted, and what remains are the parts that could not be broken down. The rest has become moss, has become lichen, has become the slow orange growth that clusters near the chimneys and the orange-and-grey of the higher rooftops.

**The workers** are the most obviously human. They are also the most obviously not. They are few. They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sickness — the pallor of things grown underground, in caves, in cisterns, in the long low cellars where the city's pipes run. Their eyes have grown large, or seem to have, the way the eyes of fish grow large in dark water. They can read the smallest shift in the orange smear of a gas lamp three streets away. They find their way by the texture of stone under their fingertips, by the angle at which the water runs down a wall, by the faint mineral smell of damp in a particular corner.

Where their skin shows, it is the color of old granite. The rest is colonist — moss on the backs of their hands, lichen at the hinges of their jaws, the slow orange crust that lives near the vents where the steam comes out. They do not wash it off. They have not washed it off for so long that they no longer think of it as foreign. When they lie down at night, the moss continues to grow on them in the dark. By morning they are slightly more of the city than they were the night before.

They work, the few of them, in the squat dark factories with their yellowed glass. They turn the wheels. They engage the gears. They feed the engines — coal, ore, time itself, whatever it is the city eats. They do not know what is being made. They have stopped asking. The product is collected by someone, or something, and removed to a part of the city they do not go to. They are paid, perhaps, in something. They are paid, perhaps, in the right to stay.

They move slowly. They do not hurry. The city has not hurried in a long time, and the workers have stopped hurrying in sympathy, or because hurry is a thing that requires light and they have so little of it. They are not unhappy. Unhappiness requires a comparison, and they have nothing to compare themselves to. They are present in the way that a beam is present, the way a pipe is present, the way the moss is present on the north side of a wall.

They speak rarely. A word at the beginning of a shift. A direction given to someone who has lost their way. The name of a man who died forty years ago, whose grave no one visits because the cemetery has been taken over by the moss. They speak the way water drips — only when there is something to be said, only when the vessel can hold no more.

They are old, most of them. There are no children. There have been no children for a long time. The city does not reproduce; it maintains. The workers are residual — the ones who did not leave when the leaving was easier, the ones who stayed after the rest had gone down into the valleys. They stay because they no longer know how to leave. They stay because the moss has rooted in them and the damp has gotten into the bone and the steam has opened the lungs in a particular way.

**The cats** are different.

The cats are the sovereigns. They have always been the sovereigns. They came up with the first stone-cutters, perhaps, or they were here before, in the ruins of the mountains, in the scoops the glaciers left. They have lived here so long that they have become a kind of lichen themselves — small, dry, silent, surviving on what the city provides. They are everywhere. They are in numbers no one has counted, because no one can count them. They are in the alleys and on the rooftops and in the gutters and in the cellars and in the pipe-runs and in the spaces behind the moss where the city's bones show.

They own the city because they understand it. They know which door leads to which roof. They know which stair arrives somewhere to the side of where it started. They know the geometry — the drunken, tilted geometry that the people have given up on. The cats move through it without hesitation. They sit in doorways that are not doorways. They disappear into walls and come out three streets away. They are the city's memory, or its nervous system, or its second self.

They are small and lean and the color of old stone, with eyes that take the orange gas-light and give back something green. They do not fight each other. There is no need. There is enough. They eat what the city provides — rats, mostly, and the birds that get blown up from below and cannot find their way down again, and scraps from the kitchens that are not really kitchens anymore. They are not fat — fatness requires more energy than the dimness provides — but they are content in the way that things that have found their place are content.

They are not tame. They are not wild. They have no use for the distinction. They let the workers touch them sometimes, on the cold mornings when the steam is heavy and the engines have not yet started their day's work. They sit on the windowsills of the factories and watch the wheels turn with an expression that might, if you were inclined to read it, be one of mild contempt. They have outlived whatever purpose the cats were kept for. They are the only ones who remember that cats were ever kept at all.

**The dogs** are few, and have always been fewer. The dogs are confused by the geometry. They bark at corners that lead nowhere. They get lost in the alleys and have to be retrieved by the cats, who lead them out with a patience that is almost human. They are larger than the cats and not as clever and not as content. They do not understand the damp. They shake themselves on the cobbles and the water flies off them in arcs that catch the gas-light. They are not unhappy, exactly, but they are out of place. They are trying to fit into a city that was not built for them and that has decided, in its slow way, that they are optional.

**The rats** in the cellars are not like rats elsewhere. They are larger. They are paler. They live in the long pipe-runs and the cisterns and the spaces behind the walls where the brick has given way to root. They eat the moss, or what the moss grows on, or what grows on the moss. They have been down there long enough that they have developed a kind of sight that does not require light. They navigate by vibration — the pulse of the pipes, the hum of the engines, the footsteps of the workers above. They are not afraid. They have no predators. The cats come down sometimes, but the cats do not hunt them with any conviction. There is a kind of truce. They share the city.

**The birds** are rare and do not last. A bird blown up from the valleys is a thing out of its element. It does not understand the dimness, the damp, the geometry. It sits on a chimney and opens its mouth and sings a song that belongs to a brighter place. The song dies in the damp before it has gone a hundred feet. The bird stays for a few days and then dies, or leaves, or becomes so still that it is hard to tell which. There are skeletons on the higher rooftops. There are skeletons in the gutters. The cats do not eat them. The moss grows over them in a season.

**The elders** do not have a name. They are the people who have been here longest — the ones who remember the industrial expansions, or the brickwork above the stonework, or the stonework itself. They have been here so long that they have become continuous with the architecture. They sit on the terraces where the old stone is fitted to the slope. They do not move much. The moss grows on them in earnest. The lichen colors them. Their breathing is the breathing of the city. When they die, no one is sure they have died. The moss closes over them. They become part of the terrace.

There are not many of them. There are fewer every year. They speak sometimes, when the conditions are right. They tell the workers things the workers do not understand — the names of products that are no longer made, the names of streets that no longer connect, the names of people who died in the plagues that came before the city learned to be careful with its damp. They speak the way the pipes groan — with effort, with a kind of mechanical patience.

They are the only ones who know what is being made in the factories. They have known for a long time. They have not told anyone. The product is something that requires the city to keep digesting itself, requires the workers to keep feeding the engines, requires the moss to keep growing and the damp to keep dampening and the steam to keep steaming. They do not know whether the product is for anyone. They suspect it is not.

**There are other things.** Not people, not animals, not the city itself, but present nonetheless. The steam, sometimes, takes a shape that is more than steam. It gathers in a doorway and stands there for a moment with a kind of intention, and then disperses, and no one remarks on it because no one is looking. The moss, in the deeper cellars, has learned to grow in patterns that are not random — slow spirals, half-readable shapes, the suggestion of letters in a language no one still speaks. The water in the cisterns moves when no one is looking, in currents that have no source, that circle and circle and do not settle. The light, in the late afternoon when the sky is most committed to its refusal of brightness, takes on a quality that is not quite light and not quite shadow — a third thing, a kind of weather that has entered the air and forgotten how to leave.

These things do not speak. They do not move with purpose. But they are present, and they are alive in the way that the damp is alive, in the way that the slow green is alive. They are the city's own inhabitants. They are what the city makes when it is left alone.

**And the city itself.**

The city is its own inhabitant. The moss is its skin. The water in the pipes is its blood. The steam in the vents is its breath. The engines are its digestion. The chimneys are its voice, when it speaks at all, which is rarely. The workers are its hands. The cats are its eyes. The elders are its memory. The rats are its marrow. The birds, when they come, are its visitors, and they do not last.

It is not aware of itself. It is not a mind. It is a body that has forgotten it is a body — that has been digesting so long it has forgotten what digestion is for. It eats the mountain. It eats the coal. It eats the ore. It eats the workers, gently, one at a time, replacing them with moss and lichen and the orange-and-grey of the rooftops. It eats the cats, but slowly, and the cats do not mind. It eats the dogs, but the dogs fight it, and the dogs are unhappy, and the dogs do not last.

It is not unhappy. It is not happy. It is present. It has been present for so long that it has forgotten what absence was. It hums. It steams. It drips. It grows. It turns the wheels and engages the gears and feeds itself with what it finds in its own depths. It is a place that has had too much happen in it and has lost the ability to be simple. It is a place that is ferociously, impossibly alive.

It is the fog at the top, where the shoulders flatten out into something almost like a plain. It is the orange smear of the gas-lamps. It is the green of the moss on the north sides of walls. It is the sound of the water in the pipes. It is the slow pulse of the chimneys. It is the absent birdsong. It is the footsteps on the wet cobble, fading. It is the clatter of the shutter in a wind that never quite arrives.

It is the strangeness. The accumulation. The layered time. The drunken geometry. The tilt that has been falling in a direction no one has agreed upon. It is itself. It has been itself for so long that it is the only thing it knows how to be.

And it goes on.
