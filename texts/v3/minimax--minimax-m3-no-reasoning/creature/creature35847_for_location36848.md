**What Lives in the Weeping Heights**

It is debatable whether anything here is alive.

The wind does not live, but it moves through the thermals rising off the heated stone with the constancy of a resting breath. It pours over the lip of the plateau and cascades down the terraced faces, catching the steam from the fumaroles and carrying it across the pale rock. It finds the cairns and whistles through their gaps. It is the loudest thing here, though it is only air obeying the laws of heat and cold.

The mist does not live, but it is the closest thing to a body this place possesses. It rises from the hot seepages below, meets the freezing temperature of the extreme elevation, and condenses into a fine, persistent sweat that coats every surface. It pools in the tufa rims. It hangs in the air and scatters the intense, shadowless light until the whole plateau glows with a flat, white brilliance. It breathes in and out, thickening when the vents below exhale, thinning when the sun strikes too fiercely.

The mineral crusts do not live, but they grow. In the spray-zones of the hottest vents, crystalline tufts push up from the basalt—pale, translucent needles that look like frost but are warm to the touch. Glassy flowstones creep sideways across flat rock, leaving trails like the tracks of something massive moving very slowly. White efflorescences bloom on wet surfaces overnight and dissolve by midday. If you watch a square meter of this landscape for a long time, you will see it digest itself and rebuild. It is a metabolism, patient and blind.

And then there is the wrongness. 

The place has been here long enough that it has worn a groove into reality. Distances are unreliable. A cairn that looks a hundred meters away turns out to be beside you, or two hundred meters away, and it has a twin on the next ridge that looks exactly like it. The paths of the water are sideways across vertical faces, spilling over rims that are higher on one side than the other. Pools reflect things that are not above them. 

Whatever built the upright stones and the rough cairns is gone. They left their primitive hammer-marks on the dark basalt, then walked away, and the terraces are slowly burying the evidence. No one has come back to maintain the paths. The geometry of the plateau is entirely its own, a settled, stable strangeness that no longer has anything to do with the people who passed through.

Nothing here wants anything. The light is just light. The sound is just the wind and the hissing fumaroles and a low harmonic that seems to come from the stone itself. The water is hot where it emerges and cool where it pools, and it will scald you if you touch it without testing, and this is the only warning it gives. 

It is a place that has forgotten that anyone was ever going to come. It is wet, and extremely bright, and cool, and so high up that the air is thin, and it goes on with its slow, mineral existence, entirely indifferent to being seen.
