# The Inhabitants of the Iron Terraces

## The Workers

They are small, mostly. The air and the dampness and the steady diet of mineral培养基 have, over the long generations, bred a leaner sort of person—shorter of limb than the lowland peoples, broader across the shoulders and the lower back where the years of bent work have made them thick. Their skin is the color of weak tea, ruddier in the cheeks from the forge-heat, paler in the under-eyes where the steam has been settling for a lifetime. Their hair, when they have it, goes early to iron-grey, and then to a kind of dim copper that nobody dyes and nobody hides. The children are born nearly pink, the way all children are, and darken by their second year. It is a marker, the elders say, of *belonging to the place*. Outsiders remark on it. The residents shrug.

They speak loudly. You have to. The condensers breathe, the hammers ring, the conveyors never stop, and a sentence pitched for a quiet room simply vanishes. There is a whole secondary language of lip-movement, cheek-tilt, chin-raise that the residents read across a workshop floor without thinking about it, and the children pick it up before they pick up words. Visitors find themselves mimed at, gestured to, sometimes shouted into compliance, and never quite understand why the workers look faintly offended when asked to repeat themselves.

They are not a people given to complaint. This is not because they have been beaten into silence—it is because, after a few generations of living in a place like this, complaint itself comes to seem a kind of bad engineering. *The condensate line is fouling. The fan on tower six has thrown a bearing. The lift-pump's piston seal is weeping again.* These are problems. Problems are *worked on*. The notion that the city might simply be *wrong*—that one ought to live somewhere else, where the air was dry and the water ran downhill—that notion has not occurred to anyone in living memory, and the archaeologists who trace the older strata find no evidence it occurred to anyone in dead memory either.

The families are deep. A child born in Vellum is often born to parents who were born in Vellum, who were born to parents who were born in Vellum, for nine or ten or fifteen generations of unbroken residence. The surnames are territorial: *of Tower Six*, *of the Lower Lift*, *of the Condenser Line*. Marriages within the city are common; marriages outward are vanishingly rare, and the few outsiders who settle in are absorbed slowly, across decades, into the slow color-shift and the loud voice and the *patina-stage* of it all.

The older ones — and they often live long, in spite of everything, because the air is clean of the things that usually kill a body — the older ones have a quality that the young find both comforting and faintly alarming. They have begun to slow down. Not in body. In *rhythm*. They sit on the warm stone and their breathing falls into step with the condensers' cycle. They speak, when they speak, in the long even pulses of someone who has stopped counting seconds. The very oldest, who have outlived their trades and sit in the upper residential cells looking out at the forges below, will sometimes be asked a question and answer an hour later, in a single sentence that has been assembling itself all that while. The young find this maddening. They also find it, secretly, the thing they would most like to become.

## The Rust

The creatures are not made for a careful description. They move too quickly, and there are too many of them, and what they *are* in any individual sense is less interesting than what they *are* in aggregate.

A single one, if you could catch one — and you can, with a gloved hand and quick reflexes, though it is not advised, because they shriek louder when caught and the others come to investigate — a single one is about the size of a human hand held flat, the body a tight knot of something that looks like copper wire stripped of its insulation, dark and wiry, with a heat in it you can feel through the glove. The wings — and there are more than four, always more, as if each individual had grown them in response to a private itch and never agreed on how many were correct — beat in patterns that are not quite synchronous, producing not a hum but a kind of clatter at close range, like a thrown handful of fine chain. The eyes are red-orange and faintly phosphorescent, picking up the thermal world in a way human eyes cannot; in the daylight they are dull, but in the dusk and through the steam they catch the city's warmth and throw it back in points of moving light.

They do not nest in any way that human carpenters would recognize. They cluster. They hang in the upper arches and the undercut chambers of the cliff-face in dense, shifting masses, clinging to the wet stone with the hooked undersides of their wings, and they rearrange themselves through the day according to gradients nobody has fully mapped — temperature, humidity, the chemical composition of the air at that hour, the phase of some obscure internal rhythm. In the heat of the afternoon they spread out and climb toward the upper rim, where the air is cooler. At dusk they pour back down into the warm channels between the condensers, and that is when the noise begins.

The noise is the sound of the place the way the surf is the sound of a coast — present, enormous, persistent, and somehow *interior*, as if the city had grown it from inside itself. It is a high sustained shriek, modulated in ways that suggest *something*, even to the residents who pretend they have stopped hearing it. Some of the engineers have spent years trying to parse it. They have produced waveform diagrams. They have argued, inconclusively, about whether the variations are signal or noise, communication or mere metabolic vent. The older workers have a more economical view: *the rust is saying what the rust is saying, and it has been saying it for a long time, and what it is saying is not for us.*

They are not dangerous. This bears repeating because visitors ask. They do not bite, do not sting, do not carry any of the diseases that flourish in more biologically diverse climes. Their droppings are a fine orange powder that adds, modestly, to the patina-stage of every horizontal surface in the upper terraces. They die, sometimes, in great numbers — a bad cold morning, a wrong wind, a fluctuation in the chemical air — and their small wire-bodies come down in drifts along the canals and are swept up by the maintenance crews and fed back into the heat exchangers, where their mineral content is welcome. The residents do not mourn them. They do not, exactly, fail to notice. There is a quiet acknowledgment, the way a farmer acknowledges a hard frost.

Some workers have favorites. This is not encouraged but not forbidden, either. A *rust* that has taken to roosting on a particular windowsill, or wheeling in a particular pattern outside a particular forge door, becomes, over weeks, a kind of unofficial mascot. It is given a name. Its absences are remarked on. When it dies, the windowsill is empty for a season, and then a new one comes, and the naming begins again, and nobody is embarrassed about any of it.

## The Smaller Things

The furnace tufts are what happens when a life-form commits fully to chemistry. They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are not even quite analogous to the lichen-analogues that spread their slow orange blooms across the wettest stones, though they share a color and a patience. The tufts grow in the heat-shadow of the forges — those places where the radiant warmth is intense but the air is still, where the chemical gradient between hot metal and cool vapor is steepest. They are small, brittle, branching things the color of fresh-cut rust, no taller than a finger, and they feed — in some sense of *feed* that the refinery biologists have spent decades refining without settling — on the differential between the two.

They are useful. A tuft that turns pale is a tuft in distress, and a distressed tuft means the local heat-balance is off, and an off heat-balance in a forge-shadow is a warning nobody wants to ignore. The crews check them on their rounds, the way a doctor checks a patient's tongue. When a tuft blacks, they bring in the engineers. When a tuft flowers — which happens only at the highest gradients, and is considered a minor festival in the relevant shops — they bring in the engineers anyway, out of caution, because a flowering tuft is a tuft at the edge of its tolerances, and the edge of a tuft's tolerances is sometimes the edge of a forge's tolerances, too.

The lichen-analogues — the *iron bloom*, the residents call them, with the kind of casual poetry they would deny possessing — spread everywhere the capillary water reaches. They are slower than the tufts by an order of magnitude. A bloom you can see with the naked eye may be a century old; a bloom you can stand inside may be ten. They do not die visibly. They simply stop spreading, and become, in time, indistinguishable from the patina-stage around them. The crews do not clean them. They are, by long consensus, part of the *finish*.

## What They Eat

The vats are tall, ceramic, vented at the top with copper hoods that feed the exhaled moisture back into the condenser network. Inside, in a warm mineral broth that smells faintly of pennies and old iron, the cultures turn. They are not one thing. They are a layered ecology of their own — primary feeders on the dissolved metals, secondary feeders on the primary, tertiary feeders that nobody has successfully named but that everyone agrees are necessary, because when they are absent the whole column goes grey and dies. The broth is replenished from the settling basins. The basins are replenished from the canals. The canals are replenished from the cliffs.

The cuisine is honest. It is ferrous and faintly sweet, with a mineral undertone that visitors call metallic and residents call *the taste of home*. It is served in thick ceramic bowls that hold the heat, and it is eaten with flat iron spoons, because the broth will eat anything lesser. It comes in textures — firm, yielding, brothy, gelatinous — that the residents distinguish with a vocabulary they have not bothered to teach outsiders, because outsiders find the whole subject uncomfortable and the residents have learned not to press.

Children eat it without complaint. Old ones eat it without thinking. Visitors eat it under sufferance and are quietly surprised, sometimes, to find that they have asked for a second bowl.

## The Children and the Visitors

Children are born to the sound of the hammers and the breathing of the condensers, and they learn, very early, that there are certain hours when you do not go out without your hood up, because the *rust* is moving through. They learn to read lips before they can read. They learn to walk the wet stone without slipping, and to tell by the sound of a fan whether it is healthy. By the time they are eight they have a trade; by twelve they are useful at it; by twenty they are better at it than their parents were. The city does not sentimentalize childhood. It also does not waste it.

Visitors are rare and treated with a kind of patient bemusement. They come — once a decade, perhaps, in ones or twos — from the lowlands, from the other industrial plateaus, from places where the air behaves and the water falls and plants are a normal fact of life. They are given a cell in the residential tier. They are given a pair of boots with good soles. They are told, with great seriousness, not to drink from the walls. Most of them last a season. Some of them last a year. A few, over the generations, have stayed, and their descendants are among the workers now, indistinguishable from the rest except for a faint remembered longing in the older ones for some color other than orange.

## The Slow Whole

What holds all of this together — workers and *rust* and tufts and lichen and the cultured food and the long-settled water — is not, in the end, a story. It is a *metabolism*. The place turns, the way a body turns, with all its parts in their slow conversation: the cliffs weeping into the canals, the canals feeding the condensers, the condensers breathing into the upper air, the *rust* feeding on the breath, the tufts feeding on the gradients, the workers tending the whole, the workers themselves fed from the vats, the vats fed from the settling basins, the settling basins fed from the canals, and so on, around and around, with the lift-pumps carrying water and rubble upward and the erosion carrying stone downward and the *rust* shrieking overhead and the hammers ringing below and the children growing up into it and the old ones slowing into it and the lichen spreading an inch a century across the wet stone, the whole patient wheel of a place that has been turning long enough to have become, in itself, a kind of living.

It is not beautiful. The residents know this. They have made their peace with it, the way one makes one's peace with one's own face in the mirror after enough years. It is *theirs*, and they are *its*, and the two facts have stopped being distinguishable, which is, they will tell you if you ask, the only kind of belonging that holds.
