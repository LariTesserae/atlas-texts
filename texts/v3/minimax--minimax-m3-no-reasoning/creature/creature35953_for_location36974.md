The woman who keeps the inn has been called, in her time, by every name that ends in a soft consonant. She answers to most of them. She answers, in fact, to whatever you call her when you first see her, which is usually nothing, because what you usually say first is *oh*, or *ah*, or a small sound that has no word, and she receives this sound as if it were a name and a passport and a confession, all at once, and she nods, and she says, *yes, you, come in*.

She is tall, but only the way a question is tall. Her face is the color of the saltref blocks — pale, faintly luminous, with the suggestion that if you struck her, gently, against a saltref wall, both she and the wall would ring. Her eyes are dark in the way the canals are dark at noon, which is to say: present, watchful, and not entirely committed to reflecting what is in front of them. She has, I think, a mouth. I remember it. I cannot describe it. It is the kind of mouth you only remember in motion, and the motion is over.

Her name — and I am telling you this against her wishes, though her wishes are a weather system and I am standing, perhaps, on the wrong side of it — is Peller. She was born in Coyle's Reach, in the house that is now the small white dog, or beside it, or in a house that was the dog before the dog was the dog. She does not have parents. She has *forebears*, which is a word she uses and means. The forebears are not dead. They are *no longer currently in evidence*, which is different.

She has run the inn for forty-one years. She says forty-one. She has also, on other occasions, said one hundred and twelve, and four, and *longer than the canal*. All of these are correct. None of them is the answer.

She gives you a room. The room is on the second floor, or the third, or the floor that is not a floor but a pause between floors. It has a bed. The bed is made of the wood of a witness tree, and it is warm for the first three hours you sleep in it and cold after, and you will know, by the temperature, when you have been forgiven for whatever it was you did to deserve ending up here. The sheets smell of iron and lilac. The pillow remembers your head before your head arrives. There is a window. The window is what she said it would be. The window is the kind of window that is mostly a suggestion.

She brings you coffee in the morning. The coffee is good. The coffee is the best coffee you have ever had, which is to say it is the only coffee that has ever been made *for you specifically*, and you can tell, and she knows you can tell, and neither of you mentions it. The eggs are good. The eggs are eggs. There is a small white thing on the table that might be butter and might be a folded note from someone you used to know and might be a piece of the morning, condensed.

You will want to ask her questions. She will let you. She will answer some of them. The answers will be true in the way the population is true, which is to say: mostly, approximately, in a direction.

The children of Coyle's Reach are born mostly in the weeping. Their mothers, who are not always women, go out into the heavy mist of the afternoon and they come back, later, with the child, or with the *fact of the child*, which is sometimes enough. The children are thin and dark-eyed and quiet in the way that old houses are quiet. They do not cry, mostly. They do not laugh, mostly. They *attend*. From the first, they attend. They look at things, and the things they look at become, briefly, more themselves, and then the looking passes, and the things go back to being what they were.

There is a boy named Sallow. He is fine now. He is a small grey canal at the south end of Main Street, between the dry goods and the place that was once a chapel and is now a kind of long pause. The boys in town wave to him when they pass. He remembers being a boy. He is, by general agreement, gentle. His light — and he gives off light, in the phosphorescent way, in the slow way — is the color of something you almost remember from a dream you had as a child, the dream where you found a room in your house that you had not known was there. He is not unhappy. He is *ongoing*. He is the town's only public monument to the danger of looking too closely at canal-light at night, and he is also a place where small boats used to tie up, and he is also the reason no one swims at the south end of Main Street anymore, and he is also a boy who is twelve, still, forever, who waves back, sometimes, with a slow ripple of his surface.

There is a woman whose name is no longer spoken. She is a name that has been breathed out of the air. She lives on the island in the canal at the south end of town. The island is, on closer inspection, a chair. The chair is hers. She sits in it. She has been sitting in it for a long time. She is why the sighing has a slight hitch in it at the seven-minute mark, the kind of hitch a heart makes when it has been asked to do something and is not sure it wants to. She is not unhappy. She is *a decision the town made once and has not unmade*. You will not meet her. You will see her chair. You will, if you are polite, and if you are the kind of person who can still be polite in the way the town requires, nod to her. She will nod back, in the way chairs nod, which is to say: not at all, but with a quality of attention that is, in its own way, a kind of nod.

There is a man named Erren. He is the mayor. He is tall. He moves slowly, not because he is tired but because he is *considering the air ahead of him before he enters it*. He keeps a small white dog. The dog does not bark. The dog does not drink water. The dog is, Erren assures everyone, *entirely real, thank you, yes, I know what it looks like, yes, I have heard the word, no, it is not that, please, please do not call it that, thank you*. The dog is real. The dog is also, somehow, a small white door. These are not contradictions. This is Coyle's Reach. The dog will follow you, sometimes, at a distance, on the nights when you walk the town. It will not approach. It will not speak. It will, if you are quiet enough, and if you sit still long enough on a porch, come and rest its head against your ankle, and the ankle will feel, briefly, like the threshold of somewhere, and you will want to step through, and you will not, and the dog will understand, and the dog will leave, and you will not be sorry, and you will be, for the rest of your life, slightly more *porous* than you were.

There is a woman named Tilde. She keeps the records at the back of the inn. The records are not records of people. They are records of *the way the town has been*. She has timed the sighs. She has been timing them for thirty-one years. They come every seven minutes and twelve seconds. She says they have not varied. She says this in a tone that is not boastful but is the tone of someone who has been *keeping faith* with a thing, and the faith has been reciprocated, and she is not surprised, and she is not relieved, and she is, in a small way, proud in the way a person is proud of a friend who has done what they said they would do.

There is a man whose job you have not learned. There is a woman whose name you have learned and forgotten. There is a child who waves at Sallow. There is an old person who sits on a porch and looks at the same thing everyone is looking at and has been looking at for longer than you have been alive, and who will, if you sit beside them, after a long time, take your hand, and the hand will be very thin, and very warm, and will feel like the handle of a door that has been waiting to be opened, and the door will not open, and that is fine, that is the whole of it, that is the kindness the town does you, which is to let you hold the handle and not turn it, and to let you know, by the warmth of the hand, that the door is there, and that it has been there, and that it will be there, and that you are, for as long as you sit on this porch, in this heat, in this quiet, in this town that no one named Coyle has lived in for longer than anyone can remember, *almost*, almost, almost *accounted for*.
