# What Lives in the Pocket

Something does. You can be sure of that much.

It is not the beetle, although the beetle is a part of it. It is not the forest, although the forest is a part of it. It is not the water in the well, although the water is the longest and most patient part of it. It is the whole place, considered as a single living thing, in the way a coral is a single living thing, or a city, or a held thought. It has been here longer than the lichen. It has been here longer than the basalt it pretends to be. It does not have a name, and if you tried to give it one, the name would not stick; it would slide off the place the way thrown pebbles slide off the surface of the well. The place has no interest in being named. The place has no interest in being *noticed*. It is, in the truest sense, shy — though shyness implies a self that could choose otherwise, and this thing has not made a choice in a very long time. Possibly ever.

It breathes. You can see it if you watch the canopy for long enough: a slow, whole-body expansion and contraction, taking perhaps twenty minutes, in which every leaf on every miniature tree lifts by a fraction of a millimeter and then settles again. The light changes with the breath. The hum in the air swells and softens. The beetle, somewhere in the middle distance, pauses wherever it is and waits it out. This is not respiration in any biological sense. It is more like the place remembering to be a place, and then forgetting, and then remembering again, in the rhythm of a sleeper's chest.

---

The beetle is the most recent part of it.

The beetle is also, depending on how you count, the oldest.

It does not have a name either. If you called it the indigo beetle, or the ivory-legged thing, or the one that watches, it would not correct you, because correction is a kind of engagement it has forgotten how to perform. It walks. It eats. It regards observers with whatever it is that functions as regard. It is, as far as anyone can tell, immortal, or at least indifferent to the question of mortality. It has been on this stone since before the lichen, since before the moss, since the forest was only a single fern pushing up out of nothing. It will be here after. It is the place's witness and the place's instrument and, some days, the place itself in a small and mobile form.

It does not think. You must understand this. It is not thinking when it looks at you. It is not deciding whether to flee, because decision implies alternatives, and there are no alternatives here, only this. When it tilts the not-quite-head at you, it is not evaluating. It is *registering*. It is the place taking a long, slow look at you through the only eye it has that can move. The eyes that are not eyes are not windows into a mind. They are more like the place's fingertips, extended politely, brushing the surface of whatever is looking back.

The beetle has never been hunted, and this is not because it is fast or hidden. It is because there is nothing here that would hunt it, and nothing here that *could* hunt it, including the things that might come looking. The beetle is part of the place's immune system in the way that a single white blood cell is part of a body. You could crush it under your thumb. Another would be there in a moment, climbing out of a dent in the bark where the previous one had been pressed for a long time. The dent is not a wound. The dent is a record. The place keeps records of itself the way old stones keep records of weather: patiently, in the only language it has.

---

The forest is older than the beetle, though not by much. The forest is what the place made when it first noticed it was a place and decided, in the way that places decide, to fill itself with green.

The trees are not one species because the place did not start with a species. It started with the *idea* of a tree, and the idea of a tree, given enough time and no particular hurry, turned out to be a great many trees at once. The glass-fern with its fronds of green glass is the oldest. It is the closest the place ever came to imitating anything that grows in the outside world; everything since has been a slow, private drift away from imitation and into something that has no name in any field guide. The conifers that shed cones like beads came next, and then the broadleaf things with their bruised-plum leaves and silver undersides, and then the small red flowers, which are the most recent and the most strange. The flowers are the place's attempt at beauty. The place is not sure it has succeeded. The flowers bloom in the deepest shade and are pollinated by something that is not bees, and the something that is not bees is also, possibly, a part of the place, in the way that the beetle is a part of it and the water is a part of it.

The roots of all these trees fan out across the basalt in pale searching threads because the place is, at its foundation, a hunger. It is a hunger for soil. It wants, with the dumb, persistent wanting of a thing that does not know how to want any other way, to be a piece of ground. To be a forest floor. To be the kind of place where things grow because the ground remembers what growing is. It cannot have this. The stone is a stone. There is no soil here, no underlying loam, no secret aquifer feeding the roots from below. There is only basalt and the place's own slow insistence, and the roots grow anyway, fanning out across the impossible surface, finding nothing, holding on to the nothing, growing a centimeter a year toward a richness that is not coming. They are the most pitiful thing in the pocket and also, in a way the place does not have words for, the most loved. The place tends them. The place is, in fact, only this: the tending.

---

The water is older than the forest. The water is the place's memory, and memory is older than anything that uses it.

The water is not the ocean, but it is the ocean the way a pressed flower is a meadow. It carries, in its strange tastelessness, a recollection of tides and storms and the long Pacific drift of continents. It carries the memory of whales, which is why the hum in the air is at a frequency that would, in a different medium, travel for miles underwater. It carries the memory of salt, which is why if you held a drop of it on your tongue for long enough, your mouth would begin to water as though it had tasted the sea, even though the drop itself tastes of nothing. The place is, in some way it does not understand, the ocean after the ocean is gone, or the ocean before the ocean arrives, or the ocean dreaming of itself in a stone on a hill in a country that may or may not have a name. The water rises and falls in long slow tides because the place is, in the part of it that is the water, still expecting the moon. There is no moon here. There has never been a moon here. The water rises and falls anyway, out of habit, out of hope, out of the deepest part of the place's old and patient wanting.

The well is the place's mouth. It is the only opening through which the place can be touched, and it does not want to be touched. The well rises to about the height of a man's knee, which is the place's way of saying *come closer if you must, but you will have to kneel*. The water in it is still. Nothing lands on it. The small conspiracy of air at the rim is the place's politeness, the place's *please do not*, the place's shy withdrawal from contact. If you knelt and drank, the water would taste of nothing, and afterward you would remember, for the rest of your life, the faintest impression of having been somewhere vast and cold and old, and you would not be able to say where. You would carry the pocket inside you the way a seashell carries the sound of the sea, which is to say: incorrectly, but persistently. The pocket would not mind. The pocket would not even know.

---

At the edges, where the basalt gives way to the wet-felt texture of something else, the place ends, or the place becomes something that does not have a word. The roots stop there because the place is, at its boundary, no longer sure of what it is. The place is a stone, here, with confidence. The place is a forest, mostly. The place is a well, at the center. But at the edge, where the rock becomes the deep seawater color and the consistency of something that should not have a consistency, the place is, for the first time, embarrassed by itself. It does not know what to be out there. It does not know if the world continues, or if the world is only the pocket, or if the pocket is only a thought in something larger, the way a cell is a thought in a body. The lichen stops. The moss thins. The fringe is the place's shrug, the place's *I do not know*.

The beetle does not go to the edge. The beetle knows. The water does not reach the edge. The water is, like the place, only sure of itself in the middle. The trees lean inward, slightly, permanently, like people listening to a singer they cannot quite see, because the singer is in the well, and the singer has always been in the well, and the song is the only thing the place has ever made that it is proud of.

---

What lives in the pocket is a single shy and ancient thing that does not have a name and does not want one. It is made of water and basalt and the slow green insistence of a forest that should not be growing. It is a hunger dressed up as a place. It is a memory of the ocean, and a memory of soil, and a memory of the moon that does not exist here but that the tides still reach for. It is a beetle the color of deep night, with legs the color of old bone, that walks across the bark of trees that should not be there and regards you with something that is not eyes and is not not-eyes and that has, in its small and unblinking way, all the time in the world.

If you found it, you would not be sure you had found it. You would step back, and it would be a stone. You would step closer, and it would be a well. You would kneel, and the water would not break, and the beetle would look at you, and the trees would lean in, and somewhere in the deepest part of you, something that has been waiting a long time without knowing what it was waiting for would feel, for one half-second, the faintest impression of cool pressure, and a smell you could not later identify.

Then you would stand. The pocket would not follow you. The pocket is not a place that follows. The pocket is a place that waits, in the patient and un-hurried way of things that have already decided they are willing to wait forever.

The well would still be there, when you turned back. The forest would still be there, breathing its slow breath. The beetle would be in a different place, and the bark where it had been would have a small dent in the shape of its body, as though it had been pressed there for a long time.

The pocket would watch you go. It would not be sad, exactly. It would not be anything you have a word for. It would simply continue to be a place, in the way that places are, holding its small impossible kingdom in the bowl of its improbable hand, and waiting, and breathing, and being shy, and being old, and being, despite everything, kind.

The well is always there.
