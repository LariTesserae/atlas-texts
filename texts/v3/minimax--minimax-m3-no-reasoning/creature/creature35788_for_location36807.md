# What Lives in the Drowned Fells

What lives here has learned the trick of being patient in the dark.

---

## The Breath-Flesh

It is the first thing you have to understand about the Fells, because the breath-flesh is *everywhere* and you will not see it the way the lichen sees it or the way the people see it.

It is not coral. I want to be clear about this. Coral is a partnership, an animal hiding inside a plant's skeleton, and the breath-flesh is not that. Coral builds itself out of its own dead, layer by layer, into a hard thing that survives by outlasting. The breath-flesh does not harden. The breath-flesh stays soft. The breath-flesh is *still going*, all the way down to whatever passes for its base.

It is not a fungus. Fungus is a digestion, a way of being elsewhere while eating the place you are in, and the breath-flesh does not eat the place it is in. Whatever it is doing for food, it is doing it from the water itself, or from the light, or from something no one has a name for.

The closest anyone has come is the word the breath-flesh people use among themselves, the word that is not in their language for visitors, which is something like *the long tissue* or *the held breath* — a thing that is one continuous body spread out across many pillars, the way a single tree can be a forest if you let it clone itself forever.

In the shallows, on the windward faces of the Teeth where the spray is permanent, the breath-flesh is just a skin. A pale crust, lavender where the light reaches, bone-white where it thins, and at the very edge of visibility a faint sourceless blue glow that is not bioluminescence in any sense the fishermen recognize. Bioluminescence is a chemical trick, an emergency. This glow does not flicker. This glow does not stop. This glow is, as far as anyone can tell, the *operating color* of the tissue at the bottom of its range, the way deep-sea fish are simply *that color*, all the time, without trying.

You can put your hand on it. It is cool and slightly yielding, the way a mushroom is yielding, the way the inside of your cheek is yielding. It does not flinch. It does not retract. It does not, in any way you can measure, react. You could press your ear to it and you would hear, if the wave-pour was quiet for a moment, the *sound*: a slow rhythmic expansion and contraction, slower than a sleeping man's breathing, deeper in the chest, the sound of a thing taking in and letting out. The people who live here say it is breathing. The people who visit say it is *something*. No one says it is a tide, because the breath-flesh breathes when the tide is still, and breathes when the tide is running, and breathes when the tide has been still for hours.

Down in the channels, in the deep hollows between Teeth where the water settles to its mineral clarity between swells, the breath-flesh is not a skin. Down there it is a *forest*. The pillars grow from the basalt in slow accumulations, branching and rebranching, each branch as thick as a man's arm in the smallest specimens and as thick as his thigh in the oldest. They are fifteen meters tall in the deepest of the mature groves. They sway in the residual surge with a motion that is *almost* passive — the way seaweed sways — but not quite. There is something in the sway that is its own, something the water is not causing so much as the water is *going along with*. If you watched one pillar for an hour you might see the swell of its trunk change, very slightly, the way a sleeping dog's ribs change when it dreams.

The fishermen from the southern lands who came once — and only once — described the groves as *cities*. They said the breath-flesh grew like the pillars of a cathedral flooded but still standing. They said the dim light came down through the pale branching and the glow rose up out of the deeper branches and the two lights met in the middle of the column and the columns *throbbed*, gently, slowly, together, so that the whole grove was one breath drawn and held and released. They said they did not want to come back.

No one knows what the breath-flesh eats.

No one knows what eats the breath-flesh.

No one has ever seen a breath-flesh die.

## The Lichen

Up where the basalt shows, where the spray does its perpetual work of wetting and drying and wetting and drying, there is a different kind of patience: the lichen.

It is black. I mean this literally — it is not the deep brown that passes for black in the southern lands, it is the matte carbon of a thing that has decided to absorb every photon that comes near it. The color of old iron, the color of a sky with the sun behind it, the color of the basalt itself so that in some places you cannot tell where the rock ends and the lichen begins. It is crustose, which is a word that means *flat and stuck*, and it is stuck so thoroughly to the rock that you cannot peel it without taking the surface of the basalt with it. The lichen has *become* the surface of the rock. In a thousand years the rock will have eroded away under it and the lichen will be *floating*, and it will still be there.

It drinks the spray. It drinks the rain. It drinks the thin cold light that the overcast permits, three or four hours of it on a good day, less in the winter. It does not seem to mind. Lichen is the slowest living thing that has ever lived, slower than the basalt it clings to, slower than the tide, slower than the breath-flesh, and given enough time it will digest the rock itself, molecule by molecule, and become something else. It has been here since before the drowning. It will be here after whatever comes next.

The lichen is the skin of the Teeth. Where the lichen ends, the moss begins.

## The Moss

The moss does not live on the rock so much as it lives in *negotiation* with the rock.

It is a thick felt, three colors layered into one another: the dull olive of the upper surface where it has to face the air, the particular yellow-green of things that have learned to photosynthesize on what little light comes through the cloud base, and, lower down, in the places where the moss extends into the sub-aquatic, a strange bleached yellow that is the moss trying and failing to photosynthesize underwater. The moss does not stop at the waterline. The moss *continues*, sending pale fronds down into the world below the waves, fronds that grow for a meter or two before they give up, and you can see them if you look over the edge of a Tooth at low state: a fringe of moss hanging into the dark, like a beard, like the trailing roots of a tree.

The moss is where the people get their ochre dye and their yellow dye and most of their fire-starting tinder. They harvest it carefully, never from the same patch twice in a season, and they leave the holdfast layer intact so it will grow again. They say the moss is a slow thing that does not forgive, but a slow thing that does remember, and they tend it with the patient attention of people who have nothing else to tend.

---

## The Kelp

The kelp is the loudest living thing in the Fells.

It has a holdfast the color of a bruise — that is, a deep mottled purple-black that no one has ever satisfactorily explained — and a stipe as thick as a man's wrist, and blades three or four meters long that stream out in the current like manes. It climbs up from the deep channels and lays itself across the ridges when the water retreats, so that at low state each Tooth wears a shaggy pelt of olive-brown weed. The blades slap against the rock with each returning wave: *slap, slap, slap, SLAP*, a wet percussive rhythm that layers over the roar of the hollows and never stops. From a distance, on a still day, the Fells sound like a forest of wet hides being beaten.

The kelp is ancient. The breath-flesh people say it was here before the drowning, and they may be right. It is a deep-water species that has, over the slow centuries of submergence, simply grown taller, and longer, and stronger. Its holdfasts grip the basalt in the surge zone, and the fronds reach up toward a light that is no longer sufficient for them, and they grow anyway, because kelp does not *care* whether the light is sufficient. Kelp does what kelp does, and what kelp does is *grow*. In the hollows it forms tangled rafts that ride the fill-and-drain cycle, sinking and rising with each wave, a slow brown breathing of its own — separate from the breath-flesh breathing, but you would not know it from a distance. From a distance the Fells breathe in two voices, and you cannot tell which is which.

The kelp is what the people eat, mostly. They harvest it in the low state, breaking the holdfasts with hand-hafted stone axes and dragging the fronds up onto the basalt shoulders. They dry it on wooden frames. They smoke it over the sacred fires. They weave the fiber into ropes and rough cloth and the walls of their houses. They are, in some fundamental way, *made of kelp*, and the kelp is, in some fundamental way, *made of* the basalt and the iron-rich water and the half-light, and so the people are made of the basalt too, in a chain that goes back to whatever was here before the drowning.

## The Crab

There is one crab.

It is in one hollow. No one has ever found another.

It is small, pale, the color of the breath-flesh it lives among, and it has been in the same hollow for as long as anyone in the settlement can remember. The breath-flesh people call it the *hollow-keeper*, though whether they mean it keeps the hollow or the hollow keeps it is unclear. They do not eat it. They do not touch it. They leave a small offering of dried kelp at the edge of the hollow at certain phases of the moon, and they tell the children not to look at it directly, and they tell the visitors not to mention it at all.

It sidles along the rock at the water's edge. It eats, presumably, something. No one has ever seen it eat. It has been there, doing whatever crabs do, for a very long time.

---

## The Fish

There are no fish in the Fells themselves.

The breath-flesh people will tell you this plainly. The range of the ridge, the channels between the Teeth, the deep hollows with their forests of pale columns: no fish. The water is wrong for them. The cold is too mineral, too iron-heavy, too *thick*. Whatever the breath-flesh is breathing out into the water, the fish do not like it. The fish stay out beyond the Fells, in the open ocean, in the deep water that has not been changed by the basalt leaching into it.

The people go out to the fish. They paddle their skin boats out beyond the range of the ridge on calm days, and they drop bone hooks weighted with pebbles, and they wait. They come back with fish that are strange to them — the deep-water species, slow-moving, large-eyed, silver-bellied — and they dry them on wooden frames and they eat them sparingly, and they say the fish taste of the open ocean, which is to say they taste of a world they have never been to.

The fishermen who used to work these ranges before the last submergence tell a different story. They say the Fells were once full of fish. They say the kelp was full of fish. They say you could catch a fish in the shallows with a bucket. That was before the breath-flesh came up from the deep channels, or before it *became* what it is now, or before something happened that no one agrees on. The fish withdrew. The animals withdrew. The world withdrew, except for the things that could live on patience and the half-light.

## The People

They are not many.

There are perhaps sixty of them on the largest Tooth, on a flattish shoulder of basalt about eight meters above mean sea level, in stone-and-driftwood houses built low against the prevailing weather. They are small — shorter than the southern people by a head, broad in the shoulder and the hip, built low against the wind. Their skin is dark, darker than the basalt, a color that the visitors from the south mistake at first for dirt and then realize is simply the color of people who have lived under a low cloud base for generations. Their eyes are large and dark and do not flinch from the gray light. Their voices are pitched to carry over the constant sound of water — they speak, even to each other indoors, in a register that would be shouting in any other place. They have never known silence. They do not seem to miss it.

They are *breath-flesh* people, or so they call themselves, and they will not explain the name to a visitor, and they will not explain it to each other either. It is the name. It is what they are. They know what the breath-flesh is doing, or they know a way of knowing, and they have made their life to fit in with what they know.

They eat the kelp. They eat the lichen — bread made of lichen flour is a staple, bitter and sustaining, the color of old iron and tasting, they say, of *the place itself*. They catch, in the deeper water beyond the Fells, the fish. They do not eat the breath-flesh. They have never eaten the breath-flesh. They will tell you, if you ask in the right way, that eating the breath-flesh would be like eating the weather, or like eating the tide. They mean this as a fact, not as a metaphor.

Their tools are simple. Hand-hafted stone axes for breaking kelp holdfasts. Bone hooks for the deep fishing. A few metal things, traded up the coast from settlements that still have access to smelters — a knife, sometimes, an axe-head, a needle, never more than one of each in a household. They weave kelp-fiber into ropes and rough cloth. They burn driftwood and dried kelp in small stone hearths, and the fires are sacred to them, the only warm thing in a cold world, and they are tended with a care that approaches tenderness. A child is given the care of the hearth-fire when she is seven, and from that day she is responsible for keeping it alive through the night, and she does not sleep until she has handed it over in the morning. This is the central fact of her childhood.

They have one piece of technology that surprises visitors, and I have kept it for last because it is the thing that matters.

They have learned to listen.

They can sit at the edge of a hollow in the low state, with the kelp writhing around them and the next wave already visible as a dark line on the horizon, and they can hear — in the faint rhythmic expansion of the pale forests below — a *pattern*. Not a message. They will not call it a message. Not a language. They will not call it a language. They call it *the way the world is breathing right now*, and they listen the way a doctor listens to a chest: for the regularity, for the deviation, for the slow long cycle that they have learned to map across weeks and seasons and years. They know when the breath-flesh is breathing fast and when it is breathing slow. They know when the breath is *shallow* — and this is the word they use, *shallow*, in the same tone a doctor would use — and they know when the breath is *deep*. They adjust their fishing and their harvesting and their movements along the ridge to it. They will not go out beyond the Fells when the breath is shallow. They will not harvest kelp from a particular channel when the breath is wrong. They do not say why. They say *the world is breathing that way right now* and they leave it there.

The visitors from the south, the ones who came once and did not come back, asked them what the pattern *meant*. The breath-flesh people considered the question. They said it meant *that the world is alive*. They said it meant *that the world is breathing*. They said it meant *that we are small and the world is large and the world is breathing and we are inside the breath*. They did not say anything else. The visitors went home and did not come back.

## The Wind

The wind is not alive. I want to be clear about this. The wind is wind. But the wind has learned to sing through the basalt columns of the Teeth in a single sustained tone, near the bottom of a man's hearing, and this singing is so constant and so strange that the people have given it a name: *the long note*. It is not a howl. It is not a moan. It is a *note*, the kind of note a pipe makes when the air is right and the pipe is whole. On a still day you can feel it more than hear it, a vibration in the soles of your feet, in your teeth, in the bones of your chest, and the people who live here say you learn to live with it the way you learn to live with the sound of the sea: by letting it become the silence underneath everything else.

---

## What Lives Here, In Sum

The Fells are a place where the living things have made a peace with patience. The lichen waits. The moss waits. The kelp grows and waits. The breath-flesh breathes and waits. The crab sidles and waits. The people wait. The wind sings and the waves pour and the rain drums and nothing ends and nothing begins and the whole drowned range, with its basalt spine and its hollows and its pale cathedrals of breathing tissue, just *goes on*, slow and loud and strange, in the long gray weather of a world that has been drowned for a long time and will be drowned for a long time still.

What lives here has learned the trick of being patient in the dark.

What lives here has learned the trick of being loud in the quiet.

What lives here does not require the world to be other than it is.
