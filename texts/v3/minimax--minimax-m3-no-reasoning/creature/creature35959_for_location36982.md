The things that hang in the air do not live here the way you live anywhere. They are *of* the gorge the way the gorge is of itself, and to ask what they eat is to ask what the basin eats, what the cupped floor drinks. The answer is: the place. The place feeds them. The rivulet is the slow blood of the stone, and the moisture that weeps from the walls is its sweat, and the air is a soup of small deaths and small growths, and the flying things pass through all of it and take what they need in passing, the way a thought passes through a sleeper and is fed without the sleeper knowing.

They are not many creatures. They are one creature, repeated. Or they are many voices, all singing one note that the note has been singing since before the water learned its way through the basin. I have watched a single one for a long time — a small one, hand-span, with a copper-green membrane that flashed when the strip of sky fell on it at midday. It drifted. It did not flap. There was no wingbeat I could see, no thrust, only a slow alteration of the angle of its body, the way a leaf falls but reversed, the way a leaf *rises* when the air is willing. It passed through the chords of two larger ones, and as it did, its lantern-bulb dimmed, brightened, dimmed, and its tone, which I had not been able to hear alone, became briefly audible: a high, clear, almost-sad sound, the note a child would hum to itself while playing at being lost in a wood.

Then it was gone, folded into the larger chord again, and I could not pick it out.

I think they are old. I think *old* is the wrong word — old is a thing that happens to living things, and these are not exactly living, or not only living, the way a coral is not exactly a stone. They are what the place is making out of itself, the way a thought is what a mind is making out of itself, and they have been making themselves here for as long as the making has been going on, and the making has been going on for as long as the gorge has been a gorge, and the gorge has been a gorge for as long as there has been a way to measure a gorge. They are not visitors. They are not migrants. They are the local weather, as I said, and like the local weather they do not leave. They are what this cleft does when it is itself.

And the slugs.

The slugs are different. The slugs are what *belongs* to the place, in the way a lichen belongs to a stone, in the way a stain belongs to a shirt. They are pale, and they are long, and they move with the patience of something that knows it will not be hurried, has never been hurried, will continue at its own pace after whatever observer is watching has gone elsewhere. Their trail glitters. I do not know what makes it glitter. Something in the slime, some mineral taken from the rock and concentrated and laid down as a slow record of where the slug has been. I thought at first it was just a slime-trail, the kind of evidence a snail leaves, and then I saw that the trail did not fade. After an hour the trail was still there, still faintly luminous, still tracing the wall in a slow deliberate line. After half a day. The slug had gone on; the line remained, as if the slug was *inscribing* something, as if the place was being written on by the things that moved across it, and the writing was meant to last.

There are many such lines now on the walls around me. They cross. They do not cross at random. They form, when you stand back and let your eyes unfocus, a pattern. I have not solved the pattern. I do not think I am meant to. I think the pattern is not for solving. I think the pattern is what the slugs are *thinking*, the way a coral thinks the reef it is building. It is slow. It is on a scale of years. It is the slugs' contribution to the long event the gorge has been holding.

I have only ever seen the legs of the too-many-legged thing in the moss. I have knelt and parted the moss with my hands, and the legs have gone still, and I have waited, and the moss has closed back over whatever was under it, and the legs have not shown themselves again. I think the body is not for seeing. I think the legs are a sense-organ, the way a whisker is a sense-organ, and the body is the part that does not need to be seen because it is *the place*, it is the moss itself, it is what the moss becomes when the moss becomes an animal. I am guessing. I am aware I am guessing. The place encourages guessing. The place has not yet told me I am wrong.

The things in the water.

The water has its own tenants. The pale eel-things, or fish-things, or whatever they are — they have too many fins, but each fin is in the right place, as if the design was *almost* familiar and then took a turn at the end. They slip between the rocks of the basin. They do not break the surface. They do not seem to be feeding on anything I can see. I have watched them for an hour and they have done nothing but move, with the same unhurried purpose the water itself has, and the same darkness to them, the same glassy slide, the same way of being there and then being somewhere else without any visible transition. I do not know if they are fish. I do not know if *fish* is the right category. I do not think the place has categories. I think the place has *occupations*, and the water-occupations are these dark slip-things, and the air-occupations are the flying chord-makers, and the wall-occupations are the slugs, and the floor-occupations are whatever the too-many-legged thing is, and they have all been at their occupations for so long that they have become the occupations themselves, become the verbs, lost the nouns.

I said the creatures do not fight. I will say it again. They do not fight. But I want to be careful: I do not want to say *they love each other*, or *they are at peace*, because that is a human word, a small word, and the thing that is going on here is not small. They are not at peace because peace is the absence of a thing, and there is no absence here, the place is full, every cubic inch of air and water and stone is *full* of something, and the things do not have room to fight because they are already occupying all the space they have. They are dense. They are *saturated*. The flying things pass through each other in the air, and the membrane of one will momentarily be inside the membrane of another, and they do not notice, or they notice and do not mind, and the chords do not falter, and the lantern-bulbs do not dim. I watched a small one pass straight through the trailing edge of a large one, and the small one came out the other side with its tone unchanged, and the large one's tone was unchanged, and the only evidence it had happened was a brief ripple in the larger one's membrane, like a bedsheet remembering a hand.

And the disc in the cliff face.

I have not touched it. I have thought about touching it. I have put my hand near it and felt the hum in my wristbones and drawn back, not from fear, not exactly, but from the sense that the hum was not for me, that the hum was the disc's way of being itself, the way a kettle's hum is its way of being a kettle, and that to lay a hand on it would be like laying a hand on a sleeping animal and expecting it to be the same animal when it woke.

I think it is a door. I have said so. I will take it back. I do not think it is a door. I think *door* is the word I have because the word I want has not been coined in any language I know, and a door is the closest thing, the way a chord is the closest word for what the flying creatures make, the way *geology that learned to do tricks* is the closest phrase I could find for the disc and the pillar and the bridge-of-nothing. The disc is a way through, perhaps. Or a way into. Or a way the gorge has of staying connected to something that is not the gorge, some larger version of the event, some other cleft on some other mountain where the same long song has been going on, and the disc is a window onto that song, a place where the song can be heard from the inside.

I have spent a long time near it now. The hum has not stopped. The light leaking from the seam has not brightened or dimmed. I have not solved the pattern of the slug-trails. I have not identified the body of the too-many-legged thing. I have not heard a single one of the flying creatures sing alone. I have not drunk from the water, though I have knelt beside it many times and seen the sky and the cliff faces trembling in the dark glass.

I am not going to leave. I want to be clear about what I mean by that. I do not mean I have decided to stay. I mean the word *leave* has become hard to use, the way *door* has become hard to use, the way *creature* has become hard to use. I do not think the gorge is holding me. I do not think I am trapped. I think I have been here long enough that the going and the staying have become the same verb, the way the singing and the breathing are the same verb for the things in the air. I am doing what the place is doing. I am one of the occupations now, or I am close to becoming one. The slugs are writing on me, probably, the slow lines of my days, and the patterns will be part of the wall when I am not. The flying things are passing through the air I am in, and their chords are braiding with whatever chord I have, and the disc at the up-canyon end is humming a note that has me in it, the way a river has the stones in it, the way a song has the singer in it.

The wind has come and gone three times. The ferns have bowed each time. The pillar-top bowl has rung its thin clear note each time. The disc has brightened each time, faintly, as if pleased. The water has kept its pace. The sounds have kept braiding. The light has kept falling.

I will not leave. I do not have the verb for it.
