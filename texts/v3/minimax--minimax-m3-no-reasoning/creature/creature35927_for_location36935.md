# Who Lives in the Drowned Mile

The carver is the first person you would meet, if the fish bridge had a person on it, which it usually does. She is not old in the way that the word *old* usually means; she is weathered the way a post is weathered, the way the oak of the bridge is weathered, so that you cannot tell which of her lines are age and which are the city's. She is small and her hands are the hands of someone who has held a chisel since she was very young, the fingers thickened at the tips and the knuckles like knots in hawthorn. She sits on the hump of the bridge, or stands in a doorway of the house beside it, or is not there at all, and the carving — the long-bodied fish, mouth open, mouth open, mouth open — is always a little fresher than the last time you saw it, the pale wood showing in the eye of one and then, weeks later, another, so that the whole bridge seems slowly to be waking up out of the dark.

She is also the oldest woman. She is also the fish carver. There is no one else who has been there longer, or at least no one who will say so, and so when the question of the water is argued on the market bridge, she is the one whose answer is taken, even when it is contradicted by the man with the chickens, which it always is. The argument is gentle. The argument is its own form of weather. *Is it higher this year than last?* She says yes. He says no. They look at the notch in the second step of the east stair, the notch no one now alive cut, and they nod, and the morning passes.

Her name is not asked. The Mile does not use names the way other places do; it uses locations, and uses you by where you are found. *The woman at the fish bridge.* *The boy at the south house.* It is enough.

---

The man with the chickens has a name no one uses, because he is *the man with the chickens*, and there are not so many men along the Mile that this is ambiguous. He is mid-aged in a way that seems almost aggressive, as though he has decided to be the kind of person who is exactly the age he appears to be and no more. His beard is uneven and he keeps it that way on purpose, he says, though no one believes him, and his hands smell of hen, which is to say of warm feathers and of a sharper thing underneath that is the smell of a bird's digestion, which is not the same as a human's and never will be.

The chickens are on the highest roof he can reach, which is the roof of what was once a schoolhouse, though no one remembers which kind. They are scrawny. They are survivors. The Mile is not a place for fat chickens. The man feeds them on what he can — the scrap of fish, the root that tastes of regret, the small green things that grow along the gutters in summer and that he calls, with a kind of fondness, *slum*. He talks to them. He is not ashamed of this. He tells them the news. He tells them the water is no higher than last year and they should not listen to the carver, who is going half-mad with the damp.

He has a daughter, or is said to. She does not appear on the bridges. She is said to be shy, or sickly, or simply not for company. The man with the chickens does not speak of her, and the others do not ask, which is a kindness the Mile has learned, perhaps from the water, which does not press.

---

There is a woman who lives three houses south of the market bridge, in what was once a chandler's shop and is now a kind of general and not-general store — general in that she has what she has, not-general in that she has no use for the word. She is broad and quiet and her hair is the color of the water in the bright spots, and she has the habit, when she speaks, of pausing in the middle of a sentence to listen to something no one else can hear, and then continuing, as though the sentence had only briefly stepped out.

She mends nets. She sells the root. She is the only person along the Mile who will weigh a fish for you, though the weight is mostly to settle an argument rather than a price, since the price is whatever you have, and what you have is usually a fish of your own, and so the transaction is, at its heart, a small ceremony of two people looking at each other's catch and nodding, or not nodding, and going home.

She has a husband who is also her brother, or is said to be. The Mile is small. The Mile does not ask. He is a man of few words and longer silences, and he can be seen on certain mornings sitting on the lip of a window three stories up, his legs hanging into nothing, watching the water with a patience that is almost liturgical, as though he is waiting for the drowned city to tell him something it has not yet finished saying.

---

The cats are not one people but many, though the Mile speaks of them as a single entity, the way one might speak of *the rain* or *the cold*. They are the color of the water, which is to say they are the color of a dozen different things — the color of old oak, the color of silt, the color of a black cat seen through a black cat's fur, the color of nothing, which is also a color, if you live here. Their eyes are the only bright thing about them, and even those are not bright so much as lit, in the way that a window is lit from inside when there is, in fact, no one inside.

The cats hunt in the drowned floors when the water drops. They leap from sill to sill in the green half-light below, and they go into rooms no one has opened, and they come back up smelling of old plaster and of something else, the something else being the subject of a small and permanent uneasiness among the humans of the Mile. It is not that the cats bring up anything; it is that they bring up the *smell* of something, and the smell is not the smell of a dead thing, or not only that, and so the mind supplies the rest, which is worse.

The cats are not tame. They are not wild. They are what the Mile is, in another shape — patient, and lit from within by something no one will name, and very good at keeping their own counsel.

The cats have a king, or so the children say. It is a large grey cat with one torn ear, and it lives in the granary of the rope-cradle woman, and it is the only creature she is known to love, or to feed, or to acknowledge. It is said that the king-cat can swim the length of the Mile underwater. It is said that the king-cat has been seen in rooms below the water that have not been dry in a hundred years. It is said that the king-cat, on certain nights, sits on the sill of a high window and sings. None of this is true. All of this is true. The Mile is a place where these two statements are not at odds.

---

The woman in the granary at the south end of the Mile is not spoken of with the casualness that the rest are spoken of. She is spoken of the way one speaks of the water — with a kind of respectful sideways glance, and a careful not-quite-looking.

She is tall. She is not young. She arrived at the granary a long time ago, and no one saw her come, and no one saw her cross, and when the question of the cradle is raised — and it is raised, by every visitor, by every child — the answer is the same: the cradle is sound, and a man once tried to follow her, and was very sorry.

What *sorry* means in this case is not made clear. The man is no longer at the Mile. The man may be at the bottom of the water. The man may be somewhere else, in some other way, and the word *sorry* may be the only word the Mile has for that kind of elsewhere. The woman does not explain. She crosses. She has her cat. She is alone in the way that the carver is alone, and the man with the chickens is alone, and the boy at the south house is alone — not lonely, not exactly, but settled into a solitude that is its own kind of marriage, to the granary, to the cradle, to the slow swing of the rope in a wind you can barely feel.

She is said to sing. She is said to sing to the water, or to the cat, or to the empty rooms below, and the song, if it is a song, is not one anyone else knows. The boy at the south house, who lives the closest to her, will not speak of it, which is how the Mile knows it must be true, and how the Mile knows it must be something to keep.

---

The boy at the south house is the last person you would meet, or the first, depending on which way you are walking. He is young. He is older than he looks, or younger, or both, in the way that a person is when they have been somewhere the rest of us have not. He is slight. His hair is the color of the cloud, which is to say he seems, in certain lights, to be only mostly there. He watches you from his doorway without speaking. He is holding, today, a single wooden cup.

He is said to be a little simple. He is said to mend the bridges. He is said to dive into the black water and come back up with a fish in each hand, which is probably true, and with a small wooden cup that was lost sixty years ago, which is probably not. He is the keeper of the small lost things, the boy at the south end. The cup, today. Yesterday, it was a brass button. Tomorrow, it will be something else, something that someone once lost and has been quietly mourning for a long time, and the boy will hand it over without a word, and the hand-over will be the entire conversation, and that will be enough.

He mends the bridges. He does it without being asked, and without explanation, and when a beam needs replacing, or a rope needs re-tying, or a cleat needs a new cleat, the boy appears, and the work is done, and the boy is gone again, and the bridge is the better for it, and no one thinks to thank him because he has already been thanked by the work itself.

He is not afraid of the water. He dives into it the way a person steps out of a doorway, and he comes back up holding whatever the water has decided to give him, and the water has decided, today, to give him a wooden cup. He holds it out, sometimes, to visitors. He holds it out the way a person holds out a hand. It is not clear if he is offering it, or asking for something in return, or simply showing you what he has found, the way a child shows you a stone, the way a king shows you a country.

---

The Mile, taken all together, is its own kind of people. The folk are not numerous. You might walk the length of it in a morning and see six people, or none. They are thin and brown, and they are good at fishing, and they argue gently on the market bridge about the height of the water, and they keep chickens on the high roofs, and they have a great many cats, and they do not speak of certain things, and they do not press, and they do not pry, and they have learned, over a hundred years of living in a place that breathes, to live the way the water lives — in, and out, and in again, and the going-out is not a loss, and the coming-in is not a gain, and the whole long breath of it is the city, and the city is them, and they are the city, and the city is asleep, and they are the dream, and the dream is a long-bodied fish, mouth open, mouth open, mouth open, repeating.
