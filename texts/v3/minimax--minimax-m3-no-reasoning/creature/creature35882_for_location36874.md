# Who Lives at Tengmire

They are few, and they are quiet, and the quiet is not the absence of sound — there is no absence of sound here — but a quality the people have in common, as though they have been trained to listen for things that live below the threshold of speech. They number about thirty. They have been more, in some seasons, and fewer, in others, but the basin holds no more than thirty bodies with any comfort, and the comfort is not the point. The point is the work.

The work is what they are.

The diggers arrive in the basin before dawn. This is not a rule; it is what their bodies do. The path down is treacherous in the dark and they know it in their feet, and so they come down in the grey hour when the eastern sky has just begun to declare itself, when the chipping from the day before has died and the basin still holds the cold of the night. They find their alcoves. They sleep, if they sleep, in rooms they have helped to carve, with lintels they have themselves fitted and hide curtains they have themselves cured. They share these rooms sometimes — two to an alcove is common, three if the alcove is deep — and they have arranged themselves, without discussion, by depth of work. The most senior sleep in the deepest alcoves, the ones closest to the central chamber. The newer diggers sleep higher up, where the rock is rougher and the weeping is constant, and the sound of the chimney comes to them only as a distant uncertain note.

A digger, of the older kind: her name is Ashael, though she is not the only one of her kind, and she is not necessarily their leader, though she is the one visitors tend to address first. She is small, and pale in the way that people become pale when they live indoors, and her hands are her most notable feature. They are wide and slightly flattened at the pads, the fingers ending in square tips that have lost all the whorls of fingerprints to years of pressure against chisel-handles. The nails are cut brutally short. The skin across the knuckles has gone thick and somewhat waxy, the color of old tallow. When she opens her hands they look almost like implements — as though she herself is part of the toolkit. She has been working on the frieze, she says, since before she could remember to count her years. She will not say anything else about this. She will offer you her hand if you are introducing yourself, and the handshake will feel like being shaken by a piece of shaped wood.

She works, when she works, on the deep polished sections, the parts of the frieze that look like they have been there forever. She does not chatter with the other diggers. She does not pause to look at her work from a distance. She is inside the work; she and the stone are a single enterprise. The young diggers, when they come near her on the wall, go quiet in the way that you go quiet near something you respect but do not quite understand. It is not awe, exactly. It is recognition.

The young diggers are rougher, and louder, and they have more to say — though even they do not say much. There is a man named Pevre who has been here perhaps eight years and still feels the newness of it; he sleeps in one of the higher alcoves, his curtain patched and re-patched with sinew, and his alcove is the smallest in the basin because he has been too busy with the rougher galleries to deepen it. He has the jaw of someone who used to argue, and the eyes of someone who has stopped. He is good, the other diggers will tell you, at the roughing-out — at the first violent reduction of a section of wall, the work where you have to swing a heavy iron-point and accept that the figures you are starting will not be the figures that emerge. He is patient with the stone in the way that a person is patient with something they do not yet trust. When he finishes a section he stands back for a long time and looks at it, and then he begins again.

There are perhaps four or five roughers at any time. Then there are the finishers — two or three who take a roughed section and bring it forward, deepening the lines, smoothing the curves, making the figures begin to surface from the rock. Ashael is a finisher, and so is a thinner, quieter man named Yuian who has been here longer than anyone remembers him arriving. Yuian is almost never seen in the daylight. He sleeps through the brightest hours and works by lamplight in the deeper galleries, where the finished carving has gone so far back into the wall that the light doesn't reach him anyway. He has not, in the years anyone has known him, spoken of what he is making. When he is interrupted he simply stops, sets down his tools, and waits. He does not seem to resent the interruption; he seems almost not to register it. He is one of the people who gives the settlement its quality of being perpetually just out of earshot of itself.

There are other roles, and the people who fill them are not quite diggers and not quite something else, though they call themselves diggers, because everyone here calls themselves that. The fire is the largest of these. Someone is always sitting near the spring-pool in the central chamber, and what they are doing, mostly, is nothing anyone could name. They are tending the fire, in the sense that they feed it and rake it, but mostly they are sitting with it. The current fire-tender is a woman whose name no one will volunteer to a visitor, because she does not want to be known, and the diggers respect this the way they respect the unfinished galleries — without comment, and entirely. She is older than the fire, the diggers say, in a voice that does not make clear whether this is jest or report. She knows the chimney's moods the way a sailor knows weather; she can tell you from the smoke's behavior whether the wind aloft is steady or veering, and the diggers trust her predictions more than they trust the sky. She drinks tea constantly. She will offer you tea. The tea is dark and slightly bitter and seems to be brewed from something she grows in a small wooden trough behind the fire — a slow green plant with leaves the color of pond-scum. You will not be told what it is.

There are a few people who do not work on the frieze and do not tend the fire. They are the most ordinary of the basin's residents, the ones you would notice least, and they are the ones who keep the settlement alive in the smaller senses. A man who cooks — not on the central fire, but on a smaller one built into an alcove of his own, and the smell of what he cooks is the only truly warm smell in the basin, and the diggers often drift toward him at the day's end. A woman who carries water from the spring to the higher alcoves; she is the most active body in the settlement, always in motion, always on some errand or another, and she knows every gallery and chisel-mark by feel. A person, perhaps young, perhaps not, who gathers the chips of stone that fall during the day's carving and piles them in a low cairn at the basin's western edge — a growing white mountain of fragments, slowly accumulating, as though the basin is exhaling its own substance.

The children, if there are children, are not seen. This is not remarked upon. The diggers will not say whether there have ever been children here. They will say only that the work is the meaning, and that the meaning does not require anyone in particular, and that those who come add themselves to it and those who leave leave, and the stone does not keep account.

The visitors, when they come — and they come rarely, and less rarely than they used to — are folded into the work without ceremony. There is a task. There is always a task. A visitor may be set to carrying water, or sorting chips, or sitting with the fire-tender while she is occupied elsewhere. The diggers do not interrogate them. They do not interrogate them later, either. The work itself either takes them or it doesn't. Those whom the work takes become quiet, and quick, and rather pale. Those whom it doesn't leave, after a time, and do not return, and the diggers do not remark on their going. The basin exhales them, the way the spring exhales its water, with no comment.

The visitors do not often write down what they have seen here. The ones who try cannot later remember it accurately. The chisel-marks blur in the memory into a single continuous band. The faces of the diggers blur into a kind of pattern. The sound of the chipping, which at the time had a quality so immediate it seemed to be happening inside the listener's head, becomes, in recollection, only a sound, indistinguishable from any other sound of small repeated blows. The wind in the chimney, which had seemed almost to speak, becomes simply the wind in the chimney. The spring at the center, which the visitor remembers always as burning, becomes only a pool of water in the floor of a strange room.

Those who stay longer do not have this problem. They learn, slowly, that the basin is not strange. That it is the place they have been coming to. That the figures on the walls are not done, and they are not undone, and they are perhaps not figures at all but something the stone is teaching the diggers, very slowly, in the only language the stone knows.

Their hands become like Ashael's hands.

Their eyes become like Yuian's eyes.

The water runs.

The chipping goes on.
