**The Inhabitants of the Kneaded Plateau**

To call them *trees* is to use the wrong tool on a nail made of meat. They are not *of* the forest so much as they *are* the forest, and the forest, in turn, is the digestive system of the plateau itself. They are a single, distributed organism — or a colony so tightly integrated that the distinction has ceased to matter — a vast, cold-blooded metabolism turned outward, grinding the geometry of the world into a paste it can breathe.

But the eye, even an unaided human eye, craves a center. It looks for a creature, a self, a thing with a face. And so the eye finds the **Pale Steppers**.

They move in the mires between the great woody columns, low and sinuous, eel-thick, their skins the color of peeled willow: a bleached, bloodless white that drinks the dim, sideways light. They do not slither, exactly. They *articulate*. Their spines are too long for their bodies, or their bodies are too short for their spines, and the effect is of a thing constantly folding and unfolding itself, a knot of pale muscle slipping through the saturated peat. Where they pass, the bog closes over their wakes with a slow, watery sigh, as if the substrate resents being reminded of its own liquidity.

They are not predators, or at least not primarily. They are the first stage of the harvest. The great columns above — the grinding factories, the mills — exhale a processed vapor into the cold air: a thicker, heavier mist, laden with whatever the lattices have fractionated from the original sky. The Steppers move through this exhaled breath with their mouths pressed to the surface of the muck, breathing it in through gill-slits that run the length of their flanks. They are the lungs of the lower world, taking what the sky-feeders have already chewed and reducing it further. They are pale because they have never seen the sun. They are slow because urgency is a luxury in a place where time has been kneaded into dough.

Above them, riding the stilts of their own too-tall legs, stand the **Watchers**.

They are the color of weathered bone. They are mostly eye — a single, vast, lidless lens set in a head that is otherwise just a hood of translucent cartilage. They stand motionless in the gaps between the columns, eight, ten, twelve feet off the wet ground, and they do not blink because they have no lids. They do not need to blink. What they watch is not movement, exactly. The Watchers are sensitive to *gradient* — to the slow, imperceptible shifts in the way the mist falls, to the faintest changes in the angle of the lattices above, to the long, deep breaths of the substrate beneath. They are the nervous system of the plateau, distributed across a hundred stilts, each one a peripheral nerve ending tuned to the pulse of the great mills.

When a gradient shifts — when the geometry of the plateau flexes a little too far, when the kneaded ground begins to forget its own shape more than usual — a ripple of movement passes through the Watchers. A head turns, fractionally. A stilt adjusts. The change is registered, and the information radiates outward through the constant, muffled soundscape: a low, dry *click* that is picked up by the Swarmer-things and translated into motion.

The **Swarmer-things** are small. They are the only small things here. They are the color of old iron, and they live on the firmest ridges of the bog, where the peat has been worked so long it has become something almost mineral. They are jointed in too many places, and their joints bend in directions that imply a geometry of the body that the rest of the world does not subscribe to. They move in quick, chittering bursts, and their sound — a high, dry clicking, like a thousand small stones being struck together in a hollow room — is the only quick sound in the place. Everything else moves at the speed of grinding.

They are the clean-up crew. They are the white blood cells of the plateau. When a Steppers dies — when a pale eel-things finally fails to fold itself correctly and lies still in the muck, dissolving back into the cold peat — the Swarmer-things find it within minutes, or within hours, or within a day. They reduce it. They eat the meat and leave the bone, and the bone is taken up into the substrate, and the substrate is churned by the unseen cylinders beneath, and what was a Steppers becomes, in time, a thin layer of mineral in the peat, which becomes, in deeper time, the dark, wet foundation upon which the next generation of mills will stand. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves. The plateau eats itself, and grows from the eating, and the eating has been going on for so long that the original stone of the mountain has been ground down to a memory of stone, and the memory has been ground down further, and what remains is almost nothing at all, and the almost nothing is the plateau, and the plateau is still, against all expectation, running.

And above them all, or beside them all, or woven through them all, the **Mills** continue.

They are not named, because no one has ever stood close enough to one to name it and survived the listening. They are the columns. They are the grinding factories. They are the reason the air is always moving, always rearranging, always propagating in slow banks like waves in a medium thicker than air. Their roots — vast, unseen, turning in cylinders beneath the bog — are not roots at all, or not only roots. They are pistons. They are the engines of a machine that has been running so long it has forgotten how to stop, and in the forgetting it has become a kind of life, and in the becoming-life it has built itself a body out of wood and lattice and hydraulic rod, and the body stands upon the plateau and breathes the mist and exhales the mist and the mist is heavier now, and darker, and it falls back down through the lattices, and the cycle continues.

The Mills do not think. The Mills do not feel. The Mills are the plateau's way of remembering that it is still here.

And so the inhabitants continue their long, cold, wet, dim, loud, turbulent, and quietly wrong commerce with one another. The Steppers breathe the breath. The Watchers watch the watching. The Swarmers clean the cleaning. The Mills grind the grinding. The geometry of the world, beneath their feet, continues to flex and forget and be remade.

It is an industrial ecology. It is a pure-plant ecology. It is ancient, cold, wet, sparse, dim, loud, turbulent, abundant with strange fauna, and quietly, persistently, wrong.

It has been here a long time. It intends to continue.
