The chamber's inhabitants are not visitors. They are what the chamber has become — the slow, accumulated answer the brick has given back to the warm dark. To stand in the room is to stand inside a single, breathing thing whose organs are smaller than a finger and whose breath is the rising of the warm air from beneath the floor and the falling of the drops from above. The eye, when it first descends the stairwell, expects to find a place. It finds, instead, a community — or the kind of community that has stopped being a community and has become a body.

The walls are its skin. The lowest three feet of brick are furred with a soot-black fungus that climbs in a tide-mark, as though the room were weeping from below and the weeping had grown a velvet. Higher up, in the damper seams, pale shelf-fungus brackets out in rows, each one a small ledge the size of a thumb-pad, layered like the pages of a book that is only ever read from one side. They look, for a moment, like the remains of wooden shelving, until the eye sees that they are growing, that they are eating the brick slowly, the way the brick once ate the light. From the dome hang the threads — long, pale, almost weightless, drifting in curtains that the condensation keeps heavy and that the barely-moving air makes to sway. They are not spun by anything you would call a spider. They are the room's own hair.

The slugs live on the skin. They are the room's grazers. Each one is the length of a finger and the color of a peeled almond, and there are so many of them that to look at the lower walls is to see the walls in motion, a slow upward and downward tide of pale bodies leaving behind them the silver tracks of their passage. The tracks dry to silver, then to a faint dark line that the next wetness will wash away and the next slug will trace again. They eat the fungus. They are eaten in their turn. A slug moves up the wall at the pace a clock's hand moves around its dial; a newt waits at the bottom of a seam, sleek and black as a wet seed, and at some moment the slug crosses the threshold of the seam and is gone. The newt pulls it in slowly, the way a thread is pulled into a needle's eye. The silver track continues a little way past the seam, then stops.

The newts are the room's quick blood. They live in the zone where the floor meets the wall, in the dark damp where the water sits longest, and they move in the way that quick things move among slow things — by fits, by vanishings. You see one. You do not see it. You hear, instead, the small wet sound it makes as it goes, the sound of a thumb drawn off a slate, and you understand that the room is full of these small disappearings, that the walls are stippled with the doorways of things that have just left. They eat the slugs. They eat, perhaps, the smaller fry that wander too close to the floor's edge. They use the fallen shards of clay vessels for cover, slipping between two pieces of pot as a fox slips into a hedge. In the basins along the far wall — the brick-lined basins that the room has silted up to within a hand's breadth of the rim — they have made themselves a smaller room, a still wetter room, and they lie there in clusters, breathing through their skins, and the water that gathers around them is theirs.

The centipedes live in the seams. They are rust-colored, and many-legged, and they move with the particular patience of things that are not built for stopping. They do not graze. They hunt. They go into the cracks in the brickwork and come out of other cracks, sometimes carrying something, sometimes not, and the going-in and the coming-out are so quiet that you know them only by what they leave — a fragment of a beetle's lacquered back on the lip of a crevice, a half-eaten spider folded into a corner. They are the room's long memory. They are what the room uses to keep its accounts.

The spiders are not many, but they are everywhere. They are small and grey and they do not build webs. What they build is a single thread, weighted with dew, strung across a channel of water from one brick to the next, and the thread is so fine that you see it only when the light catches the dew along its length, when it becomes for a moment a line of strung pearls, and then a gnat strikes it and the pearls jump and the spider drops from its place in the brickwork above and does what spiders do. The threads are fishing lines. The channels are the room's slow rivers, and the room has learned to fish them.

In the water the fry are the room's long, silver question. They are not native here. Nothing is native here; the room did not come with anything living in it, and what lives in it now came in the way things come in to rooms that no one is guarding. The eggs came down the stairwell on the feet of birds. The eggs came down inside the bellies of birds. The eggs came down in the wet leaves that the autumn storms blew through the grating far above. The eggs came down on the boots of whoever last walked the worn stairs, and on the things those boots carried. The fry that hatched from them are small, dark-backed, silver-bellied, and they swim in the film of water between the silt and the brick in such numbers that the channels seem to be made of them. They rise, sometimes, with a small silver yawn to gulp at the air, and then they are gone again. They eat what falls into the water — the beetles that fall from the ceiling and cannot right themselves in time, the gnats that the dew weighs down until they drown, the soft bodies of the things that die on the brick above and are washed down by the next trickle. They are the room's small conversion. They take what falls and return it to the channels.

The insects are the room's voice. Near the dome, where the condensation gathers heaviest, the gnats hang in a fog so dense that the eye registers them as a faint, moving dimness of the air itself, and the ear registers them as a single, fine, continuous note, like the note of a glass when a wet finger runs around its rim. Lower down, the moths settle on the brick with their wings folded, and they are the color of the brick, and they do not move, and the eye passes over them and passes over them again and does not see them until one of them shifts and the brick is, for an instant, the wrong color. The beetles are heavy and lacquered and they live higher up than the slugs, in the drier brick, and they fall — they fall continually, with a small dry report, and they lie on their backs and wave their legs in the air, and most of them right themselves in time, and some do not, and the ones that do not are taken by the channels. There are insects you cannot see. There is a high, dry ticking from one corner, as though a clock the size of a pinhead were losing its seconds into the brick. There is a low, continuous hum from another corner, felt more than heard, as though a wire were singing in the damp. There is a sharp, infrequent chirp from a third, a chirp that is too clean for the room, too percussive, as if the chirper were striking a tiny bell. None of them are in tempo with any other. The room is a music of small instruments, each played by a different small musician, and the music has no conductor.

And beneath all of it — beneath the slugs and the newts and the fry, beneath the beetles and the chirp and the hum — beneath even the slow tick of the brick itself, there is the warmth. The warmth is the room's furnace and the room's secret and the reason any of the rest of it is here at all. It rises through the bronze cap at the center, the bronze cap that was once a fire-pot and is now a well-cap and is being thinned, grain by grain, by the traffic of so many small feet across it. It rises through the brick under the brick. It rises because somewhere below this floor, somewhere the brick sits on, the earth is warm, and has been warm for longer than the brick has been here. The room did not make the warmth. The room found the warmth, the way the birds found the stairwell, the way the eggs found the water. The room is what the warmth has made, in the slow conversation between heat and damp and the patience of small things.

The room does not know it is a room. The slugs do not know there is a stairwell above. The newts do not know there is a dome. The fry do not know they came down on the feet of birds. Each of them knows only the brick it is on, the seam it is in, the drop of water that hangs above it, and the next thing it will eat or be eaten by. And yet the room is whole. The mortar is falling and the room holds. The brick is bowing inward and the room holds. The bronze cap is thinning and the room holds, and will hold, because the balance that has been struck between the warmth from below and the wetness from above is older than any one of its small lives, and the next small life will take the place of the last, and the channels will adjust, and the fungi will grow a little faster when the bronze is gone, and the gnats will breed a little faster, and the newts will have more to eat, and the room will be louder, and the room will be warmer, and the room will go on being what it is.

It is a place that has forgotten what it was. It is a place that has remembered what it is.
