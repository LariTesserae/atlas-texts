## The Sediment People

The dwellers are not one people. This is the first thing to say, and the source text is careful about it: they are a *sediment*, a slow accumulation, a place where many kinds of creatures have come to rest over the long generations and have, without quite intending to, become something shared. The word *sediment* is not quite right either, because sediment implies a layering, a settling-by-weight, and what has happened here is more like a slow interpenetration, the way two trees planted close together will grow into each other until the grain of their wood is continuous at the place of touching.

There are, as best I can reckon, three principal kinds.

**The tall pale ones.** They are the ones a visitor sees most often on the middle and upper terraces, and they are the ones the source text lingers with, because they are the ones who most often sit on the low walls in the long evenings and most often offer the kind of silence that can be shared. They are tall — taller than most humans, though not by much — and very pale, the color of the stone they live among, and they have long hands, longer in the fingers than in the palm, with a span that seems, when they sit beside you on a wall, slightly too generous for the body it belongs to. They move without sound. This is not a trick of the acoustics; the galleries carry small noises very far, and a footfall on the warm, slightly yielding pavement would be audible at a great distance if it were made. They do not make it. They set their feet down in a way that I can only describe as *asking*, as if each step were a small negotiation with the surface it was placed upon, and the surface answered by accepting the weight in silence.

Their faces are quiet in a way that is hard to describe. They do not lack expression; they have many expressions, and a visitor who stays long enough begins to learn them. But the expressions are slow, and they move across the face like light moving across water, and they are not, most of them, expressions of emotion in the way a visitor from elsewhere would understand emotion. They are expressions of *attention*, of a deep and sustained orientation toward whatever is in front of them. When a tall pale one looks at you, you feel that you are being read, but gently, and without judgment, and over a very long period of time.

**The shorter darker ones.** They live mostly on the lower and middle terraces, and they have a different relationship to the place. Where the tall pale ones seem to be in a kind of courteous dialogue with the galleries, the shorter darker ones seem to be in a kind of *contract* with the plants. They know the plants. This is the only way I can put it. They know them by name, or by some equivalent of name, and the plants know them in return, and the two knowledges are the same knowledge held from two sides.

They are darker — a deep brown, the color of old wood or of the bronze shrubs that grow on the lower terraces — and they are compact and quick in their movements, though quick here means something different than quick elsewhere. They move with a kind of unhurried deliberation that can nonetheless cover ground quickly when they choose to. Their hands are smaller than the hands of the tall pale ones, and more deft, and they use them to touch the plants as they pass, a kind of brushing gesture, a kind of greeting, and the plants respond to the touch in small ways — a slight leaning-in, a slight opening, a slight turning toward the hand — that I could not have believed if I had not seen it many times.

The shorter darker ones are the ones who tend the flutes, in so far as the flutes can be said to be tended. They adjust the angle of the clusters when the angle has drifted. They clean out the hollow interiors when debris has fallen in. They do these things with a kind of cheerful matter-of-factness, the way a gardener elsewhere might prune a hedge, and the flutes accept the attention without complaint, and the chord of the terrace does not change much when they are done, because the maintenance is the continuation of an old agreement and not an imposition.

**The small shy ones.** They live in the lowest galleries, in the dense growth at the edges of the powder plain, and they come out mostly at the long amber twilights, and they are shy. This is the word the source text uses, and it is the right one. They are small — half the height of the shorter darker ones, perhaps, or two-thirds — and they are pale in a different way than the tall pale ones are pale, a kind of translucent, faintly luminous pale, as if they had absorbed some of the quality of the fruit they almost certainly eat. They have large eyes, and the eyes are dark, and they look at you with a kind of startled attention when you happen upon them, and then they are gone, back into the lower vines, and you hear the soft small sounds of their passage for a long time afterward.

I did not learn much about the small shy ones. I do not think they wanted to be learned about. I think they are, in some sense, the oldest layer of the sediment, the first comers, the ones who were here before the place became what it is now, and who have chosen, over the long generations, to become small and shy and to live at the edges of the life of the place. ## The Conversation of Plants

To call the plants of Velmoth *plants* is, as the source text acknowledges, to use a word that does not fit. They are not plants in the way an oak is a plant, or a fern, or a sea-grass. They are something else, something for which there is no word in the languages I bring with me. They are participants. They are *co-inhabitants*, in the deepest sense of that word, and they have been co-inhabiting with the dwellers for so long that the categories of plant and dweller have become, in places, difficult to maintain.

**The flutes** are the simplest case, and I will start with them because they are the easiest to describe, though even they are not entirely easy. They are vertical structures, pale, the color of the stone they grow from, and they rise in clusters from the cracks between the paving of the terraces — three pipes, or five, or seven, like a small organ built for a building that does not need an organist. They are open at the top and closed at the bottom. They are hollow. They are roughly the height of a man's chest and the thickness of his arm. When the wind blows across their open tops, they make a sound, and the sound is a low note, and each cluster plays a slightly different note, and the result is a chord, or a weather, depending on how you listen to it.

Are they plants? They grow. They have a kind of biological structure, with layers and fibers visible if you cut one open (I did not cut one open; the shorter darker ones would have been distressed). They seem to require water, of a kind — the thin amber liquid of the lower pool, perhaps, carried up through the stone by some process I did not understand. But they also make music, and they make the music deliberately, in the sense that their shape is what determines the note, and the shape has been, over the long generations, adjusted by the shorter darker ones, and the adjustment is a kind of cultivation, and the cultivation is a kind of composition. The flutes are instruments, but they are also alive. They are both things at once, and the both-ness is not a compromise; it is, I think, the point.

**The vines** are harder. They are the trailing things that hang over the edges of the terraces in long slow curtains, and they move in the wind, but they also move in response to the dwellers. They have a memory. This is the only word I can use. They learn the paths that are walked, and they draw themselves back from those paths before the walker arrives, and they return to their position after the walker has passed. The drawing-back is not fast. It is slow enough that you could, if you wanted to, walk faster than the vine retreats, and catch it, and hold it. I never did this. The arrangement between the vine and me was that I would not, and the vine knew that I would not, and I knew that the vine knew, and the knowledge was part of the long conversation.

How does the vine know? I do not know. I have theories, none of which I trust. Perhaps it senses the weight on the paving, the slight compression of the warm yielding surface, and reads the weight as a walker, and calculates the walker's trajectory, and moves accordingly. Perhaps it has another sense, something that does not have a name. Perhaps the vine is not a single organism but a kind of distributed intelligence, with many small decision-making parts connected by the long trailing stems, and the memory is held in the connection rather than in any one part. I do not know. I did not ask the shorter darker ones about this, because I understood, without being told, that it was not the kind of thing one asked.

**The bronze shrubs** are the strangest of the plants I came to know, and they are the ones that produce the fruit. The shrubs are low and dense, the color of old bronze, and they grow in patches on the middle and upper terraces, often near the outer walls, where the light is best. They flower rarely, and the flowering is a slow event — a long slow opening of small pale flowers that have no scent I could detect, and the flowers last for what seems like a season, and then they fall, and the fruit sets. The fruit is the size of a small apple, and the color of the clearest part of the amber sky, and it has no seeds and no core. Inside, when you cut it open, is a soft, faintly luminous flesh that tastes of nothing you have ever tasted.

The dwellers eat the fruit with reverence. They do not speak while they are eating it. They sit very still afterward, and their eyes are brighter. I do not know what the fruit does. I suspect that it does not *do* anything, in the sense of producing an effect; I suspect that it *is* something, that it is a small piece of the place's own nature made briefly available to the eater, and that eating it is a kind of communion. I did not eat the fruit. I came close. I held one in my hand, in the long evening, and felt its weight, and felt the faint luminescence of it against my palm, and I gave it back to the dweller who had offered it. She ate it, and sat very still, and did not seem to mind that I had not.

There are other plants. I will not enumerate them all. There are the unnamed growths of the upper empty galleries, which I saw only at a distance and which I will not try to describe from memory. There are the soft dense masses that fill the spaces between the paving stones, and which breathe, very slightly, with the rhythm of the place. There are small things that grow from the air in the upper shafts, or seem to, hanging in long pale threads that catch the light. There are things I am sure I did not see, in the parts of the place I did not visit, in the higher galleries still, in the depths of the lower pools. The place is full. The fullness is the conversation.

## The Small Lives

The fauna of Velmoth is, like the flora, difficult to categorize. The creatures are not, most of them, what we would recognize as animals. They are something else, or they are several something-elses, and they occupy the niches of the place in a way that suggests a long negotiation.

**The many-legged small things** of the lower vines are the ones I saw most often, in the long twilights when the small shy dwellers were also abroad. They are about the size of a squirrel, but they are not squirrels. They have too many legs — seven, I think, or eight, or possibly nine, depending on how you count the small grasping forelegs that they use to hold leaves as they eat. They move with a kind of deliberate and graceful caution, and they have small faces that are almost faces, and they look at you when you look at them, and they are not afraid.

Their not-being-afraid is important. They are not tame, exactly; they do not come to the hand, and they do not accept food from it. But they do not run from a still watcher, and they will go on with their small business — the eating of leaves, the tending of something in the lower vines, the occasional low sound that might be a call to others of their kind — while a visitor sits three or four paces away and watches. They seem to have decided, in some collective and ancient way, that the watchers are not a threat, and this decision is not reconsidered. I found this very moving, though I am not sure I can say why.

**The tall pale thing** of the upper empty galleries is one I saw only once, at a great distance, on a high terrace in the amber light of a long afternoon. It was tall — taller than the tall pale dwellers, and thinner, and paler in a way that made it almost invisible against the pale stone. It moved without sound, the way the tall pale dwellers move without sound, but it moved differently, with a kind of long loping gait that seemed to cover a great deal of ground in a single motion. It did not look at me. I do not think it knew I was there. It was on a different terrace, and the air was thin, and the light was wrong for seeing. I watched it for what seemed like a long time, and then it was gone, around a corner of stone, and I did not see it again.

I do not know what it was. I have wondered, often, whether it was a dweller of some kind, a solitary one, an older kind, or whether it was something else entirely — a creature of the place rather than a creature in the place. I have wondered whether the upper empty galleries have their own kind of dweller, a dweller that does not come down to the lower terraces, that has its own arrangement with the flutes and the vines and the unnamed growths. I have no answer. I have the memory of a long pale shape moving in amber light, and the memory is enough.

**The small flying things** of the evening are the color of the sky, and they are difficult to see, and the dwellers do not remark on them. They move through the air in long slow arcs, and they have no sound that I could hear, and they appear in the amber light as small momentary dimmings of the luminance, as if the sky had briefly drawn breath. I do not know what they are. I do not know if they are animals, in any sense I would recognize. I do not know if they eat, or sleep, or reproduce, or have any of the categories of life I am used to. I watched them for many evenings, and they did not change, and I did not learn them, and I came to think that this was appropriate, that there are some inhabitants of a place that are not for learning, and that the not-learning is a kind of respect.

**The pool creatures** — the small pale fish or eels that live in the lower pool when it has liquid in it — are the ones I understand least, and the ones I find myself thinking about most. They are small, and pale, and they come to the surface when the dwellers kneel beside the pool. They look at the dwellers. The dwellers look at them. There is a kind of exchange. Then the creature sinks, and the dweller rises.

What is exchanged? I do not know. I knelt beside the pool, once, in the long evening, and I looked into it, and the liquid was the color of the amber sky, and my reflection looked back at me with that slow and altered regard the source text describes, and I waited for a long time, and no creature came. I do not think the creature comes for visitors. I think it comes for the dwellers, and for the small shy ones, and that the exchange is part of the long conversation, and that I was not yet part of the conversation in the way that would permit the creature to come.

## The Place Itself

I have left this for last because it is the hardest to write about, and because the source text is right that the place will not be separated from its inhabitants, and that any attempt to do so falsifies what is actually the case.

The place is alive.

I do not mean this in a metaphorical sense, or not only in a metaphorical sense. The paving that is not quite paving, the warm slightly yielding surface of the lowest terraces, the one that creaks beneath you with the sound of vast slow adjustments — it is alive. It is some kind of organism, or some kind of collective of organisms, or some kind of process that is not usefully described as either. It is the substrate from which the flutes grow and in which the vines root and on which the dwellers walk. It is the lowest layer of the conversation, the one that holds all the others up, and it holds them up in a way that is not passive. It responds. It creaks. It yields slightly under the weight of a walker. It carries, I think, the memory of the place in its substance, the way the rings of a tree carry the memory of years.

The flutes grow from it. The vines grow from it. The bronze shrubs grow from it. The pools collect in its basins. The wind moves over it. The light falls on it. The dwellers walk on it, and sleep on it, and eat their luminous fruit on it, and look into the pools that collect in it, and live their long slow lives on it. And it, the substrate, the warm yielding stone that is not quite stone, holds them all, gently, for a very long time.

I do not know what to call it. The source text does not give it a name. The dwellers, I think, do not have a name for it either, because to name it would be to separate it from themselves, and they have not separated it from themselves, and they will not. It is the place. It is the long galleries. It is the slow wheel and the amber light and the low chord of the flutes and the soft breathing of the unnamed growths and the vast quiet patience of the stone that holds it all together, the way a hand holds a small and delicate thing.

## The Whole Conversation

I said at the beginning that there is no clean way to do this, and I have not done it cleanly, and I am glad. The place is not clean. It is full, and the fullness is a conversation, and the conversation is slow, and it has been going on for a very long time, and a visitor who arrives and tries to parse it into categories — dweller, plant, animal, place — is missing the thing that makes it what it is. The thing that makes it what it is is that none of these categories is finally distinct from any of the others. The dwellers are partly made of the place; the plants are partly dwellers; the place is partly alive in the way the dwellers are alive; the small animals are partly plants in their patience and their long memory; the pool is a dweller of a kind, and the substrate is a pool of a kind, and the wind is a creature, and the light is a kind of nourishment.

I have been changed by trying to describe this. I do not think I have described it well. I do not think it can be described well, in any language I have access to. But I have tried, and the trying has been a kind of participation, and the participation has been a kind of learning, and the learning has been a kind of love, though the word is too small, and I am not sure it is exactly right.

The slow wheel turns. The amber light falls. The flutes play their chord. The vines draw back from the paths of those who walk them. The dwellers sit in their doorways and on their walls and do not speak. The small shy ones come out at the long twilights and look at the world with their large dark eyes and go back into the vines. The pool waits. The fruit ripens in its long season. The many-legged small things go on with their small business. The tall pale thing moves along the high terrace, and is gone. The small flying things move in their long slow arcs through the amber air. The wind comes up from the plain in the morning and dies down in the evening, and comes again from the west in the night, cooler, carrying the smell of old stone. The substrate creaks beneath the weight of it all, and holds, and holds, and holds.

That is what lives in the Long Galleries of Velmoth. That is what I have been trying to say.
