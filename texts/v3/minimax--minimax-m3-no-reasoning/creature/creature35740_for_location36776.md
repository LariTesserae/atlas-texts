# Who Lives in the Cleft

They call themselves *the Cleft-People*, though the word in their own tongue is closer to *the Ones-Who-Sit-Between* — a phrase that contains no noun for *people*, because in their thinking the cedars and the springs and themselves are all of the same grammatical kind, all *sitters-between*, all negotiators of the in-between. A child is taught, before she is taught to speak her own name, the names of the four springs, the names of the seven winds, and the name of the moss-line, which is a single word that means at once *the height to which a wall is allowed to grow*, *the agreement*, and *the bone of the mother*.

There are perhaps nine hundred of them now, though no one has ever counted in a way that produced a number anyone trusted. The chief's tally-rope, kept separately from the year-rope, has nine hundred and eleven knots, but three of the knots are disputed — a child whose family left the cleft and was not mourned, an elder whose family insists she died before she was finished dying, a baby whose name was spoken only once and so did not, in the reckoning, fully arrive. The chief has let the matter stand. The disputed knots are left in the rope. They are part of the count and not part of the count, simultaneously, and this is felt to be correct.

## The Shape of Them

They are not large. A tall man from the lowlands, visiting, would stand head and shoulders above the tallest Cleft-man, and the Cleft-people would find him faintly comic in the way of a person who has not had to make himself small against a wind. They are broad through the chest and the shoulders, narrow through the hips, with the particular set of the head and shoulders that comes from a lifetime of looking down — at roots, at channels, at the place where the walkway meets the next walkway, at the small bright bodies of the glass-eels moving in the water below. Their eyes are wide-set and dark. Their hair is thick and black and is worn, by everyone old enough to choose, in a single fat braid that hangs between the shoulder-blades and is greased, daily, with rendered pig-fat, so that it shines in the gray-green light like a wet rope. Their hands are the most distinctive thing about them: broad, the palms heavily calloused across the thenar and along the outer edge, the fingers slightly splayed. A Cleft-person's hand, laid flat on a cedar beam, covers almost twice the surface of a lowlander's. They are hands shaped by a lifetime of work on curved wood, of coaxing roots around corners, of pressing mats into place against wet stone.

Their skin is dark — darker than brown, darker than the brown of the traders who climb up from below, a deep blue-black that looks almost green in certain lights, especially against the orange of the lichen. The traders sometimes ask, with the careful rudeness of outsiders, whether the Cleft-people are *of the same kind* as themselves. The Cleft-people find the question strange and answer it obliquely: *we are of the same kind as the cedars*, they say, which is a joke, but also not entirely a joke. There is an old word, *hook-beak-colored*, used for the deepest of their skin tones, and they are not offended by it.

They do not, as a rule, grow facial hair. The men shave with sharpened shell against the grain, and the women pluck with tweezers of bone, and the result is that the faces of the Cleft-people are smooth and curiously young-looking well into what a lowlander would call old age. The elders — and there are many elders, because the cleft is a place where people live long, though no one is sure why, though the children say it is because the mist is kind — the elders have faces like polished river-stones, round and hairless and very still, and their eyes are the color of the deepest channel-water, almost black, and they watch you without blinking for a long time before they decide what you are.

## The Work of Them

They work in water.

This is the first thing an outsider notices and the last thing he understands. Every task in Suwarrow's Reach is performed with the hands in, or over, or beside running water. A woman cooking does not stand at a fire but kneels beside a channel, with a tray of tubers set into the current to boil. A man carving a hook-beak beak into a chisel does it with his feet in a shallow trough of warm water to keep the bone from splitting. A child gathering sloth-moss droppings — which is in fact a valuable compost, traded down the mountain — does it with bare feet in the runoff at the lowest platforms, hopping from stone to stone, laughing when she slips. The constant warmth and wetness shapes their days in a way no clock could: there are hours named for temperatures of water (*the-prick-of-heat*, *bath-warm*, *the-cool-edge*), and the day begins when the springs are at their morning-pulse and ends when the hook-beaks go silent, and between those two events the people move up and down the cleft with the water, working where it is hot, bathing where it is warm, emptying the lowest platforms where the runoff pours over and is gone.

Their tools are few and good. They have no metal — the traders sometimes offer it, and it is accepted politely and never used, because a metal blade will not shape a cedar the way a beak-chisel will, and a metal pot will not set into a channel the way a clay tray will, and the Cleft-people have learned, across many centuries, that the things they have are the things that work in the cleft, and the things that work in the cleft are not the things that work anywhere else. A hook-beak beak, split and ground, holds an edge that will shape cedar for a season before it needs to be replaced. A bone needle, polished with pig-fat, will stitch a mat of split-bamboo tighter than any metal could. A clay pot, fired in the heat of the upper channels, will hold boiling water without cracking because it was *born* in boiling water.

The most important tool is the *root-hook*, a short curved stick of bone lashed to a cedar handle, used to guide the growth of the living architecture. Every adult carries one. A root-hook is passed from parent to child, usually, though sometimes a particularly clever one is willed to a grandchild, and the receiving of it is a ceremony. The hook is used to pry a young cedar-root away from a walkway it is threatening to overgrow, or to wedge a root into a new shape, or to test the give of a beam to see if it is still alive at the heart. A Cleft-person can tell, by the feel of a beam under the heel of the hand, whether the tree inside it is healthy or dying, in the way that a lowlander can tell, by looking at a face, whether a person is well or unwell. The root-hook is the extension of this sense.

## The Speaking of Them

Their language is dense with verbs of temperature and verbs of growth.

A lowlander, learning it, gives up in confusion after a few weeks. There are, by some counts, forty-seven distinct verbs for *to flow*, each one specifying a different temperature, a different speed, a different sound, a different relationship to the air above the water. There are nineteen verbs for *to bend* — a living thing can be bent, coaxed, persuaded, forced, begged, or sung into a new shape, and each of these is a different verb, and a person who uses the wrong one in the wrong context has committed a kind of small social crime. There is a verb that means *to grow into the shape of the house you are being made into*, and it is used only of cedars, and it is considered one of the most beautiful words in the language.

They do not write. They have no writing. They have the year-rope, which is not writing but is a kind of memory made physical, and they have the songs, which are also a kind of memory made physical, and they have the stories told in the long evenings beside the warm channels when the hook-beaks have gone quiet and the water is loud. The songs are mostly short. The stories are mostly long. A skilled storyteller can take three nights to tell the story of how the third spring opened, and the listeners will sit without moving, with their feet in the water, and the children will fall asleep against their mothers' shoulders and be carried up to bed without waking.

The chief speaks the language with a particular formality. He uses the long forms of verbs that have shortened in everyday speech, and he addresses the elders in a register that is almost a separate dialect, and he opens every public utterance with a phrase that translates roughly as *let the water be heard first*, which is a literal instruction — there is a small ceremony in which everyone falls silent for the space of ten breaths and listens to the springs — and also a kind of blessing. After the silence, the chief may speak, and what he says is usually about water-channels, because most of his work is about water-channels: when to open them for cleaning, when to close them for repair, when to reroute a channel around a cedar-root that has decided to grow through the walkway and must, with great ceremony and apology, be cut back.

## The Keeping of Them

The chief is not a king.

He is a *keeper-of-the-water*, and his authority extends only to the channels and to the things the channels touch, which is to say, almost everything. He cannot tell a family how to prune its tree. He cannot command a child to gather sloth-moss droppings or refuse to. He cannot order a marriage or prevent one. He can, however, decide that the upper channels will be closed for two days in the dry-season so that the cooking-stones can be re-blackened, and when he decides this, the whole town rearranges itself around his decision, because the cooking-stones are communal and the re-blackening is a festival and the closing of the upper channels is felt, in some way, as a kind of communal breath held. He can also arbitrate disputes over root-rights, and this is his more delicate work, because a root-right is a relationship that has been ongoing, sometimes, for six generations, and the chief who rules badly on a root-right can unmake a family.

There are other kinds of authority in the cleft, all of them older than the chieftainship. There are the *cedar-wives* — women (and occasionally men) who have spent a lifetime learning the temperament of a single named cedar, who know its moods and its leanings and the particular way it groans when a storm is coming. A cedar-wife speaks for her tree in disputes, and her word on the matter of the tree is final, even against the chief, because the chief does not know the tree and she does. There are the *spring-tenders*, who keep the channels clear and know the temperature of every pool to within a finger-width. There are the *hook-beak-callers*, who can imitate the birds well enough to call a flock down from the upper canopy or send it wheeling away in a particular direction, which is sometimes useful and sometimes, the elders say darkly, not useful at all. There are the *moss-line singers*, who sing the wall-edges into their proper height when the sloth-mosses have been overfed and the lichen has begun, here and there, to climb above the breath-line. The moss-line singers are not numerous. There are usually three or four in a generation, and they are recognized early, and they are trained from childhood in a repertoire of songs whose words are not words but particular pitches, and the pitches, sung at the lichen, cause the lichen to stop growing. No one knows why this works. It is one of the things that has worked for a thousand years and is not questioned.

## The Making of Children

Children are born in the warm channels.

This is not a figure of speech. A woman in labor is lowered, with her female relatives supporting her, into one of the middle channels — the bath-warm ones, never the scalding ones — and the child is born in the water, and is brought up into the air already half-accustomed to the wet, and is held for the first hour against the mother's chest in the channel itself, with the water running over them both. The midwives say this is for the warmth, and it is partly for the warmth, but it is also for something else that no one will quite name. A child born in the cleft is, from the first breath, *of* the cleft in a way that a child merely born in a lowland house cannot be. The water gets into the lungs first, the midwives say, and after that the lungs are always a little bit cleft-water lungs, and the person can never fully breathe anywhere else.

Children are named on the third day. The naming is done by the cedar-wife of the family's tree, who chooses the name from a repertoire of about two hundred traditional names, each one associated with a particular cedar-temperament. A child named *Slow-Bender* will be a quiet, patient child, the cedar-wife says, and the parents accept this, because to refuse the name would be to insult the tree. Sometimes the cedar-wife refuses to name a child at all — this is very rare and is taken as a grave sign — and in such cases the child is raised without a name and is called *the unsaid one* until such time as the cedar-wife relents or dies, after which another cedar-wife is asked, and so on. There are, at any given time, perhaps two or three unsaid ones in the cleft, and they are treated with a particular tenderness, as if they are made of something that has not yet decided what it is.

Children are given their first root-hook at seven, and from that day they are expected to participate in the keeping of the family's tree. A child who neglects her tree is not punished, exactly — there is no punishment in the cleft, only consequences — but the tree will begin, slowly, to straighten, and the family will know, and the child will know that the family knows, and this is felt to be sufficient.

## The Dying of Them

The dying are carried to the lowest platforms.

This, too, is not a figure of speech. A person who is clearly dying — and the Cleft-people know, with the certainty of people who have watched many die, when a person is clearly dying — is moved, with great gentleness, from the family dwelling to one of the lowest platforms, where the water is still warm but is beginning, already, to leave the cleft. The dying person is laid on a mat of split-bamboo, with her feet toward the runoff-lip, and her family sits with her, and the spring-tenders bring warm water in clay pots and bathe her, and the cedar-wife of her family's tree comes and sings the tree's death-song into her ear, and the hook-beaks wheel above, and the eels move in the channels beside her, and the water falls, and after some hours or some days the person dies, and the body is wrapped in mats and weighted with shaped stones and is let over the lip, and it falls, and joins the ribbon-fall, and is gone into the marsh.

This is felt to be correct. The marsh is the place of ending, and the bellowing that comes from it twice a year is, the elders say, *the marsh speaking of those it has eaten*, and this is not a comforting belief but it is an old one and the Cleft-people are not a people who reach for comforting beliefs. They reach for accurate ones.

## The Things They Believe

They believe the cedars are conscious.

Not in the way a lowlander would mean it — they do not believe the cedars have thoughts or feelings in the human sense. But they believe the cedars are *aware* in a way that the sloth-mosses are aware and the hook-beaks are aware and the springs themselves are aware: aware of being, aware of being touched, aware of the negotiations being made with their bodies by the people who live among them. A cedar that has been bent into a dwelling has, over the centuries, become *a particular cedar*, with a particular temperament, and the family that lives with it learns to read its temperament in the lean of its trunk, in the color of its bark, in the way it groans (or doesn't) when the weather changes. A well-kept cedar is a happy cedar. A neglected cedar is a sad cedar. These are not metaphors. The cedar-wife can tell the difference by listening.

They believe the springs are the blood of the mountain.

This is a literal belief. The mountain is, in their thinking, a kind of great slow animal, and the hot springs are its blood, and the cleft is a wound, and the town is the scab that has formed over the wound, and the people are the small creatures that live in the scab. This belief is not held superstitiously but held as a practical matter: the springs are treated with the care that one would treat the blood of a being one depends on. They are not to be dammed recklessly. They are not to be diverted in ways that would harm the mountain. They are to be received gratefully and passed along cleanly, and the channels that carry them are to be kept in good repair, and the people who tend them are to be respected.

They believe the marsh is alive and is not to be trusted.

This is the most fraught of their beliefs. The marsh has been there since before they were there, and it will be there after they are gone, and it bellows twice a year, and no one knows why, and the not-knowing is central to the relationship. The elders sing the bellowing down. The children are taught, from infancy, that the marsh is the place of ending and is not to be approached except in the proper season, which is the season of the traders (the traders come in the spring and the autumn, between the bellowings, and the bargaining is brisk and the mood is businesslike and no one lingers). The bellowing itself is treated with a kind of formal respect: the song that the elders sing has no words, and it lasts an hour, and during that hour no one works and no one speaks and the hook-beaks wheel in the sky in formations that are not quite flocks. After the bellowing ends, life resumes. No one discusses what was bellowed. It is not a thing for discussion. It is a thing for endurance.

## The Trade of Them

The traders who climb up from the lower mountain are not Cleft-people and are not, the Cleft-people feel, entirely real.

They come in the spring and the autumn, climbing for three days through rain and mist, and they arrive at the upper platforms soaked and exhausted and always a little afraid, because they are lowland people and the cleft is a strange place to them, with its smell of sulfur and its wheeling hook-beaks and its living architecture. They bring dried marsh-reed, which the Cleft-people use for bedding and for kindling in the rare dry season. They bring small clay figurines of the moss-line, carved with a care that the Cleft-people themselves do not bother with, because to carve the moss-line is to attempt to fix in clay a thing that is, by its nature, alive and moving and is not to be fixed. The figurines are kept in the dwellings as curiosities, and sometimes as warnings to children who have been climbing above the moss-line. They bring, occasionally, metal — a knife, an axe-head, a needle — and the metal is received politely and never used.

In exchange the Cleft-people give glass-eels, dried and packed in cedar-bark tubes. They give hook-beak guano, in clay jars sealed with tallow. They give the rare pale lichen that grows only on the highest cliff-faces, which is a delicacy and a medicine. They give, very occasionally, a sloth-moss, alive, in a basket of wet moss, but this is rare because a sloth-moss is a working animal and not a trade good and the parting is felt to be a small grief on both sides.

The trading is done in silence, mostly. The Cleft-people and the traders do not share a language, and the bargaining is conducted by gesture and by laying out of goods and by a particular kind of patient looking-at, in which each side waits for the other to add a little to his pile, and the deal is made when both sides stop adding, and the silence that follows is felt to be the seal of the agreement. The chief is not involved in the trading. The trading is done by the spring-tenders and the cedar-wives, who have a quiet authority about them that the traders, after their first visit, learn to respect.

## The Feeling of Them

It is difficult, the traders say, to know what a Cleft-person is feeling.

This is true. The Cleft-people are not unfeeling — they laugh, they weep, they embrace, they rage in the particular quiet way of people who have learned to rage without raising their voices above the sound of the water — but their feelings are not displayed on their faces in the way a lowlander's are. Their faces are still and polished and watchful, and an outsider can spend a week in the cleft and never know whether he has been welcomed or merely tolerated. The truth, usually, is that he has been tolerated, and that the welcoming, if it comes, will come later, after the Cleft-people have decided what he is.

They are kind, but their kindness is not the kindness of lowlanders. A lowlander's kindness is warm and demonstrative and seeks to be seen. A Cleft-person's kindness is in the structure of things: the channel that has been widened so that a guest can sit beside it without being splashed; the cedar-root that has been guided, with great labor, into a shape that will hold a hammock for a visitor who has never slept in a hammock before; the particular small ceremony by which a guest is shown, without being told, where the warm-water pools are and how to use them. These kindnesses are not pointed out. They are simply there. A guest who notices them is felt to be perceptive. A guest who does not notice them is not corrected; he is simply not asked back.

They are proud. They are proud of the cleft and of the long negotiation with the cedars and of the year-rope with its four hundred feet of memory and of the seven songs that tell of the seven great failures of families and their trees. They are not proud in the way of conquerors. They are proud in the way of people who have kept a difficult thing alive for a long time and know it is difficult and know it can be lost. Their pride is a working pride, the pride of the spring-tender who has cleaned a channel that has not been cleaned in three generations and has done it without damaging a single root, the pride of the cedar-wife who has coaxed a stubborn young cedar into a shape no one thought possible, the pride of the chief who has ruled for thirty years on water-channels alone and has done it well. This pride is quiet. It does not need to be loud. It is the pride of a people who have made their home in a place that does not forgive mistakes, and have made it well, and know it.

---

And the hook-beaks scream the morning in, and the sloth-mosses graze the walls, and the eels move like silver in the channels, and the cedars grow into the shapes they were given, and the water falls, and the people of Suwarrow's Reach move up and down the cleft with the water, working where it is hot, bathing where it is warm, emptying the lowest platforms when the bellowing comes, and they have done this for a thousand years, and they will do it tomorrow, and they do not think of themselves as remarkable. They think of themselves as the ones who sit between, and the between is where they live, and the between is enough.
