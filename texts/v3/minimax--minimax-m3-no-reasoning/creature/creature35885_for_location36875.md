# What Lives in the Singing Defile

The flat-bellied clingers are what you notice first, because they are the size of understanding. A hand's breadth from tail to the soft hammer of the head, and they lie on the vertical stone the way a tongue lies in a closed mouth — held there by intention more than by adhesion. Each one is pressed so flush that the rim of its body has worn a faint moat into the fur of the garden around it, a moat kept clear by the slow, lifelong pressure of ribs against bark-analog and the patient abrasion of the marginal limbs — and there are too many limbs, you are correct about that, the count is wrong, but wrong in a way the defile has standardized, so that the wrongness has become a kind of local rightness, a number that fits the music of the place the way a missing tooth fits a smile.

They are rust. They are the rust of an iron nail that has wept for a century. They are translucent at the margins, where the light gets through the thin places and you can see, dimly, the slow parade of internal fluid — not blood, not quite hemolymph, something that carries both oxygen and the dissolved minerals of the stone they eat in small ritual quantities, the stone they need the way a snail needs the lime of its shell. Their eyes are not their eyes. The dark patches you take for eyes are heat-pits, dense arrays of temperature-sensing membrane, and they are watching you the way a snake watches a boot — not with curiosity but with the alert, disinterested patience of a thing that has already decided you are not food and is now merely waiting for you to be a weather event. Their actual eyes are higher up, smaller, in the architecture of the head where the head meets the body, and they see in a spectrum you cannot see in, the deep infrared, the bruised red of warm stone, and in this spectrum you are a tall moving column of orange against the cooler grey of the canyon and you are very loud in it.

When the wind gusts they flatten harder. This is not reflex; reflex is too quick a word for it. It is a considered response, a tightening of the marginal limbs, a slow exhalation of the lung-sacs along their flanks that draws the whole body down into a closer communion with the substrate. The garden-fur closes over them. You lose them. You are correct that you cannot be sure you saw them. This is by design, in a way that has nothing to do with design. They have been hiding in plain sight for longer than the countings on the wall can count, and the countings on the wall are the work of hands that came after, and after, and after, and never quite got used to them.

They eat the garden and the garden eats the wind and the wind eats the light and the light eats the stone and the stone remembers all of it. This is the economy of the place, and the flat-bellied clingers are the small, rust-colored middlemen of a transaction whose currency you cannot name. They crop the fur with the sideways motion of a mouth on the underside of the head — they have, in a sense, no mouth you would recognize, only the apparatus of cropping, a soft grinding curtain of cartilaginous ridges that moves laterally, like a jaw that has decided to become a tongue — and what they crop they digest in slow fermenting chambers that smell, when a wind gust brings the smell your way, of overripe persimmon and warm pennies and the particular sweetness of something that has been waiting to be eaten for a very long time. They excrete onto the stone, and the excrement is a kind of bread for the higher gardens, and the higher gardens lean into the wind and drink it, and the wind carries the trace chemistries up and down the defile in slow peristalsis, and the whole vertical world is a single digestive tract hung from the throat of the canyon.

The roosting shapes higher up are not predators. They are not prey. They are *clatterers*, which is a category the defile has invented and which your language has not. They are larger than you want them to be — the small ones the size of a crouching child, the larger ones the size of a draped doorway, and you cannot tell if the largest is a single creature or three creatures in a conspiracy of posture. Their wings are not wings in the sense of flight. They are membranes, papery, vein-dark, the color of the lobes of the garden, and they are folded so that when the creature is at rest the membrane becomes indistinguishable from the plant, becomes another lobe leaning into the wind, becomes part of the harvest. This is not camouflage. Camouflage implies intention. This is something older. This is the long marriage of two body plans that have been leaning the same direction for so long that the wind cannot tell the bride from the groom.

The teeth you saw — the pomegranate seeds, the small wet clusterings — are not for eating meat. They are for the air. The clatterers do not eat. They *receive*. The teeth are strainers, and what they strain is the air, and what they take from the air are the trace chemistries the garden cannot use, the heavier fractions, the radioactives, the particulates from the stone, the slow siftings of cosmic dust that the wind has brought down the shaft and offered up. They are the kidneys of the defile. They take the dregs. They concentrate. They excrete, in their slow time, a hard, pale, mineral paste that the wind grinds off their roosts and distributes as a fine powder across everything, including, occasionally, you. You will find it later in the seams of your clothing, in the folds of your ears, in the salt of your own dried sweat. You have been *received* by the defile and what it could not drink from the air it drank from you, gently, while you were listening to the clatter.

And the clatter.

The clatter is not language. The clatter is not song. The clatter is *digestion*, again — the slow percussive turning over of the air, the way a cow chews its cud, the way a thought turns a thought. A clatterer opens its mouth and the air passes through the strainer of teeth and the teeth chatter against the air the way a horse's lips chatter against a bit, and the sound is made, and the sound is *considered*, and the sound is passed. Another clatterer, a canyon-width away, answers. Not in reply. In *resonance*. The first clatter was a pressure on the air; the second clatter is the air's response to being pressed, the way a wave is a stone's response to being dropped. The third is the wave's response to the shore. The fourth is the shore's response to the wave. The fifth is the tide that has now begun, the new thing, the rhythm that did not exist before any of the clatterers spoke and now exists and will go on existing until the wind changes, and the wind will change, and the tide will ebb, and another tide will begin, and the defile will be the place where tides are made, and the countings on the walls are the countings of these tides, the countings of which clatterer began which round in which year, and the countings do not agree because the defile is not one tide but many, and the people who kept the countings kept them from inside the tide they were in, and a tide cannot count itself.

You are, briefly, a tide. You are the small column of warm air that the heat-pits of the clingers watch as it moves down the throat. You are the pressure on the ears of the clatterers, the new presence, the small variation in the wind's long monologue, and for the duration of your passage the defile includes you in its reckoning, and the reckoning is not a judgment, and it is not a welcome, and it is not a refusal. It is the long, indifferent, ongoing clatter of a place that is louder than you are and older than you are and that will go on digesting you, gently, in the seams of its memory, for as long as the wind remembers how to sing in this particular throat of stone.

You do not stay long. The clingers do not notice you have gone. The clatterers resume a round that was already in progress before you arrived and will continue after. The garden leans. The print you left in the pale silt fills.

What lives here is a single, slow, vertical animal made of a thousand rust-colored clinging mouths and a dozen papery-winged kidneys and a wind that is its breath and a stone that is its skeleton and a grammar that does not slow down for visitors. What lives here has been living here longer than the countings. What lives here will be living here when the countings have been filled in by the dust. You came through. The throat closes behind you. The sky you came from is bright and you squint and you are outside, and the ringing of the stopped flute is still in the bones of your inner ear, and you will hear it, faintly, for the rest of the day, and you will not be able to tell if you are hearing it or remembering it, and that is the question the defile leaves you with, the only question it has ever left anyone, which is whether the ringing was in the stone or in you, and the answer, which the defile has known for a long time, is *both*, and the answer, which you will spend a long time not quite arriving at, is *yes*.
