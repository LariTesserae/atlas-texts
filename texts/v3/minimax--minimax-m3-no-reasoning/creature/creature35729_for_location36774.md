# The Last Floor of the World

The woman is named nothing you would recognize. She has a name — three short sounds, the middle one hummed rather than spoken, the way her mother had it and her mother's mother had it — but the sound of it in your ear would register as a kind of hesitation, a comma in a draft, and if she were to translate it for you she would only shrug and point at the moss, and you would understand that she means something closer to *the color of wet green before a fat rain* than any word you have ever held in your mouth. She is thirty, or near enough. She has been thirty for a long time. She does not know how old she is in the way you know how old you are, because no one has ever sat her down and subtracted one year from another, but she knows that she is old enough that her knees hurt her when the barometric pressure drops, and young enough that her hair has not yet gone the color of the bottom silt, and that is the only arithmetic of age she has ever been given.

She is walking home along what was, nine hundred years ago, before the water came up to take the streets, the Avenue of the Painted Banners. She does not know this. To her it is the Long Ridge, and it runs from the place where the catches are good to the place where the shelters are dry, and that is all a street needs to be.

She is carrying, in a basket woven of the long black stems of the drowned grasses, a small clay jar stoppered with a twist of moss-fiber, and inside the jar is an oil she pressed this morning from the velvet maroon blossoms that grow, three feet under, on the crests of the lower walls. The blossoms open only at the time of year that is not winter and not summer — the in-between, the dim, the time when the light comes down through the pewter ceiling at its most forgiving grey — and the oil pressed from them is dark and smells, if you smell it closely, of something you would call *moss* if you were a careful poet and *wet wool* if you were not. It is used for the small fires. It burns slow and gives off a low red glow that lights only the faces nearest to it and makes the rest of the room retreat into a deeper dark. There is a virtue in this oil. It is the only virtue anyone has ever claimed for it. It is enough.

She walks with her bare feet in two inches of water, and her feet know the floor the way a tongue knows the roof of a mouth. There is a place, three ridges over, where the paving has buckled upward into a knob of older brick that rises a full hand-span above the general line, and her left foot knows that knob is coming and her weight shifts onto her right foot a half-step before she reaches it, the way your foot knows a stair in the dark. There is a place, twenty paces back, where a piece of rebar has worked itself out of the deeper substrate and lies just under the silt like a finger pointing, and her right heel knows to slide over it rather than catch on it, and she does not think about this. She has never thought about this. It is the floor.

She hums as she walks, or she half-hums, or she breathes in a pattern that her son might call a song if he were asked, but that you would call only a long vowel held between the lips and released slowly through the nose. It has no words. The words were worn off it generations ago, and what remains is the *shape* of the words, the contour of the melody, the way the tune goes down on the second beat and lingers there. She learned it from her mother, who learned it from the chair her mother used to sit in, in the way that children learn songs, which is to say she learned it before she learned speech, which is to say she learned it from the sounds her own body was making before it knew it was making them. It is not a song about anything. It is a song about the shape of a song. Its memory is a memory of memory.

The hallway she turns into was once, it must be said, a hallway. The walls are still there — low, a foot and a half high, dense with moss, indistinguishable from the floor at any distance — and they still run parallel, and they still turn at the corner where the hallway bent to meet another hallway, and she follows the turn without thinking about following the turn. If you could peel the moss away, you would find ceramic tile underfoot, glazed a color so faded it has become the color of absence, and along the lower courses of the walls you would find fragments of mosaic in which you might, after a long look, make out the shapes of small fish and curling fronds and a figure holding something in a hand. She does not look at the mosaic. She has looked at it, perhaps, in childhood, in the way children look at everything, and seen only colors, and grown past it. The mosaic was not put down for her.

She comes to the door of her shelter, which is to say a gap in a wall that was, eight hundred years ago, the wall of a smaller room. The shelter is low. She must duck to enter, and she has ducked to enter every day of her life, and she does not register the ducking as a ducking — it is simply the way one moves through the world, the way a fish registers water. Inside, the ceiling is supported by the long stems of the tree-posture things lashed together with twisted moss-fiber, and the stems lean inward at the top under the weight of the water-moss that hangs from them in long green curtains soaked so dark they are nearly black. The floor is two inches lower than the floor of the corridor, and the water does not come in, and the difference is the only definition of *inside* and *outside* she has ever known.

Her son is there, in the way that sons are there. He is ten, or eleven, or some number that means nothing to him. He is sitting on a flat stone in the deeper water at the back of the shelter, and he is doing something with his hands that is so small and so patient that it could be a craft, or it could be a thought, or it could be a prayer, or it could be nothing at all. He is threading a needle, or threading a thought into the shape of a needle. The line he is using is a single fiber of the dark grass, and he has been wetting it in his mouth to soften it, and the thread has the quality of all such threads, which is the quality of having been wet. He does not look up when she enters. He will look up, eventually. He will look up and she will hand him the jar and he will take it to the sunken pit in the corner and feed it carefully to the coals, and then there will be a small red glow, and the walls will lean in to see it too, and the evening will be the evening as her mother's evenings are.

She sets down the basket. She sits on the flat stone at the front of the shelter, the one that is just above the waterline, and she looks out the doorway at the grey. The grey is the grey of her childhood, which is the grey of her mother's childhood, which is the grey of the oldest woman in the settlement, who is sixty-eight, or some number that means nothing to her either, and who sits sometimes on the highest point of the Long Ridge and looks out at the grey the way you look out a window. The old woman is the only person in the settlement who knows that the floor under her feet was put there by hands. She is old enough to have been told this by *her* mother, who was old enough to have been told it by *her* mother, in a chain that goes back further than anyone has memory of. It is a fact that has been passed down so long it is no longer a fact. It is a kind of weather. *The floor was put here by hands,* the old woman has said, every year of her life, on the day of the long rain, when the family gathers to press the oil. *The floor was put here by hands, and the water came, and the hands went away, and we are here now.* The saying has the cadence of all such sayings. It does not require belief. It requires only that it be said.

The woman in the doorway does not know that the floor was put there by hands. She has heard the saying, the way you hear a song, or a sound, or a rain. She does not disbelieve it. She has no opinion on the matter. The saying is the saying. The floor is the floor. The grey is the grey.

Somewhere below her, below the silt and the brick and the older brick and the bones of yet older brick, there is a room. In the room there is a chair. In the chair there is the outline, in the dust that has settled there over the years, of a person who sat there, who waited there, who left, who is, in the way that all the dead are kept in the rooms of the dead, still waiting. The woman does not know about the chair. The chair does not know about the woman. They are separated by the kind of distance that separates time of, and they have nothing to say to each other. The chair was put there for someone who is no longer there. The woman is here for no one in particular. She is here for herself. She is here for the oil. She is here for her son. She is here for the evening and the small red glow and the song without words and the long sleep in the low shelter with the green curtains hanging. She is here for the morning, and the pressing, and the walk back along the Long Ridge.

She is the last floor of the world, in the way that all things are the last floor of the world when there is no floor below them, and she does not know it, and the moss does not know it, and the water does not know it, and the grey does not know it, and it does not matter that they do not know it, because the floor does not require that it be known. The floor requires only that it be walked on.

She walks on it.
