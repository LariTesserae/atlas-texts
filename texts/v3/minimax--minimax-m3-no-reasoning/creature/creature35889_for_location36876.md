They are not numerous. You could count them in a single afternoon, walking the spaces between the buildings, and you would not need to use both hands. There is no census anyone has spoken of, but the figure is small enough that it does not require one. Eleven. Twelve. Perhaps fewer. The number is not quite fixed — it is possible that one has not been seen for some time, and that no one remarks on this, and that the place has not adjusted.

Their clothing is the same as the stone. Not in color exactly — that would be too deliberate — but in the way it has accepted what the air has done to it. They wear layers, because the damp requires layers, and because the coolness here is structural and will not be argued with. Coarse wool, the color of wet slate, or the darker grey that comes after a season of it. Knit caps pulled low. Cloth wrapped at the wrists and the ankles where the cold finds skin. Boots of a leather that has long ago given up its original brown and gone the indeterminate grey of a thing that has stopped deciding. Their garments are heavy. They move inside them slowly, the way a person moves inside an envelope of cold.

Their faces, when you see them, are not remarkable. They are the faces of people who have been looked at by grey light for a long time. The skin has taken on something of the stone's cast — not the pallor of high-altitude dwellers in other places, but something slightly translucent, slightly mineral, as though the moisture has worked its way into them and stayed. Their eyes are the color of wet rock. Their hair, when it escapes the caps, is dark and lies flat and does not seem to mind. They do not appear tired, exactly, but they do not appear rested either. They appear to be in the middle of something that has no end.

Their hands are the most articulate parts of them. The hands do the small work — they lift a latch, they adjust a slate that has slipped, they gather a fistful of the pale grass from a pavement crack and put it somewhere or do not. The hands are slow. The hands do not fumble. They know what they are about to do and have known for a long time. The fingers are a little thicker than you might expect, a little stiff at the last joints, in the way of hands that have done the same few things for many seasons.

They speak to one another, but rarely. When they do, it is at a volume that matches the place — which is to say, almost not at all. A word exchanged at the door of one of the buildings. A murmur that does not carry across the spaces. The sound becomes part of the weather for as long as it lasts, and then it is gone, and the absence of it is no different from its presence. They do not appear to need more. Whatever the conversation is about, it is not urgent, or if it once was, it has settled into the same grey equilibrium as the rest.

They are not unfriendly. They will, if you arrive, acknowledge you — a movement of the head, perhaps, or the briefest shifting of the eyes in your direction, the way one might acknowledge a stone that has newly come to rest among the other stones. They will not invite you in. They will not refuse you entry either. They will simply continue what they were doing, and you will be allowed, over some time, to understand that you have been folded into the slow pattern. You will find that the building you are in is warm — or not warm, but less cold, which is the same thing here — and that someone has put a vessel of something warm near you without comment, and that no one has asked you to explain yourself.

They do not look up at the sky much. The sky is part of the light, and the light is not news. They do not mark the passage of the seasons with any ceremony you would recognize. There may be a difference in the wetness of the air, or in the frequency with which the channel at the eastern edge runs, or in the slant of what little sun gets through, but they do not name it. They have not named it for a long time. If you pressed them on it — and you would not, because pressing is not something the air here permits — you might learn that there are names still, but that the names have thinned, and that what remains is more like an orientation than a calendar.

What they do, you cannot quite say. There is work, but it is not the work of industry. A stone is moved, occasionally, and reset in a wall where it has loosened. A roof is checked after a hard rain, and a single slate is turned, and the alignment is restored by eye to what it was. The channel is cleared of a leaf, or of a small accumulation of grit that the water has brought down. The basins in the upper surfaces of certain rocks are tipped empty when they fill. The pale grass in the cracks is sometimes pulled, sometimes not. These are the tasks. They are performed without discussion of whether they need to be done. They are the small maintenance of a system that has found its form, and they are done at the same hours, and the same hands do them, and the place accepts them and continues.

In the evenings — though the evening here is only a deepening of the grey, not a true dark — they go into their buildings. The doors close with the small sound of the latch lifting and settling. The lights, if there are lights, do not throw much warmth into the air; you can see them only as a slight thickening of the dimness behind a window. You might see, through that window, a figure seated at a table. You might see another standing with a hand on a wall, as though listening to the stone. They are not doing anything you would call work. They are simply present, in the way that the stones are present, and the evening passes over them the way the damp passes over the lintels.

You could spend a week here and not learn a single one of their names. You would learn the lines they walk. You would learn which door is opened at which hour, and which of the small second stories is occupied, and which is not. You would learn that one of them — the one with the slightly different cap, or the slightly different gait — tends the channel, and that another tends the roof of the largest of the small buildings, and that a third seems to do nothing in particular but is always in evidence at a certain corner of the settlement at a certain hour, as though the corner required a witness and would not be the same without one. You would begin, after a while, to understand that they are not so much individuals as variations on a single theme — that the place has produced them, and that they have produced the place, and that the two are no longer quite separable.

They are not unhappy. That is the strangest thing about them, and the thing you would notice last. You would arrive expecting something — bleakness, or hardship, or the held-in endurance of people in a hard place. You would not find it. What you would find instead is harder to name: a settledness so complete that it has passed beyond contentment into something else. They are not content because they have not been denied anything. They are not peaceful because they have made peace with something. They are simply in the place they are, and the place is what it is, and the two have agreed, and the agreement is so old that no one remembers there was ever anything to agree to.

If you asked one of them, finally — if you could bring yourself to ask, and if you could find a moment when speaking did not feel like an intrusion into the weather — why they stayed, they would not answer directly. They would look at you with the eyes that are the color of wet rock, and they would say something that was almost an answer but not quite, something that turned the question gently back on itself, and you would understand that the question was not one they had ever asked themselves. They had not stayed. They had simply been here, and here had been here, and the two had not separated, and the word for it, if there were a word, was not a word they would use.

They will be here tomorrow. They will be here in a season you cannot name, in a year you will not return to count. They will walk the same lines and open the same doors and wear the same clothes that have taken on the same color. The moss will be the moss. The stones will be in the arrangement they have held. And if you come back in a year, you will find them, and they will be the same, and you will not be certain whether time has passed at all, or whether the place has simply continued to be what it was, with you now folded quietly into its pattern, walking the lines it has permitted you to walk, your own steps becoming, before you have quite noticed, part of the small permitted set.
