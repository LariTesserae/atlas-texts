# What Lives in the Long Dark of the Forges

What lives here is the place itself, in a hundred thousand bodies, and none of them are quite what you'd expect.

## The Forest

The trees are the dominant intelligence, though no one would call them intelligent in any way you'd recognize. The birches on the slag heaps have grown in the strange geometry of plants that have learned to root in unstable substrate — they lean at odd angles, their trunks curved like the legs of long-distance walkers, and they communicate with each other through the mycorrhizal web that runs under everything. When you break a stem, the sap runs faintly luminous, a dim red bioluminescence, and the wound closes within seconds. The alders, which have colonized the half-collapsed refinery shed, are the most altered. They are not alders anymore, or they are alders plus something. Their bark is patterned with copper — actual copper, hammered and riveted into the living wood in thin veins that pulse when the dynamos run. Their root systems have fused with the old electrical conduits beneath the shed, and they draw current the way other trees draw water, and they grow faster than the other trees, and they are warmer to the touch than the other trees, and at night they are the brightest things in the forest, except for the lights themselves.

The hazel-like brush on the slag heaps is not hazel. It is something that has not been named by anyone living, though there are references in the oldest records to a "responsive undergrowth" that the surveyors would not walk through alone. Its leaves turn in unison when no wind touches them, and they turn toward anything that enters the grove. Not toward heat. Not toward light. Toward *attention*. The plant knows when it is being observed, and it orients toward the observer the way a flower orients toward the sun, except the sun does not concern it. It is not photosynthesizing in the way you understand. It is doing something else with the photons that filter through. The old workers called it the Watching Hazel and avoided it. The newer name, the one in the records from the last survey, is less poetic and more accurate: the Attention Plant. It is not dangerous. It is only very, very interested.

There is a moss that covers every fallen machine. It is the moss that has done most of the work of burial. It grows thick and dark and holds water like a sponge, and in it live the things that eat the things that eat the things that eat the things. It is, in itself, a city.

## The Voles

The voles are the substrate. There are millions of them, and the place sounds the way it does because of them. They are small and dark and very fecund, and they run in the leaf-litter in such numbers that the rustle of them is a continuous tone, a many-mouthed whisper, the sound of a system processing its own throughput. They eat the fungi. They eat the seeds. They eat each other, sometimes, the larger ones taking the smaller ones in a casual carnivory that no one has ever studied because no one who studies voles has come back from this place unchanged. They are the calories that keep the owls aloft and the foxes fed and the soil turning. They are the small made continuous. They are the proof that the system is alive. When the mist comes in thick and the visibility drops, you can hear them louder. When the sourceless light rises through the canopy and the leaves go green-gold, they go quiet. They are attuned to the phases of the place in ways that the larger animals are not. They know, somehow, when the kilns are about to breathe.

## The Foxes

The foxes are the color of wet bark and their eyes throw back the blue of the tree-lights at night. They are not large. They are very quick, and they are very watchful, and they will not let you approach. They bark at things you cannot see — at shapes in the mist, at the lights when they move wrong, at whatever is making the modulations in the kilns. The foxes are the sentinels of the place, or they are simply afraid of everything, which amounts to the same behavior. They have dens in the slag heaps, dug deep into the warm rock, and they raise their kits in chambers lined with the copper-colored fur of their own kind, and the kits learn the phases before they learn to hunt. The old workers said the foxes knew things. The new surveys say only that the foxes are abundant. Both are correct. The foxes know things. They will not tell you what they know. They will simply watch you from the edge of the tree-lights and decide, with a certainty you cannot see the source of, whether you are to be permitted to pass.

## The Deer

The deer here are dark almost to blackness, with antlers that fork in patterns that do not match any deer in any book. They are not a subspecies. They are something that has diverged in the long isolation of this place, fed on the strange plants, watered by the warm streams, bred in the cathedral columns of the ancient canopy. They move through the trees with the caution of things that have been hunted and have learned to be unhuntable. You will not see them often. You will see their tracks in the soft ground, sharp and deep, and you will see the places where they have rubbed their antlers against the bark of the copper-alders, scarring the metal-bark into bright lines, and you will hear, sometimes, a sound like a flute played under water, which is the sound they make to each other in the mist. They are not afraid of you. They have decided, somehow, that you are beneath their concern. They are correct.

## The Owls

The owls are huge. They are dark. They hunt in the upper canopy where the dim light is enough for them, and they make no sound except the slow creak of their wings, which is not a sound an owl should make but which is the sound this owl makes, a low wooden groan, as though the wings were made of some harder substance than feather. You do not see them. You see their shadows sometimes, passing across the higher trunks, and once you see a shape descend through a gap in the canopy and take a vole in absolute silence and rise again, and you understand, watching this, that the owl is not a bird in the way you understand birds. It is something that occupies the niche a bird would occupy, in a place where the niche has been shaped by a very long time and a very strange system. The owls hunt the voles. The owls are not hunted, because nothing in this place can hunt an owl. The owls live a long time. No one knows how long. The oldest record mentions an owl that was already old when the record was made, and the record is older than anyone living.

## The Kiln-Dwellers

The kilns are warm. The kilns are breathing. The kilns are not empty, though no one will say what is in them. There are things that live in the kilns, in the slow sulfur breath, in the warm brick chambers that were once fired by human hands and are now fired by the heat of the rock itself. They are small. They are numerous. They are not quite animals. They come out at night, or what passes for night, and they move through the slag heaps in long pale lines, and they are the things that maintain the kilns, that keep them breathing, that have learned the meaning of maintenance without ever having been taught it. The modulations that come from the kilns are their voices. The warmth that rises from the kilns is their breath. The sulfur at dusk is their excretion. They are the inheritors of the industry. They are the reason the kilns are still warm.

## The Lights

The lights are not will-o'-the-wisps. They are the system's children. They drift between the trunks in the deep dark, a deep bioluminescent blue, the color of a clear sky reflected in deep water. They are cold. They are not flame. They are not electricity in the way you understand electricity, though they move along the wires that have been grown into the trees, and they brighten when the dynamos run. They are something the system has produced, the way a system produces heat or current or the sound of voles in the leaf-litter. They are the visible sign of the place's continued operation. They will lead you to places that are not unkind but that are not for you. You will come back different. You will come back speaking a frequency you did not speak before. The lights are not malicious. They are only doing what the system does, which is to continue, and to incorporate, and to run.

## The Shapes in the Dark

You do not catalogue them. You see a shape that moves in a way that is not quite animal and not quite machine, and you decide, wisely, not to investigate. There are things in this place that are not the voles and not the foxes and not the deer and not the owls and not the kiln-dwellers and not the lights. There are things that have been made by the long marriage of the machines and the forest, things that are neither, things that move between the categories the way the lights move between the trunks. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are not anything you have a word for. They are the place's deeper thoughts, the slow processes that run beneath the rust and beneath the leaf-litter and beneath the water and beneath the stone. You are not equipped to perceive them clearly. You are not equipped to be perceived by them clearly. This is a kind of peace. The peace of two systems that cannot quite see each other, passing in the cathedral dark, each going about its own vast and patient business.

## The Dark Itself

The dark is the medium in which everything here has learned to live. The dark is patient. The dark is older than the forest, older than the machines, older than the small human histories that scratched themselves into the slag. The dark is the thing the place is made of, in the same way the sea is the thing the ocean is made of. The animals have eyes that catch the faintest light. The plants photosynthesize at the dim red end of the spectrum. The kiln-dwellers navigate by heat. The owls hunt by a sense you do not have a name for. The whole place has been tuned, over a very long time, to operate in the near-absence of the sun. The sun is rumor here. The dark is the thing. The dark is the substrate, and the water, and the hum, and the slow wooden groan of the great trees, and the many-mouthed whisper of the voles, and the blue of the lights, and the warmth of the rock.

You are in a place that has been here longer than you and will be here longer than you and is, in this moment, exactly what it is. The dark receives you. The water receives you. The hum receives you. The voles continue their rustle. The foxes continue their watching. The deer continue their unhuntable passage. The owls continue their silent hunting. The kiln-dwellers continue their maintenance. The lights continue their drifting. The shapes in the dark continue their not-quite-perceptible business. The trees continue their slow growth into the machines, and the machines continue their slow translation into the trees.

The treaty holds. The hum continues. The place is itself, and it will be itself for a long time after you have gone, and the small made continuous will continue to be made, in the dark, in the wet, in the warm, in the long.
