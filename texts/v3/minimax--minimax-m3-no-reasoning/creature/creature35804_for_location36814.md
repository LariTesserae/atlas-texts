# Inhabitants of the Weeping Hall

## The Faun

The faun is not one creature but a way of being a creature. There are perhaps sixty of them scattered through the alcoves and shallows of the hall, and yet they share something that makes the word *population* feel wrong—more accurate would be *vocabulary*, as though each one were a different inflection of a single sound.

Their bodies are low to the ground, the size of large dogs but built along an architecture that does not correspond to anything in the surface taxonomies you know. They are not vertebrates. They are not invertebrates. They are something that has chosen a third option, or that never knew there were only two. Their skeletons—if they have skeletons—appear to be exoskeletal, a system of interlocking mineral plates grown from the same chemistry as the walls around them. The hide is wet stone, dark and faintly iridescent, the same bruise-purple that furs the basalt, and at the joints it puckers into soft folds like the gussets of old leather armor. When a faun is still in its alcove it is nearly impossible to distinguish from the carved recesses it inhabits; you begin to wonder if the alcoves were carved around them, or if they grew to fit the alcoves that were already there.

Their limbs fold in three places. This is the first thing that unmoors you. An elbow, a wrist, and then another wrist further down, each with its own range of motion, so that when a faun extends itself fully it seems to *uncrumple*, like a folded paper crane being undone by a careful hand. The extra joints give them a fluidity that mammals cannot imitate; they move the way rope moves, the way water moves when it is poured slowly. When one of them shifts from one alcove to another, the motion is a continuous pour, the belly dragging wetly across the wet stone, the limbs rearranging themselves on the far side of the body before the first side has finished letting go.

Their breathing is the most unsettling thing about them, because it is visible. Along the ribcage, between the plates of exoskeleton, there are vertical slits—paired, dozens of them, running from shoulder to hip—and through these slits you can see the slow flare and collapse of something pink and feathery, like the gills of a deep-sea fish exposed to air. The slits open in waves, posterior to anterior, and the air that moves across them is visibly exchanged; there is a faint dimpling of the wet air above each ribcage as the gills breathe out, a slight brightening as they breathe in. A resting faun looks like an accordion being played very slowly by an invisible musician. The susurrus of the hall is partly this: the soft collective respiration of dozens of these bodies, plus the wet drag of their bellies on stone when they choose to move.

Their eyes are clusters. Not two eyes, but many, dozens of small reflective lenses grouped in sockets at the front of a low, blunt head. The lenses are each the size of a lentil, faceted, wet, and they swivel independently in their groupings so that a single faun can watch you from many angles at once. The color of them is a deep amber that almost looks gold in the phosphorescent light, and when one of the creatures turns its attention fully toward you, you can feel the slight psychic weight of being watched by something with forty eyes that all see slightly different things. They do not blink. They do not need to. The lenses are kept wet by the ambient moisture the way a frog's skin is kept wet; they are part of the atmosphere, not a separate organ.

What do they eat? This is a question the hall has patiently declined to answer. You do not see them feed. You do not see them hunt. You do not see them drink, though they are surrounded by water. There are no carcasses in the alcoves. There is no sign of digestion. The mineral forest does not seem to be a food source; the copper-fungus structures are not consumed, the salt tubeworms are not browsed, the fan-ferns are not chewed. Whatever their metabolism is, it does not appear to intersect with the visible chemistry of the hall. They breathe, which means they require energy. Where that energy comes from is a question the hall does not invite.

They do not seem to age. You cannot tell if this is because they do not, or because they age at a rate indistinguishable from the mineral accretion of the walls around them. A faun in a deeply furred alcove, half-merged with the lichen-rust of the stone, may be a young one that has been here only a century, or an ancient one that has been here since the machinery was still loud. There is no way to know. The patience in their many eyes is not the patience of youth waiting for something to happen; it is the patience of something that has already waited through several somethings happening and is prepared to wait through several more.

They are not hostile. You understand this clearly. They have watched you enter and they have not moved toward you, and this is not restraint but indifference—the indifference of something for which you are simply not in the category of things that matter. They are waiting for something else. They have perhaps always been waiting for something else. Whatever it is, it is not you, and they have known this since the moment you arrived.

## The Mineral Forest

It is not accurate to say the mineral forest lives, but it is not accurate to say it does not, and the hall exists in the space between those two accuracies, occupying the ambiguity the way the lichen-rust occupies the joints between stones.

There are the copper-fungus structures first, because they are the most startling. They rise from cracks in the floor and from the seams between wall-stones, their stems rising six inches to two feet before spreading into caps as wide across as dinner plates, sometimes wider, the underside of each cap gilled with parallel ridges that run from stem to margin exactly as the gills of an agaric run from stem to margin. But the caps are not caps of fungal flesh. They are sheets of corroded copper, the green of verdigris over the red-brown of older oxidation, and the stems are bundles of wire—hair-thin, copper-colored, twisted together in helices that spiral upward and outward like the twist of a drill bit. The wires once carried current. You can tell because at the bases of some of the stems the wire enters the stone and disappears into a junction, and at the junction there is sometimes a glass window, and behind the glass a needle, and the needle, faintly, still trembles. The vibration travels up the stem and into the cap and dissipates into the wet air as a hum so low it is almost a pressure, a sub-audible throb you feel through the soles of your feet when you stand within a yard of one of the larger specimens. You are standing in a forest of things that were machines and are now plants, or in a forest of things that were plants and have always been machines; you cannot tell which way the grammar runs, and the hall does not clarify.

There are the fan-ferns next, because they are the most beautiful. They grow in fans from the walls, each fan a series of overlapping layers of white mineral deposit, calcite or some cousin of calcite, layered like the pages of a book left open to weather. The pages are thin—paper-thin at the margins, thicker toward the base—and they are not flat but slightly curved, the curve deepening as you move outward from the root, so that each fan has the shape of a hand half-open, fingers spread, palm presented to whatever faint light there is. They grow a layer a decade, and some of the larger fans are dozens of layers deep, the outermost pages bright and clean, the innermost yellowed and faintly translucent, the whole structure a slow record of the wet air passing. The pages accumulate mineral on their surfaces and the mineral hardens into the next page, and the fan becomes a stratigraphy, a calendar of centuries you can hold in your hand if you are careful and the page does not crumble. They are beautiful in the way that patience is beautiful, in the way that slow things are beautiful to anyone who has learned to read their slowness.

There are the salt tubeworms, rising from cracks in the floor in clusters so dense they resemble coral, calcium-white, brittle, the hollow tubes sometimes four inches long and sometimes merely stubs depending on how long the colony has been undisturbed. The tubes are open at the top, and from the top a faint wetness sometimes glistens, the residue of water that has migrated up through the capillary channels of the stone and emerged here, carrying dissolved minerals, depositing them as it evaporates or as it reaches some threshold of concentration and precipitates out. A tubeworm is a record of water finding its way. A cluster of tubeworms is a record of a particular crack that water has favored for a long time. They grow a millimeter a year, and some of the clusters are taller than your hand and represent centuries of quiet dripping.

There are smaller things. There is a kind of black branching growth that resembles dead coral or winter twigs, growing from the upper surfaces of the copper-fungus caps, that may be a different form of the same lichen-rust that covers the walls or may be a separate organism that has found a niche on top of the corroded caps. There are small spherical nodules of pure iron, the size of marbles, scattered across the floor in places, that may have fallen from the cooling fins of the machinery as they rusted through, or may have precipitated out of the wet air in some older chemical regime. There are the tiny glassy beads that form on every iron surface and hang there like a skin of tears, refusing to fall, accumulating until their weight exceeds their adhesion and they slide down in slow sheets, joining the films of water on the floor, joining the capillary climb up the walls, joining the fog in the air, the whole system cycling endlessly with no inputs and no outputs, a closed wet economy.

Nothing here is alive in any sense a biologist would recognize. Nothing metabolizes, nothing respires, nothing reproduces. Things accrete. Things oxidize. Things precipitate out of solution and harden into forms that echo the shapes of living things—fungi, ferns, worms, coral—without ever having lived themselves. This is the grammar of rust, and the mineral forest speaks it fluently, and the lesson of the grammar is that life is not the only way to make a forest. Patience is another way. Chemistry is another way. Time is another way.

## The Machinery

The machinery is not alive. This must be said first, because everything else about it suggests otherwise.

It stands along the walls in long ranks, repeating forms with a regularity that suggests manufacture rather than growth: cylindrical housings each the size of a wine barrel, ribbed with cooling fins like the barrel of an old air-cooled engine, mounted on cradles of iron that have themselves begun to corrode into the floor. The cradles are bolted to the stone with bolts that have rusted to twice their original size, so that the bolts look like swollen joints, and the iron of the cradle has begun to merge with the stone of the floor the way the lichen-rust merges with the walls, the boundary between metal and mineral blurred, a slow wedding of one substance into another.

The cylinders are repeating, but not identical. Some are taller, some shorter, some have more fins, some fewer. Some are intact and some have cracked open, and the cracked ones are the ones that show their interiors to the wet air, and the interiors are a more elaborate grammar of decay. There are coils of copper wire inside, gone green with verdigris but still holding their coiled shape with the stubbornness of a spring that has not yet decided to forget. There are shafts of something harder than steel, darker, that has not rusted at all, that gleams faintly where the dim light catches it, that may be some alloy you do not know the name of or may be something that is not metal at all. There are lenses—actual lenses, of glass or crystal or some material that has survived where the metals around it have not—that still, when the angle is right, throw a small bright coin of reflection onto the ceiling or into your eyes. The lenses were for focusing something. The shafts were for transmitting something. The coils were for generating or conducting something. You do not know what the something was. You understand, looking at them, that the something is now the air you are breathing and the water on your face and the phosphorescence in the stone and the faint trembling in the needles behind the glass.

Cables run between the cylinders. They sag under their own weight in long catenary curves, furred with the purple-black lichen-rust, and where two cables meet there is a junction box, and on each junction box there is a small glass window, and behind the glass a needle, and the needle still trembles. You have to put your face close to the window to see it; the trembling is so faint it is almost a stillness. But it is there. The needle moves, or moves fractionally, or moves in a way that is just barely distinguishable from the vibration of your own pulse in your own temples when you lean close. The needles were measuring something. They may still be measuring something. The thing they are measuring may be the slow expiration of the thing whose lung you are standing in.

Some of the cylinders are still warm. You discover this by accident, by putting your hand on one in passing and finding it warmer than the stone around it, not hot, not even warm the way a sun-warmed rock is warm, but warm the way a sleeping body is warm, a residual warmth that has no current source but has not yet dissipated into the cold of the surrounding rock. The warmth is older than you are. It may be older than your species. It is the warmth of a thing that has been running and has been turned off but has not yet finished being on.

The machinery was for making something. You understand this not by reading any plate or label—there are none, or there were none that survived—but by the shape of the grammar. The coils and the shafts and the lenses are not decorative. They are not commemorative. They are functional shapes, the shapes that engineering takes when engineering has a purpose, and the purpose has been written into the form the way grammar is written into a sentence. You cannot read the sentence. You can tell that there was one. You understand that the sentence, when it was spoken, made the air wet and made the walls phosphorescent and made the lichen-rust learn to drink.

The machinery is quiet now. But quiet is not the same as dead. A forest is quiet. A sleeping animal is quiet. The hall is quiet the way a clock is quiet when you hold it to your ear and can just barely hear the balance wheel turning. If you put your hand on a cylinder and hold it there for a long time, you may feel what you imagine to be a pulse, or you may imagine what you feel to be a pulse. The hall does not clarify.

## The Light

The light is not an inhabitant. But it has a character, and the character is that of something that has been asked to do a job for a very long time and has done it without complaint and will continue to do it without complaint for a long time after the asking has stopped.

It has no source. This is the first thing you learn. You look for a source. You look at the ceiling. You look at the walls. You look at the machinery, at the cylinders, at the junction boxes with their glass windows and their trembling needles. None of these are the source. The light comes from the stone itself, from the deeper layers of the basalt that the hall was carved out of, and the stone is faintly phosphorescent in those deeper layers, faintly, the way a screen left on in an empty room is faintly luminous, the way the ocean is faintly luminous at certain depths. The light enters the hall the way water enters a sponge—distributed, diffused, originating from the substance of the rock itself, every surface giving off a faint glow that combines with the glow of every other surface to produce an illumination that is everywhere and from nowhere.

The color of it is a cool blue-gray. Not the blue-gray of daylight, which is warm at its core; the blue-gray of a screen, of a luminous panel, of something that has been engineered to produce light without producing heat. The faun's amber eyes are the only warm color in the hall, and the verdigris green of the copper-fungus caps, and the calcium white of the fan-ferns, and these stand out against the pervasive blue-gray the way stars stand out against the night sky.

Nothing casts a shadow longer than itself. This is because there is no directional light to cast a shadow from. The light comes from everywhere, and so shadows can only form where one object blocks another object from receiving light from a particular direction, and since light comes from every direction, the only shadows are the small ones cast by an object onto itself, the darkness inside the copper-fungus caps where the gills curl under, the darkness inside the faun's rib-slits when the slits are closed. The rest of the hall is evenly illuminated, evenly washed, evenly revealed and concealed in equal measure.

You can see, but you cannot see far. The air itself is too thick with moisture and with whatever else is suspended in it to permit clear sight beyond a few body-lengths. Shapes resolve slowly as you move toward them—the way shapes resolve in dreams, the way shapes resolve underwater, the way shapes resolve in fog. The far end of the hall is a suggestion of darker stone. The alcoves along the walls are hints of deeper recesses. The ranks of machinery are presences, repeating forms that grow more defined as you approach them and less defined as you move away. The hall has a horizon, but the horizon is close, and it is made of water, and it moves.

Somewhere in the hall there is a brighter place. You have not found it. You suspect it is where the machinery is still running, deep in the rock, in a chamber you have not yet reached, and that if you found it you would not be able to look at it. The needles in the junction boxes tremble in the direction of that brighter place, or you imagine they do. The faun's many eyes, when they are not watching you, watch in the direction of that brighter place. The capillary climb of water on the walls is more pronounced on the walls that face that brighter place. The whole hall leans, very slightly, toward whatever is at its center.

## The Water

The water is not an inhabitant. But it is the medium, and the medium is the message, and the message is that this place has been wet for a very long time and will be wet for a long time after.

It does not flow. It migrates. This is the distinction the hall insists upon. Flow implies a destination, a downhill, a mouth. Migration implies only motion, without direction, without purpose, the endless cycling of a substance through a closed system. The water climbs the walls in capillary defiance of gravity. It hangs in the air as fog so fine it feels less like weather than like a property of the place, the way temperature is a property, the way pressure is a property. It beads on every iron surface like a skin of glass that will not quite fall. It gathers in the shallows where the floor dips, in the alcoves where the faun rest, in the junction boxes where the needles tremble. It enters the stone and moves through the stone and emerges from the stone and returns to the air and falls back to the floor and enters the stone again. The cycle has no inputs and no outputs. The water that is on your face now is the water that was on someone's face a thousand years ago, and will be on someone's face a thousand years hence.

It tastes of iron and of something older than iron. Mineral, maybe. The dissolved memory of something that lived here once and stopped. The lichen-rust drinks it and incorporates it. The salt tubeworms precipitate it. The fan-ferns accrete from it. The faun breathe it through their gill-slits and exhale it back into the air. The whole hall is a single body of water in different states of solution and suspension and precipitation, cycling through itself, the way blood cycles through a body, the way sap cycles through a tree.

The condensate collects on your eyelashes if you stand still too long. The stones glisten. There is no dry surface. There may never have been one. When you leave the hall, if you leave the hall, you will carry the water on your skin and in your hair and in the fabric of your clothes, and you will smell of iron and mineral for days afterward, and people who meet you will ask where you have been, and you will not know how to answer, because the answer is a place that does not exist on any map, a lung inside a ribcage, a chamber inside a larger body, a place where water does not fall because there is nowhere for it to fall to.
