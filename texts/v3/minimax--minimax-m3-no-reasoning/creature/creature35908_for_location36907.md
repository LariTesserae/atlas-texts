# The Custodians of the Slow Plain

What lives here does not live the way a thing lives.

The custodians — if that is the word, and no other word fits better, though none fits well — are the creatures that rise from the stone like sleeping hills that have, at last, decided to rise. They are not animals. They have made no peace with being categorized. They are something the old taxonomies never had a drawer for, and so they sit, patient and uncatalogued, in the long gray silence of the plain.

## The Form

A custodian is, at first, difficult to distinguish from the stone it stands upon. Its hide — if hide is the right word, and there is no better one — is the same dark gray as the basalt around it, weathered into the same long folds, the same softened geometry. Where the plain has been polished by ages into a series of shallow swells, the custodian has its own swells, and the two seem to belong to the same sentence. The flanks are faceted in places that trouble the eye: a shoulder too planar, a ridge too even, an angle where angles should not be. The growing has a logic to it. The proportions are not accidents. There is intention somewhere in the silhouette, but the intention is not human, and it is not machine, and it is not the blind intention of a crystal. It is something else, something the categories cannot hold.

A custodian stands about the size of a small hill. From a distance of a few hundred meters it is difficult to be certain it is alive. The breath gives it away. Not audibly — the lungs of the plain make no sound — but visibly. A slow distortion of the gray light at the rim of its flanks, a steady pulse that means air is being moved through a body the size of a building. The pulse is patient. It does not hurry. Each breath might take ten minutes. The custodian could be a geological feature, were it not for the slow shimmer that says *something is happening in here.*

## The Work

The custodian works.

This is the only word that survives the encounter, and even it bends under the weight. The work is not labor as anything would understand labor. It is more like a function. A custodian is a refinery, a foundry, a mill that does not know it is a mill. Somewhere on its flank — always on the downwind side, always oriented with a precision that feels deliberate — there is a seam. A long line where the surface changes. The texture becomes faintly ridged, slightly warmer in the visible spectrum, as if the skin there is a fraction of a degree above the rest, as if the skin there is *thinking*.

From the seam, very slowly, a dark liquid is extruded. It is thick. It moves with the patience of cooling honey. It finds the grooves that the custodian's own hide has worn into itself over centuries of standing in the same place, and it follows those grooves down the flank, down toward a vent low on the body, and from the vent it falls in a long heavy thread to the stone. The thread falls. It strikes. It spreads.

Where it strikes, the stone changes.

Over hours, a channel is cut — the length of a man, the width of a finger. Over years, a small apron of new material extends from the custodian's flank and meets the plain. Over decades, a new surface is made. The dark liquid is not waste. It is not excrement. It is something the plain needs, or something the custodian needs the plain to have, and the difference between the two is not visible from where you stand. The liquid is a fertilizer, a binder, a feed. It is consumed by the stone and the stone is changed, and the changed stone feeds back into the custodian through its roots or its underside or its patient integration with the plain, and the cycle goes on.

A custodian is an industrial process. It is alive. It is silent. It does not know it is being watched.

## The Spacing

This is where the unease begins.

There are other custodians. They are visible, in the way that things are visible in the unlit gray of the plain — not seen so much as inferred, smudges against smudges, darker shapes against the lighter gray of the horizon. One to the north, a little more distinct, its heat-shimmer a faint waver in the air. One to the south, possibly two, almost lost in the atmospheric murk. None closer than a morning's walk. None farther than a day's. The spacing between them is too even. The spacing is not accident.

A custodian stands in relation to its neighbors the way cells stand in a tissue. There is a distance, and the distance is correct. The plain is being tended, and the tending requires a geometry that the custodians maintain without appearing to maintain it. They do not walk. They do not move in any way visible across the timescale of a human life. They simply are where they are, and they have always been where they are, and the distances between them are part of the work.

## What They Are

It is difficult, standing on the plain, not to feel that the custodians are the plain.

The plain is not stone. The plain is the long slow product of the custodians' work — layer after layer of processed basalt, extruded, refined, reabsorbed, extruded again. The stone you stand on was, a thousand thousand years ago, the flank of a custodian, or the apron of dark liquid that fell from a seam. The stone remembers. It remembers in its texture, in the way every edge has been folded under, in the way the pools sit in their low pockets in the same places they sat a thousand years ago, rimmed with the same thin white crust of mineral that is not quite salt and not quite ice.

The custodians do not eat, in any way visible. They do not eat each other. They do not hunt. They do not migrate. They take in what the stone and the air and the thin gray light provide, and they put out the long slow seams of dark liquid, and they stand, and they breathe, and they are here.

A custodian might live ten thousand years. It might live a million. There is no way to know. The plain does not record their births. There are no young custodians visible. There are no dead ones. There is only the patient continuation of the work, and the work has been going on so long that the work and the plain have become the same thing.

## The Watching

This is the part the place cannot explain.

The moisture collects in the same low pockets. The dark liquid from the seams flows in the same directions. The channels cut by the extrusion curve in the same arcs. The orange lichen — if it is lichen, if it is alive at all — grows on the same higher stones. The black stalk in the far distance does not move. The vapor over the pools falls and rises and falls again on a cycle that has been the same cycle for as long as the plain has been the plain.

Something is being kept. Something is being tended. The custodians are not the tenders, or they are the tenders and the thing tended is the same thing, or the tenders are the tended and the plain is the tending, and the difference between all of these is not visible from where you stand.

You feel watched. You feel held. You feel that the plain knows you are here, and that the knowing does not require a watcher, because the watching is built into the stone, into the air, into the slow patient breath of the things that rise from the stone and are, in some sense, the stone.

The custodians do not see you. They have no eyes. They have no interest in the seeing of things.

They are busy.

They have always been busy.

They will be busy when you are gone, and the gray will go on being gray, and the silence will go on being the only sound, and the plain will go on being made, very slowly, by things that do not know they are alive.

This is what lives here.

This is what has always lived here.

This is what will live here after you have stopped being a thing that can stand on it.
