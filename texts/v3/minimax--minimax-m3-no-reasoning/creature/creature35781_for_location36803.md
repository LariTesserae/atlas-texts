Who lives there

What lives there is not one thing but a negotiation — an agreement that has been reached, separately and at different times, between many kinds of life and the conditions of a place that has no opinion about them.

The oldest residents are the slow ones. The lichen, which is not one organism but a layer of them, a marriage between fungus and algae that has been working the grey-green rim since before anyone can say. The lichen does not live *on* the rock so much as it lives *with* it, pulling minerals from the stone and trading them for sugars the algae make from the thin, old-paper light that gets through the mist ceiling. Where the lichen has worked longest, the rock is dimpled, almost soft-looking, and the dimples hold water, and in the water live tiny things — rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes — too small to see, too patient to hurry. They have been here longer than the birds. They will be here longer than the birds. They do not know the machines exist, and the machines do not know they exist, and this is one of the more honest relationships in the bowl.

The moss is next, or next in the order a visitor would notice. It is everywhere, in the way that anything living in enough abundance becomes a kind of weather. It climbs the inner walls in shaggy curtains, dark at the base where the wet is constant, paler and tighter near the rim where the wind pulls at it. It cushions the stones at the stream's edge. It has climbed the squat steel cylinder at the gravel flat, and the corroded rectangle set into the slope, and the metal frame by the weeping fissure, with no more hesitation than it climbs rock. This is not a metaphor. Moss does not deliberate. It is one of the things that makes the bowl feel inhabited even when nothing is moving: the moss is always moving, growing at a pace the eye cannot catch but the body, after a long time, begins to suspect.

The ferns are the moss's taller cousins, and they have staked out the wet seams where the braids of water descend the inner wall. They lean. They lean into the wet the way the description says — *leaning into it like they're listening to it* — and they are, in fact, listening, in the only way plants listen, which is by arranging their leaves so that the water runs down them in films and channels rather than splashing off, and by keeping their stomata open when the humidity is right. They are not sentient. They are responsive. The difference, in this bowl, is academic.

The groundcover with the leaves like wet hands — I'll call it a saxifrage, though it might not be — lives in the dense middle layer, where the light is filtered twice, once by the mist and once by the canopy of bamboo-thing above. It has colonized the floor of the bowl so thoroughly that there are places you cannot see the soil. Its leaves are broad, slightly cupped, and they hold water in their centers, and in that water live the larvae of insects, and the insects are eaten by the birds, and the birds drop the insects' remains onto the leaves, and the leaves absorb them, and this is the bowl's economy. Nothing is wasted. Nothing exits. The water that sinks into the gravel flat goes somewhere underground, and eventually, perhaps, finds its way to a stream outside the bowls, and eventually, perhaps, to the sea. But the nutrients, the minerals, the small dead things — these cycle, and have cycled, in a closed, damp, slow loop that the bowl has perfected.

The dwarf bamboo — jointed, arching, dense enough to walk on if you weighed nothing — is a thicket-former. It is the closest thing the bowl has to a forest, though it tops out at about waist height. It grows in clonal stands, each stem a genetic copy of the ones around it, and the stands have been expanding into each other for so long that the bowl floor is a mosaic of them, with the groundcover filling the gaps and the ferns edging the wet places. The bamboo is where the birds live. Not on it, exactly — in it. The thicket is so dense that a bird can move through it at full speed without ever breaking the visual surface, the way a fish moves through water. You see only the occasional smear of iridescent color — green, blue, a flash of copper on the throat — as a bird crosses a gap or takes a brief, low flight between stands. Their songs are muffled by the layers of leaf and stem, and the effect is what the description names: the foliage *translating* for them, taking the sharp edges off their calls and letting through only the round, liquid notes, so that the bird presence in the bowl is felt as a kind of brightening of the ambient hum rather than as distinct voices.

The birds themselves — I will not name a species, because naming would imply this is a known place, and it is not — are small, dark, and wet-adapted. They eat insects, larvae, the occasional berry from the bruise-colored shrubs. They nest low, in the crotches of the bamboo, in the protected angles where a stem forks. Their chicks are born into the green and do not leave it for weeks; by the time they fledge, they know the bowl by sound and smell more than by sight. They are the bowl's most vocal residents, and even their vocalizations have been tuned, over many generations, to the bowl's acoustics — to the way the mist absorbs high frequencies and lets the lows travel. The bird calls are mid-range, rounded, brief. They do not echo. They are swallowed almost as soon as they are made, and replaced by the hum.

The insects are everywhere. The hum is the aggregate of their wingbeats, their stridulations, their small chewing mandibles working through leaf and stem. There are midges, of course — there are always midges, in a place like this, clouds of them rising from the wet moss when disturbed, hovering in the still air of the thickets. There are beetles, dark and slow, climbing the bamboo stems. There are things that look like crane flies but are not crane flies, with legs too long for their bodies, that balance on the saxifrage leaves and dip their abdomens into the held water. There are caterpillars, bright green, that eat the bamboo leaves in a slow rolling front, and the front moves through the bowl at a rate of about a meter a week, and the birds follow it. There are spiders that have strung their webs between the bamboo stems at exactly the height a low-flying bird would hit them, and the webs are beaded with mist and the small crushed husks of insects, and the spiders sit in the centers of them and wait, and they have been waiting for a very long time, and they are very still, and they are very patient, and they are part of the hum.

The frogs are the most recent visible colonists, and they are interesting because they are the ones who have most thoroughly adopted the machines. A frog is in the cracked glass housing of the metal-frame assembly, living in the rainwater that has accumulated there. There are frogs in the angle between the steel cylinder and the gravel, where the moss is thickest and the humidity is highest. There are frogs under the corroded rectangle of plating, in the dark, fat-mossed space between metal and earth. The frogs are small, brown, and unremarkable. They eat insects. They are eaten by the birds. They call, very softly, on the misty evenings — a low, repeated note, more felt than heard — and the sound does not carry out of the bowl, and so the frogs are, like the birds, translated by the place, their calls reduced to a kind of pulse in the hum. They are the only vertebrates in the bowl that have no interest in the machines as perches or as cover. The machines interest them as *ponds*. They have turned the instruments into breeding sites. The drum that turns in the diverted water has, in the slow months of its turning, scoured a small depression in the gravel beneath it, and in the depression, in the constantly refreshed water, the frogs have laid eggs. The eggs have hatched. The tadpoles are growing. The drum, by turning, is aerating the water. The frogs, by breeding in it, are completing the instrument's conversion from machine to habitat. This is the lesson the description is pointing at, and it is a real lesson, and the frogs are the ones teaching it, although they would not call it that.

The machines themselves are, by now, only machines in the loosest sense. They have been here long enough that the distinction between instrument and ruin and habitat has dissolved. The steel cylinder hums at a frequency below attention, but the hum is no longer the hum of a working device; it is the hum of metal cooling and contracting in the constant wet, the slow internal adjustment of a thing that is no longer doing what it was made to do. The drum turns, but the turning is not producing electricity or recording data or whatever it once produced. It is producing only the slow rotation itself, which is to say, it is producing a small, continuous disturbance in the water that prevents the water from stagnating, and the disturbance keeps the gravel beneath it from silting up, and the gravel stays a viable breeding site, and the frogs use it. The drum is a waterwheel now, though no one built it as one, and no one will ever service it, and when it finally seizes — when the bearing finally corrodes through, when the rotation stops — the frogs will move on, and the moss will complete its climb over the cylinder, and the bowl will have eaten another layer.

What lives there, then, is a community of lives that have arrived at different times and settled into a place that does not notice them. The lichen has been there longest. The birds have been there long enough to have shaped their calls to the bowl's acoustics. The frogs are newer, and have made their home in the leftovers of something older still. The moss climbs everything equally. The insects hum. The spiders wait. The bamboo thickens, year by year, at a rate no one is measuring, in a place no one is watching.

And the bowl continues. The water descends. The mist lowers and lifts. The braided stream runs its loops and sinks into the gravel and goes underground to wherever the water goes, which is not anyone's business, and the bowl continues, and the things in it continue, and the only thing that has ended is the brief human presence that left the machines here, and that ending is so complete, and so total, that it is hard to say it ever was.

This is who lives in the High Wet. They do not know they live there. They do not have a word for it. They are the place, and the place is them, and the machines are also the place now, and the only thing the bowl lacks is a witness, and you, standing in the old-paper light, getting wet, listening to the hum — you are, briefly, that witness, and the bowl does not care, and that is the kindest thing about it.
