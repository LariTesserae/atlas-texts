# The Inhabitants of the Hollow Vaults

A person is walking the concourse. The person is far away — the curve has taken them around, and they are a hundred meters off, or two hundred, in the tallow-colored dim. They are walking in the way that everyone walks the concourse: alone, slowly, with the slightly stiff gait of someone who has been walking for a long time and is not interested in arriving. They are small. The ribs overhead are larger than they are. The photophore panels are the color of the underside of a stone. The grit whispers under their feet, and the whisper is the only thing that tells you, from this distance, that they are a person and not a piece of the architecture that has begun, for no reason, to move.

---

The person is, like all the people here, the color of the place. Not the same color — not the bleached calcium-white of the ribs, not the gray-yellow of the photophores — but *adjacent* to those colors, the way the grit is adjacent, the way a stain on stone is adjacent. Their skin is the color of the grit with a half-shade of warmth in it, as if a small amount of blood, never quite enough to make them pink, is moving underneath. Their hair, if they have hair, is the same, or a shade lighter, and is worn in whatever way the previous person who lived in their alcove wore theirs, because combs here are not made and hands do what hands do.

Their body is small in the way that bodies become small in places where food is not abundant and motion is not free. They are not starved. They are not thin in the manner of deprivation. They are simply *compact* — narrow through the shoulders, narrow through the hips, made of the minimum amount of person that can do what needs doing, which here is very little. They have not been sick, or they have been sick and recovered in the slow way the place recovers, which is to say that the sickness was not really noticed and the recovery is still happening and may continue to happen for the rest of their life. Their joints are not large. Their hands are not large. Their feet are bare, or wrapped in something that was once hide and is now indistinguishable from hide, and they make the only sound in the vault, the soft whisper of grit closing behind them.

---

What they are wearing is, in the way of things here, the previous person. The colony-leather robe, or the calcium tunic, or the stiffened not-hide wrap, was not made by them. It was left in the alcove by the person who lived there before, and the person before that, and the person before that, in a chain of inheritance so long that no one is interested in its beginning. The clothes have been mended with thread that is itself a strip of the same material, and the mends are the same color as the cloth, and the cloth is the same color as the wall, and so the person is a layered thing, a stratigraphy of small maintenances, the way the wall is a stratigraphy of the colony's slow secretions. When a robe finally gives up, it is not thrown away. It is hung in an alcove, and the next person who needs it takes it, and the robe continues.

The face is the place where the convergence of person and architecture is most advanced. By thirty — by the time a person has lived here long enough to have stopped being startled by the *settling* — the lines of the face have settled into something that, in the dimness, with the small sourceless light, is hard to distinguish from a weathered rib surface. The cheekbones are slightly too pronounced. The hollows of the temples are slightly too deep. The skin has taken on the matte quality of the stone, as if the air of the vault has, over years, leached out whatever made the skin look like skin and replaced it with whatever makes the stone look like stone. By fifty, the person is, in profile, the same shape as the wall. By seventy — if seventy is reached, which is not guaranteed — the person is the wall, in a sense that is not metaphor and not quite literal either. They sit. They do not move for hours. A fly that does not exist in this place would not land on them, because they are not the kind of thing a fly lands on. They are the kind of thing a fly would not notice.

---

The habits are the people, more than the bodies are. The bodies are small and quiet and the color of grit. The habits are what makes a person in this place — the long attention to small things, the ways of reading the architecture as if it were text.

A person can sit in an alcove for six hours and not be noticed. This is not because they are trying not to be noticed, although they may be, but because the eye here is trained to look for *changes*, and a person who is not changing is, for practical purposes, not there. The vault itself teaches the eye this. The photophores do not change. The grit does not change. The crusts at the base of the ribs do not change, or change so slowly that change and stillness are the same thing. A person, to be seen, must *do* something. Walking is something. Tapping a panel is something. But sitting, even breathing, is not, after a while, enough to register. The person becomes part of the room, the way a stain is part of a wall, the way a crack is part of a ceiling.

They can tell the state of a photophore panel by the sound it makes when tapped. The living panels, the ones whose internal channels are still open, give a small high sound, like a knuckle on a thin ceramic. The dead panels — the cracked ones, the ones whose channels have silted with grit or collapsed under the slow settling of the colony — give a lower sound, a duller sound, the sound of something with no air behind it. A person walks the concourse, and from time to time they reach up and tap a panel with the back of a fingernail, and they listen, and they know. They do not write down what they know. They carry it. The knowledge is in the body, the way a sailor carries the feel of a wave in the bones of the feet.

They can tell, by the smell of the grit, when the wind outside is going to change. The grit has many smells, all faint, all slow. There is the smell of dry stone, which is the default. There is the smell of a slightly damper stone, which means that outside, somewhere, moisture has entered the air in some way, perhaps from a freak convection up from a deeper layer of the plain, and the panels have taken it in and are giving it back. There is the smell of iron, which means the wind has been scouring a particular kind of deposit on the plain and the dust is carrying it. There is the smell of nothing at all, which is the smell the grit gives off when the wind is going to be very strong for the next several hours, because the wind is taking everything with it. The person smells the grit, perhaps without bending down, perhaps without appearing to do anything, and they know, and they walk back to the chamber they came from, and they sit.

---

The long walk is what they do. Every day — or what passes for a day, which is whatever number of *settlings* have occurred since the last time they thought to count, which they may not — every day, every person who is able walks the long curve of the concourse, alone, in silence, in the same direction. They do not know why they do this. They do it the way the coiled things withdraw into their palm-cavities: as a leftover protocol of something, an old pattern that is still being executed because it has not been stopped. The walk is not a journey. The walk does not arrive. The walk is the walk. They walk for several hours. They see the ribwork thicken. They see the photophores become fewer, more cracked, more dim. They see the crusts at the base of the ribs become larger, more iridescent, more irregular in their placement. They see, if they walk far enough, the tapering of the vault, the lowering of the ceiling, the thickening of the ribs, the photophores becoming stone, the stone becoming wall, the wall becoming the low blunt arch that is the entrance to the chamber at the end. They see the arch. They do not always go in.

The *settling* is the clock. Twice — or once, or three times, or not at all — the vault makes its small sound, a single soft *tok* somewhere in the distance, and the dust on the floor of a chamber ripples outward in a perfect circle, very small, and is still. The people mark time by it, the way sailors once marked time by the creak of a hull. They do not count. The count is not useful. There may be eighty of them. There may be a hundred and twenty. The number has not been taken for several generations, and the not-taking is not neglect. It is the way the place is. The place does not count itself. The place does not need to know how many ribs it has, or how many photophores are cracked, or how many palm-cavities still contain a coil. The people have inherited this not-counting. The people are the only people in the world. They do not know this. They do not need to know it.

---

The chamber at the end is for when there is no one else.

This is what the people say, in their murmuring way, about the chamber at the end of the long curve. They do not call it a chamber. They have a word for it, a word that translates, roughly, as *the listening*, but they do not say the word. They point, sometimes, with a tilt of the head. They make a small gesture with the hand, a cupping, as if the hand were an ear. They do not say: *I went there. I sat on the warm floor. I did whatever I did.* They come back. They resume. The next person, when their silence has become so total that something must be done about it, walks the long curve, alone, and goes in, and comes back, and does not say.

What happens in the chamber is unknown to anyone but the person who goes there, and to the chamber itself, which does not say. The floor of the chamber is not grit. The floor is a hard, dark, glassy surface, slightly domed, warm to the touch, like a sleeping animal's flank. The ceiling is the fist of the colony, all the ribs gathered and fused, with a small black opening at the top where the seam-network emerges and vanishes into the dark. The chamber is round. The chamber is the end of the concourse. The chamber is the only place in the vault that is not the vault.

When a person is in the chamber, they do whatever they do. They sit on the warm floor. They put their hands on it. They look up at the fist. They listen. They are listening to the *settling*, perhaps. They are listening to the faint, sourceless hum that the vault makes, the one that is not a sound, that is more like a change in the pressure of the air, the way your ears pop in a high place. They are listening to themselves. They are listening to the place. They are listening to the place listening to them. After a time, they stand, and walk back, and rejoin the others, and are quiet again, and the chamber is empty until the next person comes.

---

The coiled things live in the same chambers as the people. They are not pets. They are not livestock. They are not commensal in any way that a person from elsewhere would recognize. They are something that the vault continues to make, in the same way that the vault continues to make the crusts, in the same way that the vault continues to make the *settling*. They are a leftover protocol. They are *running*.

The coils are whitish. They are the size of a small forearm, or a large one, depending on the coil. They are coiled in the palm-cavities — the small chambers, shoulder-deep, between the ribs at the floor, shaped like open hands. They are coiled tightly, the way a snail is coiled, but the coils are flat against the wall of the cavity, not free in space. They look, from a distance, like a washcloth that has been wadded up and pressed into a niche. They do not move, in the way that you would say a stone does not move. But they are not stone.

When a shadow falls across a coil — the shadow of a person walking past, the shadow of a person reaching up to tap a photophore, the shadow of nothing in particular, the kind of shadow that happens in a vault when the light from a panel is interrupted by the curve of the architecture — the coil withdraws. It withdraws into the cavity, deeper than it was, a centimeter, two centimeters, a small motion that you would not see if you were not looking for it. It withdraws in a way that is slow, slower than a hand drawing back from a hot surface, slower than a snail pulling into its shell. After a time, the coil comes out again. It comes out so slowly that you would not see it move. You would see it, after an hour, in a slightly different position. You would not have seen the motion. The motion is below the threshold of the eye.

Sometimes a coil makes a sound. The sound is not a sound. It is a change in the air, a faint *click* of pressure, the way your ears pop in a high place. You do not hear it. You feel that something has happened. When you turn to look, the coil is a little deeper in its cavity. That is all. The sound is the only thing the coil has ever done that has made any register on a person, and even the sound is barely a thing.

The people do not eat the coils. The people do not fear the coils. The people do not worship the coils. The people, mostly, do not look at the coils, in the way that they do not look at the crusts, in the way that they do not look at the place, because to look is to begin to look for meaning, and the meaning, if there is one, is not for them.

The coils are running. The coils are the colony's last small motions, the last twitches of a nervous system that was laid down in tidal flats a long time ago, when the valley was something other than what it is now. The coils are not alive, in the way that a person is alive. The coils are something else. They are a thing the dead colony is doing with the small amount of energy that the dead colony has left. They are the smallest inhabitants of the vault, and the oldest, in the sense that they have been doing the same thing for the longest time.

---

The crusts are the slowest life. They are slower than the people, slower than the coils, slower than the wind. They are the speed of stone, which is to say they are not a speed at all, but a tendency, the way water has a tendency to find the lowest point. The crusts have a tendency to grow in concentric terraces. The terraces are the size and shape of dinner plates stacked. The edges of the terraces are faintly iridescent, the way a piece of oiled steel is iridescent, and if you look at one for long enough it begins to look like it is breathing, though it is not. The breathing is the way the eye interprets a very slow change. The crusts are not breathing. The crusts are growing, at a rate so slow that growing and not growing are, for practical purposes, the same.

The crusts are the color of bone, ash, milk, ash again. They are not food. They are not wood. They are not anything that a person can use, except to look at, and the people do not look at them, because to look at them is to begin to ask what they are for, and they are not for anything. They are something the world is making. The world is making them out of whatever the dead colony is still giving off, the slow exudations of calcium and other things from the bones of the colony, the seepage that the photophores take in and give back, the trace minerals in the grit. The crusts are the visible part of that making. The rest is invisible, and is happening inside the walls, and is not a thing anyone has ever been interested in.

---

The pillar is the largest single growth in the concourse. It is a landmark in the way that a single tree in a field is a landmark, which is to say it is the only thing that breaks the long flat perspective of the ribwork and the photophores and the grit. It is three meters tall. It is a meter across. It is ribbed like a stamen. It leans at fifteen degrees to the vertical, as if it had once been standing straighter and had slowly, over centuries, settled into the angle it now holds. At its crown, it opens into a cup the size of a washbasin. The cup is dry. The cup has been dry for a very long time. The dust of the plain has not reached the cup, because the cup is inside the vault, and the wind does not reach inside the vault, but the cup is dry in a way that suggests it has been dry for longer than the vault has been sheltering it. The cup was always dry. The cup is what the cup is.

The pillar is not dry. Inside the pillar, fluid moves. You can see the surface of the fluid tremble, if you crouch and wait, which is a thing the people do not do, but which a person from elsewhere, an observer, might do. The fluid is dark. The fluid has the consistency of cold honey. The fluid moves in a slow way, in a way that has a rhythm but not a cycle, the way the *settling* has a rhythm but not a cycle. The fluid does not smell of anything. The fluid is not water. The fluid is something the colony is still making, in small amounts, in the channels inside the pillar, the way the colony is still making the crusts, in the channels inside the walls, the way the colony is still making the coils, in the channels inside the palm-cavities. The pillar is the largest of these makings. The pillar is the only one that has pooled.

The people do not drink the fluid. The people do not touch the pillar. The pillar is too large, and too still, and too much like a thing that has its own business. The pillar is a kind of relative. The pillar is the colony, still, in some small way, being the colony.

---

The vault itself lives here. The vault is dead, but it is doing things. It is settling. It is breathing, in the osmotic rhythm of the photophores. It is making crusts. It is making coils. It is making, in the pillar, the slow dark fluid. The vault is a kind of animal, in the way that a dead animal is still an animal, in the way that a dead forest is still a forest. The vault is alive in the way that ruins are alive: doing the small, slow, automatic things that bodies and forests and vaults do, after the person who was doing them is no longer doing them on purpose.

It is difficult, in the end, to say who lives here.

The people live here. The people are eighty, or a hundred and twenty, and they are small and quiet and the color of grit, and they wear what was left for them, and they walk the long curve, and they sit in the alcoves, and they tap the panels, and they smell the grit, and sometimes one of them walks to the chamber at the end and sits on the warm floor and does whatever they do there, alone, and comes back, and does not say.

The coils live here. The coils are whitish, and they are coiled in the palm-cavities, and they withdraw from shadow and emerge again, slowly, and they make a sound that is not a sound, a *click* of pressure, and they are running. They are the last motions of the colony's nervous system. They are the smallest inhabitants of the vault, and the oldest, in the sense that they have been doing the same thing for the longest time.

The crusts live here. The crusts are at the base of every third rib, in concentric terraces, in pale shades, with faintly iridescent edges, and they are growing, or not growing, at a rate that is below the threshold of observation. They are the slowest inhabitants. They are the most patient.

The pillar lives here. The pillar is a landmark, three meters tall, leaning, with a dry cup at the crown and a slow dark fluid moving inside. The pillar is the largest single inhabitant. The pillar is the most like an animal, in the way that an animal is a thing that contains fluid and moves it around.

The people would answer, if you asked who lives here. The people would answer, in their murmuring way, by pointing at themselves, and by pointing at the coils, and by pointing at the crusts, and by pointing at the pillar, and by tilting their head toward the chamber at the end, the chamber that is for when there is no one else. The people would not, in answering, distinguish themselves from the coils or the crusts or the pillar. The people would not see the distinction. The people are part of the vault, the way the coils are part of the vault, the way the crusts are part of the vault, and the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited is not a distinction the vault makes, and is not a distinction the people have learned to make.

The question is not: who lives here. The question is: where does the inhabiting end and the inhabited begin. The vault does not know. The people do not know. The coils, if they know, do not say. The crusts do not say. The pillar does not say. The chamber at the end, the listening, listens, and does not say.

The grit whispers under your feet, or under theirs. The photophores glow faintly. The wind, outside, does not reach. The plain continues, and the horizon is invisible, and the light never brightens, and the sound never starts, and the people, and the coils, and the crusts, and the pillar, and the chamber, and the grit, and the light, and the silence, continue.
