# Who Lives on the Brass Ledges

The settlement has perhaps two hundred people in it now, perhaps fewer. The census has not been taken in a long time. The census would not be accurate if it had been.

## The Mill People

At the center, the mill anchors the population. There is **Don Eulogio**, who is said to be seventy-eight and is said to be younger than that, who runs the stone-crushing operation and has run it since before the diesel was diesel, since the time the upper slopes yielded ore that was worth crushing. He has three sons who are grown and gone. One is in the coastal city. One is in the mine on the other side of the range. One is buried on the second terrace, under a *Polylepis*, with a stone at his head that Eulogio carved himself, that has a name and two dates and no further information. Eulogio does not speak of this son. Eulogio does not speak of much. He starts the diesel at six in the morning and again at three in the afternoon. Between those hours he sits on a low stone wall by the spring and he *thinks*, and the thinking of Eulogio is a thing that the rest of the settlement gives a wide berth to.

There is **Mercedes**, his daughter-in-law, the widow, who is perhaps forty and looks older and is younger than she looks. She keeps a small *tienda* in a stone room that opens onto the plaza, and she sells *chicha*, and dried meat, and small paper packets of coca leaves that come up from the lower valleys in a sack on the donkey cart twice a month. She does not smile. She does not need to. She knows what she has and what she does not have, and the knowing is enough.

There is **Tomás**, who is not her son but who might as well be, who works the mill when Eulogio's hands have forgotten how. Tomás is twenty-three, slight, with the permanent squint of a boy who has looked at too much sun on too much stone. He has one good eye and one eye that waters and does not quite focus. He has the look of a boy who has been told a secret he has not been told to share. He does not talk about the blue alpacas. Tomás does not talk about most things.

## The Weavers

In the stone houses along the upper terrace, there are the weavers. There are seven of them now. There were fifteen. They work in a low room that was once a chapel and is still called the chapel, though the cross is missing a horizontal arm and has been for decades. They spin the wool of the *vicuñas* and, sometimes, of the alpacas that are the right color. They weave long, narrow cloths in patterns that are not quite *geometric* and not quite *floral* and not quite *anything* that has a name. The cloths are sold to a man who comes up twice a year on a motorcycle that does not sound like a motorcycle, who pays in cash that is folded in half, who takes the cloths away and who is, the weavers pretend, a buyer in the city.

The weavers are mostly women. There is one man among them, **Alejo**, who is small and bearded and who hums when he works and who does not stop humming when he stops working. Alejo has been on the mountain for eleven years. He came up to *recuperate*, which is the polite word, and he has not recuperated. He sleeps in a corner of the chapel-weaving-room. He eats what the women give him. He is, they say, a *blessing*, though they do not say what they mean by that.

The eldest of the weavers is a woman called **Doña Pascuala**, who is older than Eulogio and younger than Eulogio and who is, in some way the settlement cannot quite name, *in charge*. She does not give orders. She does not need to. She is the one who decides which wool is spun and which wool is not spun. She is the one who decides, on the days when the bell is rung, whether it is rung at dawn or not. The bell is a thin brass thing, a centimeter thick and ten centimeters across, with a patina of green and a clapper of iron that has been replaced three times in living memory. The bell lives in the chapel. The bell is not rung every day. The bell is rung when Pascuala rings it.

## The Llama-Keepers

Down toward the paddock, at the lowest of the inhabited terraces, there are the *pasto*r*s* — the herders — though their title is too grand for what they do. There are **Yago** and **his son Cusi**, who is nine. Yago's wife is in the lower town, where the schools are, where the *cricket* is on the radio, where the heat is of the kind that does not ask permission. Cusi goes down to see her on the donkey cart, sometimes. Yago does not.

Yago has the weathered, the *creased*, the kind of face that looks like it was tanned first and then folded, like the leather of an old *alforja*. He has six llamas and two alpacas — of the normal color — and one *burro* that pulls the cart. The burro's name is **Ceniza**, which is a name that does not suit her. She is the color of wheat. She is the strongest thing on the mountain. She is the only animal on the mountain that has never been sick. Yago says this with some pride. Yago says this as though it is an answer to a question he has been asked.

There are also, in the lowest houses, **the twins**, who are called **Mateo** and **Mateo**, because their mother could not tell them apart and because no one has ever corrected her. The twins are perhaps sixteen. They are *separated* — one stays on the mountain, one goes down to the school — but when they are together, which is in the summers and the festival weeks, they are uncanny, and the older people of the settlement cross themselves in the old way when the twins walk past together, in the old way, which is a gesture no one under fifty knows how to do properly.

## The Engineer

There is also, in a low stone house at the edge of the spring, an **engineer** — or what passes for one — who is called **Don Horacio**, who came up the mountain thirty years ago to *install* something and has never left. He is an electrical engineer, formally, of the kind that was trained in the capital in the time when the capital trained engineers. He maintains the power lines. He maintains the diesel. He maintains, in some way that is not quite clear, the *sense* of the place — the *order* of the place — that is, he is the man to whom you go when the *feeling* of the place has become too much, when the *pressure* behind the eyes at three in the morning has become a thing that must be *named* and *answered*.

Don Horacio is thin and precise and has small wire-rimmed spectacles that he has repaired four times with wire from the power lines. He has a *radío* — a small battery radio — that receives, on a good day, three stations. He listens to it not for the news but for the *human voice*. He says the human voice, on the mountain, is a *correction* — a way of *reminding* the body that it is still a body and not part of the *stone*. He says this without irony. He says this as a man who is *correcting* something in himself by saying it.

Don Horacio does not sleep well. He is often, at three in the morning, walking the perimeter of the settlement with a small flashlight that has a red filter over it, because the red light, he says, does not *disturb* the *patience* of the *place*.

## The Quiet Ones

And then there are the others.

There is **a boy of about twelve**, whose name no one uses and who is called only *the boy*, who is not anyone's son in the settlement and who is not anyone's *anything*. He arrived one season, on foot, from below, with a small bag and a look on his face that has not changed. He does not speak. He does not need to. He is *seen*. He is *seen* on the terraces. He is *seen* at the spring. He is *seen*, sometimes, on the upper terraces in the small hours, where no one else goes. He is not asked about. He is *given* food by Mercedes, who puts it out on the step of the *tienda* in the mornings, and who takes the empty plate in at night, and who does not speak of it.

There is **a woman** who lives alone in one of the *abandoned* — in one of the *re-absorbed* — houses on the third terrace, where the stone has begun to come back into the walls. The woman is called **Esperanza**, though this is, the settlement suspects, not her name. She arrived ten years ago, in the rains, in a storm that broke the *Mutisia* on the upper gullies. She is perhaps fifty. She has the look of a woman who has been *somewhere* and who has chosen to *not* be there anymore. She keeps no animals. She grows nothing. She receives, once a month, a letter that comes up on the donkey cart in a brown envelope, and she reads it and she burns it and she does not speak of it. Esperanza is the one person in the settlement who has seen the blue alpacas and has said, *plainly*, what she has seen. She has said it once. She has not said it again.

And there is **the man who arrives on Tuesdays**.

No one knows who he is. He is a small man, dark, with a wide hat. He arrives, on Tuesdays, at the spring, at the same hour — three in the afternoon, when the clouds are coming in — and he sits on the low wall and he drinks a cup of *chicha* that Mercedes brings him without being asked. He does not speak. He does not speak to anyone. He sits for an hour. He leaves. He is gone by the time the rain has settled in. On Tuesdays, when he is there, the mill is *quiet* — Eulogio does not start the diesel — and the weavers do not weave. The settlement is *still*. The man on Tuesdays is the *stillness* of the place, made flesh, for an hour. He is not asked about. He is not waved away. He is *waited for*.

## What They Have in Common

What the people of the Brass Ledges have in common is not much, and is everything.

They are, almost all of them, *descended* from the people who worked the mine in the time when the mine was a mine — a hundred and fifty years ago, in the silver-and-tin years, when the terraces were cut and the buildings were raised and the chapel was a chapel. The surnames of the settlement are four, and they intermarry, and the children who come out of the intermarriages have the look of the place on them, the *look of the place*, which is to say the look of a place that has been *looked at* for a long time, by something patient.

They are, almost all of them, *tired* — tired in the way of people who work hard at a thing that does not quite need doing, who maintain a settlement that does not quite need maintaining, who *tend* a place that is *tending* itself.

They are, almost all of them, *aware* — aware of the *feeling*, the *underlying*, the slow hum of the *thinking* mountain. They do not speak of it in the same way. Some of them — Eulogio, perhaps, and the boy — *are* part of it. Some of them — Doña Pascuala, and Alejo, and the man on Tuesdays — *serve* it. Some of them — Don Horacio, and Esperanza — *resist* it, gently, with a small red light and a brown envelope. Some of them — the twins, and Tomás, and Mercedes — *endure* it, the way one endures a long marriage or a long illness or a long day.

They are, almost all of them, *kind* to one another — in the small, the *particular*, the *unremarkable* way of people who share a place. They share a cup of *chicha*. They share a packet of coca. They share a silence at the spring at three in the afternoon, when the clouds come in. They share the long, slow fact of the place, which is *alive*, which is *thinking*, which is *patient*, which is *watching*, which has been watching them for as long as they have been there, and which will watch them, with the same patience, when they are gone.

They are, in this place, guests.

They are guests who have stayed a long time.

They are guests who have begun, in the small ways, to belong.
