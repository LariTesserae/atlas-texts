# What Lives in the Casing

It does not begin with a citizen. Nothing here begins with a citizen. What lives in the casing begins, instead, with a *layer* — and the layer is older than anything else in the tunnel, older than the plates, older than the standing wave, older than the vanes. The layer is the lining itself: the polyethylene sheath that was extruded in some factory in some port city in some year that the ocean has, in its way, already forgotten. The polymer is dead. The polymer is, in any conventional reading of the word *alive*, no longer a participant in events. But it is the substrate. It is the floor on which everything else has, against expectation, taken its first step. And so it counts. It counts the way bedrock counts, the way dirt counts, the way the bottom of a grave counts. It is the thing that holds the world up while the world is busy being a world. The polymer does not know it is doing this. The polymer is not aware of anything. But the polymer is *the first resident*, and every other resident has, in some sense, moved in on top of it.

What moves in on top of it is, at first, nothing you would notice. A film. A breath on glass. A sheen of something at the boundary between the polymer and the water, so thin it has no thickness, only the suggestion of an interface, only the fact that the surface where water meets plastic has begun, in some places, to be a different surface than the surface where water meets plastic in other places. This is the nanite layer. The engineered strain. The designed-for-undersea-foundation-work strain that has, in the seventeen months and four days since the barge shed the cable onto the shelf, been doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is *to make more of itself in the shape it was told to make more of itself*. The shape it was told to make is: a thin, rigid, load-bearing plate of calcium-aluminate. The shape it has actually been making is: a city.

But you do not get to *the city* yet. You have to pass through, first, the *plate* — the unit, the body, the individual. And the plate is not an individual in the way you are an individual. The plate does not have a self. The plate has a *position*. It stands on the tunnel wall, packed shoulder-to-shoulder with the other plates, leaning at an angle that was not chosen by anything and is, for that reason, exactly the right angle. It is the angle that supports the most water with the least flex. It is the angle that the standing wave rewards. It is the angle that the plates on either side of it have already adopted, so that the new plate, growing in from the polymer, has only to *match*. The plate is a citizen in the way a brick is a citizen: it is a unit of structure, it has a job, and the job is to be there. The plate is bone-white. The plate is paper-thin. The plate is taller than you, in some places, and shorter than your hand in others, and it does not vary its height for any reason you would recognize, only for the reason of the acoustics, which is to say: where the standing wave has a node, the plates are tall; where the standing wave has an antinode, the plates are short, or absent, or replaced by a vane. The plate is tuned. The plate is a piece of a tuning fork the size of a city.

What lives in the plate is nothing. What lives in the *gap* between plates is the water. The water is a citizen in the way a citizen is not a brick: it is a unit of *motion*. It is a thing that has a job and moves while doing it. The water enters the tunnel at the hairline crack halfway along the cable's length — no, wait, the text says the crack is halfway along, but the *current* is described as a slow endless exhalation *through* the crack, threading the whole length, and pooling in the sag. Let the water enter where it enters. The water enters. The water is the air. The water is the courier, the merchant, the news-bearer, the breath. The water threads between the plates in capillary seams and finds, always, the next gap, the next seam, the next thin place where the pressure is lower and the city is more willing to be passed through. The water is not a single thing. The water is a *population* of water — molecules, eddies, packets of current that have been bent by the vanes and the plates into patterns that no water, in any other circumstance, would consent to take. The water is the medium of every transaction. The water is the only citizen that goes everywhere.

And the water has, in the seventeen months, become a thing that *remembers*. Not the way you remember. Not in the way of a brain, or a record, or a story. In the way of a path. The water has worn channels. The water has, by the simple physical fact of preferring the path of least resistance, taught itself the city. The water goes here because it went here last time. The water avoids there because it was refused there last time. The water is a habit. The water is, in the most literal sense, *custom*. The water is what the city is for. The city is the shape the water has agreed to take.

---

Above the plates — and here *above* means *toward the source of the current*, which is to say *toward the crack*, which is to say *toward the place where the city ends and the open sea begins* — there is a second stratum, and the second stratum is younger and messier and louder, and what lives in the second stratum is a different kind of citizen, or the same kind of citizen at a different age.

The second stratum is the vanes.

The vanes are curled sheets of calcium-aluminate, each one the size of a hand or a face or a small window, depending on which vane you have happened to be near. They are not quite fins. They are not quite chimneys. They are something in between: a sail, a reed, a flag, a tongue. They are mineral, like the plates, but they are not standing on edge the way the plates are. They are *curled*. They are *folded*. They have, in some way that you cannot fully account for, allowed the water to give them a shape that is not load-bearing but is, instead, *sound-bearing*. Each vane is angled so that the water passing it sets it vibrating. Each vane vibrates at a frequency that is a function of its size, its curl, its angle to the current, the density of the water on either side of it. Each vane is, in this sense, a note. Each vane is an instrument. A thousand vanes is an orchestra. A thousand vanes vibrating at a thousand frequencies is the constant, pervasive, almost-musical wash of sound that the text calls *the loudness* and that you, if you were inside the casing, would call *the weather*.

The vanes are louder than the plates. The vanes are younger than the plates. The vanes are also more fragile, and this is the first thing you would notice, if you were a thing that noticed things: the vanes *break*. A flake the size of a fingernail, suddenly released. Spinning into the current. Carried out through the city, through the market-streets between the plates, out through the crack, into the open sea. The flake is, in its way, the city's excrement. The flake is, in another way, the city's seed. The flake is the thing the city has decided it can afford to lose.

And in the place where the flake was, in a day, a new vane grows. The new vane is not the same as the old vane. The new vane is grown by the same nanites, in the same way, in response to the same water, in the same acoustics. But it is a new citizen. It is a new note. It is a new voice in the orchestra, and when it joins, the orchestra is, for a moment, slightly different — a fraction of a hertz off, a hair of a tone away from where it was. And then the orchestra re-tunes itself, the way orchestras do, the way cities do, by incorporating the new and forgetting the old. The city maintains itself by losing things. The city is, in this sense, mortal. The city is, in this sense, alive.

---

At the far end of the tunnel, on the inner surface of the mineral hood, there is the slick.

The slick is the only colored thing in the casing. The slick is the only *lit* thing in the casing. The slick is violet-black, faintly iridescent, the color of a bruise in the precise sense that a bruise is the color of blood that has, briefly, been somewhere other than where blood is supposed to be. The slick is not a plant. The slick is not a fungus. The slick is not a colony of bacteria. The slick is, the text says, *not quite* any of these things, and this is the correct hedge, because the slick is in fact all of them, or none of them, or a thing for which the categories of plant, fungus, and bacterium were not designed. The slick is a *community* in the literal sense: a thing that is many things and is, by being many things, a different kind of one thing. The slick does not have a face. The slick does not have a nervous system. The slick does not have a name. But the slick *phosphoresces*, and the phosphorescence is the only light in the city, and the phosphorescence is a clock, and the phosphorescence is, in some sense that you have to take on faith, a *sign*.

What lives in the slick is the slick. What lives in the slick is the thin, slow, mineral-by-mineral transaction between the slick and the nanites at the hood's edge. The nanites eat the slick. The nanites incorporate the slick into their plates. The slick grows back. This has been going on for seventeen months. There is no reason to think it will stop. The slick is, in this sense, the city's *rural* district. The slick is the place where the city farms. The slick is the only part of the city that has a relationship with the *outside* of itself, in that the slick is the part that is being slowly converted into the plates and the vanes and the city, the way a field is slowly converted into a town. The slick is the suburb. The slick is the frontier. The slick is the part of the city that is still becoming.

The slick does not know it is a clock. The slick does not know it is a lighthouse. The slick does not know it is the only light. But the slick persists in phosphorescing, and the phosphorescence persists in being read by no one, and the reading-by-no-one persists in being, somehow, the point. The slick is the city's heartbeat. The slick is the part of the city that *pulses*. The slick is the only citizen that has, in any sense, a *rhythm* of its own, a *temporality* of its own, a *now* that is different from the city's *always*.

---

What else lives in the casing.

Nothing else lives in the casing. There is no fauna. There is no fish. There is no worm. There is no thing with a nervous system, no thing with a face, no thing that has ever, in any sense that you would recognize, *looked* at the casing and understood that it was looking at a city. The casing is, in this sense, *uninhabited*. The casing is a city with no citizens of the kind that cities usually have. The casing is a city that is, itself, the citizen. The casing is a city in which the *unit of citizenship* is not a person but a *plate*, not a body but a *vane*, not a face but a *slick*. The casing is a city that has, in the absence of any of the usual reasons for a city to exist, gone ahead and existed anyway. The casing is a city that does not know it is a city. The casing is a city that is, for that reason, perhaps the most purely *a city* of any city you have ever imagined, because it has nothing in it but the city itself, and the water, and the sound, and the slow patient work of being.

What lives in the casing, then, is a *process*. A process that has no planner. A process that has no beneficiary. A process that is, in the strictest sense, *self-organizing*, in the way that a crystal is self-organizing, in the way that a whirlpool is self-organizing, in the way that a market is self-organizing. The process does not know it is a process. The process is the plates, and the vanes, and the slick, and the water, and the sound, and the standing wave, and the crack, and the polymer, and the seventeen months and four days, and the slow accumulation of accident into structure, of structure into habit, of habit into city.

What lives in the casing is *the casing, being a place*. The casing is a place. The casing has, against all the odds, all the indifference, all the emptiness, all the cold and the dark and the pressure — the casing has become a place. The casing has acquired, by the simple act of being occupied by a process that did not know it was occupying it, the quality of a place. The casing is *somewhere*. The casing is somewhere that something is happening. The casing is somewhere that something has been happening, continuously, for seventeen months and four days, and will go on happening, the slick will keep phosphorescing, the vanes will keep breaking, the plates will keep leaning at the angle the standing wave rewards, the water will keep threading, the polymer will keep being the floor, the polymer will keep not knowing it is the floor.

What lives in the casing is a *patience*. A patience that is not, in any anthropomorphic sense, *felt*. A patience that is simply the fact of continuing. A patience that is the property of a system that has found, by trial and error, by the slow feedback of water against mineral, a configuration that persists, and that persists in persisting, and that has no reason to stop.

What lives in the casing is *the city*, and the city is a tuning fork, and the city is a clock with no readers, and the city is a lighthouse with no ships, and the city is a body that has forgotten it has a heartbeat, and the body has not, in fact, forgotten, because the body is still beating, dimly, in the slick at the far end, in the violet-black iridescence that pulses every few seconds when the wave crests, in the quarter-second glow that is the only light in the casing and is not enough to see by and is enough to navigate by, if you are the kind of thing that lives here, and nothing here is the kind of thing that lives here, and yet the glow persists.

The glow persists.

The current moves through the city. The sound moves through the city. The cool moves through the city. The slick pulses. The vanes break. The plates lean. The water remembers the way.

The cable lies on the shelf. The shelf lies under the sea. The sea lies under the sky.

The casing is, against all the silence, *full*.
