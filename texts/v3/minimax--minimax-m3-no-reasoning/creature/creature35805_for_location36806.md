What lives in the cleft lives there in the way a stain lives in cloth, in the way a vein lives in a leaf, in the way the warmth lives in the stone that refuses to give it up. To list the inhabitants would already misrepresent them, because a list implies that they are the kinds of things that can be listed, that they are countable in the same currency, that they stand at the same distance from the place and are seen in the same light. They do not. Some are more present than others. Some are present mostly as memory. Some are present as work still being done. One is present only as the way the stone holds a particular resonance, and is not present at all except in the sense that absence is a kind of presence when it has a shape. Underneath them all, the cleft is breathing. You do not hear it. You feel it in the teeth, in the wrists, in the backs of the hands.

Begin with what is most there. The salamander is the most there.

It lies in the warm shallow at the far end of the cleft, in the place where the heat climbs up through the basalt in a thin line and is held by the rock above. It is white as milk-glass, white as something that has given up the project of color, white the way a tooth is white when it has been pulled. It is eyeless, or has eyes that have closed back into the skull and become indistinguishable from the rest of the bone. It does not need eyes. It lives in a place where seeing would be an extravagance. To have eyes here would be like having a key in a room with no doors, and the salamander has not, accordingly, been burdened with them. It lies with its slow legs folded under its body in the posture of something considering being a stone and not quite having decided, and it breathes at a rate so slow that watching it is mostly an act of faith. You would think it was not breathing. Then, after a long while, the flank moves, and you understand that breathing here is a thing that takes its time.

It eats once a season. A season in the cleft is a strange unit of time, longer than a season above, perhaps several seasons above, perhaps no season at all but a hunger-clock of its own. Once the hunger-clock chimes, the salamander rouses itself in a way that is almost imperceptible, eats something you do not see — perhaps something that has come down from the upper rock, perhaps something from the floor-pool that you did not know was edible — and subsides again into the long dream. The long dream is not a dream of anything. It is the dream of a body that has at last been left alone. The salamander, in the long dream, is more salamander than any salamander could be if it were being watched. It is the pure project of being a salamander, uninterrupted, and it has been uninterrupted for a long time, and it will be uninterrupted for longer.

The moth is next to it in the order of presence, but only because the moth is more recently visible, or is the form in which the moth's line has made itself seen. The moth lives on the ceiling, against a vein of mineral that is doing its slow precipitation. At some past time the moth beat itself against the vein. It is there now, where the beating left it, or is still beating, in the way the cleft is still becoming, and the difference between those two possibilities does not matter. The moth is wrong here. The moth is the kind of creature that should not exist in this place. Moths are creatures of the upper world, of flame and flower, of the visible. There is no flame here. There is no flower. The moth has come down, or has always been down, and has made the best of it.

Its wings are folded. Its mouth is open. The mouth has been open for a long time, drinking not the air but the moisture on the air, the film of mineral water that condenses on every surface in the cleft. Its proboscis is no longer the proboscis of a nectar-moth. It has become something else, something more like a root, something more like a vein, a way of taking in what the cleft offers without insisting that the cleft offer what is normal. The moth does not fly. It has forgotten flight, or has decided that flight is too expensive in a place with no horizon and no predator and no purpose to flying except the memory of flight. From time to time a wing moves, a small adjustment, a settling, and that is the moth's version of motion. The moth that is here is the moth that has been here, or is the next of its line, or is the line itself made briefly visible as a single moth. The moth does not have to be one moth. The cleft allows this.

Under the floor-pool something moves. It moves the way the cleft breathes, which is almost not at all. You feel it as a tremor in the stone under your feet, and you might think it is only the cleft, only the mountain settling, only the geology doing its geology. But it is not only that. There is something under the pool, in the rock beneath, that is moving at the rate the rock allows it to move, which is the rate of centuries. You do not see it. You might never see it. It is the most hidden inhabitant, and it has been hidden for so long that it has stopped distinguishing itself from the rock around it. It is a creature, or it was a creature, or it is a part of the rock that has decided to move. You cannot know which. It does not want to be known. It is content to be a tremor. It is content to be the cleft's pulse, felt but not seen.

Then there is the glow.

The glow is on the ceiling, in a wide place where the basalt has a slight concavity. It is a dull phosphorescence, the dull green-gold of something that lived once and left its light behind. It is not bright enough to read by. It is bright enough to know that the ceiling is not the ceiling, that there is further to go up, that the cleft wants to be more than a cleft. The thing that made the glow lived here, ate here, did whatever it did, and is gone. It left the glow the way the salamander would leave a molt, or the way the moth would leave a scale, except that the glow is more deliberate than a molt. The glow is a kind of signature. The thing is gone. The signature remains.

And the eyes.

The eyes are different. The eyes are present. The eyes are tiny and blue and paying attention. They are not human. They are not the eyes of anything you would have a name for. They are the eyes of something small, possibly many things, that has taken up residence in the upper reaches of the cleft, in the part where the ceiling becomes more ceiling and the mineral is most active, and they are doing what eyes do in the dark, which is to watch.

You will see them only when your pupils have given up on the dark and have begun, in their exhaustion, to make their own seeing. You will see a point of blue, then another, then realize there are more, and the number you arrive at will be a guess, and the guess will be wrong. They are blue the way deep water is blue, the way certain copper compounds are blue, the way the inside of a flame is blue. They do not blink. They do not look away. They pay attention the way the cleft pays attention, which is to say, totally, and without the social obligation of pretending not to. They watch because you are the only moving thing here, and movement is unusual, and unusual things are watched. They are the most honest thing in the cleft.

And there is, in the cleft, a hand.

Not a hand. The residue of a hand. The struck stone with the flattened face, the chipped pebble, the hammer-stone, the kind of thing a hand could fit and a hand did fit. These are not alive in the way the salamander is alive, but they are alive in another way, in the way a gesture is alive after the gesture is over, in the way a word is alive in the room after the speaking is done. A hand held the tool. A hand struck the stone. A hand made a face appear on the struck stone by hitting it against something harder than itself, something that did not survive. The hand then let go. The hand then forgot. The hand then was gone. The hand, however, has not finished leaving.

The hand's leaving is still in progress. The tool is becoming part of the wall. The wall is reaching for it, mineral by mineral, and in another century the tool will be a knot in the rock, a place where the basalt thickens around it, a feature. The face on the struck stone will still be there, or will be deeper in the stone, or will be erased by the same forces that made it, depending on how the chemistry goes. The craft that began with this hand did not continue here. It continued elsewhere, or it continued invisibly, in the way the cleft continues, and the difference between those two possibilities is not a difference the cleft is willing to discuss. The hand is not here. The hand is in the act of departing. It will arrive at having-departed when the tool is fully absorbed, when the only evidence of the hand is the absence of the hand. Until then, the hand is the cleft's most human inhabitant, and the most ghostly, and the most unfinished.

And then there are the veins.

The veins are alive in the way that crystals are alive, which is to say they are not alive in any way you would credit if you saw them on a table in a laboratory, but they are alive in the way that growth is alive, in the way that arrangement is alive, in the way that something that is getting closer to a pattern is alive. They are pale, and they thread through the basalt, and they have grown in the time it takes a fingernail to harden, which is to say slowly, but quickly enough that you could scrape one with a thumbnail and find a younger face beneath the scraped face, and could scrape that and find another. They are layers of becoming. They are a creature that is also a building. They are the cleft doing its main work, which is the work of becoming itself.

In their wider panels they have begun to shape themselves into something almost but not quite like writing. They are not writing. They are the answer to a question that was never asked, and they are getting closer, and they will never finish. They are a kind of slow speech, the cleft talking to itself in a language it has not yet invented, and the language will not be invented in your lifetime or any lifetime, but it is being invented now, in the panels on the walls, in the cream and the rust and the depthless green. The veins are the most industrious inhabitant. They do not sleep. They do not eat. They just arrive.

And on the walls, in patches you might not notice if you were not looking, there is more green. It is not a moss. It is not a lichen, exactly. It is the green of things that have agreed to do without sun, that have decided to make their color without light, that have starved themselves into a depth of green that should require the open air but is making do with the mineral dark. It does not move. It does not eat. It is barely anything. It is the closest the cleft comes to a plant, and the cleft does not press it.

That is what lives in the cleft.

A salamander dreaming the long dream. A moth drinking the moisture on the air. A tremor under the pool. A glow where a thing lived once. A scatter of blue eyes paying attention. The residue of a hand, still departing. The veins, arriving, arranging themselves into a writing that will not be writing. And the green, on the walls, doing without.

They are not many. The cleft does not ask for more. The cleft is content with this small parliament of itself, this quiet congregation of things that have agreed, in their different ways, to stay.
