# The Inhabitants of the Crease

## The Pale Ones

They are the first thing you feel before you see them—a tremor in the air, a vibration that starts in the bones of your feet and climbs.

They cover the walls the way a second skin covers a wound: completely, with a kind of apologetic insistence. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The numbers are hard to hold in the head because they are never still, never entirely separate, always shifting against one another in slow tides that follow the light, or the warmth, or some rhythm older than either. Where one moves, three more shift to fill the space it has left, and the wall seems to breathe.

A single one, taken from the cluster and held in the hand—if you could bring yourself to take one, if you could bear the chittering scream it would make, which is different from the chitter of the colony and would bring a dozen others to investigate, then thirty, then the whole wall pouring down toward you like a slow pale flood—would be the length of a man's hand from wrist to fingertip. They are thick-bodied but not heavy; you would guess them weightless until you felt the dense, muscular writhing of them against your palm. Their fur is the color of tallow, of old church candles, of the fat skimmed from a bone broth and left to cool. It is fine and translucent enough that you can see, beneath it, the branching rivers of blue veins that supply the skin of the belly and the inner arms. They are warmer than they should be. They are warmer than the air around them.

The face is what holds you. The face is almost too much. The muzzle is short and wide and soft, the mouth set in an expression that, in a dog, you would call eagerness. The eyes are large and black and wet and set high on the skull, and they are not the eyes of an insect, which is what you might expect from something this numerous, this communal. They are the eyes of a thing that watches. They are the eyes of a thing that is watching *you*, right now, from a hand's width away, with an attention that is not hostile but is not quite friendly either—curious, perhaps, in the way a deep-sea fish is curious about a light lowered into its window. They do not blink as often as you do.

The forepaws are clever. They have six digits each, and the inner two are opposed, like a very small thumb, and they use them with a dexterity you would not predict: grooming the fur of their neighbors, picking small insects from the cracks in the stone, holding a piece of moss to the mouth while they work it between the teeth. The teeth. The teeth are the wrong thing, the thing that does not belong to the face. There are too many of them. They line the mouth in three rows, and they are fine and sharp and white, and they are always, in every individual you can see, in motion—a grinding, side-to-side motion, as if the creature is perpetually chewing something even when nothing is in its mouth. The sound of all those teeth, all at once, in all those mouths, is part of the chitter. It is the bass note under the chitter. It is the part that gets into your molars.

When they are alarmed—and they are so rarely alarmed that the colony seems to have forgotten the choreography for it—they spread a flap of skin from the foreleg to the flank, a membrane like the wing of a sugar glider but furred on both sides, and they throw themselves at the threat with a hissing shriek. The membrane makes a sound, a wet cracking pop, when it unfurls. The shriek is the only loud sound they make. After the shriek, if the threat has not passed, they bite, and the bite is not pleasant, and there are so very many of them. But the threat always passes. Nothing in the Crease has ever pressed the matter.

When they court—and they court at any time, in any season, without regard for the calendar of the world outside—the male throws back his head and opens the throat-flap, which is a different membrane, beneath the chin, and inflates it with a long slow breath until it is a pale balloon the size of the creature's own body. He sways. He sways back and forth on the ledge, slowly, the balloon swaying with him, and the female, if she is interested, will lean toward him and put her small clever forepaws against the balloon and press, gently, testing the warmth of him. Sometimes she presses too hard. Sometimes the balloon ruptures, and the male dies, and the female eats him. This is not considered a failure. This is the way of the Crease.

The young are born furred and walking. The female does not make a nest. She finds a ledge, or a wide crack, and she simply lies on her side and the young comes out of her in a single wet rush, and it is the size of the father's thumb, and it is already covered in the pale translucent fur, and its eyes are already open, and its teeth are already in motion, and within moments of landing on the stone it has oriented itself toward the colony's chitter and begun to crawl, with a desperate uncoordination, toward the nearest cluster. If it reaches the cluster, it is accepted. If it does not reach the cluster—if it falls, or becomes lost, or is picked up by something on the floor—it does not last the night. The mother does not follow. The mother does not call. The Crease is not a place where following helps.

They feed on moss, on lichen, on the small pale insects that breed in the guano, and on one another. The weak ones fall. The slow ones fall. The old ones, whose teeth have worn down past the gum line and who can no longer chew, fall. The colony does not grieve them. The colony simply eats them, and the bones go to the floor, and the floor is the floor, and the chitter goes on. This is not cruelty. There is no cruelty in the Crease. There is only the slow working-out of a thing that has been working itself out, in this one place, for longer than the man has been coming here.

## The Blind Insects in the Guano

You do not see them unless you look. You do not look unless you kneel, and you do not kneel in the Crease unless you have a reason, because the floor is wet and the floor smells of ammonia and the floor is, by depth, made of the creatures.

But if you kneel, and if you hold a flame low, you can see them: small, pale, many-legged things, no longer than your smallest fingernail, swarming in the uppermost layer of the black soft guano like fish in a shallow pool. They have no eyes, or what eyes they have are the vestigial dark spots of creatures that have not needed to see for a very long time. They feed on the guano itself. They are, in their way, the stomach of the Crease—the part of the system that closes the loop, that takes what falls and makes it, again, into something that can be eaten.

The creatures come down to the floor at night and hunt them. You can hear them at it—a higher, sharper chitter, almost a giggling, and the soft wet sounds of the insects being gathered up by clever forepaws and pushed into grinding mouths.

## The Fungus at the Back

It is pale. It is frilled, like a small cabbage, or a brain, or a flower that has not yet decided what it is. It grows in a single cluster on the wall beside the snowpatch, in a place where the water runs in a thin sheet down the stone and the light, when it reaches this deep into the Crease, is a suggestion rather than a presence. It smells of nothing. It has no chitter. It has no teeth.

It is the only living thing in the Crease that does not seem to be afraid.

When the warm breath comes from the walls—the once-in-a-long-while exhalation from below that quiets the colony and stills the pool—the fungus brightens. Not much. A degree. A shift in the color of the pale flesh of it from bone-white to something almost luminous. It brightens, and then it dims, and then it is again the quiet thing it was.

No one has ever seen it make a spore.

## The Man

He comes in the summer.

He is not tall. He is broad in the shoulders and narrow in the hips, the build of a man who has climbed since he was a boy, and he carries nothing but what he needs: a hide sack, a stone knife, a twist of cured sinew for binding, a small bladder of rendered fat for the waterproofing of his boots. He is brown from the sun and grey from something else, and his face, in the half-light of the Crease, is the face of a man who has been in the same place a very long time and has not yet made a peace with it.

He does not speak to the creatures. He does not speak at all, in the Crease. He climbs the holds he has cut—fifteen paces, to the ledge where the bones are arranged—and he sits, and he takes his stone knife, and he begins.

He is quick. He is careful. He takes the creatures from the lower ledges first, where the colony is thinnest, and he takes them one at a time, by the scruff of the neck, and he kills them with a single decisive cut behind the skull. The death-chitter of one brings the others around it into a brief, confused agitation, but he is faster than their confusion, and after a few moments they settle, and he continues. He takes what he can carry. He leaves the bones on the ledge, in the row that has been kept, by him or by those who came before him, for as long as the row is. He does not take the young. He does not take the females heavy with young. He is not, in his way, a careless man.

When he is done, he descends, and he shoulders the sack, and he walks out of the Crease into the blinding of the sun, and he goes back down the path that should not exist, on a mountain that has not existed long enough to be smoothed. He will come again. He has always come again. His father's father's father came again. The row of bones on the ledge is longer than his arm.

He does not paint the pictographs. He does not know who painted them. The ochre of the last one—the figure that is not a man and not a creature but shaped a little like both, with no eyes, looking out from the cleft—is thicker than the others, as if more hands worked on it, or as if it was redone, again and again, across many seasons. He looks at it, sometimes, when he is resting on the ledge. He does not know what it is. He does not ask.

He has never heard the warm breath from the walls. He has never seen the fungus brighten. He has never heard the higher, thinner call, the one that lifts and falls in a slow patient arc, the one that is not mating and is not alarm but is a kind of asking. If you asked him, he would say the Crease is quiet.

## The Pictograph

It is on the back wall, behind the snowpatch, where the light never quite reaches and the ochre has been protected, more or less, from the water that runs down the stone in long branching fans the color of dried blood.

The first three are simple, almost diagrammatic. The cleft, shown from outside, with its narrow tall mouth. The small dark mouths of the creatures on the walls. A figure with upraised arms, which may be the painter or may be the man, or may be the man's father's father, or may be a thing that is not a man at all but has chosen to be drawn as one.

The fourth is the one that is not like the others.

It is shaped a little like a man. It is shaped a little like one of the creatures. It is shaped like something that has decided, at some point in the long slow becoming of the Crease, to try on both shapes and to wear them at once, the way a glove wears a hand. It has no eyes. Its arms are raised, but the angle is wrong; the arms are raised the way a thing's arms are raised when it is pressing against the inside of something and trying to get out. The hands, if they are hands, have too many fingers.

The ochre of it is thick. It has been re-painted. It has been re-painted so many times that the figure now stands a finger's width proud of the stone, a low relief of pigment and what may be blood, layered one season upon another, and the layers at the edges have begun to crack and lift and curl like dead leaves. Whoever has been re-painting it has been doing so for a very long time. Whoever has been re-painting it is not the man. The man does not re-paint. The man does not know who re-paints.

The figure is looking out from the cleft. It is looking out at whatever is in the Crease with it. It is looking out at the creatures, at the man, at the pool, at the column of pale light that moves across the floor through the day. It is looking out at you.

It has no eyes.

## The Thing in the Walls

It is older than the Crease.

It is older than the mountain. The mountain is young. The mountain is a thing that has only just decided to be a mountain, a thing that is still gritting its teeth and breaking off in fresh slabs, a thing whose lichen is the first thing that has been able to make a living on its slopes. The Thing is older than that. The Thing was here before the rock was rock. The Thing was here when the rock was something else, something deeper and hotter, something that has since been thrust up and cracked through and made, by the long slow working of the world, into a peak that did not exist before men climbed it.

It is in the walls. It is in the cracks. It is in the place where the water runs and the stain grows, finger's width by finger's width, in the long branching fans the color of dried blood. It is in the warmth that comes from somewhere deep in the rock, the trace heat that is not quite enough to be the heat of the earth's interior, that is something else, something more local, something that has a source. It is in the snowpatch, which has wedged itself into a back crack and will not, this year or any year soon, finish melting, because the cold that should melt it is, in this one place, not quite enough. It is in the air when the air is tasted of iron and of something older.

It does not have a shape. It has not had a shape for a very long time. It had a shape once, when it was younger, when the world was younger, when the shape was a useful thing to have. It has let the shape go. It has let almost everything go. It is, now, mostly patience. It is the patience of a thing that has been waiting in a wall of rock for a length of time that would make the man's row of bones look like nothing, and it is still waiting, and it will continue to wait, because it has nowhere else to be and nothing else to do.

Once in a long while, so rarely that the man has never felt it, and his father's father only spoke of it once at the end of a long silence, it exhales. A breath of warmer air from somewhere below, carrying a faint mineral sweetness, a faint wrongness. The creatures go quiet for a moment. The pool stills. The man, if he is there, feels the hair on his arms lift and does not know why. The fungus brightens a degree.

The breath is not a threat. The breath is not a hunger. The breath is, if anything, a kind of stretching, the way a sleeper stretches in the long slow hours before waking. It is the Thing, in the walls, becoming, for a moment, more itself. It is the Thing, in the walls, turning, very slightly, in its sleep.

It has no eyes. It does not need eyes. It has been watching the Crease for longer than the Crease has been a Crease, in a way that does not require eyes, in a way that is more like listening, or tasting, or simply *being in the same place as*, and it has watched the lichen come, and the moss, and the small blind insects, and the creatures, and the man, and it has watched the man paint the pictograph that has no eyes, because the man felt, perhaps, in the dim way that men feel such things, that the thing he was trying to draw did not have any.

It is patient. It is old. It is not, in any way that would be recognizable to a man, alive. But it is in the walls, and it is in the air, and it is in the taste of iron and of something older, and it will wait.

The Crease is loud with the chitter of ten thousand pale thick-bodied things. The light moves another degree across the floor. The stain grows. The man comes, and goes, and comes again. The pictograph is re-painted, by hands that are not the man's, in ochre and what may be blood. The fungus by the snowpatch brightens, and dims, and brightens again.

The Crease is open. The Crease is always open. The Crease is becoming more itself, and in the becoming, the Thing in the walls is becoming more itself too, and one day— not this year, not the next, not in the lifetime of the man or his father's father's father, but one day, in the long slow way of such things—the patience will end, and the shape will return, and the eyes that are not eyes will open, and the Crease will be, at last, what it has been trying, all along, to be.
