*The fox* is the most assured inhabitant of the compound, and she is not a permanent one. She has a den in the rubble bank behind the pump room—you can find the entrance if you know it is there, a low dark mouth between two chunks of concrete that were once something else, half-choked now with a young ivy that the fox pushes past every evening the way a person pushes past a curtain—and she crosses the yard at dusk with the unhurried confidence of a tenant. The verb is exact. She does not skulk. She does not hurry. She comes out of the bank at a particular corner where a hawthorn has begun, and she stands for a moment with one forefoot lifted, and she puts it down, and she goes. She goes along the line of the buildings, past the south wall of the main hall where the moss is waistcoat-thick, past the workshop with its iron doors, past the boiler-house, and she turns up the millrace toward the alders, and she is gone. You would not have known she was there except for the slight compression of the grass where she came out, and the way the air smelled briefly of fox, and the absence of the voles in the coal-store birches for an hour afterward.

She has been here since the autumn before last, or the autumn before that. She had cubs once, in the back of the den where the rubble makes a chamber; you could hear them in the spring, the thin high yap of them, when the engine was off and the river was the loudest thing. She has not had cubs this year. She may next year. She has not decided. The den is permanent. The cubs are optional. The compound is hers the way a rented flat is a tenant's—until the landlord says otherwise, and the landlord, in this case, is the slow accumulation of ivy and rubble that is closing the entrance, and the landlord will not say so for many years, and the fox knows this the way foxes know things, which is by lying in them and breathing.

*The heron* is older than the fox's tenure, though no one has measured either. There is a pair of them, and one—the female, perhaps, or simply the one who is here more often—has been on the iron grille above the millrace in the early morning for as long as anyone who has paid attention can remember. She stands in the pale, certain way of a heron, and she is there on Tuesday at eleven when the engine has been running for an hour, and she has not moved. She is not watching the engine. She is fishing. The water passes beneath her in its bright, continuous roar, and the small trout that are funneled against the grille by the geometry of the race are hers. She takes them in the long, slow, unhurried way a heron takes anything—without interest, without hurry, with the patient, vertical attention of a thing that has been a heron for a long time.

She is the figure that stays in the mind after the rest has been absorbed—the vertical line of her against the brick, the slow blink, the way her head folds back along her shoulder after a strike and then extends again. The swallow that comes in low across the yard veers around her. The fox, crossing at dusk, gives her a wide berth, not out of fear but out of a kind of mutual recognition. The heron sees the fox and does not move. The fox sees the heron and adjusts her line by a yard. They have been doing this for years. Neither has ever spoken.

When the engine runs and the pump engages and the river below the grille is thrashed by the additional current, the heron relocates. She flies upstream, low, with the heavy, oared wingbeats of her kind, and she stands on the cobbled yard of the next property or on the alder limb over the bend, and she waits. The trout do not come to the grille while the pump runs. They are elsewhere. When the engine stops, she comes back, within the hour, sometimes within the quarter-hour, and she stands on the grille again, and the fishing resumes.

She has a nest somewhere. Not here. Upstream, in the alders along the higher river, where the trees are older and the heronry is. She goes there in the late winter, when the compound is at its stillest, and she returns in the spring, and she is not seen for a few weeks, and then she is on the grille again, and the cycle has turned over once more. The compound has not, in this respect, changed since she first came.

*A kingfisher* hunts the same water in the opposite direction. It is a streak and an apology—the flash of it gone before the eye has registered the bird, the small, sharp, single note it gives from the alder over the bend as it goes past, the sense that something has happened and that you were not quite the intended recipient. The kingfisher is the rarest of the compound's birds and the most intermittent. It comes in the autumn, when the water is low and the small fish are concentrated, and it comes in the spring, and it is gone in between, and no one knows where it goes in the winter except that it is somewhere on the lower river, where the banks are softer and the perches are better. It stands on the iron grille sometimes, in the heron's absence, and it goes.

*The swallows* are the most populous. Twenty nests, perhaps more, in the rafters of the small dwelling that was once an office and is now something between a barn and a chapel for them. They arrived this year in the second week of May, on a Tuesday, in the rain, and the first one came in through the broken pane of the main hall, and the next through the gap above the workshop door, and within the hour there were a dozen, and within the day the air over the yard was the loose, darting, panicked air of arriving swallows. They are not panicked, of course. They are orienting. They are finding their nest of last year, or finding that it has been taken, and finding another, and beginning the long work of lining it with mud and feather and the small, pale, dried grass that the valley provides. The droppings on the floor of the dwelling are a stratum now—pale and powdery, the work of years—and they crunch underfoot the way the moss in the gutters is heavy as wet cloth. The compound has, in some narrow biological sense, been converted into a swallow.

A late one comes in through the broken pane of the main hall and begins, high up, the long, intricate, half-heard song that the swifts will not know how to sing. This is true in the particular moment of standing in the doorway of the pump room on a Tuesday at eleven. The song is not quite a song; it is a series of small, dry, conversational notes, given from the rafters with the throat working and the tail depressed, the way a swallow gives them, as if the swallow were explaining something at length to no one in particular.

*The swift* is the only inhabitant that is not really an inhabitant at all. It nested in the pump-room ventilator last year and did not nest there the year before, and may not nest there next year. Its coming and going is the only fast thing in the compound—faster than the swallow, faster than the rain, faster than anything else here, the sickle wings and the scream given as they tear past the eaves in the brief summer evenings when they are here at all. The swift is the inhabitant of the compound's absent tense: it comes to the place in order to leave it, and its presence is most felt in the late summer when it has already gone and the pump-room ventilator is just a ventilator again, a hole in a wall through which the swallows will not enter because the swallows are not swifts and have never mistaken themselves for swifts.

*The pipistrelle* emerges at the first dark from behind the iron cap of the chimney in a long, dipping thread. There is one, or perhaps there are several, and the thread is the weaving line of a single bat working the insect layer above the yard. It is the only mammal in the compound that is not on the ground. It is also the only mammal in the compound that the swallows tolerate; the swallows will mob a kestrel, will mob a crow, will mob the heron if the heron came too low, and they ignore the pipistrelle, which is correct, because the pipistrelle is hunting the same insects the swallows will hunt tomorrow in the daylight, and at a different hour. The chimney is the pipistrelle's because the chimney is too high for anything else. The iron cap was fitted to keep out the jackdaws. It was not fitted to keep out the pipistrelle. The pipistrelle came after.

*The rats* are the secret inhabitants. You hear them sometimes when the engine is off—a scratching under the workshop floor, a sudden, alarmed silence, the resumption. They live in the space between the joists and the cobbles, in the dry-ish stratum (not dry, never quite dry) where the air moves but the light does not. They have been here longer than the diesel has been painted green for the second time. They are gray-brown, in the manner of all rats, and they are the right size for the spaces they have chosen, and they eat what the valley provides, which is mostly what the alders drop and what the swallows miss and what the fox leaves. They are the food of the fox. They are aware of the fox. They are the reason the grass at the corner of the rubble bank is compressed for an hour after the fox has crossed.

*The voles* are smaller and less negotiable. They work the leaf litter under the two birches that have come up inside the shell of the coal store, and their runs are the thin, pale, sinuous lines you can see in the cinder if you kneel, and they are busy in there in a way that is not anxious, just continuous. A weasel has been here once or twice; you can tell by the wider hole in the bank and by the small, pale, oval pellet that is not a rat's. The weasel does not stay. The weasel does not stay anywhere.

*The grass snake* was seen in the race-grating last August, and it has not been seen since, and it may be there now. The race-grating is a particular place—the iron grille above the millrace, downstream of where the heron stands, in the shadow of the workshop, where the water moves at a different speed and where the small frogs and the newts and the invertebrates collect. A grass snake in a race-grating is a grass snake in a perfect place: damp, cool, with cover, with food, with the option of slipping into the water and being gone. It was a small one, a juvenile, perhaps a foot and a half long, and it came up through the grille in the warm part of the afternoon and lay on the warm iron, and it was the most visible reptile the compound has hosted in living memory, and it was seen by one person, who told no one in particular.

*The frogs* are the loudest inhabitants, in volume per capita, in continuity of sound, and in the surprising persistence of their contribution. In the warmer months, the drains and the wet cellars and the sump under the boiler house are the site of a small, individual, persistent clamor that begins at dusk and continues, with variations, until the light comes back. Each frog gives his own note. Each frog gives it from a slightly different place. The sum is not a chorus in the organized sense; it is more like the sound of a small, distributed argument going on under the floorboards of the compound, with each participant certain of his own position and uninterested in the others. The argument is about territory, or about mates, or about the temperature of the evening, or about nothing. It has been going on for the entire time the compound has existed, and it will go on for some time after the engine has stopped for good and the pump has been replaced by whatever replaces a pump, and the cellars have filled, and the frogs are still in them, calling into the dark water.

*The bees* are in the comfrey. The comfrey is in the rank grass between the buildings, in the place nobody walks, and it is obliging in the way that comfrey is obliging—it flowers heavily, in pale pink bells, and the bees work it from mid-morning to late afternoon, and the bees are the bumblebees of the kind that do not nest in hives, the carders and the white-tails and the early ones that come out before the swallows are awake. The bees are not the pollinators of the compound; the compound does not need pollinating; but the bees are present in the way that bees are present in any neglected place that has let its wildflowers go to flower, and they are a particular sound—a low, busy, close-to-the-ear hum that is not the engine and not the pump and not the river but is the sound of the place at work in the small register, the register of the comfrey and the meadowsweet and the hogweed.

*The moss* is the slowest inhabitant, and the most patient, and the one that does the most to the buildings. It has begun on every brick course that ever stayed wet, on the iron, in the gutters in pale, dry-looking pillows that are heavy as wet cloth when you lift them. On the south wall of the main hall, where the river's adjusted level keeps the lowest course of brick permanently damp, it is no longer a beard but a waistcoat, and the wall is no longer the wall but a slightly thickened thing with a greenish bias. It spreads at a rate that is measurable in fractions of a millimeter per year. It will take decades to do what it is doing. It has begun. It has not finished.

The hart's-tongue fern has established in the lip of the pump housing, in a single tuft, and its long leaves tremble in the draft of the pump's cycle. The hart's-tongue is the most particular of the ferns here; it likes the cool damp of the concrete-block wall, and it likes the lime in the brick pointing, and it has found the place where these two things meet. The ivy is young, not yet at the eaves, debating how high to go, in the company of a clematis that has been at it longer and is further along but not yet committed. The ivy and the clematis come up out of the wet gravel at the base of the chimney, and they are both still in negotiation with the brick. They will be in negotiation for some time. The alders in the yard are not in negotiation; they are in the gravel and they are growing, and they will be the size of the chimney within fifteen years if no one takes them. No one is taking them. The two small birches that have come up inside the shell of what was once the coal store have their roots in the cinder, and they are drinking what the cinder holds, and they are doing what birches do in the place a coal store used to be, which is to forget that it ever was one.

*The human* is the least settled of the inhabitants, and the most provisional, and the one whose relationship to the place is the most contingent. Two mornings a week—a Tuesday, a Friday—the compound receives a single visitor who does not visit so much as service the place. The visitor arrives in a van or a pickup or on foot, depending on the weather and on something internal. The visitor knows the iron doors of the workshop weigh what two men can lift; the visitor has been here long enough to operate the diesel by smell, by the particular rattle that means the fuel is reaching the cylinder, by the moment in the cranking when the flywheel catches and the thing begins to think of itself as turning. The visitor opens a valve. The visitor listens to the pump begin. The visitor goes into the main hall and stands for a moment in the long shapes the mullions lay on the floor, if it is afternoon, or does not, if it is morning, and the visitor leaves. Sometimes the visitor carries a key on a length of baler twine. Sometimes the visitor is the only person for whom the iron doors open.

The visitor is not a tenant. The visitor is a remnant. The visitor is what is left of the people for whom the chimney was built straight, for whom the slate was laid in courses that have held for seventy years, for whom the turbine was installed and the rising main was trenched up the hill. The visitor maintains the only contract the compound still has with the next parish—the standpipe, the water, the twice-weekly pumping—and in exchange the compound is allowed, in its slow way, to continue. The visitor is not what the compound is for, anymore. The compound is for what is in it now.

*The river* is the original inhabitant. The river was here first, and the river will be here last, and the river is in everything. The river runs through the millrace under the workshop, under the pump room, out below the boiler house, and the river runs past the east wall where the old race is drawn off through the iron grille, and the river runs over the low course of brick in the main hall when the river is high, which is more often than it used to be, because the weirs downstream have been adjusted by people who did not know they were adjusting them. The river is the baseline of the soundscape. The river is the reason for the moss. The river is the reason for the place. The river is the reason the compound is, in fifty years, going to be a low, green mound with brick cores, and the river will run over it, and the heron will stand on it as she stands now on the grille, and the pump's successor will be run, perhaps, by a child of one of the present children, who will come to the compound on a Tuesday in the rain and press a button, and the water will go up the hill, and the engine will hammer, and the moss will have crept another quarter inch around the eastern window, and none of this will be remarkable to anyone except, perhaps, the heron.
