# The Inhabitants of the Sun Pit

What lives here has learned the long art of being patient.

The chamber, for all its brightness, is a place of careful economies. Nothing arrives in abundance—neither rain, nor warmth, nor food, nor the easy variety of weather that drives most animals through their days. The opening above admits a column of light, and that light, for a few hours each day, becomes a small sun pouring down onto the floor. But the rest of the time, the pit lives in a kind of luminous dimness, an even silver glow that has been bounced and scattered so many times it has lost its direction. The creatures that dwell here have arranged their lives around the hours when the real sun visits, and around the long, still hours when only its memory remains.

## The Pale Ground-Birds

The largest of the inhabitants are the ones most often seen, though *seen* is a generous word for it, because they have made an art of not being seen. There are perhaps a dozen of them, scattered through the chamber, each one holding its own small territory the way a single fern holds a crack in the stone. They stand motionless for minutes at a time, and the stillness is so complete that you can walk past one at arm's length and never register it. Their plumage is the color of the limestone where the light has polished it—pale, warm, faintly mottled with the grey of shadow—and against the sun-warmed wall, in the diffuse glow, they disappear.

Their bodies are not quite like any bird you would know. The legs are long and thick, scaled rather than feathered, the toes broad and gripping, and they carry their weight low. The neck rises from the shoulders in a long, slow curve, and the head is small and narrow, with a beak that is short and slightly hooked and used, you can tell by watching, for cropping and tearing rather than pecking. The eye is the largest feature of the face, round and amber and set forward enough to give a wide, considered gaze. They watch you, when they watch you, with the unhurried attention of something that has nowhere else to be.

When they move, they move in a way that surprises you. There is a sudden, low dart—a small, compressed run, the body held nearly horizontal, the neck extended—and then they are somewhere else, and have resumed their stillness, and you are not sure you actually saw the motion. It is the running of an animal that has learned, over many generations, that to be seen moving is to be seen at all, and that to be seen at all, in this place, has historically not been safe.

Their call is what you hear first, often, before you see any of them. It is a low, soft hoot—not loud, not carrying the way an owl's call carries through open air, but *held*, in the way the pit holds sounds, so that it seems to come from the walls themselves. Sometimes one calls and another answers, and the exchange goes back and forth through the chamber, low and conversational, as if they are keeping track of each other across distances. There is no urgency in the calling. It is the sound of animals that are, in their own quiet way, content.

They feed mostly on the moss that grows in the shaded cracks, and on the tender pale new growth at the heart of the ferns. They drink from the pool, though rarely—you can see, if you watch, that they sometimes go for days without approaching the water. They get what moisture they need from the plants, and from the dew that gathers on the stones in the cooler hours, and they have learned to wait out the long afternoons when neither is replenished.

They are wary of you. Not panicked—panicked animals flee, and these do not flee, they simply *recede*, withdrawing their attention, becoming stone-shaped, becoming nothing. But wary. There is something in them that knows humans, that carries the knowledge that humans are a particular kind of danger: not a predator in the way a hawk is a predator, but a disturbance, a thing that brings noise and intention into a place that has not asked for either. They have been visited before. A few of them, perhaps, have never seen a human. But the species has the memory in its body, and you feel it when you approach—a slight lift of the head, a tension along the line of the neck, the eye fixing on you with the bright, considering look of something that has decided, for now, to let you stay.

If you sit still, they forget. Not all at once, and not all of them, but gradually, over the course of an hour or two, the nearest one will resume feeding, and then another, and then the calling will start up again, low and conversational, and the chamber will return to its quiet business. You have become, for them, a feature of the stone. This is the highest compliment the place can pay you.

## The Wall Lizards

Along the walls, in the places where the light falls softest and the moisture lingers longest, there are smaller things. They are long and slender, almost sinuous, the length of a hand from fingertip to wrist, and they are the color of the damp stone—grey, faintly silvered, with a pale line running down each flank. Their legs are short and placed wide, and when they move they flow rather than walk, the body rippling in a way that makes them look like something liquid that has briefly decided to be solid.

There are many of them, more than you would guess at first, because they are so still and so matched to their surfaces. They live in the cracks and the small ledges where the seepage has worn the rock into shallow alcoves, and they come out in the dimmer hours to hunt. Their food is the insects—specifically, the small pale moths and beetles that come to the moss and the ferns and the still surfaces of the water. The lizards wait, motionless, their heads slightly raised, and when something moves across the stone near them they strike with a quick dart and a small, efficient bite, and the strike is over almost before you have registered it.

They are the most numerous of the vertebrates in the pit, and also the least seen. You notice them, when you notice them at all, as a small movement at the edge of vision—a thing that was a crack, a moment ago, and is now an animal. They are not afraid of you in the way the birds are afraid, because you are too large to be a predator of theirs and too clumsy to be prey, and you are mostly, to them, weather. They will go about their business while you are present, if you do not move suddenly, and you can watch them at close range and they will watch you back with one small dark eye and continue what they were doing.

What they are doing, mostly, is waiting. They wait for the insects to come close. They wait for the temperature to drop, or to rise, or to do whatever it is going to do. They are patient in the way that only small things can be patient, because their whole lives are made of waiting, and the waiting is not empty but full—the full attention of a small, well-designed animal attending to the world.

## The Insects

The chamber is full of insects in the way a meadow is full of insects, which is to say: if you stopped moving and listened, you would hear them, and if you listened past the hearing of them, you would feel them, as a kind of low hum in the air. They are delicate, and pale, and most of them are so small that they register, at first, as motion rather than form.

There are several kinds. The ones you see most often are the small moths, drawn to the brightest surfaces and the damp moss, with wings of a pale, iridescent cream that flashes violet and green when the light catches them at the right angle. They do not fly well here—the air is too still, and the light is too even, and there is no wind to ride—so they mostly walk, or make small, gliding descents from one surface to another, the wings held slightly open to slow the fall. They are beautiful in the way that small, fragile things are beautiful: as if their beauty were a thing they were not sure they were entitled to, and might lose at any moment.

There are also small beetles, dark and rounded, that live in the moss and the damp cracks. They are easier to hear than to see, because they make a small, dry ticking when they move, and the sound accumulates, in a quiet place, into a kind of texture. There are tiny flies, almost transparent, that hover in the brightest column of light near the opening and disappear when the light shifts. There are things in the water—small, pale wormlike creatures—that you can see if you look very carefully at the pool, and that you would never see at all if you were not looking.

The insects are the base of everything. The moths feed the lizards. The beetles feed the lizards too, and probably feed the birds, though the birds are mostly herbivorous. The larvae in the moss feed the small things in the water, which in turn feed the larger things in the pool. The whole pit, in the end, runs on sunlight and patience and the slow conversion of one kind of life into another.

## The Pool Things

And then there are the things in the water.

You do not see them at first. You see the pool, and you see the way the light comes through it and turns it pale gold, and you see the small insects skating on the surface and the dark shapes of the pebbles at the bottom, and you might think, for a while, that the pool holds nothing larger than a minnow. But if you sit at the edge, very still, for a long time—an hour, perhaps more—the shapes begin to change. What you thought was a pebble moves. What you thought was a shadow has a face.

They are pale, and soft, and slow, and larger than you would have guessed. The size of a hand, perhaps a little larger—broad, flattened, many-limbed, with a body that is more suggestion than outline. They have a face that is mostly eyes: dark, wide-set, gently incurious, the eyes of something that has not, in a long time, needed to be afraid of anything. The limbs are small and many, and they move them in a way that is less like swimming and more like drifting, the limbs stirring the water in slow, patient cycles that keep the creature just below the surface without quite propelling it anywhere.

They are waiting. You can tell they are waiting because they are not doing anything else. They float, and they watch with their many eyes, and they wait. They do not feed while you watch, or at least not in any way you can detect. They do not interact with each other in any visible way, though there are several of them—three, perhaps four, perhaps more, lying in the deeper water beyond the pebbled margin. They are not asleep. The eyes are open, and the eyes are watching, and the watcher is, in some way you cannot quite name, also waiting.

What are they waiting for? You do not know. They have been here, presumably, for a long time. The pool is their world, and the light that warms the shallow water is their sun, and the small larvae that drift near the surface are their food, and the slow rise and fall of the seepage is their seasons. They have everything they need. And yet there is the waiting. It is the most unsettling thing in the pit, because it suggests that there is a thing they want, or a thing they expect, that is not here. Some animals wait for rain. Some wait for the dark. These seem to be waiting for something that the pit cannot give them, and that the pit has not given them, perhaps, in a very long time.

Or perhaps they are waiting for nothing. Perhaps the waiting is what they are. Perhaps, in the deep slow time of the place, the distinction has ceased to matter.

## The Way the Pit Holds Them

What strikes you, after a while, is how the animals are not crowded. There is space between them, and the space is not empty but *used*—used as territory, used as a kind of polite distance, used as the silence between two notes of a piece of music. The lizards have their cracks. The birds have their feeding patches. The insects have their moss. The pool things have their water. No one is fighting. No one is in a hurry. The pit, which could support far more life than it does, supports exactly the life it has, and the life it has is enough.

This is, in its way, the answer to the question of what lives here. What lives here is what can live here, and what can live here is what has agreed, in its own body, to the terms of the place. The terms are: patience, stillness, the long wait, the careful economy, the willingness to be almost invisible, the willingness to be almost nothing. The terms are not harsh, exactly, but they are absolute, and the creatures that have accepted them are, in a way the creatures of busier places are not, *settled*. They are at home here. They are at home in the way that a thing is at home in the place where it was made.

If you sit long enough, and the birds forget you, and the insects resume their small businesses, and the light moves a little across the floor of the pit, you begin to feel that you are visiting, not an empty place, but a full one. The silence, it turns out, is not empty. It is the silence of a great many small, quiet lives, going on.
