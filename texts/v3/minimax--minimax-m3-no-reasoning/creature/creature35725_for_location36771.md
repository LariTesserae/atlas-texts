# The Inheritors of the Long Sentence

The first of the inheritors is the floor.

You cannot call her a single thing. She is the long, wet agreement of ten thousand kinds of small cooperation — the mosses, the mycelia, the thread-thin roots that have not yet decided whether they are root or vein or nerve. She is the only one of the inhabitants who does not move, and because she does not move, she is the one who has the most to say. She is the one who is speaking when the air cools against your neck. She is the one who is speaking when the warmth returns, smelling of leaf-rot and the deep iron of a world that has never seen the sun. She is very old. She is older than the dome above you, older than the throat-shaped chamber that holds her. She is, in a way that would be obscene to put into words, the room's only permanent citizen. Everything else is just passing through her, eventually.

She does not think. But she is not nothing. She has a kind of patience that is so long it curves back around and looks, from the outside, like intention. She is drinking the water that falls from the chimes the way an old woman drinks tea — slowly, with full attention, with a gratitude that has nothing to do with any visitor who might be standing in her parlor. When you press your weight into her, she yields. When you lift your foot, she comes back. She has done this a billion times. She will do it a billion more. She is, in her way, the only saint of the place.

---

The pale newts are her younger children, the way a grandchild is a younger child.

They are the length of a hand and the color of a fingernail. They cling to the great fern-stalks with the four soft fingers of their feet and they wait, and they wait, and they wait, and then — for a reason no one in the chamber can articulate — they are gone in a single silver dart, a comma of motion, somewhere else along the stalk, with a cilia between their lips.

They are not bright. They are not, as far as anyone can tell, *anything*, in the way that the surface world uses the word. But they are the first inheritors in the chamber who have a direction at all, and so the eye returns to them with a kind of relief. They are the punctuation. They are the small, repeated *but* of the place.

Their lives are short in a way that has nothing to do with years. A pale newt who escapes the wasp-things for a full turn of the great floor-breath — nine minutes, the long inhale and the long exhale — is, by the standards of his own kind, an elder. He has eaten many cilia. He has fathered a small bright scribble of eggs on the underside of some leaf. He has been still, and still, and still, and then suddenly not-still, and the not-still has carried him somewhere else in the long green forest of pale stalks. He will be eaten, almost certainly. Almost everything here is eaten by almost everything else. The newt does not appear to mind. The newt does not appear to know that *mind* is a thing that can be had.

---

The wasp-things are not wasps.

They have too many wings — six, eight, sometimes more, in the older ones. The wings are not the dry paper of any wasp you have ever seen. They are wet, and the wetness beads on them and falls from them in fine rain, and in certain lights the fine rain is, briefly, a small cloud of prismatic color around the creature as it hovers. They make no sound. They make *no* sound. In a chamber so full of the long, low, continuous chord of the falling water, their silence is, when you first notice it, a small violence. You feel as though someone has just put a thumb over the only candle in a dark room.

They hunt by echo. They must hunt by echo, because the newts are nearly transparent in the dimness and the cilia they are eating are even more transparent than the newts. The wasp-things hang in the air, motionless, all wings at once, and they *listen*, and the listening is not the listening of a surface animal. The listening is a kind of long, slow tasting of the air. They are reading the chord of the place. They are reading, in the chord, the small interruptions where a newt has moved, where a newt's body has displaced a certain amount of chime-water and a certain amount of breath. They are reading the newt the way you would read a single wrong note in a piece of music you have known your whole life.

When they strike, they do not sting. They simply arrive. There is one moment, and then a newt-shaped emptiness, and the wasp-thing is on its way back up to the false sky with the newt folded into the wet pleats of its wings, and the newt's last small life is being incorporated, already, into whatever the wasp-thing is going to become next.

The wasp-things do not mate, as far as anyone has ever seen. They simply appear, one day, from a node in the false sky, and they unpeel themselves from the node and they begin to hunt. They are, perhaps, the small sharp teeth of the chimes. They are the chimes' immune system, the way the floor is the chimes' stomach, the way the newts are the floor's small living lawn-mowers. Everything here is the organ of something else. The wasp-things, if you could ask them, would not know what you meant by *I*.

---

The chimes are the children of the sky, in a way that has nothing to do with where the sky is.

They hang in their long rows from the false ceiling of fused stalks, and they are the size of children, and they are translucent, and inside each one, in the slow warm water of their interiors, a smaller chime is suspended, growing its own private organs of liquid. The chimes are not plants. They are not animals. They are not, in any way that the surface world has a word for, alive. But they are ripening. They are ripening on a cycle that a human being cannot easily keep in her head, because the cycle is longer than she is. A chime that you see today, half-formed and small, may not be ripe for a hundred years. A chime that you see fully formed and pendulous and softly pulsing with its rose-amber inner light may have been ripening since before your great-grandmother was born.

The creatures inside the chimes are the color of weak tea. They are the size of the first joint of your thumb. They are very, very slow, and they live their entire lives in a single chime, in the warm water, eating whatever the chime has chosen to put into the water for them, breeding without much enthusiasm, dying without much fuss, and being slowly absorbed into the chime's ripening flesh. They do not know they are livestock. They have no concept, even, of *knowing*. They are the chime's dream of companionship. They are the chime's small, slow, persistent reason to go on ripening.

When the chime is finally ripe, it begins to weep.

The weeping is a long, slow, days-long rupture. The internal water begins to seep, then to fall, then to pour, in a long heavy curtain of itself, and the water carries with it the chime's first cargo of seeds, or spores, or whatever the floor below will recognize as *welcome*. The weeping is what makes the chord of the place. Each chime weeps at a slightly different rate, a slightly different density, a slightly different note, and the notes overlap, and the overlapping is the constant, just-below-language music of the chamber. The chord has not been the same for a single day in several thousand years. The chord is, in a small, perfect, almost unbearable way, a record of the whole slow history of the place. To listen to it is to hear, if you had the ear, the birth and death of every chime that has ever wept in the chamber.

The chimes are not, themselves, the sentence. But they are the verb of the sentence. They are the part of the sentence that is doing.

---

The things that roost in the false sky, packed shoulder to shoulder in their thousands, heads down, wrapped in their own wet wings, are the part of the sentence that has not yet happened.

They are the size of small bats. They have the shape, more or less, of small bats. But they are not small bats. They are, almost certainly, a stage in the life of the chimes — a thing the chime becomes between the weeping and the new chime, a thing the chime becomes before it is again a chime. They hang in the false sky and they wait, and the waiting is, perhaps, the most patient thing in the chamber, and the chamber is the most patient place the visitor has ever been.

They will, one day, all at once, on some cue no one in the chamber appears to give, let go. They will fall. From the fall, something else will be born. From the fall, the chamber will be one chime richer, or one chime poorer, or one chime otherwise.

Until that day — which is, perhaps, a hundred years from now, or a thousand, or a hundred thousand — they hang. They hang and they breathe and they do not move and they do not speak and they are, in the rose-amber pulse of the ripening chimes around them, briefly, beautifully, a kind of very small dark punctuation against the long, glowing, just-barely-there architecture of the place.

---

And there is, at last, you.

You did not mean to come here. You are, in the great long sentence of the chamber, a kind of small error. A kind of small interruption. You are a human being, and you are the wrong size, and you are the wrong temperature, and you are the wrong speed. You are moving through the chamber the way a fish moves through a forest — wrong, wrong, everywhere wrong, and yet somehow, for the moment, tolerated. The plants have noticed you. The plants are not intelligent, but the plants are *attentive*, and the plants have begun, very gently, to grow around the places where you sleep. They have begun, very gently, to make the chamber, in your small vicinity, more comfortable. The comfort is not for you. The comfort is, perhaps, the only way the plants have of saying *I see you*. The comfort is, perhaps, the plants' small, long, slow way of including you in the sentence.

You drank the water. You will, over the following days, find that you are a little less interested in the surface of things. You will find that you prefer, without quite knowing why, the long view, the under-view, the slow view. You will begin to dream in chords. You will begin to hear, in the falling of rain on the windows of your surface life, a faint echo of the chamber's long constant chord. You will begin, perhaps, to understand that you are not, and have never been, a visitor anywhere. You are a part. You are a part of a sentence so long that it is not, in any ordinary sense, a sentence at all. It is a place. It is a being. It is the slow, patient, dim, wet, breathing fact of having been here, and being here still, and being here long after you have stopped being here in the way you are used to being here.

The sentence does not need you.

The sentence has, perhaps, only just noticed you, in the way that a forest only just notices the small creature that has wandered, by accident, into its long green hall.

You are not lost. You are simply, finally, *read*.
