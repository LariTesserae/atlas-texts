The crab lives here.

She is old. Not in the way that the engine is old — she is not a hundred years old, perhaps not ten, perhaps not five; her kind do not leave reliable records — but old in the way that matters, which is that she has worn down the sharp edges of her own carapace against the iron grid until she fits it the way a key fits a lock. She walks the beams. She has walked them so many times that her path is a groove, and in the groove, where her orange claws have scraped away a hundred seasons of silt, the iron is bright again. A thin vein of brightness, following the pattern she walks.

Her name, if she has one, is not a name. It is a feeling she has when the light comes through the canopy in a certain slant, and the water turns gold, and she stops on her beam and her stalked eyes lift, and the marsh holds her reflection steady for a moment. That feeling is her name.

She eats what falls. Fruit from the canopy, dropped by bats she has never met. Insect larvae that come up from the grid-iron to breathe. A small frog, once, that misjudged the engine's offbeat and found itself between her claws. She is not cruel. She is not anything in particular. She is the warden of the floor because there is a floor and she walks it, and the floor has accepted her.

The frog lives in the seam between two I-beams, just above the waterline. He is small. He is green and brown in the mottled way of a thing that wants not to be seen against a background of moss and oxidation. His throat is pale yellow and, when he inflates it, briefly the brightest thing in the cathedral.

He has a song. It is not a long song. It is a single note, repeated, with a breath between. *Brrr. Brrr. Brrr.* He has been singing it longer than he can remember, which is not long at all, because frogs do not remember long.

What he remembers is rhythm.

The engine breathes. The engine breathes *thump-hiss, thump-hiss, thump-hiss*, and the frog, who was here before he understood anything, learned to put his own breath in the spaces. He sings on the *hiss*. He has done this so long that he no longer thinks about it. If the engine were to stop — it will not stop, the engine does not stop — he would feel the silence like a held breath and he would croak, confused, into the gap.

He is the cantor. He did not choose this. He is the cantor because there is a rhythm and he is the small voice that has woven itself into it, and the weaving has made the rhythm a music, and the music has made the cathedral a church. Without him it would still be a cathedral. Without him it would not be a church. He does not know this. He would croak anyway.

He eats the moths that come down the light shafts at dusk. He is afraid of the crab. He has never seen her but he knows she is there, the way you know a thing that lives in the next room.

---

The bats live in the canopy. There are perhaps thirty of them, hanging upside-down from the highest branches of the strangler fig, wrapped in their own wings like small leathery parcels. They are pale. They are smaller than the bats of open country, because there is no open country here, and they have evolved to navigate the cathedral's interior the way fish navigate a reef.

They drink from the bromeliads. The bromeliads are pools in the air, suspended in the crotches of branches and the elbows of pipes, and each one has its own tiny ecology — mosquito larvae, a single species of worm, algae, the drowned pollen of the orchids that flower in the high places. The bats come at dusk and dip their muzzles into these pools and drink, and the ripples spread out from each drink and lap against the bromeliad's leaves, and the mosquito larvae, who have learned this rhythm, rise to the surface in anticipation.

The bats do not see the cathedral. They see it the way a fish sees a reef: as a system of passages, a topology of surfaces, a place where one moves and rests and moves again. They know the steam pipes by the warmth they give off at night. They know the light shafts by the insects that congregate there. They know, with the precision of generations, that the frog's song means dusk is coming, and that the engine's chuff means the small relief-valve moths are stirring.

They have never seen the sky. They do not miss it. The sky is a thing for other bats.

Their droppings fall. The droppings feed the orchids that flower in the high places, and the orchids are pollinated by the bats in a slow, weird commerce of guano and nectar, and the orchid seeds drift down on their gossamer parachutes and lodge in new places — in a pipe, in a crevice of the engine, in the crook of a buttress-root — and so the cathedral renews itself in a cycle the bats neither understand nor need to.

---

The figs live in the cathedral as the cathedral lives in the quarry. They are the largest things here, after the engine. They are many. The strangler that has eaten the smokestack is the largest, but there are others — root-buttressed giants in the corners, figs that have cracked through the floor of the marsh and sent pale roots down into whatever is below the marsh, which is iron and then more iron and then, perhaps, the original bedrock of the quarry.

The figs do not think. The figs grow. They grow with a patience that the crab would envy if she could see them, because the figs are growing on a timescale that makes her own slow aging look like a mayfly's bloom. They send down roots. They send up branches. They take the engine's warmth and the cathedral's humidity and they *use*, and what they make is wood, and what the wood does is hold more fig, and what the fig does is make more wood.

The strangler that has the smokestack is digesting it. Slowly. The strangler began as a seed dropped by a bird — or a bat, in some older telling — and germinated in the smokestack's lip, because the lip was the only dry place in the cathedral in some long-ago season. The seed sent a root down. The root reached the engine and found warmth. The root thickened. The strangler thickened. Now the smokestack is hollow, and the hollow is lined with roots, and the engine breathes its steam through the fig's body before the steam reaches the pipes that climb the walls.

The fig does not mind this. The fig is warmed by the steam. The fig grows faster for it. The fig will, in time, eat the smokestack entirely, and when it does, the engine will still breathe, because the fig will be the smokestack, and the fig will not know that it is strange.

The fig, in its slow vegetative way, is the most ambitious thing in the cathedral. It is trying to be the cathedral.

---

The orchids live in the high places. They are small, fleshy, and they flower in colors that have no names because the names were invented for the colors of meadows and these are the colors of *here* — a bruised purple, a yellow that leans toward green, a white so pale it is almost the color of the steam itself.

They are pollinated by the bats. They are fed by the bats. They are, in some quiet way, the bats' counterpart: where the bats drink, the orchids bloom; where the orchids bloom, the bats drink. They are locked in a slow dance that is older than either of them can remember.

They root in pipes. They root in the seams of the engine's housing, in the cracks of the switchboard, in the moss that has consumed the wooden desk in the treehouse. They are not parasites. They are tenants. They give back what they take — they give back flowers, and the flowers give back scent, and the scent gives back the memory, for the bats, of where the nectar is.

One orchid, very old, has rooted directly into the brass label of the switchboard. The label says something. The label once said something clearly. The label now says, of the letters that remain, *P O _ E R H O U S _* or perhaps *P O W E R H O U S E* with the missing letters eaten away. The orchid has flowered over the brass, and its roots have followed the engraved grooves, and the orchid is, in some small way, the keeper of the name. Without it, the name would have been lost. With it, the name is illegible but *there*, a green inscription in a living hand.

---

The fish live in the marsh. There are few of them. They are pale, because the cathedral is dim, and they are small, because the marsh does not feed large things, and they are slow, because there is nowhere to go.

They turn in the light shafts. They turn because the light is there and they are fish and fish turn toward light. They look, in the gold columns of late afternoon, like the saints in the windows of an actual cathedral — pale, suspended, slightly turned, surrounded by a colored atmosphere that is not of them but through which they are visible. They are not saints. They are carp, or something like carp, that were here when the engine was a new thing, or were stocked later by hands now long gone, or arrived by some improbable passage through the underground water.

They eat the mosquito larvae. They eat the algae that blooms on the underside of the iron grid. They are eaten, occasionally, by the frog, who is not a large predator but is, occasionally, lucky. They do not know about the bats. They do not know about the orchids. They know about the light, and the water, and the grid-iron beneath them, which they brush against as they turn, and which is, to them, the floor of the world.

---

The single boot lives in the treehouse. It is leather, full of ferns. It was left by a man who came here once and did not come back, or who came back without his boot, or who came back and left his boot on purpose, for reasons the boot cannot know.

The ferns have grown up through the laces. The ferns have rooted in the leather. The ferns are, by now, the boot's chief inhabitants; the boot is their house, the way the cathedral is the engine's house. The boot is being digested, slowly, into the strangler that holds the treehouse, and the strangler does not distinguish between the boot and the planks of the treehouse and the wood of the desk and the brass of the telegraph key. They are all just wood, or leather, or metal, and they are all food.

The boot, when the light hits it in a certain way, looks like a small brown cathedral of its own. A relic. A saint's foot reliquary, emptied of its saint.

---

The man who left the boot is not here. He may be dead. He may be alive elsewhere, an old man, who sometimes remembers the sweating cathedral and wonders, briefly, whether it is still there, and then puts the thought down and goes on with his life. He does not know that his boot is a fern-garden. He does not know that the telegraph key, which he tapped once, hopefully, into the silence, has been tapped by no one since, and that the silence it tapped into is still there, unchanged, patient, waiting for an answer that will not come.

The cathedral does not remember him. The cathedral is not the kind of thing that remembers. But the boot remembers the shape of his foot, in a small hollow in the leather, where the ferns have not yet grown, and the hollow is a kind of memory, and the memory is all that is left of him here.

---

These are the inhabitants. They do not know each other by name. They know each other by rhythm — the engine's breath, the frog's song, the crab's slow patrol, the bats' dusk, the orchids' blooming, the figs' slow digestion of the architecture, the fish's slow turn in the light.

They are a kind of parish. They are a kind of congregation. The cathedral is patient with them. They are patient with the cathedral. The cycles here are long, and the negotiations between iron and vine have been going on for a hundred years, and will go on for a hundred more, and the negotiations are not finished, and will not be finished, because the cathedral is the kind of place where the negotiations *are* the life, and the life is the negotiation, and the negotiation has produced something that is not a ruin and not a garden but is itself, and does not know it is strange.

You sit on the iron beam. The engine breathes. A drop falls into the marsh on the offbeat. The crab turns her bright eyes toward you, briefly, and then away. The frog sings. The bats murmur in the canopy, unseen. The orchids bloom in the high places, in colors without names. The strangler fig grows another foot toward a beam it will, eventually, eat.

The cathedral is warm. The cathedral is wet. The cathedral is patient with you.
