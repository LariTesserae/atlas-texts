# Who Lives in the City at the Roof of the World

The people of the city are not a people in the way that word usually means. They are not a people in the way that nations are peoples, or tribes, or the kind of communities that write themselves into ledgers and count their own births. They are a people in the way that mosses are mosses — a presence, distributed, answered-for by no census. There is no word in the language of the city for "we." There may once have been. The word, if it existed, is now held only in the shape of the silence that replaced it.

They are short. They are short in the way that high-altitude peoples are sometimes short, and also in the way that people who have lived in low-ceilinged stone rooms for a very long time are short, and the two shortnesses have agreed to be one shortness. Their bodies have made a compromise with the architecture, and the architecture has made a compromise with their bodies, and the compromise has settled into a kind of fairness. They can stand upright in any room in the city. Every room was built for them, or they were built for every room. They are unsure which.

Their skin is the color of the stones they live among, and this is not coincidence and it is not camouflage and it is not adaptation in any sense the word usually takes. It is something stranger. Their skin has simply agreed, over generations, to be the color that the light of this place makes things. When the light shifts — when the overcast brightens for an afternoon, when the snow falls and the whole world whitens for a week — their skin shifts with it, but slowly, the way stones shift in color, over the course of seasons. A child born in a gray spring will be a gray child. A child born in a winter will be paler. No one remarks on this. No one could tell you when it started.

Their eyes are large. Their eyes are the largest part of them that you see first, before you see the rest, because the eyes are what their faces have leaned toward, the way the arches lean, the way the streets descend. The eyes are the color of wet slate, of glacier-water, of the particular gray that the sky is here in the late afternoon that has been extended across centuries. The eyes do not blink often. This is not a staring that is meant to intimidate or communicate or signal anything at all. It is simply that blinking has not been necessary for a long time, and the reflex has become as vestigial as the small bone in the wrist that evolution forgot to remove. They see just fine. They have always seen just fine. They do not need to refresh the image.

They are few. There are not many of them. You could walk the streets of the city for what feels like a long time and see only a handful — an old person on a step, two figures in a doorway, a child or what might be a child crouched at the edge of a puddle watching the way the water moves along the cracks. They are few the way that certain lichens are few: not rare, but sparse, distributed according to a logic that is not density but suitability. They live where they fit. They do not live where they do not fit. The distinction is the entire architecture of their lives.

They wear clothing. The clothing is undyed wool, and undyed linen, and the hide of something that might once have been an animal and might once have not been. The clothing is layered. The clothing is layered in the way that stones are layered — each piece a stratum, each stratum a different season, the outermost being what is being shed and the innermost being what is being kept warm. They have no buttons. They have no fasteners that are visible. The layers hold themselves to the body through some negotiation that the cloth has worked out with the cloth beneath it, and the body has agreed to be patient.

Their hair is long, and their hair is the same color as their skin, and this is also not remarked upon. The hair is worn loose, or bound with a cord of twisted wool, or simply gathered into a knot at the back of the head that has the geometry of something meant to last. It does not move in the wind, because there is rarely wind here, and when there is, the hair moves with the wind as though it had always been part of the wind and is only briefly visiting the head.

They speak rarely. This is the most important thing about them, and the most easily mistaken. They are not silent. They are not withholding speech. They have not taken a vow. They speak, and when they speak, what they say is heard, and what they say is understood, and the understanding does not always require the words. The language they use is mostly gesture, as has been said, but to call it gesture is to call the weather gesture, or to call the way the water moves along the cracks gesture. It is the body speaking to the body. It is the hand speaking to the stone. It is the breath speaking to the breath of the listener, and the listener knows.

When they speak aloud — and they do, sometimes, in the long evenings that the overcast makes of every hour — what they say is short. A single word might be a sentence. A single breath might be a paragraph. The words they use are old. The words they use are not archaic, because archaic implies a break, a sense that something has been left behind. The words are continuous. They are the same words, or they are the words the words came from. They speak in a tongue that is the mother of whatever tongues might descend from it, or they speak in a tongue that no tongue has ever descended from, and they are not sure which, and the uncertainty does not trouble them.

They are old. They are old in a way that is not biological. They are old the way the stones are old. They are old the way the moss is old. They age, but the aging does not accumulate the way aging usually accumulates. They do not become frail. They do not become forgetful. They become, instead, more themselves, in the way that a stone becomes more itself the longer it sits in one place. An elder in this city is not someone who has been worn down. An elder in this city is someone who has had longer to be the particular stone they are. They die, but they die as the lichen dies — slowly, at the edges, the center still green, the death a kind of completion rather than a cessation.

The children are strange. The children are strange the way new things are strange in old places. They are small and curious and their skin is a slightly different gray than their parents' skin, a gray that has not yet settled into the final color it will become. They do not speak at all, or they speak only in the language of breath and gesture, and their gestures are quick, and their breath is fast, and the quickness and the fastness are tolerated in them the way the noise of water is tolerated in the city — as a thing that belongs, that is allowed, that does not disturb. The children play. They play with stones. They play with the water in the cracks. They play with the bones they find in the upper chambers. They play by themselves, mostly, or in pairs, and their play has no rule that an outside observer could discern, and yet it is not chaotic, and it is not rule-free. It has the structure of things that have been done for a very long time.

They do not worship. They do not worship in the sense of building temples and offering prayers. But they are not without reverence. The reverence is for the stones. The reverence is for the water. The reverence is for the cold, and the light, and the damp, and the silence. They have a relationship with these things that is not worship and not stewardship and not personification. It is closer to kinship. They are related to the place the way a lichen is related to the rock — not by blood, not by contract, but by being the only thing that can grow here, and the place being the only thing they can grow in.

They make things. They make things slowly, and they make them well, and they do not distinguish the categories of their making. A copper knife and a stone knife are the same knife. A bone needle and a metal needle are the same needle. A wall is a wall. A wall is also a way of being in the world. They do not have art in the way that other places have art. They have craft. They have craft that is so complete and so unselfconscious that it has become indistinguishable from the place itself, and the place has become indistinguishable from the craft, and the people who do the craft have become indistinguishable from both. You cannot tell, in this city, where the making ends and the maker ends and the made thing ends. They are all the same substance, arranged differently.

They know things. They know things that are not taught and not learned and not written down. They know where the water will go before it goes there. They know when the snow will come, not by reading the sky but by the way their knees feel, by the way the stones are cool against their hands in the morning. They know which of the upper chambers are safe to enter and which have become, over the centuries, simply rooms that exist in the architecture but not in the city. They know this the way you know your own name.

They do not greet strangers. This is not rudeness. This is the recognition that you are not a stranger, in the way that the word is usually meant. You are simply another presence in the city, the way the moss is another presence, the way the water is another presence. You will be here, or you will not. They have no curiosity about which. They have no curiosity about you, in the sense that curiosity usually takes — the curiosity that wants to know your name, your origin, your purpose. They are curious about the way you move through their streets, in the same way they are curious about the way the water moves along the cracks, which is to say: they notice, and the noticing is complete, and the noticing does not require more.

When you leave — if you leave, and here, as with arriving, the word is uncertain — they will not watch you go. They will not call after you. They will return to the small tasks that have occupied them since before you came, the mending of a wall, the carrying of water, the long sitting on a step in the damp gray light. They will not speak of you. They will not speak of you to each other in the long evenings when speech occasionally happens. You will become, if you become anything at all, a footprint in the wet paving stones that is then walked over by the next person, and then by the next rain, and then by the next thousand years. You will be a layer in the strata of the city. You will be a stratum in the clothing of one of them, perhaps, if they happened to have need of your thread.

They are patient. They are patient the way the cold is patient, the way the damp is patient, the way the light is patient. They have not been waiting for anything. They have not been waiting for you. They have been here, and they will be here, and the city will continue to settle into its final form, and the final form is not finished, and they are part of the settling, and the settling is part of them, and the two have agreed, a long time ago, to be the same.

This is who lives in the city at the roof of the world. They are the people of the place. The place is the people of them. There is no difference anymore, and the difference, when it was last perceptible, was so long ago that even the stones do not remember which came first.
