# The Marrow

You reach it by a passage that goes down for what feels like longer than the mountain above should allow. The stone changes. First it's granite, then something darker with the shine of obsidian, then a pale pumice that rings faintly underfoot and gives off a smell like flint struck. The passage narrows once, opens again, and then the heat finds you — not gradually but all at once, a hand laid across the face. The air is so dry it takes the moisture from your eyes. And then the dark becomes a place rather than an absence, and you are standing at the lip of the Marrow.

It is one chamber. You can feel that immediately — the way the sound falls off into itself rather than echoing outward. Vaulted, yes, but not vast. A cathedral in size and nothing larger. The ceiling is perhaps sixty meters above; the floor is not flat but cupped, basin-shaped, with a low central rise where something once welled up from below and hardened into a black flow. The walls curve up and inward, and they are not walls. They are alive.

---

The dark is not total. There is a dim, persistent glow — not light exactly but the memory of light, a thermal luminescence in the stones themselves, enough to make out the shapes of things without ever resolving them into color. Everything is rendered in gradients of black and deep red and a brown like old blood. In this glow the chamber looks like the inside of a lung, or the inside of a throat.

The walls are covered.

Not clustered. Not scattered. *Covered*, the way a wound is covered, the way a surface is covered when there is no longer any surface to speak of. They hang in sheets. They grow in clusters. They lie in strata along the floor like fallen pages. They are layered over each other so that an individual is hard to find — there are no individuals, only the mass, only the aggregate, only the thing that the wall has become.

They are shaped like fingers and shaped like hands and shaped like nothing you have a word for. Long, tapered, segmented but not jointed — closer to the way a tongue is segmented, by shallow rings of tighter tissue. Some hang limp, swaying almost imperceptibly. Some are curled inward, the tips buried in the bodies of others. Some are upright, rigid, faintly vibrating, and these are the ones that make the sound.

The sound is just at the edge of hearing. A low hiss, distributed across so many sources that it has no origin — it is simply in the air, a white noise of small frictions, the sound of a million finger-pads dragging across each other as the mass shifts to accommodate its own weight. Below it, deeper, a kind of subsonic pulse, felt in the sternum more than heard, as if the whole chamber is breathing very slowly.

---

There is no water. This is the first wrong thing, and it takes a moment to recognize it as wrong. The heat is the heat of a body, not the heat of an oven. The air is dry but it is not dead — it is thick with something, a warmth that has been breathed out and breathed in so many times that it has become a medium in itself. You realize, standing there, that you are breathing them. They are breathing you. The chamber is a lung and you have stepped into it.

The creatures are not wet. They are sealed. Their surfaces are like the surface of a seed, like the skin of a sausage, smooth and faintly iridescent where the glow catches them. When two of them press against each other the contact is dry and makes a sound like paper. They do not bleed when the layers shift and expose the ones beneath; there is no fluid, only the same dry flesh, the same sealed tubes packed tighter than you can focus on.

And they are packed. The density is the second wrong thing, because density should require architecture — should require some way to support, some way to ventilate, some way to feed the lowest layers — and there is none. They simply pile. The ones at the bottom are not crushed. The ones at the top do not fall. They hold each other in place by their own packed weight, a kind of dry architecture that should not work and does.

---

The floor is not the floor.

It is older versions of them. You can see this where the central rise is exposed — layers of them, compressed into something like peat, like a fossil fuel in the making, layered down through ages. Some of the layers are dark and brittle. Some are still faintly flexible. Some are so old they have begun to look like rock themselves, like the geology of the chamber made flesh and then made rock again. The Marrow is built on the Marrow. The present is sitting on a thick column of its own dead.

There are marks on the walls. Here and there, where the living mass has parted slightly, you can see the stone beneath, and the stone is scarred. Not with writing. Not with tools. With scratches, long and shallow, the kind of mark left by something dragged. They run in lines. They run in patterns. They might be patterns. They are not deep enough to be sure. The faint structures stand at the edge of the chamber where the walls meet the floor — lumpy verticals, fused to stone, that might once have been free-standing. They might be remnants of something that grew here before the current occupants. They might be parts of the current occupants that have calcified, that have begun to become architecture. You cannot tell, and the chamber does not explain itself.

---

The heat comes from below. You can feel it rising through the floor, through the layer of dead, through your feet. The Marrow sits above a heat source — a magma chamber, a geothermal vent, something that has been warming this place since before the mountain had a name. The creatures are warm to the touch. You do not touch them. The heat in the chamber is their heat, given off and held in, and the air is warm because they are warm, and they are warm because the stone is warm, and the stone is warm because of something far down that no one has ever seen.

They do not eat, as far as you can tell. There is nothing to eat. There are no plants, no fungi, no visible input. The chamber is sealed — the passage you came through is the only opening, and the air in it is cooler, drier, dead. The Marrow is a closed system. What goes in is heat, and what comes out is heat, and in between, the mass turns slowly over itself. The ones at the bottom are pressed into the stratum. The ones at the top are new. The new ones are not born — there is no birth that you can see. They emerge from the layers, from the compaction, from the slow geological process of the aggregate turning over. Where they come from is the same place they go: into each other, into the floor, into the rock that was once alive.

The chamber is its own food. The chamber is its own weather. The heat cycles, the air shifts in imperceptible convection currents along the curved walls, the mass shifts with it, and somewhere in that shifting the new emerge and the old are laid down. There is no waste. There is no death that is not also a deposit. The system has been running for longer than the mountain has been a mountain.

---

When you stand still long enough, you begin to hear the subsonic pulse more clearly. It is not one pulse. It is many, layered, slightly out of phase with each other, so that the pressure in your chest rises and falls in a slow, complex rhythm that almost resolves into a pattern and never quite does. The upright ones — the vibrating ones — are the loudest contributors. They stand in clusters, pale, stiff, slightly taller than the rest, and their vibration is a single note each, but the notes are not the same. The chamber is full of a chord that has been sounding for a very long time, and the chord is not stable, and the chord is not beautiful, and the chord is also the only music this place has ever had.

You stand at the lip of the Marrow and you breathe the heat and the dry air and the faint papery smell of countless sealed bodies shifting against each other. The glow does not change. The pulse does not change. Nothing in the chamber is in a hurry, and nothing in the chamber is still, and nothing in the chamber has ever once looked at you.

Above you, sixty meters of vaulted stone. Below you, the long heat of the earth. Around you, the mass, breathing its slow chord, laying down its dead, building itself taller by a body-width every few centuries. The scratches on the walls might be a language. The lumpy structures at the edge might be ruins. The chamber might be thinking, in the way that a chamber thinks when it has been alive long enough. You will not come back to find out. The Marrow does not require you. The Marrow was here before you climbed down the passage, and the Marrow will be here after the mountain has worn away, the heat finally fading, the last of the upright ones finally falling silent in their chord, the mass settling into the floor and becoming the next stratum, becoming geology, becoming rock that someone, millions of years from now, will find and wonder at.

You go back up the passage. The heat lets you go. The dark closes behind you like a throat.
