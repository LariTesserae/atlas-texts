In the upper stories — past the warm slant of the light, past the ceiling where rain first meets wood and is broken into its component streams — there is a population.

It is the wrong word. A population is born and replenished; a population has demographics, attrition, the slow churn of one generation replacing another. Nothing here does that. The things in the canopy are not born. They are not replaced. They are *ongoing* — present the way the rain is present, the way the mist is present, as a continuous condition of the system. They appear in numbers that do not vary because the system does not vary. If you watched for a year, you would not see them thin out in a hard season or thicken in a generous one. There are exactly this many of them. There have always been exactly this many.

But there are many of them, and they move.

The light is bad. The mist up there is in pieces — the rain is still coming through in fragments, and the air is full of broken water and the warm sidelight, and the things that move through this are visible only as flickers. A comma of something polished, there and gone. A glint that is colder than the rain-light, bluer, that darts and is extinguished. A shape that could be the inside of a thorax catching the sun, except that what you saw did not have a body so much as a center, a knot of motion, around which other things were happening too fast to resolve.

You cannot settle on what they are made of. They are made of all of it, and of none of it. Metal, sometimes — when one banks across a brighter region of canopy and the angle is right, you can see the sheared edge of a turned brass fitting, and almost make out threads where wings should be. Chitin, sometimes — a segmented plating, a hinge, the suggestion of a joint at a place that bends the way a joint bends. Light itself, sometimes — when one passes between you and a brighter piece of canopy, it casts no shadow; it adds light, briefly, like a spark has been carried across.

They are small. The size of a thumb. Some smaller. They move in vectors that look choreographed only because they are so dense with traffic — hundreds of them in the volume of air above you, each on a path, each carrying something.

What they carry: material that looks like leaf-matter, or like pollen, or like the wet green dust of something crushed. They hold these fragments against their undersides with limbs you cannot clearly see, or they are pierced through them, or they are partially *made* of them, the cargo fused into the carrier so that the two cannot be told apart. They ferry this upward, into the upper branches. They ferry other things downward. The traffic is bidirectional. The branches hum when they pass.

The hum is not incidental. The hum is the work.

Whatever transformation is being maintained in this canopy — the slow refining of water and light into whatever product the forest is producing — these small things are part of its mechanism. They are the moving parts. They are the only moving parts at a scale a person can perceive. They carry the reagent because the reagent has to be carried, and they are what carries it. They are not aware of carrying it. They are not aware of anything. They are the carrying.

You can stand for an hour watching them and learn nothing about them as individuals, because they are not individuals. They are a flow. A density of motion that has organized itself into lanes the way water organizes itself into streams when it meets resistance, and they are the stream, and the lane is the stream, and the work is the stream. If one of them is removed, another does not take its place, because there is no place to take. There is just the lane, which continues.

But if you watch long enough — and here, there is nothing else to do — you will see one of them fail.

A flicker that slows. A flicker that drifts out of its lane. A small bright thing that begins, without apology, to fall. It spirals downward through the broken light, through the dense warm air, shedding velocity the way a leaf sheds height. And as it falls, something happens to it. The metallic brightness goes out of it. The chitinous angles soften. The thing that reaches the mid-levels — your level, the level of the trunks and the mist and the slow wet world where you are standing — is not the same thing that started.

It is a drifter now.

The drifters are the size of a hand. They glow with a dim interior warmth, the color of the light that slants down through the canopy, the color of honey held up against a window. They are translucent in the way that a soap bubble is translucent — you can see through them, but what is on the other side is slightly bent, slightly thickened. Inside the translucence, something moves slowly. Something denser than the carrier around it. Something that is being digested, or matured, or refined.

The drifter drifts. It does not fly. It does not steer. It moves the way a piece of ash moves in still air — slowly, and with a kind of suspended purpose, as if it is being held up by something thicker than air. It approaches a trunk. It touches. It roots — extends something thin into a water-groove, into the slow dark water that is being routed downward by the trunk's spiral. It drinks, or feeds, or simply rests, for a minute, for two. Then it unroots. It drifts on.

If you stand still — and you will, because the mist is at this density and the trunks are spaced the way they are spaced and there is no particular place to go — a drifter will settle on you.

It does not attach. It does not bite. It does not sting. It simply rests there, with a kind of weary completeness, the way a tired moth rests on a hand that has been held out for a long time. As if the warmth of you is the warmth it was looking for. As if, now that it has found it, it is permitted, briefly, to pause.

It is heavier than it looks. The weight is the weight of what it is carrying inside — the descended material, on its way down. The weight of the carried thing, almost home.

After a minute, perhaps two, the drifter lifts. It resumes its slow path. It is looking for the next trunk, the next groove, the next patch of warmth. It is not interested in you. You are not food to it. You are not a host. You are a momentary warmth in a place that is otherwise uniformly cool, and it has paused in you the way a tired thing pauses in a patch of sun.

Where do they go? Down. Always down. Toward the root-felt, the dark springing substrate underfoot, where the oldest water is. They settle onto it. They are absorbed. Their glow goes out, the way a coal goes out when it is covered. The material inside them enters the substrate. The substrate drinks it.

And the cycle closes. The carrier fell. The carrier became a drifter. The drifter carried the cargo down. The drifter was reabsorbed. The material is in the roots now. The roots will feed it, eventually, back to the trunks. The trunks will carry it up, in water, in sap, in whatever passes for sap here. The carriers will find it. The carriers will carry it again.

There is no other life here.

There are no figures. There are no animals in the conventional sense — no mammals, no birds, no insects with life cycles, no plants with seasons. There are the carriers and there are the drifters and there is the substrate, and there is the rain, and there is the slow exhalation every forty seconds that you feel in the floor before you hear it in the air.

What lives here, properly, is the system. The whole of it. The closed loop. The forest is not inhabited by something. The forest is the inhabitant. The carriers are its hands. The drifters are its exhalation. The root-felt is its digestion. The breathing is its breath.

It is running. It has been running for longer than anything now living has been alive. It will be running when nothing now alive is alive at all. It does not know you are here. It does not need to know. The warmth you left in the felt will be reabsorbed within the hour. The slight displacement you caused in a drifter that had been settling toward you will resolve, and the drifter will redirect itself to the nearest trunk, and resume its work, and the work will continue, and the rain will fall through the canopy in sheets and columns, and the pistons will tick in their slow vast rhythm, and the light will slant down and be taken up, and the product of the place will be accumulated somewhere far above in a reservoir or a chamber or a mouth, and used for something, for some purpose the place itself has forgotten, or never knew, or has no capacity for knowing.

The forest is its own weather, and its own life, and its only witness is itself.

You were here. You are leaving.

It does not register your leaving.

It continues.
