# The Inhabitants of the Convection Ward

They live in a long, slow, vertical rhythm, and they do not know how to do otherwise.

---

The lowest tier belongs, by right of warmth, to the **old**. Not only the old—the sick, the recovering, the heavily pregnant, the half-grown children who have not yet earned their tier—but most of all the old. They have migrated downward, over years, in the way a river migrates toward the sea. They sleep in the long common dormitories that flank the foundry-hall on either side, where the stone floor is gently, ceaselessly warm beneath thin pallets, where the air smells of machine-oil and of the iron-sweet steam that seeps around the foundry doors. They have not been cold in a long time. Some of them are afraid of cold now, in the way a person is afraid of a thing they once knew well and have stopped knowing.

They are the most numerous, this way. There are a lot of old people. There is a kind of joke about it, which is: *if you want to live a long time, come here*. The joke is not entirely a joke. The air, the warmth, the mineral steam, the steady, gentle pressure of the deep heat—something in it keeps the machinery of a body turning longer than it ought to. Coughs ease. Joints loosen. The ones who arrive half-dead sometimes walk out of the lower dormitories after a season, having decided to live after all. The ones who were born here, who have lived all their lives with the engine-hum in their inner ear, are strange to outside physicians. Their blood runs a half-degree warm. Their fingernails grow faster than they should. They are not certain, these old people, whether the ward is keeping them alive or whether it is keeping them *in*.

They keep small things. A ceramic bird given to them by a grandchild. A pressed bloom of the pale lichen, which they have framed in tin, though the bloom is the same as the day it was picked and will be the same in a hundred years. A folded letter, written in a hand that has not changed in decades, because the writer has not come back. They keep these things on the narrow shelves above their pallets, and the shelves are the most decorated surface in the ward.

---

The **engine-wardens** live a tier above, in a long gallery that has no doors on the inner face, only a low iron railing, so that the hum of the engine rises to them through the night. There are perhaps forty of them at any given time, and the line goes back, in unbroken male and female succession, to a great-grandmother who is recorded as having *laid the first hand on the great shaft*—meaning she greased it, presumably, in the early days when it was a different engine, but the phrase has acquired a more ceremonial weight. The engine-wardens do not, as a rule, marry outside their own. It is not forbidden. It is just that the work takes a certain kind of household.

Their children are raised to the sound of the hum. A baby born in the gallery sleeps through anything, it is said, except the sudden silence that would mean the engine had stopped—and the engine has not stopped, in anyone's living memory, though there are drills, and the children are taught the drills from the time they can walk, and the most frightening of them is the one in which the engine stops.

The engine-wardens are quiet people. They speak in low voices even when there is no need. They have a sense for the engine that is not in any ledger; they can hear when a bearing is going, when a seal is starting to weep, when the deep heat is running a tenth of a degree hotter than yesterday and is therefore *saying something*, though what it is saying is not yet clear. They go down into the engine's guts in pairs, with leather aprons and small bright lamps, and they come back smelling of oil and sulfur and of the particular warm dust that gathers on a thing that has been running without cease for longer than anyone remembers. They are not unhappy, exactly. They are *attentive*, in a way that other people find tiring to be around.

In their off-hours they keep a kind of small still-life tradition: they carve the shapes of the engine's parts, in soap-stone, in the way that some fishing villages carve the shapes of fish. A warden's house, when you are invited into one, has a row of these carvings on a shelf: the great shaft, the small shaft, the first valve, the second valve, the long pipe that goes down into the deep rock—carved, the last, with particular care, because no one has ever seen the end of it.

---

The **masons** are the most numerous of the working castes, and they live on tiers two and three, in the dense middle band where the slag-brick buildings press close. They are the descendants, mostly, of the people who poured the first bricks, and they have, like the engine-wardens, a long and unbroken practice. They do not cut stone in the way you might imagine. They *pour* it. The slag from the refineries below is still molten when it comes up the inner switchbacks in ladle-carts, and the masons shape it in wooden forms that are themselves made by the form-makers, who live a tier above the masons and who have, in some families, been making forms for six generations. The forms are taken apart when the brick is cool. The bricks are stamped, on one face, with a small mark that is the maker's mark. The maker's mark is the maker's name. The maker's name is in the ledger at the foundry-hall, on tier one, in a column that has been kept for two hundred and sixty years and that no one has ever been willing to skip a line of.

The masons' hands are scarred, every one of them, on the same places: the web between thumb and forefinger, the inside of the wrist, the base of the small finger. The scars come from a particular work-gesture, the same gesture made ten thousand times, the same gesture their great-great-grandparents made. They are proud of the scars. They are proud of the bricks. They are not, on the whole, prone to talk about either, except in the long slow evenings in the common rooms of their tier, when a man might pass a brick across the table to a friend and say, *look at this one*, and the friend would look, and there would be a silence, and the brick would be passed back, and that was the whole conversation.

Their wives and husbands—the *form-makers* in particular often marry *masons*, the work pulling the two trades together—are quiet, patient people with flour on their hands and the kind of voices that are used to being heard over a long distance. They keep small dogs of the six-legged kind, for warmth in the legs on cold nights, and they are the only people in the ward who are willing to admit to liking the taste of the dogs, when the time comes. They make a ceremony of it. They give the bones back to the slag.

---

The **water-keepers** are fewer, and stranger. There is, at any time, only one main water-keeper, and one or two under-keepers, and the office is held for life and is not, in the ordinary sense, sought. The main water-keeper lives in a small stone house on tier four, beside the well-court, and the house has a single window that looks down, always, into the cistern at the bottom of the ward. The water-keeper is the person who decides, each day, how much water each tier will receive. This is done by a series of wooden gates in the long carved channels that run down the inner face of the cone, and the gates are opened and closed by the water-keeper's hand, and the under-keepers carry word, when word is needed, by running.

The water-keeper does not drink water. This is a small private tradition, and the office-holders have not always kept to it, but the most respected of them have, and it is taken as a kind of sign. The water-keeper drinks, instead, a weak broth made from the pale lichen of the shaded cracks. The broth is colorless and almost tasteless. It is said to be very slightly sweet.

The water-keeper's leather-bound book, in which the day's allocations are recorded, is in fact a great many books, each beginning where the last ended, and the most recent of them is the great-grandfather's book and is kept, in a sealed case, in the back of the small stone house, and the current water-keeper has read it all the way through, three times. The current water-keeper is a woman of about fifty, who has not been out of the ward in her life, and who has, on her writing desk, a small cloth bag of the very first allocation her great-grandfather ever made. The bag contains a few grains of slag, from the ladle he used to pour it, and a few flakes of rust, from the first gate he ever closed, and a small folded note in his hand that says, only, *the upper tiers were thirsty*.

---

The **children** are everywhere, and they belong, in a sense, to no one. They are the most democratic thing the ward has produced. They run the switchbacks in packs, in the slow warm afternoons, and they have their own names for the tiers—the *tier of the long horn*, the *tier of the small bells*, the *tier where the man who never opens his door*—and they are the only people who will go to the upper empty tiers without a reason, because the upper tiers are a little frightening and the children like a little fright, in small doses. They have been known to put offerings in front of the closed doors of the upper works, on the rare occasions when a closed door is left ajar. The offerings are usually small stones, or a folded paper, or a fragment of the pale bloom. No one knows what is done with the offerings, because the doors are closed again before anyone can ask.

The children do not go down to the engine. This is a hard rule. They have heard the engine-hum all their lives, and they know it is there, and some of them will be engine-wardens, in the end, but they do not go down to it, not until they are old enough, and then only in pairs, and then only when they are called. The youngest engine-warden anyone can remember was fourteen. Her name is in the engine's logbook, in a hand that is unsteadier than the hand beside it, which is her father's.

In the upper air, in the late afternoon, when the pale column of the deep heat is visible above the foundry roof, the children of the upper tiers stand at the rim of the cone and watch it bend. They make wishes on it. They do not tell anyone what the wishes are. The wishes are not, on the whole, ambitious. The most common of them, as far as anyone has been able to learn, is: *let there be rain, soon, but not yet*. They are children of a place that has learned to want things in moderation.

---

The **lost birds**.

A bird that loses its way into the ward is a small event, and it is met with a particular tenderness. The people of the upper tiers keep small woven cages, hung from the corbels of their windows, and when a bird comes down the cone, spiraling, confused, the children of the upper tier will try to catch it, gently, in cupped hands. The cages are baited with crumbs of the mill-pellets and with a few drops of water, carefully hoarded from the day's ration.

The birds do not always survive. The air of the ward is not their air. But when one does survive, and is well enough to be released, it is taken up to the rim of the cone in a small procession, with a horn-player if one can be found, and the cage is opened, and the bird is let go into the upper winds. The children watch it climb. They do not clap or call out. They watch, in silence, until the bird is a speck, and then they go back down the switchbacks to their dinners.

A released bird is considered a small omen, but of what, exactly, no one in the ward will say. The omen is small, and so the thing it omen-izes is also small: a thing about to be found, perhaps, or a thing about to be lost, or a small shift in the day's weather, or nothing at all. The people of the ward are careful with their omens, the way they are careful with their water. A small omen is a small cup, but it is not nothing.

---

And then, of course, there is the **man who never opens his door**.

He is real, in the sense that there is a closed door on the upper empty tiers, and that the door has a small lamp burning beside it, and that the lamp is never, in the long memory of any of the neighbors, unlit. He is real, in the sense that there is food put outside the door, every second day, on a stone plate, and the food is taken in, and the plate is set back out, and no one has ever seen the hand that does this. The food is a small portion: a bit of bread, a piece of the dried six-legged, a small cup of water. It is not enough, surely, to live on, and yet he lives on it, or the food is taken, or the door is opened in the night, when no one is watching.

The neighbors do not gossip about him, exactly. They speak of him in a lower voice than they use for the weather, and they refer to him, when they must refer to him, as *the man*, with the definite article, the way you would refer to a saint. Some of the older ones have theories. The man is a penitent. The man is an engine-warden who has taken a vow of some kind. The man is a person who has seen the bottom of the long pipe, and was not supposed to, and is paying for the seeing. The man is a person who has decided, for reasons of his own, to live in a small private darkness, and the small lamp is a courtesy to the rest of the ward, so that they will know he is still alive.

The children put their offerings in front of his door. The offerings, when he takes the food, are sometimes also taken, and sometimes not. No one has ever asked him about it. No one has ever knocked. The door is not, on the whole, the kind of door that one knocks on.

---

The ward is a single organism, in the way that a coral is a single organism, in the way that a long slow river is a single organism, in the way that anything is a single organism if you look at it for long enough. The old at the bottom, warm. The engine-wardens, attentive, with their ears tuned to the hum. The masons, scarred and quiet, building the next tier. The water-keeper, with her leather-bound book, deciding who shall drink. The children, in their packs, with their small private frights. The birds, when they come. The pale blooms, patient in the cracks, which were here before the town and will be here after.

The engine hums. The town breathes. The cone holds it, patient as geology. The people of the ward live, in their long, slow, vertical way, with the small lamps burning at the closed doors, and the water metered out by a woman who does not drink water, and the offerings set out for the man who never opens his, and the bird, when there is a bird, climbing back up into the upper winds, watched in silence by children who will, in time, become the old, who will, in time, sleep in the warm dormitories at the bottom of the world, and keep on their shelves a ceramic bird, and a pressed bloom, and a folded letter in a hand that has not changed in decades.
