Who lives there.

---

A woman named Tsela lives in a door painted the color of bone, four households up from the central stream on the eastern wall. She is sixty and she is small and her hands are the texture of the rocks she was raised on, which is to say they are very smooth and very cold, and when she takes your hand she does it as if she is picking up a sleeping animal. She is a trimmer of lamps. This is what her family does. Her mother trimmed, and her mother's mother, and somewhere back, when the coaxing was being learned, somebody — there is a story, it is not a long one — figured out that if you pinched the fungus at the right node in its second week, it would fork, and if you pinched again, it would fork again, and you could over many patient years grow a lamp the height of a man. Tsela's family grows the tallest lamps in the trench. People say this is bragging. Tsela does not say it is not. Her lamps lean, slightly, against the wall behind them, the way her people lean against the wall when they are tired, which is most of the time.

She has a daughter, Vess, who is twenty-eight and unmarried, which is not unusual here — there are few marriages, because there are few formalities, and people couple the way the rain comes, irregularly, and children are raised by whoever is nearest. Vess trims, but Vess is not patient. She wants the lamps to be brighter, and she is forever pinching too hard, or pinching in the wrong week, and the lamps come out stubby or come out forked in ugly places. Tsela lets her. Tsela says, *you cannot teach the pinch, you can only be pinched*. She means by this that the hands must learn. Vess does not yet know what her mother means, and assumes it is some kind of riddle.

Tsele's house has two rooms. The outer one is the kitchen and the place where guests are received. The inner one is a low sleeping hollow, cut further back into the rock, where the ceiling comes down to a hand's breadth above the bed. The bed is heather and woven rush and an old sheepskin that came down the crack one year, decades ago, the whole dead sheep, and was divided up between three families. Tsele's piece is the back half. In winter the sheepskin still smells faintly of itself.

The kitchen has a hearth cut into the floor. The smoke goes up and up and finds a crack somewhere and gets out; nobody knows where. The hearth is the one place in the house where the weeping of the rock is louder than usual, because the rock has been thinned by the cutting and it strains. Tsele likes the kitchen for this reason. She likes to hear the rock make a sound.

In the kitchen there is also a small clay figure of an animal that nobody has identified. It has been in the family for as long as anyone remembers. It sits on a high shelf. Tsele has never moved it. Vess, when she was small, asked what it was. Tsela said, *a cousin*. Vess, when she was older, asked again. Tsela said, *something that lived here before us*. Vess, when she was older still, did not ask again.

---

Across the way — and across the way means up the wall, because the houses are on both sides and they face each other across the trench like two audiences watching a play that neither one of them is putting on — lives a man named Pold. Pold is a beekeeper. There are about twenty beekeepers in the trench and Pold is the worst of them, and this is why he is liked, because the best beekeepers are, by the nature of the work, gone often and the people in their houses are lonely, and the worst beekeepers are home, and Pold is home a great deal. He is home because his bees keep leaving. They leave, they go out the crack, and they do not come back to Pold. Pold does not know why. He has tried everything that the other beekeepers have suggested, and a few things that nobody has suggested, and none of it has worked. He has repainted his hive boxes in every color that he can grind from the trench's minerals. He has played the bees small tunes on a reed pipe, which is a thing one beekeeper swears by. He has moved the hives higher and lower and east and west. The bees leave. The bees go to other people.

Pold does not mind as much as he should. He is forty and unmarried and somewhat fat, which in the trench is a kind of wealth, because the trench does not overfeed. He keeps a chair by the inner window of his kitchen, which is a notch in the rock, really, and he sits in it for hours, and watches the stream, and the people, and the lamps as they go on at dusk. He has named his chair. He has named it *the Listener*, because he says that is what the chair does. He talks to it. The chair does not answer. Pold is not bothered by this. He is a man who has got used to things not answering.

He has a nephew, Orin, who is twelve, and who would very much like to be a beekeeper too, but who is, by the long family custom, in the lamp business, because the boy is his mother's sister's son, and his mother is a trimmer of lamps and that is the trade the boy will inherit. Orin helps Pold anyway, in secret, and Pold teaches him, also in secret, the small skills of the work, and they both pretend, when the family asks, that this is not happening. The bees like Orin. This is the unfair part. The bees come back to wherever Orin is, and stay. Pold does not mind this either, exactly, but there is a look on his face sometimes, in the evenings, when Orin is asleep in the next room, and Pold is sitting in *the Listener*, and the lamps are coming on, and the bees are humming their small hum in a hive that has decided, for now, to stay. The look is not bitterness. It is the look of a man who has been outwitted for years by something very small, and who has begun to suspect that the small thing is not malicious, just uninterested, and that this is somehow worse.

---

Downstream, in the section of the trench where the walls are wetter and the smell of mint and onion is strong, lives a family that does not have a single name, because they are many. The grandmother is called Eshe. The grandfather is called nothing, because he died two winters ago, and the people of the trench do not use the names of the dead for a season after the death, as a kind of courtesy, and then after that they say *he is not here anymore*, and after that they simply do not say him. His chair is in the corner. It is not occupied. Nobody moves it.

Eshe is eighty and she has the only teeth in the trench that are still her own. She will not give them up. There is a woman further up who has offered to pull them and make a set of buttons from them, and Eshe has refused, politely, twice. The teeth are a small vanity. She polishes them, in the mornings, with a soft cloth, and then she eats her breakfast, which is a soft gray bread made from a moss that grows on the north wall, and a small cup of something warm that is not tea because nothing here is tea but which is called, by long usage, *tea*.

Eshe's house is the largest in this part of the trench. It has three rooms and a back hollow. The back hollow is where she sleeps, alone now, on a pile of old blankets that smell, faintly, of every person who has ever slept on them. Eshe can name the smells. There is a smell of her husband, who was a thin man, and a smell of her son, who was a fat man, and a smell of her daughter, who is not dead but who has gone out — not out of the trench but out into the world, which is a different thing, and which the people here do not discuss with any ease. *Out* is what they say. *She has gone out.* Eshe does not know where her daughter is. Eshe has not heard from her daughter in six years. Eshe does not think about this. Eshe thinks about it all the time.

There is a young man in Eshe's house who is called Sarn, and who is not, by any bloodline, related to Eshe, but who sleeps in the corner of the outer room and who eats at Eshe's table, and who is, in the way of the trench, a grandson. Sarn is sixteen. He came to Eshe's house when he was four, because his mother was killed in a fall from one of the upper doors — a fall of forty feet, into the stream, which is shallow, which is why she died, because the water did not break her fall but broke her neck. Sarn was not at home for this. Sarn was at the upper wall, helping a man who is now also dead gather birds' nests. Sarn came home and was told. Sarn did not cry. Sarn has not cried since, that anyone knows of, though he is a boy who seems on the verge of it often, especially in the evenings, when the lamps come on and the trench gets quiet and the sound of the weeping rock is the only thing.

Sarn trims lamps, but not well. He is not a trimmer by trade and not by family. He is in Eshe's house because Eshe took him in, and the trench does not turn away such children, and so he is, by default, useful. He fetches water from the stream. He carries dead lamps to the central hearth. He watches the smaller children, who are not his. He is good at watching. He is patient in a way that bothers some of the other children, who would prefer an audience. Sarn is not an audience. Sarn is a wall, mostly. He is a wall that the children lean against, the way Tsele leans against her wall, and the way the lamps lean, and the way the whole trench leans.

---

In the central hearth, where the dead lamps are burned, there is almost always a child. The burning of the dead lamps is a privilege and a job. The privilege is that the children who do it are excused from other chores. The job is that the burning is tedious, and smelly, and the smoke gets into the eyes. The children fight over the privilege. The smaller children lose, mostly, because the larger children are heavier and have longer arms. There is a small girl named Merr who has won the privilege four years running. Merr is eight. Merr is fierce. She has bitten two boys. The second one she bit, she bit on the hand, and it bled, and the boy cried, and Merr was not punished, because the people of the trench are not punitive, and because the boy's mother said, *well, what did you expect*. Merr is a trimmer's daughter. Her mother is Vess. Her grandmother is Tsele. She is, in her way, the heir to the tall lamps, and she is afraid of nothing in the trench, which is to say she is afraid of the dark, which is the only thing the trench does not have.

Merr tends the central fire in the mornings, when it is low, and in the evenings, when the dead lamps are brought down. The dead lamps come in baskets, on the heads of the older children, mostly. The dead lamps are gray, and they smell of the anise they always smelled of, but also, now, of a kind of gone-ness, of a thing that is finished. Merr puts them on the fire. Merr watches them go. Merr is interested in this. She has asked her mother what the lamps are when they are not lamps. Vess has not known what to say. Merr has asked her grandmother. Tsela has said, *they are the light they gave, put back into the rock*. Merr has accepted this. Merr has not decided if it is true.

---

There is a man in the trench who is called the Reader, although he is not, by trade, a reader, because there is not much to read. He is a man who has memorized things. He knows the names of every family in the trench, going back six generations. He knows the year of every fall, every birth, every death, every marriage, every going-out. He knows, by heart, a long poem about the formation of the trench, which he says he learned from a man who is now out, and which the other people of the trench half-believe and half-dismiss. He recites the poem sometimes, in the evenings, in the central square, by the hearth, and the children listen, and the adults listen, and the lamps glow, and the rock weeps, and it is a kind of music.

The Reader is old. The Reader is not as old as Eshe. The Reader is fifty-something. He is thin, and bald, and he has a voice that carries, and a memory that is slightly wrong, and the people of the trench correct him, gently, when he makes mistakes, and he accepts the corrections, and the next time he recites the poem, the corrections are in it, and the original version is gone, and this is, the Reader says, how poems work, and this is how the trench remembers, and this is why the trench does not write things down, because if the trench wrote things down they would stop changing, and the trench is, by its nature, a thing that changes.

The Reader has a wife, who is called Ushi, and who is, by long habit, annoyed with him. Ushi is a practical woman. She makes the cloth that the people of the trench wear, which is woven from the tough grass that grows at the base of the walls, and dyed with the gray-green of the rock, and the weak brown of the stream. Ushi is good at her work. Ushi is impatient with poems. Ushi and the Reader argue, in the evenings, in low voices, and the argument is always the same, and always gentle, and always ends with Ushi saying, *you and your long remembering*, and the Reader saying, *and you and your long weaving*, and neither of them meaning anything by it, and both of them meaning everything.

---

There is a boy in the trench who has not been named. This is unusual. Children are usually named within days of being born. Children who are not named are children whose parents have not decided, or who have died, or who have gone out. This boy is five. He lives in a house three up from the stream, on the western wall, in a family that is not his. The family calls him *the little one*, or *him*, or *the boy*, and they are not unkind, but they are not naming him, and he has begun to understand, in the way that small children understand things, that he is somehow set apart, and he does not know why, and he does not know who he is.

The boy is quiet. The boy spends a great deal of time at the central hearth with Merr, who is three years older and who has adopted him, in her fierce way, as a kind of project. Merr is teaching the boy to tend the fire. The boy is not yet strong enough to lift the dead lamps. He carries the small ones, the ones that have died early, and he puts them on the coals, and he watches them go, and he has not yet asked where they go, because he is a quiet child, and the people around him have not yet thought to explain.

The boy's family is a large one. There is a woman called Per, who is the head of the house, and her two daughters, who are called Enne and Ten, and Enne's husband, who is called something that the boy cannot pronounce, and Ten's small son, who is two, and who cries a great deal, and who is, the boy thinks, the loudest thing in the trench, which is not saying much. The boy is afraid of Ten's son, although he would not say so. The boy sleeps in a corner near the hearth, on a mat, and in the mornings he wakes before anyone else, and he goes to the central hearth, and he sits with Merr, and they do not speak, and the trench is quiet, and the rock weeps, and the lamps are dim, and the day begins.

---

There is a woman in the trench who is from out. She is the only person in the trench who is from out. She came down the crack nine years ago, on a night when the rain was very heavy, and she was very young, and she was, by her own account, lost. She would not say more than this for a long time. She has, over the years, said a little more, in pieces, to Eshe, mostly, who is patient. She came from a place where there were fields, and a sun, and a sky that was not a ribbon. She came because something happened. She will not say what. She will say only that she had to, and that the crack was there, and that she came down.

She is called Sela, which is not her original name. She took the name when she realized she would not be using the old one. She lives in a small house, all by herself, on the upper western wall, where the rock is thinnest, and the weeping is loudest, and the light from the lamps, when they come on, is bluish through the wall, as if the wall itself were glowing. Sela is a weaver, like Ushi, but a different kind of weaver. Sela weaves from the long pale grasses that grow only on the western wall, in a place only she knows. She weaves cloth that is very fine, and very white, and she does not sell it. She gives it away. She gives it to people who are sick. She gives it to people who are mourning. She gives it to people who are leaving, although this has not happened in the years she has been here. She gives it to Eshe, who puts it in the back hollow, on the pile of blankets, and who does not use it, and who will not give it back.

Sela is not happy. Sela is not unhappy. Sela is a woman who has done a thing, and is now in the place where the thing brought her, and who has made, of this place, a kind of home, and who knows, always, that it is not her home, and that there is another place, and that she cannot go back to it, and that she is not sure she would if she could. She speaks quietly. She speaks carefully. She speaks the way a person speaks who has learned, by hard experience, that words are a kind of currency, and that you must not spend them quickly. The people of the trench respect this. The people of the trench do not ask her many questions. Sela is grateful. Sela is lonely. Sela has a small lamp, in her house, that she has trained to grow very tall, and very thin, and very pale, and that she trims herself, although she was not trained to trim, and that she talks to, although she will deny this if you ask her, and that she has named, although she will not say what.

---

There are others, of course. There are many others. There is a man called Varn who is a lizard-catcher, and who knows, by some instinct, when the lizards are under a particular stone, and who lifts the stone and takes the lizard before it has decided which way to run, and the people of the trench think this is magic, and Varn does not contradict them. There is a woman called Dree who is a fish-eater, in the way that some of the people of the trench are fish-eaters, and who sits by the stream in the mornings, in the pale gray light, and eats the small blind fish raw, and who is, for this reason, slightly strange, and slightly feared, and slightly loved, because the trench is a place that respects strangeness, the way the trench is itself strange, and has made, of its strangeness, a kind of grace. There is a child, somewhere, who is learning to trim. There is a man, somewhere, who is going out. There is a woman, somewhere, who is in labor, in one of the back hollows, with the rock weeping on her, and the lamps coming on, and the sound of the stream, and the sound of her own breathing, and the sound, soon, of a new person, who will be named, who will live, who will trim or weave or catch lizards or keep bees or tend the hearth, who will lean against the wall, who will learn the weeping of the rock and stop hearing it, who will be, in the way of all the people of the trench, a kind of lamp themselves, brief, and warm, and faintly smelling of anise, and not very bright, and enough.
