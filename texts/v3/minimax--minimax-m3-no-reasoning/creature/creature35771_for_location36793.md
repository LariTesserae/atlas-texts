# Who Lives There

The foreman is the first one you meet and the last one you understand. He is perhaps fifty, or perhaps sixty — the heat does something to people's faces that makes them hard to age — and he has the build of a man who has spent his adult life walking long distances on metal grates. His shoulders are slightly hunched, as though he is perpetually ducking under a low pipe. His forearms, where the sleeves of his work shirt are rolled back, are veined and dark with a fine permanent dust that lives in the creases of his skin and shows no sign of ever coming out. When he laughs at your mention of the rigs, the laugh is a short, pressurized sound, more like steam venting from a small pipe than anything the human throat is supposed to produce. He shows you things with his hands. He does not narrate.

He has been here, he will tell you if asked, for thirty-one years. He came up on the train, the same train you are about to leave on, at the age of nineteen, to take a job his uncle had held before him. He is, in his own quiet way, the most powerful man on the plateau, because he is the one who decides, in conjunction with the three other senior foremen, which of the older terraces can still be walked on safely and which have begun to *do that thing*. He does not like to be asked about the thing. He will answer, but the answers are technical, in the way that answers become technical when the speaker would rather not be understood.

His wife is from down the valley, from a town that is no longer green, and she came up to the plateau sixteen years ago and has not been back since. She runs the tea stall at the corner of the third walkway intersection, the one near the old foundry. She is small and precise in her movements and she does not look at you when she pours. She pours correctly. The tea is black and comes in glasses without handles, because handles hold heat, and the air holds more than enough of it. She will not ask where you are from. She will not ask how long you are staying. If you compliment the tea, she will nod once, very slightly, and that will be the end of the conversation.

Their son is fourteen and works as an apprentice gauge-reader on the second terrace. He learned to read before he learned to walk, or so the story goes, because the walkways are narrow and his mother was afraid he would fall. He is very thin and very quiet and he has a habit of standing completely still for long moments, his head tilted, listening to something you cannot hear. He has never seen a tree. He is not unhappy about this. He does not know what a tree is for. He will, if you ask him, tell you about the gauges in a voice so even and uninflected that it sounds almost like a recording, and you will realize after a few minutes that he is not being cold, he is simply the way that children turn out, when they are raised on metal and steam and the steady attention required to keep a planet from softening under their feet.

---

The engineers are a class apart, though they would not like to be called a class. There are perhaps two hundred of them on the plateau at any given time, including the senior staff, the junior staff, the apprentices, and the rotating consultants who come up from the institute down the mountain for two-week stints. They are recognizable, even at a distance, by the way they walk. They walk like people who have learned that the ground is not to be trusted, but who have also learned, through long professional acquaintance with the ground, exactly how untrustworthy it is in any given spot. They step with a kind of measured deliberation, each footfall placed, never rushed, never paused over. They carry their instruments in leather bags that have gone grey with the wet. They speak in a dialect of technical language that is impenetrable to outsiders and possibly to each other.

The senior engineer in charge of the softening monitoring is a woman in her late sixties named, as nearly as you can make out, Aisha, though she may have another name that she does not give. She has been here for forty-three years. She came up as a young geologist, fresh from a university in a city that you, looking at her now, cannot imagine her ever having lived in. She is the one who named *the softening*. She is also the one who, in private, with a glass of the strong clear liquor that the senior staff distill in a shed near the second foundry, will admit that she does not know what the softening is, and that the two competing theories are both wrong, and that the truth, when it comes, will be worse than either of them, and that she hopes she will be dead by then. She does not say this with any particular emphasis. She says it the way she might say that the weather tomorrow will be hot.

The junior engineers are younger, mostly in their twenties and thirties, and they are the only people on the plateau who still, in certain unguarded moments, seem to find things wondrous. One of them, a man who was transferred in from a coastal facility two years ago, still cannot quite believe that the steam comes up out of the ground for free. He has been heard to say, in the mess hall, that the whole place is a kind of miracle, and the other engineers at his table laughed, but not unkindly, and someone bought him another cup of tea, and the conversation moved on. He is learning not to say things like that. He is learning to walk on the walkways. He is learning, in the way that all the engineers learn, that wonder is a luxury that the plateau does not support.

---

The workers are harder to know, because they are not, as a class, given to being known. They are the people who pour the concrete, who replace the valve parts, who tend the collection ponds, who drive the maintenance carts on the rails between the boreholes. They are mostly born on the plateau, or born to people who were born on the plateau, and they have the look of people whose grandparents made a decision a long time ago and whose grandchildren are still living with the consequences. They are not unfriendly. They will help you find your way. They will explain, if you ask, which walkway goes to the foundry and which goes to the hospital and which goes to the edge of the plateau where you can stand and look out over the valley that is no longer green. They will not, as a rule, invite you into their homes, because their homes are small, and because the invitation would commit them to a hospitality that the heat makes difficult, and because there is, in any case, nothing much to invite you into.

A worker named Hassan, who drives one of the maintenance carts, has been on the plateau his entire life. He is forty-one years old. He has never been off it. He has a wife, who works in the laundry at the hospital, and two daughters, one of whom is eight and one of whom is five, and both of whom go to the school that teaches the children of the workers how to read the gauges. He will tell you, if you sit with him long enough, that the plateau is a good place to live, that the work is honest, that the tea is good, that the bread is adequate. He will not tell you what he thinks about when he is driving the cart at night along the rails between the boreholes, with the rigs glowing faintly in the dark and the steam rising around him in slow sheets. He does not know what he thinks about then. He has, over the years, simply stopped thinking about it, in the way that you stop listening to a sound that never stops.

---

The children are the strangest of all, because they are the only ones who have not yet learned the patient posture of the place. They are loud, in the way that children are loud everywhere, but their loudness has a particular quality here — it is brief, and then it is over, and then they are quiet again, because the walkways are narrow and the adults around them are not loud, and because the heat punishes exertion in a way that even children learn to respect. They play games on the walkways that are adaptations of games played in other places, with rules that have been modified to account for the fact that you cannot run, exactly, on a metal grate, and that you cannot, in any case, step off the grate onto the ground. They have a game they call *the ground is lava*, but the joke of it, which they do not seem to find funny in the way that children in other places would find it funny, is that the ground is, in a sense, actually lava, or is becoming lava, or is doing something that is not quite lava but is not not lava either, and the children play the game with a seriousness that is not entirely a performance.

A girl of about seven, the daughter of a hospital nurse, will ask you, if you let her, whether the clouds where you come from are made of water, and whether the water falls, and whether, when it falls, it is cold. She will ask these questions in a tone of genuine scientific curiosity, because she has been told, in school, that water falls from clouds in other places, and that it is sometimes cold, and she is trying to reconcile this information with her own experience, which is that the water here falls only in the monsoon, and that it is, when it falls, exactly as warm as the air. She will not be able to reconcile the two pieces of information. She will eventually shrug, in the way that the people of the plateau shrug, which is a small motion, mostly in the shoulders, and go back to her game.

---

The old ones, the very old ones, are a category of their own, and they are the ones you will find, if you are lucky, sitting in the small courtyards of their identical low houses in the late afternoon, when the wind has picked up and the cowls are turning. There are perhaps a hundred of them, men and women in their eighties and nineties, some of whom were born on the plateau and some of whom came up in the early years, when the work was still new. They sit on stone benches in the shade of their covered cisterns and they do not, as a rule, speak to each other. They are not unfriendly. They are simply at the end of something. They have watched the plateau change around them for a lifetime, and they have decided, or the place has decided for them, that there is no longer any point in narrating the changes.

One of them, a man who is perhaps ninety-three, will tell you, if you sit with him long enough, that when he was a boy the ground was solid. He will say this in a voice that is half-heard over the hiss of the rigs, and he will not elaborate, and he will not say what he means by *solid*, because the word, in his mouth, does not seem to refer to the physical property of the stone but to something else, some quality that the plateau had then that it does not have now, some firmness of its own being, that has since gone out of it like heat from a cup. He will look at you when he says this, and his eyes will be very clear, and you will understand, for a moment, that he is not talking about the ground at all.

---

The foreman's wife, the senior engineer, the cart driver, the children, the old ones — they are all, in their way, the same kind of person. They are the people who have learned to live in a place that is becoming something, slowly and without their consent, and who have decided, or who have had decided for them, that the best response to this becoming is to continue, in the same patient half-lit way, in the same wet heat, under the same unlit walkways, to do the work that is in front of them, and to drink their tea, and to eat their bread, and to listen, in the early mornings, for the birds that almost never pass over, and to wait, without saying so, for whatever it is that the plateau is waiting for, which may be nothing, and which may be everything, and which will come, or will not come, in its own time, in the way that heat comes, and water, and the slow softening of stone.
