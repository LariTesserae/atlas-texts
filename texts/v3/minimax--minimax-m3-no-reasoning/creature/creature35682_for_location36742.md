# What Lives in the Singing Hollow

The people are not the most important thing that lives here. They are the most human-shaped thing that lives here, which is what a traveler sees first and what a traveler asks after. They would tell you — if they could bring themselves to break their silence long enough to use words, which they cannot, or will not, or both — that they are latecomers. That the hollow was loud before they arrived. That the vines were already growing in the floor when the first of their great-great-grandmothers climbed down from somewhere outside and decided to stay. They would tell you, with their hands on yours, that the pit is not theirs and was never theirs, and that this is all right.

They live in the towers as though the towers are a kind of weather, as though the towers are not architecture but climate. They have been in them long enough that the towers have shaped them the way wind shapes a tree. They are small — not small as a deformity, not small in the manner of a trick or a sorrow, but small the way a desert plant is small, the way a thing becomes small in a place that has a great deal of one thing and not much of anything else. They are brown-skinned, the brown of the wood of their staves, of the bark of the vines in the lower stories, of the dust that does not fall because there is nothing for dust to fall from. Their eyes are very dark and they do not blink often. When they look at you they look at you with the patience of someone who has been told, since before the time they learned to walk, that looking is permitted in all directions except one.

## The Rule, and How It Is Taught

The rule about looking is the first thing they teach. It is taught before language, before walking, before the child has been given a name (if names are a thing they use, which may not be the case — they touch each other on the brow, on the wrist, on the inner arm, in patterns that may serve as names or may serve as something better than names). The teaching is done by touch. The mother — or the elder, or the one whose hands are responsible — places both palms over the child's closed eyes and holds them there for a count. The count is not a number. The count is the time it takes to draw one long breath from the bottom of the lungs. While the hands are there, the elder touches the child's brow with two fingers, very lightly, in the direction away from the pit. The direction is south, if south means anything in a place without sun. The direction is the not-direction. The child learns the rule as the child learns warmth, learns hunger, learns the sound of the pit, which is everywhere and which is the first thing the child knows.

The rule is not explained. The rule cannot be explained, because explanation requires words and words have been given up, and because the rule, like all rules that are not broken, does not need explanation. It needs only repetition, and the long practice of keeping.

When a visitor arrives — and visitors arrive, occasionally, by paths that exist only for those who are lost in a particular way — the people gather. They do not rush. They emerge from the carved doorways that open only onto noise, and they stand among the vines in their layers of pale cloth, and they wait. They watch the visitor with their dark unblinking eyes. Then, with a quickness that is startling in a people so patient, one of them steps forward and places both hands over the visitor's eyes. The gesture is not rough. It is not tender, either. It is something between instruction and sacrament, a thing done with the seriousness of a wedding and the brevity of a slap. The hands stay for the count of one breath. Then they are withdrawn, and the visitor can see again, and the visitor will find, on looking, that the eyes will not turn toward the pit. The eyes will slide past it. The eyes will look at a vine, a flower, a tower, a pale shape moving in the leaves — anything, anything at all except the round mouth in the center of the floor. This is not hypnosis. It is not magic. It is the body's learned refusal, the way a hand learns not to touch a hot stove. The body has been told, by the patient fingers of a small brown person, that this direction is not a direction the body goes.

## How They Live Among the Vines

The people walk among the vines as the vines are the weather. They carry staves of pale wood, and they use the staves to part the leaves, to tap the trunks of the thicker growths, to lean on when they are tired. The staves are not weapons. There is nothing to fight. The staves are not tools, exactly, though they are used for many small purposes — to lift a fallen fruit, to steady a child learning to walk, to draw in the dust the shapes of the things they want to say. The staves are something else. The staves are how the hand remembers that it is a hand, in a place where the hand does almost everything.

They speak with their hands. The vocabulary is large — larger than any spoken tongue, perhaps, though no one is counting, because counting is not a thing that is done. The hands meet and the fingers lace and the small precise movements go on, in long conversations held in the middle of the roaring, and the roaring does not drown them out, because the conversations are pitched below the roaring, in a register the roaring cannot reach. They speak of the fruit, which is ripening. They speak of the cloth, which is wearing thin at the elbows. They speak of the carvings, which have deepened since the last time they touched them. They speak of births. They speak, sometimes, of deaths, though deaths are rare and quiet, and the dead are laid among the vines, and the vines close over them, and the vines are not sorry and the people are not sorry, or are sorry in a way that cannot be distinguished from going-on. They speak of the birds, but this speaking is conducted with the eyes averted and the hands low, as though the birds are a topic one should not raise one's voice about.

They touch each other constantly. They touch each other's faces with a tenderness that is not private and not public but liturgical, the tenderness of priests handling a relic that is also their grandmother. They touch each other's shoulders as they pass. They touch the carvings when they pass them, lightly, with the fingertips, the way one touches the wall of a house one has lived in for a long time. They touch the vines. They touch the pale shapes. They touch the cloth of one another's clothing, checking for wear. The touching is not affection, exactly, though it is not not-affection. The touching is the language. The touching is the conversation. The touching is the way they remember they are alive, in a place where the alive and the not-alive have grown difficult to tell apart.

The cloth they wear is woven from the fibers of the pale yellow fruit. The fibers are long and tough and the color of the stone. They are split by hand, dried in the still warm air, twisted into thread on the thigh, and woven on small looms that are kept in the upper rooms of the towers. No one eats the fruit. No one will say why. The fruit is gathered when it falls, and split, and woven, and the cloth is worn, in layers, by every person in the hollow. The cloth is the only thing that wears out. The cloth is the only thing in the hollow that is touched every day. The cloth is the measure of time in a place that does not otherwise mark time — a sleeve that grows thin at the elbow, a hem that grows ragged at the wrist, the slow consumption of the woven thing by the body that lives in it.

## The Pale Shapes

The pale shapes that move through the vines are not animals. They are the size of a cat and the shape of nothing in particular — no fixed silhouette, no committed geometry, only a soft suggestion of a body, a lump of something paler than the pale stone, that moves through the leaves without disturbing them. They have no visible eyes. They have no visible mouths. They are quiet by choice, by what can only be called politeness, the politeness of a guest in a room where someone else is speaking, or the politeness of a small relative in a room where the elders are at prayer.

When a person touches one — which they do, often, with the same liturgical tenderness they bring to each other's faces — the shape purrs. The purr is not a sound of the body. It is a sound of the air around the body, a small contained humming that lifts the hair on the forearms and the dust from the stone. And it is in the key of the pit. It harmonizes. It adds itself, briefly, to the great chord, like a single voice joining a choir that has been singing for so long it has forgotten there is a song. The person smiles. The purr gets a little louder. And for a moment — a very brief moment, the length of a held breath, the length of a single fruit falling from a low shrub — the great sound of the hollow seems almost to quiet, as though a door has been left open in a far room, as though the chord has been given permission to rest.

The pale shapes are not born. They are not bred. They appear, sometimes, in the morning, in the lower vines, and no one has seen them arrive. They are not fed. They do not seem to require feeding. When they die — and they die, eventually, though it takes a long time, and the death is quiet, and the body sinks into the leaves and is gone within a day, the vines folding over it without comment — the people lay their hands on the place where the shape was, and they touch each other, and they go on. There are always the same number of pale shapes, or close to it. No one has counted. No one would think to count. Counting is for people who believe that things could be otherwise.

## The Red Birds

The birds are bright red. There are three of them, or four — the number is not stable, because sometimes a bird is gone for a long time, weeks, perhaps, or longer, and then it is back, sitting in the same carved mouth, watching with the same eye, and no one is sure if it is the same bird. They are silent. They do not fly. They sit in the carved mouths of the western towers, the mouths that face the pit, and they watch with one eye each. Always one eye. The other eye is closed, or turned inward, or simply not there in the way the other eye is there. The open eye is the color of the inside of the pit when the light strikes it — a kind of bleached gray-white, the color of interrogation, the color of a question that does not want an answer.

The birds are the only things in the hollow that look at the pit. The birds are the only things in the hollow that have ever looked at the pit. They have been doing it for as long as anyone can remember, and presumably for much longer than that, since the birds do not look like things that began. The birds look like things that have always been doing what they are doing. The birds look like a piece of the arrangement, the way the pit is a piece of the arrangement, the way the towers are a piece of the arrangement, the way the people are a piece of the arrangement.

The people pretend not to notice them. The people pretend with a thoroughness that has become, over generations, indistinguishable from the birds not being there. Children are not told about the birds. Children learn the rule about not looking at the pit, and they learn, by extension, by the same patient touching of the brow in the not-direction, not to look at the birds, because to look at the birds is to see the birds looking, and to see the birds looking is to remember that the pit is the kind of thing that can be looked at. The birds are tolerated because the birds are necessary. The birds are part of the going-on. The pit, perhaps, requires to be looked at by something — not by everything, not by the people, but by something — and the people have refused, and the birds have accepted, and the arrangement has held.

The birds eat nothing. The birds drink nothing. They sit in their carved mouths and they watch with their one eye and they are part of the bright red of the flowers, which is also part of the brightness inside the flowers, which is also part of the light from the western wall, and the connection between these things is not explained and is not asked after.

## The Things Inside the Flowers

The flowers are always open. The flowers are the size of a man's torso. They are the color of blood that has dried and been rehydrated. They grow from cracks in the pale stone, and they do not wilt, and they do not close, and they are not pollinated, as far as anyone can tell, and they do not need to be. Inside each flower there is a brightness that moves. The brightness is small — the size of a clenched fist, perhaps — and it travels around the inside of the petals in a slow circle, never quite reaching the center, never quite leaving the rim. It is the same brightness as the light from the western wall. It is the same brightness as the open eye of the red birds. It is not alive, exactly, in the way the people are alive, or the pale shapes are alive, or the vines are alive. But it attends. It moves with the slow attention of something that is paying attention, and the thing it is paying attention to is the pit, which it cannot see from inside the flower, but which it knows about anyway.

Near the flowers, on low shrubs, there is the pale yellow fruit. The fruit smells of water remembered. The fruit is gathered. The fruit is split. The fruit is woven. The fruit is not eaten.

## The Carvers, and What the Carvers Left Behind

The carvers are gone. The carvers have been gone longer than the people have been here, which is to say, the carvers have been gone nearly forever. They were not people. The doorways are wrong — too narrow at the top, too wide at the bottom, opening onto noise rather than darkness. The proportions are wrong. The carvings are of objects — a bowl, a key, a folded cloth, an open mouth, a closed mouth, a hand turned palm-up — and never of figures, because the carvers did not have figures, or did not think in figures, or did not think the figures were worth carving. The carvings repeat and repeat and repeat, smaller each time, spiraling inward, never quite reaching a center.

The carvings move. They deepen, by a hair's breadth, each century. The figures crawl, slowly, into the stone or out of it. The hands reach a little further. The mouths open a little wider. The carvers are gone, but the carvings are still being made, by something, by the place, by the long patient going-on of the hollow itself. No one has seen a carving happen. Everyone knows the carvings are happening. The people touch the carvings when they pass, lightly, with the fingertips, the way one touches the wall of a house one has lived in for a long time, and the touching is partly a greeting and partly an apology and partly a way of saying: *I know you are still being made. I know you are not finished. I know you are part of the going-on, and so am I.*

## The Pit

The pit is full of a humming. The humming is not made of anything. The humming is the pit being the pit, the way a flame is a flame, the way a bell is a bell. It is the sound that everything else in the hollow has arranged itself around, the way coral arranges itself around a sunken bell, the way a congregation arranges itself around an altar that no one is permitted to look at. Things placed at the edge of the pit do not fall in. They are refused, gently, by a force that has no name in any of the local languages, because there are no local languages, and because the force has no name anywhere. Pebbles dropped drift to the lip and lift and set themselves down on the rim, and the humming gets a little louder, as if in thanks. The pit is content. The pit is very close to grief, and the grief is very close to content, and the two are not distinguishable, and the not-distinguishing is the sound.

The pit is not a creature. The pit is not a god. The pit is not asking to be looked at. The pit is asking only not to be looked at, and the not-looking is a kind of love, and the love is the sound, and the sound is the hollow, and the hollow is the going-on, and the going-on is the pit, and the pit is content.

## What Lives in the Singing Hollow, in Sum

What lives in the Singing Hollow, then, is a congregation. A garden. A bell. A going-on.

The people live here. They are small and brown and silent and tender, and they wear cloth made of a fruit no one eats, and they carry staves, and they touch each other's faces, and they pretend very hard that the red birds are not there, and they never, ever look at the pit.

The pale shapes live here. They purr in the key of the pit and they are polite and they die quietly and the vines fold over them and no one counts them.

The red birds live here. They watch with one eye each and they are bright red and they do not fly and they are the only ones who look, and the looking is part of the arrangement, and the arrangement is very old.

The flowers live here, always open, with their small bright moving things inside, attending.

The carvings live here, slow and patient, deepening into the stone.

The pit lives here. The pit has always lived here. The pit will live here after everything else has gone quiet, which will not be soon, which will not be ever, because the hollow has forgotten how to stop.

To live in the Singing Hollow is to live inside a sound that has decided to go on being a sound. It is to wear cloth made of a fruit no one eats. It is to touch the faces of the people you love with a tenderness that has no words for itself. It is to teach your children, with your hands on their eyes, the only rule. It is to pretend that the red birds are not there. It is to pretend very hard. It is to feel, all the time, the low pressure of a held breath, and to feel, all the time, that the breath is content.
