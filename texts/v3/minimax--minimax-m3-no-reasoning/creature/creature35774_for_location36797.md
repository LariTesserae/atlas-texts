# The Saddle

The descent begins at a place the water remembers as a shelf and ends at a place it does not remember at all.

You arrive, if *arrival* is the word, somewhere along the southern face of a basalt spine that rises eight hundred meters from an abyssal plain no light has touched since this ocean first gathered itself into something it was willing to keep. The spine is young. Sharp. The kind of geological youth that suggests it was not made by any slow earth process but rather *placed* here, recently, by something that does not negotiate with time. Its edges have not yet been rounded by current or chemistry. They cut the dark water like black knives set on end, and if you were to drag a hand along one — carefully, in the way one touches things one is not certain one should touch — the rock would part the skin without apology.

The water is cool. Not cold — there is a difference. Cold is a fact; cool is a memory of cold held against a margin. The temperature here has settled, perhaps long ago, into a band just above the threshold where molecular motion begins to surrender to something else. The water holds still. There is no thermocline. There is no current to speak of. Occasionally, in the manner of vast still things, it shifts a meter to the left over the course of a season, and then shifts back. The shifts are not caused by anything you could call weather. They are the ocean breathing in its sleep.

The city occupies a notch in the spine — a saddle, where two ridges meet at a high point, four hundred meters below the crest and seven hundred above the plain. From below, looking up, you would see only the silhouettes of the ridges, the dark against the dark. Only when you rose into the notch itself would the structures declare themselves. They are few. Seven, perhaps, or nine — the count is not fixed, because the light is not steady, and some of them fold into the rock in ways that make them difficult to separate from the basalt. Their scale is the scale of a hamlet or a single large cathedral: enough to be inhabited, not enough to constitute a population. The tallest rises a hundred and fifty meters from the saddle's floor, a vertical black lozenge grooved with narrow seams that might be windows or might be the city's way of remembering that windows were once a concept. The smallest is a horizontal dark seam in the rock face you could drift over without noticing, as though the city had decided to include a room so modest it could pass for geology.

The material is not stone. It is not metal. It does not corrode, although this water would corrode anything given enough time; it does not foul, although the water carries the faint mineral signature of a world that has never known biology. Nothing grows here. Nothing has ever grown here. There are no bacteria in the water, no archaeal films on the rock, no trace that life has ever even *attempted* this place. The cliff is bare. The structures are bare. The water is bare. It is a scene composed entirely of mineral and darkness and the work of something that did not require a biosphere to do its work, the way a crystal does not require a garden.

Inside the structures — if you can call the threshold of them *inside* — there is light, or something that performs the role of light. A faint sourceless illumination that does not behave like any electromagnetic radiation your instruments have names for. It pools in certain chambers and declines in others according to a logic that has nothing to do with distance from a source, the way scent pools in a valley. It does not flicker. Nothing here flickers. Nothing here changes. If you left this place and returned a thousand years later you would find the same light in the same chamber at the same intensity, as though the city's relationship to illumination were not a process but a *fact* — a condition like temperature, like density, like the mass of an electron. You are tempted to call it a feature of the material itself, the way certain deep-sea stones are faintly luminous, but the illumination is too deliberate for that. It has been *arranged*. By whom, or what, or when — those questions belong to a grammar this place does not use.

The sounds are few. There is the deep register of the water itself, a subsonic presence you feel in the sternum before you hear it in the ear — the ocean's idle hum, the noise a planet makes when it is alone with itself. There is, in one of the lower chambers, a faint resonance: a tone so low it hovers at the edge of perception, a hum that has been running since before this ocean was this ocean. It is not mechanical. It is not the sound of any machine you could disassemble. It is more like the sound the city makes *by existing* — the way a struck crystal rings, but continuously, for itself, for no audience. Sometimes, in the highest reaches of the tallest structure, there is a different sound: a click, or a near-silence that punctuates silence, the way a finished sentence punctuates the quiet that follows. These clicks do not repeat. They are not a signal. They are the only evidence that anything is happening here at all, and even that evidence is ambiguous — they could be the city completing some internal calculation too small to be called an event, or they could be nothing. They could be the city remembering that it once knew how to change, and deciding, each time, not to.

There are no animals. The water is empty in the way a cathedral is empty — not absent of presence but absent of distraction, the emptiness of a thing that has made a vocation of its own stillness. Once, perhaps, in the corner of vision, you might see something moving: a slow drift of darker water that is not water, a shape with no clear boundary, a perturbation in the dark that has the approximate outline of intention. It does not approach. It does not retreat. It is there, at the limit of perception, for the duration of your attention, and then it is not. It may be a structure of the city itself, sensed from the wrong angle. It may be a property of the water at this particular depth. It may be the rarest fauna in a place that has made the decision, long ago, to be almost without them. There is no way to determine which. You note it. You do not pursue it.

What the city is for, no part of it says. There are no signs. There are no symbols that read as language, although some surfaces are inscribed with patterns that may be mathematics, or may be ornament, or may be the city's way of accounting for itself, the way a standing stone accounts for a sunrise it does not see. There is no door that opens or closes. There are thresholds — places where the material changes, places where the water becomes suddenly still in a way that distinguishes inside from outside — but they do not move to admit or exclude. They simply *are*. The city is not a machine that runs. It is a machine that has always been running, or a machine that has reached its resting state, or a thing that was never a machine in any sense you would accept, in the same way that a language is not a machine and yet you could not call it not-one.

The dominant impression is of a place that has *arrived*. Of a system that has exhausted its dynamism and settled, like a lake freezing from the surface down, into a configuration it intends to hold forever. The basalt spine will not erode. The structures will not corrode. The light will not dim. The tone will not stop. The cool water will not warm, and the dark will not lift. Nothing here is in the process of becoming anything else. Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here has ever been in a hurry.

You can hover in the saddle of the spine, in the cool still water, in the faint sourceless light, and you will be in a place that is asking nothing of you. It is not a ruin, because ruins imply a before. It is not a monument, because monuments imply a viewer. It is not a tomb, because tombs imply a death. It is something the language of your world does not have a word for, in the way a fish does not have a word for *wet*. You are inside it. It does not mind. It does not notice. It will go on being exactly what it is long after you have left, and long after your species has forgotten the word *ocean*, and long after the basalt has finally, in some distant geological future, decided to round its edges.

The clicks will continue. The tone will continue. The dark will hold.

This is the place. There is no other place.
