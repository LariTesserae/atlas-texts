# Who Lives Here

## The Operators

They are the heartbeat.

A Cisterns operator is, in body, a person who has been made strange by pressure and light. Their chests are slightly broader than surface norms — the ribcage, over years, accommodating the slow, patient work of breathing dense water at depth. Their hands are calloused in particular places: across the outer palm where the wrench of the riser-capstan rolls, along the fingertips where the phosphor-film is scraped with a stiff blade on cleaning rotation, and at the base of the thumb where the tram-rail trolley handle has worn a permanent smoothness into the skin. They wear their hair short, almost always, because long hair in a sealed helmet is one more thing that can fail. They are pale, in the particular way that people who live under engineered 4500-kelvin light are pale — not sickly, just adapted to a sun that never was.

There are perhaps two hundred and fifty of them. They work in shifts that the surface would consider punishing: fourteen days on, fourteen days up the shaft on the hoist, fourteen days in a surface decompression hotel whose windows confuse them with their excess. The rhythm is older than most of them. Some of the senior operators have done it for thirty years. They know the groan of the Main Wall the way a sailor knows the hum of a specific staysail — by a half-degree change in pitch, by a half-second delay in the next pulse, they can hear a riser pump starting to fail in a chamber they cannot see.

They speak quietly, even on the surface. Their children, born up there, often speak loudly.

## The Biologists

They are younger, mostly. They came for the mowers.

A Cisterns biologist is a person who has accepted that their discipline has been, in some sense, kidnapped by infrastructure. They do not study wilderness. They study a system that someone has built, and yet — this is what keeps them — the system has come alive in ways its builders did not predict. The mowers are the proof. The mowers are not on any blueprint. The mowers are the descendants of a food-stock animal that someone, three generations ago, brought down the shaft in a tank, and which broke free in a chamber that no one remembers, and which found, in the warm effluent plumes above a long-since-replaced smelter, the niche that its lineage had been waiting for.

The biologists love them. They waymark them with subdermal tags that glow faintly under the right wavelength of phosphor. They name them, individually, in a small dry notebook that lives in a waterproof pouch on the biologist's belt. They argue about whether the mowers are still the same species as the original stock, or whether they have become something else, something for which a new word will need to be invented at a conference that none of them particularly want to attend.

The biologists are louder than the operators, but only in their own company. They have a habit of gesturing with both hands when they talk, even when one hand is holding a coffee bulb, which causes minor flooding of the mess modules and is treated with resignation.

## The Maintenance Crews

The largest group, and the most anonymous, and — the operators will admit this after their third drink — the most essential.

A maintenance crew is four people and a tool that is too large to fit through any standard hatch, and so has been cut in half and welded back together in the field. They follow inspection routes that are laminated and color-coded and that branch, like the waterways of a delta, across the entire structure. They scrape phosphor. They torque gantry bolts to a specification that is engraved on the tool, not printed, because printed specifications wash off. They descend into the deep chambers — the ones at six and seven atmospheres, the ones where the gantry ladders are short because almost no one goes down there — and they do their work, and they ascend, and they do not speak much about what they have seen down there, except to note that the silt on the riser collars is heavier this year than last.

They have a vocabulary of small gestures. A raised hand means *do not enter the riser column, the shear is wrong*. A thumb jerked sideways means *your trim is off, you are listing, correct*. A palm pressed flat and rotated slowly means *the current here is not what the chart said, trust the water*. They pass this vocabulary to new crew members in the same way: by gesture, in the field, in the wet, with a tolerance for error that is narrow and unspoken.

They are, almost all of them, the children or grandchildren of original construction workers. They have an attitude toward the place that is neither awe nor contempt. It is closer to the attitude of a person toward a parent they have lived with a long time: they know where the floors creak, they know which joints have begun to weep, they know the exact location of the drip in the East Cell riser collar that has been dripping for forty years and that everyone agrees is, on balance, fine.

## The Children

There are not many. There are never many, in a place that is not designed to make them.

A Cisterns child is, in the first instance, a creature of the pressurized modules — small, loud in the way that all children are loud, unbothered by the hum because to them it is the sound of being alive. They learn to swim before they learn to walk properly, in the small treated pools of the residential modules. They learn to read the current charts before they learn to read the novels the surface sends down for entertainment, because the current charts have pictures and the novels do not, at first, and because the current charts are more useful.

They are afraid of the open chambers at first, in the particular way that small mammals are afraid of large empty spaces. Then they are not. By adolescence, they have usually been down into the deep cells once, on a school trip organized by a biologist who believes that fear of the deep is a habit and not a virtue. By adulthood, most of them have left — they go up the shaft, they see the sky, they are confused by it for months, and then they either come back, or they do not.

Those who come back are, almost always, the ones who were going to come back. The Cisterns does not lose them by accident. It loses them by choice.

## The Mowers

They are not people. But the crews will tell you, after the second drink, that they are the closest thing the Cisterns has to a permanent population.

A mower is the size of a large dog and has too many fins. It is pale, in the way that deep-water animals are pale: not for camouflage, but because pigment is metabolically expensive and there is no reason to make it. Its face, when you look at it from a gantry above, has the quality of a face that has been thinking about something for a long time and is not yet ready to say what. Its eyes are large and dark and have a wet reflective quality that suggests the animal is paying attention, although to what, no one has been able to determine.

It eats the felt of microorganisms that accumulates on platform undersides and in the warm-water plumes above the smelters. It is slow and methodical about this. It moves through a patch of felt the way a person moves through a paragraph — steadily, leaving the small clean rectangle of bare surface behind it, moving on to the next.

It does not fear the lights. It does not fear the gantries. It will look at you. If you stop, on a ladder above it, and watch it, it will watch you back, with the patience of an animal that has been watched many times before and has come to no conclusion about what being watched means.

It is unafraid of the operators. It is wary of the maintenance crews, who sometimes scrape it gently off surfaces they need to work on. It is terrified of the trams, and will press itself flat against the lee of any large structure when the rails begin to sing.

No one has ever successfully bred one in captivity. The biologists are trying. The mowers are not cooperating.

## The Fish, the Wild Ones

There is a difference, in the Cisterns, between a fish that is working and a fish that is wild.

A working fish is silver and dense-fleshed and moves in long patient ribbons through the chambers, herded by acoustic fences that the operators tune like instruments. It eats what the platforms excrete. It does not, by any observable behavior, know that it is being herded. It lives its life. It dies on a rotation, drawn up through a hoist, processed, and sent up the shaft. It has done this for generations. Its lineage has been shaped, by that process, into something that is efficient and uniform and that has, in some indefinable way, lost the quality of surprise.

A wild fish is smaller, weirder, the descendant of escapees or of original stock that was never harvested. It lives in the Lee drifts. It moves in unpredictable bursts. It has color — not much, but some — and a habit of investigating things. When a maintenance crew descends into a deep cell on a routine inspection, the wild fish will sometimes follow the beam of the helmet light for a while, as if curious, before veering off into the dim.

The biologists love the wild fish. The operators tolerate them, because the wild fish are small enough not to interfere with the acoustic fences and because they are, in some way, evidence that the Cisterns is not only a machine. There is something else here, now, that was not put here. There is something else here that has decided to stay.

## The Trams

They are not alive. But they have been here long enough that the crews speak of them with a particular mildness.

A Cisterns tram is a pressurized capsule on rails bolted to the walls of the major arches. It runs on a schedule that is, in principle, fixed, and in practice, responsive — the schedule adjusts itself, by some unexamined combination of operator discretion and unspoken custom, to the rhythm of the place. The senior operators know when a tram will be late by thirty seconds and consider this a meaningful datum. The tram rails sing when a tram is coming. The crews know the note of their own line and can distinguish it, in their sleep, from the note of any other.

The trams are maintained by a single technician who has, by long custom, the right of way through any platform and is addressed, formally, only by her title. She does not mind. She has, by long custom, also been given a small habitat module of her own at the junction of the three main lines, where she can hear, at all hours, the doppler shift of approaching and departing trams, and where she considers this, in some private way, a kind of company.

## The Itself of the Place

And then there is the Cisterns itself, which is not a person and not an animal and not a machine, but which has, by this point, accumulated enough of all three that the crews will sometimes speak of it as if it were.

The Cisterns breathes. The pumps push. The water falls. The walls groan on their schedule and, occasionally, on a different schedule, when something has shifted in a chamber far below. The mowers browse. The fish drift. The children grow. The operators watch the gauges. The biologists take their notes. The maintenance crews follow their routes. The tram technician listens to her rails.

It is, in the way that a coral reef or a city or a long marriage is, an organism that has been built out of many smaller organisms and many smaller machines and that has, in the building, become a thing that is not any of them and is not their sum, and that has a life of its own, and that will, if the operators do their work, continue to have one for a long time yet.

It is young. It is not yet worn smooth. The phosphor strips still have to be scraped. The riser collars still weep in places they should not. The deep chambers are still deep enough that almost no one goes there, and the things that live there still have eyes for the color of old glass.

It is, the crews will tell you, on a quiet evening in a pressurized module, with the low groan of the Main Wall coming up through the deck at the expected hour — it is, on balance, fine.
