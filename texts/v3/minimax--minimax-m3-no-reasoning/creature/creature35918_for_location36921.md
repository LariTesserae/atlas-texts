The people are short in the way that people are short when they have been leaning, for generations, against low ceilings of fog. They have learned to fold themselves into the city the way the city has folded itself into the valley — not all at once, but in stages, with many small corrections. They move along the boardwalks with the unhurried concentration of people who have stopped expecting the next plank to behave as the last one did. They know the bridges by their complaints.

Their hands are too large. This is the first thing a visitor notices, and the second, and it never quite resolves into ordinary. The hands are not working hands exactly — they are not calloused in the way you would expect, given the labour of the place. They are soft, in fact, almost doughy, and pale in the way that things are pale that have been kept warm by other things for a long time. The nails are worn to nothing. Nobody remembers why. There is a theory, repeated in the alleys in winter when the breath is visible and conversation has nowhere better to go, that the hands do most of the city's thinking; that the people themselves — up around the small eyes and the tea-coloured faces — are mostly a kind of afterthought the hands have agreed to drag around with them.

This is not literally true. But it is the kind of thing that gets said.

The hands rebuild the boardwalks in the spring, after the rivers have changed their minds. They move the low row of houses across town when a new channel appears — a whole terrace of dwellings picked up bodily on the shoulders of a dozen people, the tenants carrying their porridge and their chairs ahead of them down the street, the kettle still on the boil. The hands set the bricks in the bricked-up doors in the alley behind the moss-pressing works. The hands, sometimes, take the bricks out again. Nobody asks about this. It happens slowly enough that it isn't really noticed.

The faces above the hands are the colour of weak tea. The eyes are small. The hair is dark and grows in a way that suggests it has given up on any particular arrangement. People wear a lot of layers — not for warmth exactly, although the warmth is welcome, but because layers can be peeled off and put on as the city's mood shifts, and the city's mood shifts often. There is a particular kind of knitted cap, tall and soft, that almost everyone wears; it has a small folded brim and a tassel that never seems to get wet, even when everything else is sweating.

Children are quieter than you would expect. They grow up watching the moss do its slow takeover of the rooftops, and they acquire a kind of mossy patience themselves. They learn early which doors in the alley are bricked up and which of those bricks are the ones with moss growing through them. They know that the carved hand on the smallest door is at a height that they will, for about a year and a half, be exactly the right size to reach. They touch it. They don't talk about it.

Old people, by contrast, talk about everything. They sit on the platforms above the channels with their feet dangling and their long hands folded over their knees, and they will tell you, if you sit down beside them, the entire history of the city's negotiations with its ground. They will tell you about the year the eastern quarter had to be moved twice. They will tell you about the house that fell into the river in '83 and was not mourned, because the people inside it had already moved out in '79 when they felt the floor begin to tilt. They will tell you about the bridge — the one that no longer touches the bank — as though the bridge were a relative. *It's a little further north this year*, they will say. *It will be at the warehouse by Christmas.*

The otters are not otters. They are something that became otters, or that otters became, after a long time spent in the channels. Their faces are too round. Their eyes are set a fraction too low, and they have, around the mouth, a kind of patient smirk that no otter has ever had. They are the length of an arm, and they are soft in a way that suggests they have been eating well for many generations. They move along the rails of the boardwalks with the unhurried ease of creatures who consider themselves the senior inhabitants and have documents, somewhere, to prove it. They take a fish now and then from the channels. They do not chase them. The fish simply rise to meet them, the way a cat rises to meet the hand that has fed it a thousand times.

The people do not mind the otters. The otters do not mind the people. There is an understanding, long-standing, that the channels belong to no one in particular and to the otters most of all. From time to time an otter will be seen carrying, in its mouth, a small object that does not belong to it — a glove, a wooden spoon, a child's carved boat. These objects turn up, weeks later, on the doorstep of the person they belonged to. This is considered a courtesy on the otter's part, and is reciprocated with bowls of fish-headed soup set out on the lowest step of the boardwalks on cold evenings.

The birds came from a long way. Nobody is entirely sure where. They are tern-coloured, but grey where terns are white, and they have black tips to their wings that look, when they wheel against the fog, as though someone has dipped the ends of their feathers in ink. They are smaller than you would expect from the noise they make. Their cries are the loudest thing in the valley, and they are not loud — they are simply the highest thing, the thing the fog cannot quite swallow, and so they carry. They roost on the warmer roofs in long rows, shoulder to shoulder the way the buildings are shoulder to shoulder, and they pick through the moss for whatever it is they pick through moss for. Once or twice a day, without warning, the whole flock lifts off at once, as if the roof had been struck from underneath, and circles the city in a slow gyre that rises, by imperceptible degrees, into the fog and is gone. Then, ten minutes later, it settles again, and the rooftops resume.

The fish are pale and almost translucent, the length of a finger, and they drift in the channels in slow columns that go nowhere in particular. They eat whatever the otters do not finish, and whatever the factories tip into the water when nobody is looking. They do not seem to mind. They are very numerous and very patient. They are, in their way, the most constant thing in the valley — more constant than the rivers, which abandon their channels overnight; more constant than the houses, which are picked up and moved; more constant than the people, who come and go. The fish were here before the stilts. The fish will be here after.

The moss is the senior partner. This is not said aloud, but it is understood. The moss was on the rooftops before the rooftops were rooftops — it was on the rocks, on the boards, on the backs of the stilts when the stilts were first sunk. It spread without asking because nobody had thought to ask it not to. It was given the rooftops as a courtesy, or perhaps as a concession, and it accepted in the same spirit, and now the arrangement is too old to be renegotiated. The moss is harvested, in late summer, by people with long knives and longer patience, and it is taken down into the moss-pressing works, where it is reduced, slowly, into a dense black fuel that smells of vinegar and burnt sugar. The boilers take this fuel. The boilers breathe. The breath keeps the city warm. The warmth keeps the moss alive on the rooftops. The cycle is so old that the moss itself seems to have forgotten that it is being eaten.

There are stranger things. There is a chimney in the old quarter that does not smoke from the top but from a hole halfway up, and the smoke comes out in slow pulses, like the chest of something that has fallen asleep standing up. People do not look at it directly. They glance at it the way you glance at a sleeping animal — to check that it is still breathing, and not to wake it.

There is a bridge that has been sliding north, very slowly, for as long as anyone can remember. It floats a few inches above the water. It has not touched the western bank in fifty years. By Christmas it will be at the warehouse. By next spring it will be somewhere no one has been yet. People have started referring to it as *the bridge*, the way you might refer to a relative. A small boy was once sent out onto it with a rope, and he came back twenty minutes later having not moved at all, relative to the bridge. He said it was very quiet out there. He said he could hear, faintly, the sound of something breathing under the water.

There is a wall in the alley behind the moss-pressing works with six doors of different sizes set into it, all of them bricked shut. Three of the bricks have moss growing through them. A child's hand has been carved into the smallest door, palm open, at a height a child could reach but an adult would have to kneel for. The bricks are repointed every few years. The carving is not. Nobody knows who carved it. Children are told that the hand will move, one day, if they put theirs against it at the right hour, but the right hour is never specified. Children put their hands against it anyway. They stand there for as long as they can bear to. Then they go home, and they do not talk about what they felt, or didn't feel, or whether the two were the same.
