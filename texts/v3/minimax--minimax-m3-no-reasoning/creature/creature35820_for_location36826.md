# The Lives Among the Green Masonry

What lives in the conservatory is, first of all, the wood. That has to be said before anything else, because the wood is older than the living that has moved into it, and will be here after. The wood lives in the way a city is said to live—collectively, slowly, with a metabolism so distributed that no single trunk accounts for it. The trunks are the body; the mycelium threading between them, through the root-sea underfoot, through the seams where bark meets bark, is the nervous system no one ever designed. Water rises, sugar descends, chemical letters pass in solvents too thin to see. The forest as a whole is one organism with a thousand mouths, and the town is a single organ it has grown, the way a tree grows a burl in response to a wound that became, in time, something the tree prefers to keep.

## The Inhabitants, Such As They Are

To call them *people* is to commit to a category they may not recognize. You glimpse them in the late afternoons, crossing a rooftop meadow in long cloaks the color of wet bark, or you hear them behind a curved trunk-wall, two voices in low conversation, one giving way to the other in a rhythm that has the cadence of long acquaintance. They are smaller than you expect from the scale of the buildings. Their hands are calloused in a way that suggests work but not labor—work in the sense of tending, of coaxing, of placing one thing against another and waiting to see what the wood will do. They move without hurry. A figure will step from a doorway into a path and stand for a long moment, palm against a trunk, as if listening to something the trunk is saying. Then the hand withdraws, and the figure goes on.

They keep themselves to the inner volumes. From outside, the dwellings are largely indistinguishable from the forest that holds them; the thatch roofs are meadows in their own right, the moss-walls are continuous with the mosses of the surrounding bark, the doorways are healed scars that open at a touch of someone who knows the grain. Inside, where the light is pine-knot fat and clay, the air is several degrees warmer than the fog outside and carries the smell of damp wood, smoked sap, dried herbs hanging in bundles from a ceiling-beam that is itself a living branch. They sleep there. They eat there. They have children there, though the children are seen even less than the adults—a small shape darting across a roof-garden and gone, or a voice pitched higher than the others, called in from somewhere deep in the trunk-cluster, answered with a low murmur that sends the child quiet.

Their faces, when you catch one, are not closed against you so much as *unfurnished*—the features present but not arranged for you, the eyes taking you in with a curiosity that does not extend to wanting to be known in return. They look at you the way a heron looks at a passing canoe: registering your presence, assigning it a weight, returning to whatever the heron was doing before. You are a weather event. You will pass through.

## The Smaller Tenants

Below and around the people, in the seams and the litter and the slow rain that never quite stops, the more numerous lives go on. Springtails no larger than a grain of pepper move through the moss in tides you can see only by kneeling and waiting—pale bodies in the hundreds, gathering on the wetter bark, retreating into the drier seams. A red-and-black millipede travels a root with the unhurried purpose of a train on a single track. Beetles, dozens of kinds, work the rotting wood: some of them excavators, opening galleries that will be inherited by spiders and rove-beetles and the small parasitic wasps that hunt them; some of them predators themselves, with the long curved jaws of things that prise other things from their burrows. There is a click-beetle somewhere that you do not see but hear—a small *plink* from inside a log, then a moment of nothing, then the *plink* again, a metronome patient as geology.

Salamanders live in the wetter crevices, where the bark weeps most heavily. You find them by torchlight if you find them at all: a small porcelain shape tucked into the seam, breathing through its skin, eyes reflecting the flame back as two points of dull gold. They are cold the way the stone under the moss is cold; they have been this cold for so long that they have made the cold into a home. A single salamander may live in the same seam for twenty years, emerging on the warmest nights to feed on the springtails, returning to the same crevice when the temperature drops. The forest, which is patient with most things, is patient with these.

A spider—*Cyclosa*, perhaps, or something local and unnamed—has strung a single line between two root-arcs at face height on the path, and rebuilt its web every morning for what looks like years. The web is a fractal of debris: the husks of eaten things, a flake of bark, a seed-tuft caught and incorporated, the spider itself sitting at the center disguised as one more piece of litter. By afternoon the web is half-destroyed by larger passersby; by morning it is whole again. The spider does not appear to learn anything from this.

## The Canopy Lives

Above the path, in the lower branches where the trunks begin to separate into individual stems, the air is colder and more mobile, and the lives there are different. Winter wrens are the most often heard and the least often seen—a small chain of chittering notes that seems to come from everywhere at once because the wren is small and the forest is dense and the sound bounces. A hermit thrush sings at dusk, the spiral of its song coming down through the branches in the same pale shafts the light comes down in, so that you hear the song as if it were a thing the light had picked up on its way down and dropped at your feet. There are chickadees in flocks of six or eight, working the canopy in long rolling tides, calling to one another in the bright *dee-dee-dee* that means *I am here, follow me, the food is this way*.

Higher still, where the trunks thin into the upper branches and the canopy closes over them in a thick weave of needle and twig, the wind begins to be audible again, and the light begins to mean something. A red squirrel lives somewhere up there—you see its tracks on a mossy root, the small paired prints with the long tail-drag between them—but you do not see the squirrel, which has its own architecture of leaf-nests up in the crotch of a high fork and watches you from a branch with a stillness that puts even the people to shame. A goshawk may hunt here in the deep of winter, when the small birds are easier to take. You will not see the goshawk either, only, one morning, a single gray-brown feather pinned in the snow at the edge of the path, and the silence afterward.

## The Edge Lives

At the upper limit, where the trees give up and the stone begins, a different community holds the rocks. Pikas, if you listen: the high piping whistle that seems to come from inside the rock itself, because the pika is inside the rock, in a talus chamber stacked with dried alpine plants cut and laid out to cure for winter. You never see the pika; you see, at the mouth of a crevice, a small neat pile of sticks and leaves, and you hear the whistle, and you move on. A weasel hunts among the rocks, longer and lower than the pikas expect. Snow buntings pass through in October on their way down, and in April on their way back, and in the windows between there is nothing on the rocks but the wind and a few lichens growing so slowly that a year of watching might give you no evidence of change.

A mountain goat, if you are very lucky, appears once in a long while against the sky at the top of the scree above the treeline, picking its way on hooves that are, in their own way, the local answer to the same problem the town answers below—how to live in a place that does not really want you, and to do it gracefully.

## The Process Lives

What lives here, ultimately, is a negotiation that has been running for a very long time and has not been resolved. The forest grows and the forest takes back, and the people grow with the forest, and are taken back by the forest, and the fungi of the rotting halls are themselves taken back into the trunks that will grow from their feeding, and the trunks will be cut into doorways and the doorways will be healed over and the healed scars will be cut again. The process is what lives here. The inhabitants are its current expressions. In another hundred years the town will have a different shape, in a thousand it will have another shape again, and in ten thousand the forest will have eaten the town back into itself entirely, or the town will have eaten the forest into a shape only the town can recognize, and there will be no one to remember which of those two things happened, because no one remembers now.

What you carry out with you, into the open air at the edge of the trees, is the knowledge that the place you visited was not a place but an ongoing argument between two kinds of patience—the patience of wood, which is patient because it has time, and the patience of people, who are patient because they have decided to be. The argument has no winner. That is why it has gone on so long.
