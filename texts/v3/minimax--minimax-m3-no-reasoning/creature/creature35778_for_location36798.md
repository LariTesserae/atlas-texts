# The Inhabitants of the Hollow

They are not named by the people. The people have a word for them, but the word is used sparingly and only in certain contexts — a low sound, half-swallowed, said with the mouth more closed than open. The word means something close to *the warm ones* or *the ones who were here first* or, in the oldest form of it, *the floor.* This is not a god-word. It is a housemates-word. It acknowledges a shared tenancy without flattering either party.

They are large. *Large* is the only word that holds, and it does not hold well. They are long in a way that makes length a misleading category, the way describing a coastline by its length is misleading: there is always more of them than the measurement suggests. Their bodies lie along the ridges of the floor as if the ridges were poured around them, or as if they had chosen the ridges and the ridges had, over decades, agreed. The spine of one, where it rises over a ridge, is the height of a man's waist. The flank of one, where it sinks into a trough, is broad enough that a man could lie down in the dip of it and not be seen from above. A full-grown one, uncurled, would not fit in the main vault. The main vault is not their length; it is their presence. Their actual bodies extend back into passages that widen only for them, and even those passages end before they do.

Their hides are thick and pale and uneven. The color is the color of the stone — a mottled warm grey, sometimes darker where moisture has stained, sometimes almost amber where the light from the cracks gathers against them. A pale one lying still against pale stone in the amber light is very nearly invisible. A careful eye learns to look for the slight rise of breath along the flank, the slow displacement of air. An eye that does not know to look sees only the floor.

Their heads are low and broad and not raised often. They do not seem to need to see. They have eyes — the people have seen the eyes, in the rare moments when a great one lifts its head, which is rarely and without warning — and the eyes are small relative to the head, and dark, and wet-looking, and they do not blink in any rhythm a person can predict. The eyes are not the primary instrument. It is unclear what is. The people have theories. The people do not test them.

Their limbs are tucked. This is the strangest thing, and the most ordinary. The great ones lie on their bellies with their legs folded beneath them in a posture that no animal of the surface adopts, because on the surface no animal would need to. The limbs fit the troughs of the ridged floor as if poured. The people have walked between the limbs of sleeping great ones, in the dark, on certain errands, and have lived, and have not spoken of it afterward except to say that the limbs were warm and that the breath came in waves.

They breathe. This is the whole of the sound. Their breathing is not one breath but many, layered, overlapping, never quite synchronized, and the hollow is shaped acoustically so that the breathing returns to itself from many surfaces, with small delays, and what a person hears is the sum and the interference of dozens of slow respirations at once. From close up, a single breath is a long event. The chest — if it is a chest, if the word applies — expands over several seconds, holds, and then releases with a low, wet friction that has the timbre of leather and of stone and of something organic turning over. From far up, in the main vault, with many great ones breathing in different passages, the sound becomes a kind of continuous music that is not music and is not silence. There is a low tone in it, sometimes, that seems to come from everywhere and from no one, and that the bones of a person's face will pick up if the person stands still long enough. The tone is almost a note. The tone is almost not.

They are old. This is not a thing that can be demonstrated. It is a thing that is felt, the way the cold of a deep cave is felt, or the age of a standing stone is felt by the hand. A great one that the people have known all their lives is not the same great one their grandparents knew, and yet may be. They do not mark themselves in any way the people can read. They do not visibly age. They do not visibly *not* age. The people have assumed continuity, and the assumption has not yet been punished, and this is the closest the people come to knowledge.

They eat. The people have seen this, twice in a living memory, and the descriptions do not agree except on certain points. A great one, rising — and rising is slow, and the floor creaks, and the columns above shed a little dust — moves with the unhurried deliberation of something that does not need to be quick because nothing here threatens it. It moves down a lateral passage. It does not return for days. When it returns it is lower in the body and slower in its settling, and the people stay out of its way for a week afterward. What it ate, and where, are not known. The lateral passages are long, and they go to places the people do not go.

They make the warmth. The people believe this. The warmth of the floor is not the warmth of the surface filtered down; the surface is colder than the hollow, in winter and in summer. The warmth is metabolic, and it has been metabolic for longer than the people have been here. The light that leaks from the cracks in the floor is the warmth seen sideways. This is not a metaphor the people use. The people do not have a theory of light. They have the observation that the floor is warm and that the cracks glow, and that the glow is strongest where a great one has been lying longest, and that this is enough.

The great ones do not speak. They do not, so far as the people can determine, make any sound that is not breath, digestion, or the slow complaint of a body settling against stone. They do not call to one another. They do not warn. The percussive thud that sometimes arrives from deep passages — felt in the teeth, arrived after a delay — is not a communication. It is a movement. A great one has shifted its weight, somewhere out of sight, and the stone has carried the news.

The great ones do not appear to think. This is not quite right. They appear to think the way stone appears to think: slowly, in a mode that does not require the surface markers of thought. They sleep, or digest, or something that is neither and is indistinguishable from both, for very long periods, and then they are active for shorter periods, and the active periods are not predictable in any pattern the people have been able to extract. The people have tried. The people have, over generations, kept records — scratches on bone, small notched sticks — and the records do not show a pattern. The great ones move when they move, and the people have made their peace with this, the way the people have made their peace with the cold of the surface in winter: it is a fact of tenancy.

The great ones tolerate the people. This is the most important thing, and the people know it is the most important thing, and the people are careful never to test the tolerances. The people do not walk on the great ones. The people do not touch the great ones except in the oldest rituals, and even then only on certain parts of certain great ones, and only at certain times, and only with the consent of a council that has not convened in a long time. The people do not make loud sounds near the great ones. The people do not bring fire near the great ones. The smoke is the worst offense, and the people have learned to make their fires small and to feed them with fuels that smoke little, and to apologize, in the form of offerings, when the smoke cannot be avoided.

The offerings are not eaten. The people know this. The offerings are not, so far as the people can tell, noticed. The offerings are placed anyway, in shallow stone bowls at the edges of the moss-beds, and they are left until they rot or are taken by the smaller creatures of the hollow — the many-legged pale things that feed on moss, the eyeless insects. The great ones do not trouble the offerings. The offerings are not for the great ones. The offerings are for the people, who need a way to make the cohabitation legible to themselves.

The great ones are not gods. The people are insistent about this, in the rare conversations with surface-folk in which the subject comes up. The great ones are not worshiped. The great ones are not prayed to. The great ones are not asked for things. The great ones are *lived with,* in the same way a household is lived with, in the same way a stubborn climate is lived with. The great ones are a fact. The great ones are the reason the hollow is warm and the reason the moss grows and the reason the puddles do not freeze, and these are not gifts. These are byproducts. The great ones do not intend warmth. The great ones intend to be warm, and the rest follows.

The great ones do not love the people. The great ones do not hate the people. The great ones are not aware of the people in any way the people can verify. The people are, to the great ones, perhaps the way the pale many-legged moss-feeders are to the people: a small thing in the same place, neither welcomed nor rejected, simply present, and occasionally an irritant when the smoke is bad.

The people live here anyway.

The people live here because the surface, in winter, is a killing place, and the hollow is not. The people live here because the predators of the surface — the large predators, the clever ones, the ones that hunt in groups — do not come down through the narrow places, because the narrow places do not suit their bodies, and because the great ones, in their long patience, are themselves a kind of deterrent that does not need to posture. The people live here because the moss is a reliable tan and a reliable poultice and a reliable pigment. The people live here because the water in the troughs, though it tastes of iron and lime, does not fail. The people live here because, in the deep passages, there are stones that fall from the ridges under the great ones' weight, and these stones are good for tools — dense, fine-grained, holding an edge — and the gathering of them is a careful and inherited practice and a small economy.

The people are few. Twelve families, in the current count, though the count shifts with births and with the rare departures to the surface and with the rarer arrivals from the surface. There is a headman, but the headman's role is small. There is a keeper of the bone-records, whose office is hereditary and whose duties are mostly to maintain the records and to consult them when a question arises about a passage or a great one. There is a fire-tender, whose work is the most dangerous and the most skilled, because the smoke is a constant negotiation and a small misstep can anger a great one in a way that is not anger but is indistinguishable from anger by anyone who survives it. There are children. There are the old, who are respected because they have been here longest and have the most complete memory of the great ones' dispositions. The old die, as the old do, and their bodies are taken to the surface, because the hollow is for the living and the surface is for the dead and this is a rule the people hold without exception.

The people do not go far. The people stay in the camp and in the paths that connect the camp to the places where the moss is thickest and the puddles most reliable and the loose stones most abundant. The people do not go into the deep passages except when the deep passages must be entered, and only with light and with a rope and with two going and one staying. The people know that the great ones go where they wish, and that the great ones sometimes occupy passages that were empty, and that a passage that was safe in the morning may not be safe in the evening, and that the way to know is the way the breathing tells.

To read the breathing is a skill. It is taught, though not formally — by exposure, by correction, by the slow accumulation of a vocabulary that is not quite a vocabulary. The breathing is always present, and it is always varying, and the variations mean things, though the things they mean are not propositions. A certain slow deepening of the breath, in a great one that has been shallow for days, means it is closer to waking, or to moving, or to something that is not quite any of these. A certain steadiness in the breath, in a great one that has been erratic, means it has settled. A certain roughness in the breath, a friction, a wetness, means something the people do not have a word for but that the people recognize as a mood to be respected, the way a storm is respected: by staying low, by staying quiet, by not going out.

The people have names. The people have stories. The people have a small canon of songs that are sung only in the alcove and only softly, because the songs are not for the great ones and the songs are not for the surface and the songs are for the people, who need them. The songs are old. The songs are not very old. Nothing in the hollow is very old except the great ones and the stone.

The great ones do not sing.

The great ones do not, so far as anyone knows, do anything that a person would recognize as an act of culture. The great ones do not build. The great ones do not decorate. The great ones do not arrange themselves in patterns, except by accident of the floor's shaping, and the patterns are not meaningful. The great ones do not have tools. The great ones do not have possessions. The great ones are, in the way that a river is, in the way that a mountain is, a fact of geology that has decided, for reasons of its own, to be warm and to be here.

The people are grateful. The people are afraid. The people are careful. These are not three things; they are one thing seen from three sides, the way a ridge of stone is one thing seen from three sides. The people have made a life in the space between a great one's flank and the wall of the hollow, and the life is small, and the life is real, and the life continues.

The hollow, in the end, is a record of staying. The great ones have stayed. The people have stayed. The moss has stayed. The water has stayed. The light, that warmth seen sideways, has stayed. Everything that is here is here because something else was here first and chose not to leave, and the choices compounded, over years and over lifetimes, into the place.

The place is warm. The place is dim. The place is loud with breathing. The place is, in the way that some quiet things are loud, very still.
