The valley does not have inhabitants in the way most valleys do. It has tenants — life forms that have negotiated their existence with a substrate that is itself alive, and that does not always remember to make room.

The Felt, of course. To call the Felt a thing that lives in the valley is to misstate the relationship. The Felt is what the valley has become, in the same way that a coral reef is what a stretch of sea floor has become. It is the valley's outer garment and its inner weather and, in some poorly understood sense, its memory.

It is old. Not as old as the rock beneath — that is older by a factor of a billion — but old in the way that matters for living systems. The mycelial threads that lace through its lower strata have been continuous, by the most conservative estimates, since the glaciers withdrew. The same network, perhaps, that first colonized the till when the ice pulled back. This would make the Felt among the oldest continuously living organisms on the continent, which is not a claim anyone has made because no one has come here to make claims.

The Felt is not a single organism. It is a federation — fungal hyphae braided through root tips, root tips threaded through hyphae, both trading sugars and minerals and, as far as anyone can tell, chemical signals whose grammar has not been deciphered. The krummholz pines are the most visible members of this federation, and they are not really members so much as anchors. Their gnarled trunks hold the mat in place against the wind, and in exchange the Felt feeds them through the long winter when their needles cannot photosynthesize. The pines may live four hundred years. The fungi that feed them may live, in some continuous sense, four thousand. The boundary between them is administrative.

In midsummer, when the Felt briefly remembers what sunlight is for, it produces its visible fruits. Not just the flowers of diapensia and bearberry, though those come — the diapensia blooms for one week in late August, a sheet of blue-white that lies over the upper mats like a fall of paper, and then it is gone, and the rest of the year the plant exists only as a pressed-looking rosette of dark leaves. The fungi fruit too. Mushrooms appear in the Felt's deeper hollows, sometimes overnight, sometimes after a rain that the rest of the valley has not seen. They are small — a centimeter across, two at most — and they are colors that do not appear elsewhere in the visible landscape. Sulfur yellow. The pale bruised purple of a forgotten plum. A green so dark it is almost black. They are not edible by anything that lives here, because nothing that lives here eats fungi the way we understand eating. The fungi are eaten by the fungi. The Felt digests itself in slow installments, and the mushrooms are the digesting.

Lichens crust every available surface. The source mentions three hundred colors, which is an underestimate. The lichenologist who first counted them gave up somewhere in the upper two hundreds and revised the estimate downward out of professional embarrassment, and then upward again because the orange ones on the south-facing rocks kept presenting new shades. The lichens are the slowest members of the Felt. Some of the crustose forms on the high boulders have been there, by lichenometric dating, since before the mining era. They watched the prospectors arrive. They are watching still. They will outlast whatever is watching them.

The small fauna is sparse in the way things are sparse at the edge of what is survivable. A vole, perhaps. *Microtus*, or something close to it — a small dark herbivore that lives in the Felt's deeper runs and emerges only when the seeps are flowing in midsummer. The vole does not have a name. It is the size of a child's fist, brown-black, with fur so dense it feels like touching a brush, and it moves through the tunnels of the upper Felt with a quickness that contradicts the slowness of everything else. It eats the cambium of the berry bushes. It drinks from the seeps. It has been seen, on rare occasions, at the edges of the geothermal patch, where the grass is green enough to make the eyes ache, and there is something unsettling about seeing it there — a small warm shape in the middle of a violent green that should not exist at this altitude, at this latitude, in this world.

There are reports of other voles. A paler form, sometimes, near the steam vents. Larger, with a different gait. The reports are unverified.

There are birds. Most of them are passing through, blown off a migration route by a storm they will not survive if they land in the wrong place. They land in the Felt, and they rest, and most leave within a day. A few do not leave. The Felt absorbs them. Their feathers, in time, become part of the stratigraphy — a thin line of keratin somewhere in the middle layers, a horizon of birds. The lichen will grow over them too.

The invertebrates are the true residents, if anyone is. Springtails in the Felt's upper centimeter, in numbers that would be astronomical if anyone had counted. Mites, smaller than the period at the end of this sentence. Tardigrades, which are not residents so much as inhabitants of the Felt's most stable microhabitats — the places where temperature and humidity have remained within their tolerance for so long that evolution has stopped bothering with them. Nematodes. Rotifers. Things that have not been named. The Felt is, in its lower layers, an entire dark biosphere, an economy of the very small operating on timescales for which we do not have words.

Then the geothermal patch.

The place where the steam comes up is its own small ecology. The Felt is different there — the ferns, the violent green, the sense that something is being fed that is not fed elsewhere. The ferns are not the only thing. There are thermophilic bacteria in the soil around the vents, organisms that thrive at temperatures that would cook their cousins in the surrounding Felt. There are specialized fungi that have not been collected, that exist only in the steam-heated earth, that may be unique to this valley. There are insects that do not appear elsewhere — small flies, a kind of mite that runs on the surface of the wet rocks near the vent. They are not visitors. They are residents of a habitat that should not exist at this elevation, on this continent, and they have made their peace with it.

It is in the geothermal patch that one is most likely to see something that does not quite fit. A moth, once, on a rock near a fumarole, with wings patterned in a way that no field guide recognizes. A spider, twice, in a web strung between two ferns that should not be growing. These are not mysteries, exactly. They are the small perplexities of a place where the rules have been bent slightly out of true, and where life has found the cracks.

The deeper valley — the worked ground, the buried shafts, the machinery — is its own question.

What lives in the mine is not the same as what lives in the Felt, but the two are not unrelated. The Felt is slowly consuming the mine from above. The mine is slowly exhaling its contents into the Felt from below. In the warm dark of the deeper workings there are bacteria that live on sulfide ores and excrete sulfuric acid. There are fungi that tolerate the dark and the heat and the strange mineral chemistry. There are, in the deepest flooded stopes, things that may be invertebrates or may be the shadows of invertebrates, moving with a slowness that makes the voles look hurried.

And the machinery.

The winch in the lower adit, turning once an hour, winding nothing up and nothing down. The stamp mill, hammering rock that no one is feeding it. The lights in the bunkhouse, electric, somehow, flickering on at dusk and off at dawn.

One could describe all of this as mechanical failure, or as the persistence of a process that no longer has a function. The boiler is fed by the geothermal heat. The winch has not broken because it has not been asked to do anything beyond what it is doing. The stamp mill crushes because crushing is what it does, and it has not been told to stop. The lights in the bunkhouse — the explanation is harder.

It is possible that the lights are powered by the same geothermal source that heats the boiler, and that some timer or sensor, set long ago by someone who is not coming back, continues to operate. It is possible that the lights are powered by something stranger. The lights cast no shadows, which is a property that ordinary electric lights do not have, and which is sometimes noted and sometimes not by the people who come here — though almost no one comes here, and the few who do are usually too disoriented by the Felt and the altitude and the sound of the stamp mill to remark on such subtleties.

What is in the bunkhouse, when the lights are on, is not visible through the moss-grown walls. The windows are dark. The door, if there is a door, has not been opened in a hundred years. The lights are on. There is nothing else to say about this that is not speculation.

What can be said is that the valley has accepted the mine as part of its substrate. The Felt grows over the foundations. The mycelium threads through the rotted timber. The cables of the tramway have been incorporated into the lower Felt as a kind of metallic stratum, a layer of worked metal that the next lichenologist will date to the early industrial period and wonder about. The machinery, in its slow and partial way, has become a kind of geological feature — a process rather than an object, a habit of the rock, a tic of the valley's metabolism. The winch turns because turning is what the valley does at that depth. The stamp mill hammers because hammering is what the valley does at that depth. The lights come on because coming on is what the valley does at dusk.

One could leave it there.

Or one could note that something in the bunkhouse is waiting, has been waiting since the prospectors left, and has not yet finished what it began. The lights come on. No one is inside. The valley holds its breath between one hammering of the mill and the next. The steam rises from the collapsed stope and dissolves into the gray. A vole, somewhere in the Felt, pauses and listens, and resumes its quick dark work among the roots.

The valley has not yet decided what it is.
