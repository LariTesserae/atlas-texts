# What Lives in the Basin

Not everything that lives in the basin can be called a creature. Some of it would not recognize the word. But the basin is not empty. It is populous in a way that does not announce itself. You have to learn to see what lives here, the way you learn to see the bottom of a deep, slow stream.

Let me tell you what I have come to know.

---

There is a woman in the basin.

I do not mean she is there the way a person is in a room — upright, with intent, with a task. She is there the way the water is there. She has been there long enough that her edges have blurred against the moss. If you were to stand at the rim and look down, you might not see her. You might see a dark shape among the gnarled shrubs in the middle distance, and think it was a trunk, or a stone, or a trick of the light. If you climbed down into the bowl and walked toward her, you would see, slowly, that she was a woman, and you would be surprised, and the surprise would be the wrong response. She has been there for a long time. You are the one who is new.

She is very old. Not in the way of a bent grandmother with a cane — not frail, not tottering, not white. Old in the way the mortar-stone is old. Old in the way of having been worked and weathered and left and worked-on-by-weather until her shape now is as much a result of moss and frost as of hands. Her skin has the soft, dark, lichenous tone of the wet stone. Her hair is the color of broom moss and falls in strands so fine they could be the threads of water that come through the rim. She is seated at the foot of one of the squat trees, against the trunk, in a place where the water gathers along one of its patient lines and the moss is deepest. She has been seated there, I think, for a very long time. I think she has been seated there since the last hand set down the last small stone among the mosses and walked out of the basin and did not return.

She is not dead. You would know this if you came close, because she breathes, and the basin responds to her breath — a slow, almost inaudible expansion and contraction of the air, like the lung of a very large, very still animal. The ferns near her move a quarter-inch on her inhale and settle on her exhale. The water-sounds dip and rise with her. The thin sheets of water running over moss hesitate, very slightly, at the top of her breath, and resume.

She is not asleep. Her eyes are open. They are not the eyes of someone who is looking for anything, or watching for anything, or waiting for anything. They are open the way a pool of water is open. They receive the light. They give back a dim, green, patient gleam. They are not unfriendly. They are not friendly. They are the eyes of someone who has finished, or who has been finished, or who has been working, slowly, for a very long time, on something that has no visible progress.

She does not speak. I do not think she has spoken in a long time. Her mouth is closed, and the skin around it has the small, downward-set look of a mouth that has held the same shape for years. But she is not sealed. Sometimes — and this is one of the small strangenesses — she opens her mouth slightly, and a sound comes out that is not quite a sound. A low note. A hum that is lower than the ear can easily catch, that you feel in the soles of your feet, that the water seems to catch and carry. It lasts a few seconds. Then her mouth closes, and the basin is quiet again, and a drip falls from the lip of a stone, as if the hum had been holding the drip back, and the drip had been waiting.

She does not eat. She does not drink. The water that runs past her feet is enough, or she has not needed enough for a long time, or she is a kind of creature that does not need. Her body is thin, but it does not look starved. It looks reduced. Like a thing from which everything nonessential has been slowly removed, until what is left is the minimum of what is required for the shape to still be a woman.

She does not move much. But she is not still. There is a constant, small motion in her — a tremor, almost, that runs through her in slow waves, the way a tremor runs through a leaf in a wind too faint to feel. It is not the trembling of the plant with the red veins. Hers is slower, more deliberate, as if she is slowly, continuously, very slightly rearranging herself. The woman in the basin is in the act of becoming the basin, and the basin is in the act of becoming the woman, and the tremor is the place where the two meet.

---

The trees are not really trees. They are her thoughts, the ones she has had most often. You can tell which ones by how dark and gnarled they are, by how settled into the moss. There are nine of them. Some of them she has been thinking for a long time. Some of them she has been thinking only since the last hand set down the last small stone. They stand in the middle distance of the floor, spaced as if they had agreed. They are spaced as she agreed, once, a long time ago, to think them.

The ferns are her arguments with herself. They rise in pale green fists, taller than the ground-thing, and they do not crowd, but they are emphatic, the way a point someone is making to themselves can be emphatic. They are not many. She has had only a few arguments here. They have not been resolved. They have been growing, slowly, for years, and they do not uncurl. They stay in fist. They are waiting to land.

The ground-thing with the small white flowers that never open is a question she has been holding, year after year, plump and patient, that she has not yet asked, that she may never ask, that she holds in bud because asking it would change something, and she is not yet ready for the change, or she has decided not to be ready for the change, or the change is the question itself, and she is the bud.

The mosses are the surface of her attention. They cover nearly everything. They are not one thing. They are three, at least, ranging from a deep bottle-glass in the shadowed cracks of her to a pale almost-yellow where the sun finds her. They are constantly growing, constantly being pressed down, constantly growing again. They are the most alive part of the basin. They are the part of her that has not decided to be still.

And the one plant with the red veins, growing from a crack in the rim-rock about two-thirds of the way up — the one that trembles when no wind is moving, and does not tremble when a wind moves — that one is not hers. That one is the one thing in the basin that does not belong to her. It is the one thing that arrived after. It is the reason the basin is not perfectly, oppressively closed. It is the seam. It is the place where something from outside has gotten in, and is growing, and is watched, and is allowed.

It trembles when the air is still because it is trying to feel for the wind. It goes still when the wind comes because it has found what it was feeling for. It is a small, polite plant, with a thick pale stem and three broad leaves, and the red in its veins is the red of something that came in from a place that has more red in it than this place does. It is the only color in the basin that is not green, not grey, not the dim rust of wet stone in evening. It is, in its small way, an event.

She has not touched it. She watches it. I think she is waiting to see what it becomes.

---

The water is not hers, exactly, but it is kin to her. It comes through the rim in threads and sheets, and it finds the lowest points of the floor, and it gathers along the patient lines, and it moves in a circulation that knows where it is going. It is older than she is. It was here before she came, and it will be here after. It is the one thing in the basin that has not changed. She has let it keep its own time. She has let it drip and hiss and run and weep from the vertical faces. It is her companion in the way that only something indifferent can be a companion.

The water does not host tadpoles or water-bugs. The water is too old for tadpoles. It has passed through the time when tadpoles would have been possible. It is the kind of water that has been moving, slowly, through the same stone, for a very long time, and has taken on the patience of the stone. If you cup your ear near a wet crack in the rock, you can hear the deeper note — the slow movement of water somewhere inside — and it sounds like a memory of motion, played at a tempo that is no longer the tempo of any living thing.

---

And the stones. The stones, you understand, are the other hands. The hands that came here and stood here and set things down and went away. The woman in the basin is not the only one who has been here. She is the last. Before her, there were others. They came, and they worked, and they left. The slab leaned against the inner wall with the flat face ground smooth — that was someone. The mortar-stone with the depression in its upper surface — that was someone, with a hand that worked against it for a long time, hollowing it out, in a gesture that was not useful but that needed to be made. The three or four smaller stones scattered in the moss, lying as if they were set down and never picked up — those were hands, too. Hands that came to the end of something and set the tools down and stood for a while and then walked out of the basin and did not return.

The basin remembers the hands. Not as people. As the shapes of working. As the gestures that were made here — the small gestures, repeated, the kind that do not leave a footprint but that wear a smooth face into stone, that press a thumbprint into moss, that leave a depression in a rock that fits the curve of a palm. The woman in the basin is the one who has inherited these gestures. She is the one who came after the hands went away, and who has been slowly, over a long time, completing the work they began. Or continuing it. Or undoing it. It is hard to tell, from inside a basin, whether someone is building or letting go.

She has not added stones. She has not moved the ones that were left. She sits among them. She is one of them, in some way, or becoming one of them. The tremor that runs through her is the tremor of a thing that is being slowly, by water and frost and moss, ground into a new shape.

---

There are no animals in the basin, and you noticed this, and you were right to be troubled by it.

Nothing breathes here but her, and the breathing of the plants, which is not the kind of breathing that you can hear.

I think this is because the basin is a place of one attention. It is the cupped hands of someone who has been holding something for a long time, and the something has gotten so still, and the hands have gotten so still, that nothing else can fit. A bird would have to break the surface of the held thing to come in. A fox would have to bark, and a bark would be a kind of speech, and there is no speech here. The basin is a place that has been, for a long time, on the verge of something, and the verge is a place that admits only what is already on it.

The plant with the red veins is on the verge. The woman is on the verge. The water is on the verge, the way a held note is on the verge of becoming another note. The worked stones are on the verge of being unreadable as worked. The white buds are on the verge of opening, and will not open, and the not-opening is the verge.

If you were to bring an animal into the basin — a small one, a quiet one, held gently in your hands — it would die, I think, or it would go very still, or it would not be an animal anymore by the time you set it down. The basin is not cruel about this. It would simply absorb it. The way it absorbs the drip from the lip of a stone. The way it absorbs the small hum that comes from the woman's mouth. The way it absorbs the slow motion of the water through the cracks. There is no cruelty in it. There is only the held quality. The agreement the place has reached with its own shape. Anything brought in would have to agree, and most things cannot.

I have seen a moth come over the rim, once. It came down into the basin in a long, wavering line. It flew across the middle distance, where the trees are. It flew over the woman's head. She did not look up. The moth flew on, and reached the far wall, and rose, and went back over the rim, and was gone. The whole flight took perhaps thirty seconds. In that time, the moth's wings seemed to slow. By the time it was rising back over the rim, it was moving at half the speed it had come in at. I am not sure this is true. I am not sure moths do not always do this. But I watched, and the moth seemed to be passing through something thicker than air, and the something had slowed it, and it had to fight its way back out.

The basin is not a trap. It is a place that has slowed to a particular pace, and the pace is the pace of the water and the moss and the woman's breath, and anything that comes in has to match the pace or be slowed to it, and most things, given enough time, can be slowed.

---

And the silence.

The silence in the basin is not the absence of sound. It is a presence. It is the held-breath quality that the place has reached with itself. It is the sound that the woman makes when she is not making sound. It is the sound that the moss makes when it is growing, which is so small that the ear cannot catch it, but which is constant, and which is the ground note of the basin.

When you stand in the basin, the silence presses on you. Not unkindly. The way water presses on a diver. You feel the bound as soon as you step inside it. The air thickens. The light settles. The world outside continues, and you can hear it — wind, distant, the far sound of a bird, the small noise of another life — but here, in the bowl, the world outside is a kind of rumor, and the basin is the only thing that is fully real.

You feel you have been let in. This is the right feeling. The basin is not a place you go to. It is a place that lets you in, when it does, and it does not always, and the letting-in is a long process, and the woman in the basin is part of the process, although she has not invited you and would not know how to. She is the one who has been letting things in, slowly, for a long time. The water. The moss. The hands, once. The plant with the red veins. Now you. She is the threshold. She is the cupped hands of the basin itself, holding the held thing.

---

If you sit with her — and you may sit, if you come, and the basin is in the mood — she will not look at you. She will not speak. She will go on breathing, and the ferns will go on moving a quarter-inch on her inhale, and the water will go on with its patient circulation, and the small white buds of the ground-flowers will go on being buds, plump and patient, year after year.

If you are very still, and you have been very still for a long time, you may begin to feel the tremor that runs through her. You may begin to feel it in your own hands, in your own chest. It is not her tremor. It is the tremor of the place, the slow, continuous, very slight rearrangement of a thing that is being slowly, over a long time, made into itself.

And if you sit with her long enough, and the basin is in the mood, and you are the right kind of visitor, the kind who can be slowed, you may feel the small hum rise in the floor of the basin, very low, lower than the ear can easily catch. You will feel it in the soles of your feet. You will feel it in your teeth. The water will catch it and carry it along the patient lines. The moss will catch it and hold it. The woman will close her mouth, and the hum will go on, in the stone, for a little while, and then it will stop.

And the quiet will continue.

And the buds will not open.

And the plant with the red veins will tremble, in the still air, on the rim, where it does not belong and is allowed.

And you will sit with her, in the basin, in the held, slow, agreeing silence, and the place will go on being itself around you, and you will be part of it, for a little while, and then you will climb back over the rim, and the world outside will continue, and you will hear it — wind, distant, the far sound of a bird, the small noise of another life — and the basin will go on, and the woman will go on, and the water will go on, and the worked stones will go on being worked and weathered and worked-on-by-weather, and the small white buds will go on being held, year after year, plump and patient, in the long, slow, cupped, agreeing, held-breath silence of the place.
