They live there.

Not the way you live in a house — with walls you have decided upon and doors that close on principle — but the way a fish lives in a current, the way a stain lives in a fabric. They live in the gaps. They are themselves a kind of gap. You will see a person and not be certain, at first, that you have seen a person, because the figure in the doorway has the coloration of the doorframe, and the moss that has begun at their sleeve continues without interruption onto the wall behind them, and the shadow they cast is no darker than the shadow any stone in the village would cast at the same hour.

They are short. They are not small in the way that small things are small; they are small in the way that a plant that has grown up against a hard wind is small, compressed, made dense by what it has had to press against. Their hands are wide. Their fingers are thicker at the tips than at the knuckles, as though they have spent generations pushing things into other things — moss into thatch, thatch into stone, themselves into the spaces the mountain has grudgingly allowed. Their eyes are dark and wet and set deeper than you expect, so that the brow seems always to be considering something. The skin of their faces has a particular quality at this elevation, at this dampness: it is the color of the underside of a fern, where the green has not yet decided to commit. It is the color of tea that has been left to steep past the point of politeness.

They wear the village. This is the phrase you will think, later, when you try to describe them. They wear the village the way other people wear clothes: as something put on daily, as something they could in theory take off but have not, for a long time, bothered to. Their garments are layers of plant-fiber and animal-skin that have been repaired so many times the original garment has become only a rumor inside the garment now being worn. The mending is its own craft. The patches are themselves patched. Threads of different ages and materials cross each other and the resulting textile is more landscape than clothing — it has relief, it has weather, it has its own small ecologies moving through it. You will see a beetle, working its slow transit across a sleeve, and the sleeve will not flinch. The beetle is part of the sleeve. The sleeve is part of the beetle's route.

They move through the webs. This is the second thing you will understand about them, after their smallness. The spiders' architectures span every gap, and the people pass beneath them and between them with a fluidity that suggests long practice, a kind of choreography learned in childhood. They do not lift their arms to clear a path; they rotate their shoulders a quarter-turn and slip through a seam in the silk that you would not have noticed was there. They read the webs the way you read a street. The webs have become, for them, a kind of map — and the people, when they want to indicate something to a visitor, will sometimes point not at the thing they mean but at the web above it, as if to say: *there, where the silk is thickest, where the threads converge. There.*

They have, you will notice, very little furniture. This is not poverty, or not only poverty. It is a working arrangement with the fauna. A chair with legs is a chair with eight small ecosystems beneath it. A bed with a frame is a frame that things will colonize. They sit on stones. They sit on the earth itself, on woven mats that are themselves alive in some way you cannot quite determine, that smell of the forest floor and seem to continue the forest floor when they are laid down. They sleep on platforms raised only a hand's breadth above the floor, and the platforms are made of slats fitted so tightly that nothing can climb up through them but air, and the air is what they want anyway, the air is the medium in which their sleep happens.

They eat what the damp provides. Mushrooms, mostly — a great variety, in colors that range from the cream of new paper to the bruised purple of an old plum. Roots, some of them long and pale and patient, that they dig with a tool you might mistake for a child's toy but that has, you realize, been worn into its shape by decades of a single family's grip. Greens that have the bitterness of mountain greens, that have had to be quick about their growing, that have had to outrun the season. Fish from the small dark tongues of water, though these fish are small and pale and you have to eat many of them to feel you have eaten at all. Eggs from the frogs, sometimes, gathered in handfuls from the undersides of leaves — a harvest conducted with a gentleness you find surprising, and a speed. They do not name the things they eat. They gesture at them. They bring them. They have names for them, surely, but the names are not for you.

Their children are quieter than any children you have known. This is not, you will decide after some observation, because they have been taught to be quiet. It is because they have not yet had to learn to be loud. They play in the webs. They climb the trees in which the dark birds stream. They come down covered in frass and silk and the small iridescent dust of beetle wings, and their parents wipe them with cloths that are themselves part of the village's textile continuum, and the wiping is part of the day, is one of the day's weathers. The children have eyes the color of strong tea and they look at you the way the spiders look at you: from a still center, with an expression you cannot quite parse, that might be curiosity or might be recognition or might be only the patience of a small creature observing something larger that has not yet understood where it is.

The old are smaller still. They sit in the deeper doorways, in the places where the smoke is thickest and the light is least. They have, by this point in their lives, almost merged with the architecture. You will see one of them and think at first you are seeing a pile of blankets; you will look again and find eyes. They are not waiting. They are not, in any sense you can name, doing nothing. They are watching. They have been watching for a long time. They have watched the mountain weather, watched the green thicken, watched the children of other children grow into the doorways behind them. They are the village's memory, and the village is theirs.

They speak seldom. When they speak they speak to each other in a language that has, you will notice, a great many consonants and very few vowels, as if the language itself had been compressed by the same wind that compressed the people. The consonants cluster at the beginning of words like small dark birds clustering at the beginning of a branch. The vowels, when they occur, are breathed rather than spoken. You will hear, sometimes, a conversation between two of them that is almost entirely consonants, and you will understand nothing, and they will understand everything, and the conversation will be over.

They do not, in any case, speak much to you. They are not unfriendly. They are not withholding. They are simply not in the habit of speaking to things that have just arrived. They will let you sit. They will bring you food. They will, after a day, perhaps two, begin to acknowledge that you are in the place, that you have been in the place long enough to be considered a presence rather than an event. After this acknowledgment they will speak to you more. Not much more. But more.

They have a word for you, you will learn eventually. They have a word for *visitor*, and it is not a single word but a small phrase, and the phrase translates, as nearly as you can determine, to *the one who has just heard it*. They use this word for the first day or two and then they stop using it, because you are no longer the one who has just heard it; you have heard it for some time now, and so the phrase no longer fits, and so they say nothing, and their silence is not unkind.

They will not ask you where you came from. This will surprise you, and then it will not. They are not uninterested. They are interested in a way that has no questions in it. They will look at your boots, at the mud that is not their mud on them, and they will know something. They will look at your hands, at the skin that has not been worked in the way their hands have been worked, and they will know something more. They will look at the way you flinch at the first spider-web across a path, and they will see that you have not yet learned the architecture of the place, and they will wait for you to learn it. They are very good at waiting.

They will not tell you to leave. They will also not tell you to stay. They will assume, correctly, that the mountain will tell you, in its own time, what you are to do. The mountain is young. The mountain has not yet decided what kinds of people it wants to keep.

When you leave — and you will leave, because your clothes will not dry and your lungs will begin to feel the altitude and the silence will become, eventually, a thing you can no longer tolerate, because the silence here is not the absence of sound but the presence of too many small sounds, and the presence is a pressure — when you leave, they will not say goodbye. They will do a thing you will not understand at the time and will think about for years afterward. One of them — usually an old one, usually one who has not spoken to you at all — will take your hand. They will hold it for a moment. They will press something into your palm. It will be a small dark stone, rounded, with a surface like the surface of a wet leaf. They will close your fingers over it. They will nod, once. They will turn and go back into the doorway from which they emerged, and the doorway will receive them, and the moss at the lintel will receive them, and you will be standing in the path with a stone in your hand and no further instruction.

The stone will be damp. It will be damp for as long as you have it, which may be a long time. You will find yourself, years later, in a quiet room, with the stone in your pocket, and you will be unable to settle, because the silence will feel like the kind of silence that is full, and you will know, then, that the silence has been full all along, that it was full in the room you are in now, that the place where the mountain has only just begun to know itself is also the place where you, having stepped off the path and into the place, are still standing, and will stand, for some time, damp at the edges, listening.
