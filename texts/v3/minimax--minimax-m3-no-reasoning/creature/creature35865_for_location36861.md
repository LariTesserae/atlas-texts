# Who Lives in the Humming Canopy

There is a kind of thinking here that does not think in words.

It lives in the mycelium first — in that continental weave of pale threads that runs beneath the red soil like a nervous system grown too large for its original purpose. The mycelium is not a single organism, though it presents itself that way. It is a federation. A council held in the grammar of chemistry. When a root-tip dies in the eastern valleys and the spore-print of the tree it belonged to drifts down through a hundred meters of bark and air to settle in the mosses of the floor, the mycelium feels it the way you might feel the loss of a particular note from a song you have always known. It does not mourn. It *records*. It adjusts. Somewhere, weeks later, in a clearing that no human has ever entered, a mushroom the color of wet slate pushes up through the leaf-litter, and it carries, encoded in the pattern of its gills, the memory of that root, and the shape of the tree that grew from it, and the season in which the tree first opened its flowers to a sun no longer remembered. This is not metaphor. This is what the forest does.

The thinking does not stay in the mycelium. It climbs.

It climbs with the transpiration of water, with the slow upward pull of minerals, with the pulse of sugars moving from leaf to root in the long green columns of the trunks. By the time it reaches the canopy proper — the three-hundred-meter ceiling where the elder-growth turns outward and the wind finally finds the leaves — the thinking has become something else entirely. Aware is too small a word. *Attentive* is closer. The Spires, the very tallest of the trees, the ones that push through their own crowns into the mist and stand there wearing weather like scarves, are not individuals in any way a human would recognize. They are *expressions*. Local concentrations of a mind that has no center. When you stand beneath one and place your hand against the bark, you are touching a thought the forest is having about itself.

The bark is warm.

The bark is always warm.

---

There are things that live in the canopy that have stopped pretending to be anything but arguments.

The gliders — and there are many kinds, six-meter wingspans stretched between elongated fingers, fur the green of lichen, eyes enormous and dark as old wells — do not glide so much as *debate* with the air. They bank between the trunks in slow spirals that look, from the floor, like the path of a thought that cannot quite commit to its conclusion. They are not predators. They are not prey, exactly. They are the forest's way of asking a question about descent, about whether falling is the same as choosing. Their young are born in the upper branches and die in the upper branches. They have never touched the ground. They do not know what ground is. The idea of ground, if you could somehow explain it to one, would be received the way you might receive a description of a color you have never seen — politely, with the dawning suspicion that the person explaining it has made some fundamental mistake about the world.

The browsers are larger. Horsesized, some of them. They have given up the floor so thoroughly that their legs have begun to articulate differently — the joints reversed, the hooves split into prehensile lobes, the whole animal shaped like an apology to gravity. They eat the new growth at the tips of the branches. They sleep on limbs the width of roads. When they give birth, the young must learn, within minutes, to cling. The ones that cannot cling do not survive. The forest is not cruel about this. The forest is simply *specific*.

The predators move in absolute silence and have done so for so long that their feet no longer make sound against bark. They are horsesized too, but leaner, denser, with eyes set forward and fur that matches, exactly, the lichen of the trunks they hunt from. You do not see them. You do not hear them. You only know they have passed when the smaller creatures fall silent in a wave that radiates outward from their passage, and then, cautiously, begin again. They are not cruel either. They are *thorough*. They have answered every question the forest has asked about hunger, and they have answered it in a way that cannot be improved upon. They are the final draft of an essay the canopy has been writing for a million years.

---

The insects are the size of birds and have been so for so long that they have, collectively, become a kind of weather.

The cicadas — and they are not cicadas, not really, but the word is close enough — produce a sound that is not a sound so much as a *condition*. It is the background hum of the forest's metabolism, audible from the floor in the same way the sun is visible from the floor: as a presence, a given, a thing that simply *is*. There are beetles that sing like bells. There are beetles that sing like the wind in a chimney. There are things that look like beetles and sing like birds in distress, and the birds that live here have learned, over generations, to ignore that particular song — to let it pass through them the way a lie passes through a person who has heard too many of them. There are creatures in the upper canopy that look like birds and produce a sound like water running over stones. The forest does not care whether you can tell them apart. The forest cares that the sound continues.

The sound is layered. It has registers. In the deep stratum, beneath everything, there is the subsonic shudder of root-systems communicating through the earth — a pulse so low it is felt in the chest rather than heard, the forest's heartbeat, the slow drum of the mycelium thinking. Above that, the insect-note, sustained, constant, the held breath of ten million small bodies vibrating their wings in rhythms that interlock. Above that, the calls of the larger fauna — the browsers murmuring to each other in the canopy, the predators silent (always silent), the gliders occasionally producing a shriek that is less a sound than a punctuation mark. And above all of it, rising like smoke, the creak of the trunks themselves, the slow percussion of bark expanding in the heat, the drip of water making its long delayed descent from leaf to leaf, the groan of wood under its own impossible weight.

The sound is a sentence. The forest has been finishing the sentence for a million years.

---

On the floor, the rules change.

Where the light fails completely — and there are pockets, alcoves, rooms in the architecture of the buttresses where the light fails so thoroughly that the dark has become a kind of solid — the creatures that live there have stopped competing for sunlight and begun competing for everything else. They are pale. Some of them are blind. Some of them have lost the concept of eye entirely and navigate by some other sense, some other grammar of perception. They feed on the fungal meadows and the perpetual rain of leaf-litter from above and the slow chemical trickle of the mycelium's exudations.

There are grazers here that have no concept of sky.

This is not a figure of speech. They are born in the dark and they die in the dark. They have no word for *up*. The vertical dimension, for them, is something you fall into when the root you are standing on gives way. They are white as old paper. They are gentle. They eat constantly, without urgency, because the dark does not reward urgency. Their predators — and there are predators, even here, even in the absolute dark — are also white, also blind, also patient. The hunt, in the deepest understory, is a thing conducted entirely through vibration and chemoreception, a conversation held in the language of pressure and dissolved mineral, and it ends, when it ends, with a sound too soft to hear but felt by every mycelial thread within a hundred meters.

The grazers do not fear the dark. They do not know there is anything else.

---

The fungi glow.

This is worth saying again. The fungi glow.

In the permanent dusk between the largest trunks, where the canopy has woven itself into a ceiling so dense that even the brightest noon cannot break through, the fungi have become the only light. They are the size of huts. Some of them are larger. Their phosphorescence is faint — lavender, the color of a bruise just beginning to heal — but it is enough. It is enough to see by, if your eyes have learned to see by it. The people who live in the buttress-dwellings have learned to see by it. They have also learned to harvest it. They do not cultivate the fungi the way you cultivate wheat. They *talk* to the fungi. They know which species will glow brighter if you sing to them in a particular key. They know which ones will not glow at all if you approach them from the wrong direction. The fungi are not intelligent in any way the people can articulate. But they are *responsive*. And responsiveness, in a place where everything else is indifferent, is close enough to friendship.

The fungi are the forest's eyes on the floor.

The fungi are how the forest sees in the dark.

---

The people.

They are few. They have always been few.

They live in the buttress-rooms of the elder trunks, in wooden platforms lashed into the crooks of branches, in lean-tos of woven vine and bark, in a few structures of stacked stone where the soil is shallow enough to stand on. They are short and dark-skinned and quiet in their movements, as if they have learned from the predators that silence is a kind of courtesy. They do not speak loudly. They do not need to. The forest carries sound for them, in the layers of damp air, in the slow columns of mist between the trunks, and a whisper at the base of one tree can be heard, with practice, at the base of another fifty meters away.

They hunt in the canopy with weighted ropes and long poles. They eat the insects, mostly — the great beetles, the cicadas, the things that look like beetles and sing like birds — and the small gliders, when they can be brought down, and a kind of bitter gruel they press from the pith of certain vines that grows only in the upper branches and must be harvested in the brief window between its flowering and its collapse into pollen. They do not farm in any sense that a person from elsewhere would recognize. They prune. They coax. They know which trees flower when, and they harvest in a careful choreography that looks, from outside, like a kind of slow conversation with a thing that does not speak but is, somehow, listening.

They have axes of sharpened stone.

They have fire, but rarely use it — the upper canopy is too dry, and the floor too wet, and the people have learned, over generations, to cook with hot stones brought up from the volcanic seams that run deep beneath the mycelium. They have baskets woven so tight they hold water. They have names for every species, and stories for every clearing, and a calendar that is written in the flowering-times of the elder trees.

They do not travel far. Why would they? The world is one tree.

They do not count their years the way you would. They count them in flowerings. In the spans between fires. In the slow lean of a tree they have known since they were small.

When they die, they are wrapped in vine and laid in the crooks of branches they themselves once climbed. The forest grows over the cleared spaces. Their descendants return to the buttresses. The song continues.

---

There is something else.

It is hard to speak of directly, because it does not have edges.

The trees, in certain places, grow in patterns that are too regular to be accident — concentric rings, perfectly spaced, as if planted by a hand that understood geometry long before geometry had a name. There are clearings that are not clearings but grids, and when you stand in the center of one, the trees at its edge lean slightly inward, as if curious. There are sounds that come from underground — not from animals, not from water — that pulse with a regularity that suggests intent. Once, a traveler reported seeing a trunk that had opened, slowly, like a mouth, and from inside it, something had looked out, briefly, with an eye that was not an eye.

The forest knows things.

This is the only way to say it.

The roots remember. The mycelium carries signals that have nothing to do with danger or food — signals that travel for weeks without arriving at any destination that can be named. When you sleep in the buttress of a tree, you dream the dreams the forest is thinking, and they are not your dreams, and they are not human dreams, but they are *intelligent*, somehow, in a way that makes you wake with your hand on your chest and your heart uncertain whether it has been yours for a moment.

No one lives here long enough to understand it.

They live, and die, and the forest grows over their cleared spaces, and their descendants return to the buttresses, and the song continues, and the thinking continues, and the eye that is not an eye continues to watch from inside the trunk that opened like a mouth, and the question it is asking has no answer, and the forest does not mind, because the question is the point.

---

The forest dreams you, too.

When you walk here — and you will walk here, if you come — the forest is walking with you. Not beside you. *Through* you. The mycelium under your feet is feeling the weight of your step and recording it the way it records everything, in the slow acid language of chemistry. The canopy above you is reading the heat of your body in the slight variations of the air's moisture. The insects are adjusting their chorus to account for the new presence in their midst, the way an orchestra adjusts to a new instrument joining mid-performance.

You are not a visitor here.

You are a *sentence* the forest is speaking about itself.

You come in. You go out — if you go out — and you are not quite the same. Your sense of time loosens. Your sense of where you end and the green begins becomes uncertain. You think about the forest, later, in quieter places, and you find that the thinking has a texture to it, a kind of grain, as if the forest were a wood and your thoughts were being planed along its surface.

The sun, when it sets, takes three hours to do it, because the canopy eats the light in layers, and the green darkens to teal, and the teal to the deep blue of the permanent dusk that lives at the floor, and the fungi begin to glow, and the larger fauna begin their night-call, and the sound, which has never stopped, simply changes key.

You lie in your buttress-dwelling and you listen to the forest breathing around you, the slow creak of wood, the drip of water making its patient way down through the leaves, the boom of something large moving far above, and you think: *I am inside something. Something that was here before me and will be here after. Something that does not need me, exactly, but has, for the moment, allowed me a room.*

You sleep.

The forest dreams you, too.
