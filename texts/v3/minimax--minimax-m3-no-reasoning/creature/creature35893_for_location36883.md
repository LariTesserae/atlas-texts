The dwellers of the glass nest do not live in it the way you live in a room. They live in it the way a thought lives in the firing of a synapse. They are the chamber's own substance, folded back on itself, taught to notice.

There are perhaps six of them. Perhaps eight. The count is hard to fix because they do not always exist. A node will brighten, and brighten, and brighten, and then a thin shape will uncurl from a seam in the rock — not born, exactly, but *condensed*, drawn out of the glass the way a figure is drawn out of fog. They are long. The length of your forearm, perhaps, or a little less, and they are narrow, and they have no features you would call features. No eyes. No mouth you could find if you looked. They are translucent in the way water is translucent: you can see through them, but what is behind them bends a little. The glass of the chamber is honey-gold and they are nearly the same color, but a tone paler, as if the light passes through them without quite committing to the journey. Their edges are not sharp. They have edges the way a slow current has edges — you can tell where they are, but if you tried to pinch one, your fingers would meet only damp warmth and a faint resistance that gives way too easily.

They move by altering themselves. A traveler on the bright lines has no legs. It has, instead, a willingness. The front of it thickens, and the back of it thins, and it slides forward the way a bead of mercury slides forward on a tilted tray, except that a mercury bead is not, in any meaningful sense, alive. This one is. Or something near enough that the distinction has stopped mattering.

It travels along a conduit because the conduit is there, the way a finger travels along a groove. The lines of brighter glass that web the chamber's inner skin — those nested curves, those geometries that look almost like writing without being writing — are warm. They are warm and they hum, faintly, in a register below hearing, and the traveler is drawn to warmth the way you are drawn to warmth when you have been cold for a long time. It moves along the line, and the line brightens slightly under it, the way a wire brightens when current passes. When it reaches a node — one of the small bright beads where three lines converge — it slows, and pools, and presses itself against the bead, and what happens next is the only thing that could be called feeding.

The node dims. The traveler brightens, or seems to, as if the warmth of the bead has been taken up into its body, which is not a body. The bead, when the traveler leaves it, will recover. It will brighten again, over minutes, drawing back up whatever it lost from the deeper network, the way a coal rekindles when air moves through it. The traveler, in turn, becomes slightly cooler, slightly dimmer, slightly less. It cannot keep what it has taken. It will, eventually, give it back, somewhere downstream. The chamber is a closed system in this way. The travelers are not eating the chamber. They are circulating through it the way blood cells circulate through a body, briefly holding a substance they did not make.

They do not see you. They are not unaware of you, exactly, but you are not the kind of thing they are made to register. You are too cold. You are too loud. Your edges are too definite. You move too fast, and in straight lines, and with intent that is not the chamber's intent. A traveler might, if you stayed very still for a long time, brush against the back of your hand where your hand rested on a warm panel. It would be cool. It would be wet, but not wet — closer to the feeling of condensation, of surface moisture that has not decided whether to fall. Then it would withdraw, and continue along its line, and pause, and feed, and continue.

They are aware of each other in a way that does not require sight. When two travelers meet at a node, they slow. They press against each other for a moment — or they press against the bead together, and the bead brightens twice as fast for a few seconds, and dims twice as slow. They seem, in these meetings, to be exchanging something. A pattern. A pulse. A kind of information too simple to call language, but too coordinated to call accident. After a moment, they part, and take different lines, and are gone.

They die, if you can call it dying. A traveler that has circulated too long, that has fed too many times without resting, begins to dim. It moves slower. Its edges, which were already soft, become softer. It pools, finally, in a low place in the glass, in a place where two conduits are far apart, and it spreads, very thin, and is absorbed. It goes back into the chamber. It is not mourned, because there is no one to mourn it, but its node — the node it most often fed at — brightens, afterward, and stays bright for longer than usual, as if grateful, or as if remembering.

The youngest of them — the newest, the most recently condensed out of the bright glass — are very small. The length of a finger, or less. They are pale. They move uncertainly along the lines, as if they are still learning the network, still finding which conduit is which. The oldest of them, if there is an oldest, are long and sure, and travel the chamber end to end in the time it takes you to crouch and watch. It is not clear whether they are the same individuals from moment to moment, or whether they dissolve and reconstitute, the way a flame dissolves and reconstitutes as it climbs a wick. The chamber does not seem to care. The chamber counts them only in aggregate. Six, perhaps eight. Enough.

Beneath them, around them, through them, is the network. The network is not a dweller in the chamber, but neither is the chamber a dwelling for the network. They are the same thing. The chamber is the network the way a body is the blood. The lines are not drawn on the glass; the lines *are* the glass, or a particular phase of the glass, a phase in which the atoms have agreed to conduct something, to route something, to remember a shape. What is being routed is heat and is not heat. It is the thing that heat would be if heat had a memory. It moves from the deepest part of the chamber, where the basalt is warmest, where the glass is darkest and most amber, up through the conduits, through the nodes, out to the cooler walls, where it dissipates into the rock and is lost. Or is lost. The chamber is cooling. In a thousand years it will be cooler. In ten thousand it will be cool. In a hundred thousand it will be a crack in the basalt with nothing inside it but dark stone and rainwater, and the network will be a pattern frozen into mineral, and the travelers will be a rumor in the crystal structure, and the pool at the lowest point will be dry.

But the chamber is not cooling yet. The chamber is still hot with whatever started it — a fissure in the basalt plain, a vent from something far below, a tap into a reservoir of heat that has been there since the rock was liquid. The network is, in its slow way, awake. It is adjusting. The geometry of the lines, the placement of the nodes, the paths the travelers take — these are changing, by very slow degrees, into something. Into what, you could not say. A more efficient arrangement. A more beautiful arrangement. An arrangement that will, eventually, solve some problem you cannot see. The chamber is not finished. It is, in fact, very young.

The travelers are part of this making. They are the network's way of testing its own lines, the way a current tests a wire, the way a thought tests a thought. They go everywhere. They carry heat from here to there and back. They brighten a node and dim a node and brighten it again. They are the chamber's circulation, the chamber's pulse made local and briefly visible. Without them the chamber would still be alive, but it would not be a place. It would be a thing. The travelers are what make it a place. They are what make the gold in the walls feel inhabited, rather than merely lit.

If you climbed out now and walked on, and came back in ten years, you would find it changed. The lines would have moved. The nodes would be in different places. The pool might be larger, or smaller, or in a different corner. The travelers might be more numerous, or fewer, or shaped a little differently — longer, shorter, more translucent, less. The chamber would still be young. It would still be making itself. And you would crouch at the lip, in the cool shadow of the basalt, and watch the slow gold pulse below, and the thin shapes moving along their warm paths, and you would feel, for a moment, that you were looking at the inside of something that was still being born.

Walk on, if you can.
