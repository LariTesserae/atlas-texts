The wind has been here longer than anything else.

You have to start there, because the wind is what shaped the basin into a basin, and the basin is what made the rest of it possible, and the wind is still here, still doing the work, though the work is slower now and the wind is patient with it. The wind comes down off the higher plateau to the west — that older, harder shoulder of the same granite body, where nothing much grows and the stones ring when you strike them — and it comes down hard, because there is nothing to stop it, and it finds the rim of the basin and it has to go somewhere, and what it does is go down. It pours over the western lip and into the bowl and the bowl is a kind of throat, and the wind is the breath in the throat, and the breath has been drawn and expelled for a very long time, and the shape of the throat is the shape of the breath. The ridges to the east and north are lower, and the wind finds them too, but it has already spent itself by then, or most of itself, and what is left slides along the stone and into the cracks between the stones and into the spaces the stones have been persuaded, over centuries, to make for it. There is a sound in the settlement when the wind is up that the people who live there have stopped hearing, or hear the way you hear your own pulse. It is a chord. It is not a comfortable chord. It is the chord of air being made to fit through passages it did not choose, and the passages do not yield gracefully, they yield the way old stone yields, with a kind of grudging resonance that is almost a voice, and the voice is almost a word, and the word is almost a name, and the name is for something that is not quite there.

The wind is the senior tenant. The wind was here before the stream, in the sense that the wind is what carved the notch in the rim through which the stream now leaves, and the wind is what keeps the notch from closing again with the patient green insistence of everything else. The wind has no body, no nest, no claim on a particular stone. It is everywhere, and it leaves nothing, and what it leaves behind — the polished faces of the exposed gneiss, the way the lichen has been kept from the windward sides of certain boulders and has taken the leeward sides in a clean line, so that the boulders look half-dressed — that is its signature, and the signature is everywhere.

The stream is the next senior tenant, and the stream is loud about its tenancy.

The stream does not begin. That is the first thing to understand about it, and it is a thing the people who live here understand without having to be told. The stream begins the way a conversation begins: someone is already talking when you arrive, and you have to listen for a while before you can tell where the talking is coming from. The water comes out of the base of the eastern ridge in a hundred places, and each place is a seep, and each seep is a negotiation between the rock and the water that has been held in the rock since the last rain or the last snowmelt or the last time the fog was thick enough to be more than weather. The rock does not want to give up the water. The rock has been holding the water for a long time, in the small spaces between the crystals, in the hairline fractures that run through the gneiss like the grain through old wood, and the water has been there long enough that it has become part of the rock, the way minerals dissolved in it have become part of the water, and what comes out of the seeps is not the same water that went in. It is water that has been taught something by the stone. It is colder than the air, even in summer, and it tastes of iron and of something faintly sweet, the taste of decay that is not yet decay, the taste of leaf-mold and dissolved peat, the taste of a place that has been wet for a very long time without ever being washed clean.

The seeps find each other. This is the strange thing, or one of the strange things. The seeps are not organized. They are not a spring. They are a hundred small surrenders, scattered along a hundred meters of rock face, and the water from each one does what water does, which is to find the lowest available path, and the lowest available path leads, in most cases, to a thin sheet of water that is not yet a stream but is on its way to becoming one. The sheets join, and the joined sheets find the slight tilt of the basin floor, and the tilt is enough. The stream is born in pieces and assembled as it goes, and by the time it reaches the first of the settlement's stones it is a thing, a presence, a voice that does not stop. The stream is louder than it should be for its size. This is because of the stones. The stones of the basin floor are the differentially eroded remnants of the same bedrock that makes the ridges, and they are not uniform, and the water has been working on them for longer than anyone can say, and the working has produced a surface that is not steps and not rapids but something in between, a series of small drops and small pools and small slides that each give a different sound, and the sounds do not blend, they coexist, and the coexistence is the voice of the stream. There is a clatter over the broken ground near the eastern seeps, where the water is still thin and the stones are still angular. There is a hiss where the current slides over the smooth shoulder of a boulder that has been polished to a kind of dark shine by centuries of attention. There is a small bell-tone where the water drops half a finger's width into a pocket of air that the stone has cupped for it, and the air, trapped for a moment, gives back a note that is startlingly clear and that does not belong to water. There is a low continuous tone, almost a hum, where the stream runs fast over a flat plate of gneiss and the plate vibrates faintly in its bed, and the vibration is the hum. The stream is polyphonic. The stream has been composing itself for a long time.

The stream knows the settlement. The stream has been diverted, here and there, into shallow channels that run between the buildings, and the channels are lined with stones that have been chosen for their flatness and set edge-up to keep the water where it has been put, and the water, obliging, runs where it is led, and the running keeps the channels clear of moss, and the clearness is a kind of conversation between the people and the stream, an agreement renewed each time a stone shifts and has to be set back. The stream does not mind the diversion. The stream is too small to mind. The stream is a thing that wants to be lower, and the people have given it a way to be lower, and the way happens to run past their doors, and the stream accepts this as water accepts anything, which is without opinion. The stream does not warm. The stream is the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of last winter, which is colder than the air in summer and warmer than the air in winter, and the stream runs through the settlement like a cooled vein, and the air above the stream is a fraction colder than the air elsewhere, and the cold makes the fog condense there first, and the fog lies along the stream in a thin ribbon that the people step through without noticing, the way you step through your own shadow.

The fog is the next tenant, and the fog is the most pervasive.

The fog is not weather. The fog is the basin breathing. The basin fills with fog on most nights and most mornings, and the filling is so reliable that the people who live here do not have a word for it, the way you do not have a word for the air you are breathing, you just have the air. The fog is what happens when the cold of the stone and the damp of the mosses and the warmth of whatever small heat the stream gives off all meet at the same moment, which is most moments, and the meeting is a negotiation, and the negotiation produces a cloud that has no business being on the ground but is on the ground anyway, and that sits in the basin in a shallow layer that is rarely more than a meter deep and is sometimes only a hand's depth, so that the ridges stand above it and the settlement stands in it and the higher stones of the settlement stand above it, and the world is reduced to what can be seen across a distance of twenty meters or less. The fog moves. The fog does not move with the wind, or not exactly, and this is one of the things that visitors notice and that the people who live here have learned to ignore. The fog has its own currents, which are the currents of cold air draining down the slopes of the basin and pooling in the center, and the currents are slow and particular, and they make the fog thicken and thin in patterns that are not quite the patterns of the wind and not quite the patterns of the drainage and not quite random, and the patterns, over the course of a morning, will rearrange the visibility in the basin so that you can see one part of the settlement clearly and another part not at all, and the rearrangement is not predictable, or not predictable in any way that a person can use, and the people have stopped trying to predict it. They move through the fog the way fish move through water. They know where the stones are by the feel of them underfoot. They know where the stream is by the sound. They know where each other is by voices, and the voices are muffled in the fog the way everything is muffled, and the muffled voices are part of the sound of the place.

The fog holds the sound. The fog is dense enough, in the mornings, to make the stream louder, because the sound cannot escape upward and so it stays, and the staying is a kind of amplification, and the amplification is part of why the basin feels loud even when the wind is down. The fog holds the cold too. The fog is what keeps the frost coming back in the middle of summer, because the fog at night is a blanket, and the blanket keeps the heat of the day from escaping, and the heat cannot escape upward into the clear sky, and so it stays near the ground, and the staying is not enough, because the stone is colder than the air, and the cold of the stone wins, and there is frost on the moss in the morning, and the frost is white and precise and disappears slowly, and the disappearance is a small event that no one watches but that happens, every morning, in every part of the basin, with the reliability of a ritual that no one has named.

The mosses are the next tenants, and the mosses are the most numerous, and the mosses are what the basin is becoming.

There are mosses everywhere. There are mosses on the stones of the ridges, where the wind has been kind enough to let them grow on the leeward faces, and the mosses are the pale celadon of exposure and the deep almost-black of the sheltered cracks, and the colors are not just colors, they are ages, the pale ones are young and the dark ones are old, and the age is measured in the thickness of the cushion and the depth of the color, and the depth of the color is the depth of the water held in the cushion, and the water held in the cushion is the water that becomes the seeps, and the seeps become the stream, and the stream becomes the mosses again, and the cycle is so slow that it is not a cycle, it is a condition. There are mosses on the floor of the basin, where the mosses are thicker, because the floor is wetter, and the wetter mosses are the sphagnum, and the sphagnum holds water in a way that the other mosses do not, it holds water in itself, in the dead cells at its base that have become a kind of sponge, and the sponge is so effective that the ground under a patch of sphagnum feels like a held breath, and the held breath is what the people are walking on in the wettest parts of the basin, and the walking is careful, and the care is the right care, because the sphagnum is a place where the ground is not quite ground.

There are mosses on the settlement. The mosses are on the walls and on the roofs and on the paths between the walls, and the mosses are the reason the settlement looks, from a little distance, like a heap of stones that has gone soft, because the mosses have softened it, deliberately or not, and the softening is a collaboration. The mosses grow on the walls because the walls are old and the mortar of the walls is not mortar but time, and the time has made the stones fit loosely enough for the mosses to find a purchase, and the mosses have found it, and the finding is the beginning of the wall becoming something other than a wall. The mosses grow on the roofs because the roofs are of heather and turf, and the turf is a living thing, and the living thing is mostly moss, and the moss grows over the thatch and the heather and the binding and makes the roof indistinguishable, in a few years, from the ground, and the indistinguishability is the point, because the roof is a thing that is supposed to shed water, and the mosses are a thing that are supposed to hold water, and the contradiction is resolved by the mosses growing thicker at the eaves and thinner at the ridge, and the gradient of thickness is the gradient of water-shedding, and the roof works the way roofs are supposed to work, and it works by being as much like the ground as a roof can be. The water that the mosses do not shed runs off the eaves in thin streams, and the streams fall like curtains, and the curtains are one of the sounds of the settlement when the fog is heavy, and the sound is a soft percussion, and the percussion is the sound of the basin giving back what the basin has been given.

The lichens are the next tenants, and the lichens are the oldest, and the lichens are the slowest, and the lichens are the reason the stones of the ridges have any color at all.

The lichens are the maps on the stones. The lichens are the orange and the yellow and the pale gray that you see on the exposed faces of the gneiss, and the colors are not decorations, they are ages, and the ages are long. A lichen takes a century to colonize a hand's breadth of stone, and a hand's breadth of stone is a long time to be a hand's breadth of stone, and the lichen knows this, and the lichen takes its time, and the time is part of the lichen, the way the slowness of the place is part of the place. The lichens do not compete with the mosses. The lichens grow where the mosses cannot, which is on the dry exposed stone, and the mosses grow where the lichens cannot, which is on the wet sheltered stone, and the division is so old and so reliable that the two kinds of growth have learned to share the same boulder, and the boulder is half-moss and half-lichen, and the boundary between them is a line that is not quite a line, and the line moves over the years, and the movement is the lichen colonizing a little more of the moss's territory in a dry year and the moss colonizing a little more of the lichen's territory in a wet year, and the wars are slow and inconclusive and are not wars, they are conversations, and the conversations are part of what makes the place look the way it does, which is the way a place looks when it has been having the same conversation for a very long time.

The dwarf willows are the next tenants, and the dwarf willows are the most ambitious of the plants, and the dwarf willows are the reason there is any height to the vegetation at all.

The dwarf willows grow in the pockets of deeper soil, which are the pockets where the wind has deposited dust and the dust has mixed with the moss and the moss has died and become peat, and the peat is thin but it is enough, and the willows have taken the thinness as a challenge, and the willows have grown to the height of a knee and not a hand's breadth more, and the not-more is the willows' acceptance of the wind, and the acceptance is a kind of deal, the wind agrees not to break the willows and the willows agree not to grow tall enough to be broken, and the deal has held for a long time. The leaves of the dwarf willows are small and round and a green that is darker than the mosses' green, and the undersides of the leaves are silver, and the silver shows when the wind turns the leaves, and the showing is a kind of signal, the basin signaling to itself that the wind is up, and the willows are the first to signal, and the signaling is the willows' main contribution to the place, and the place is grateful for it, or would be if the place were the kind of place that could be grateful, which it is not, but the gratitude is implied in the way the willows are allowed to keep their place in the lee of the larger stones, where the wind cannot reach them as hard and where they can be, for a few minutes at a time, a little taller than they are allowed to be elsewhere.

The crowberry and the bearberry are the next tenants, and the crowberry and the bearberry are the most complete, and the crowberry and the bearberry are the reason you sink into the vegetation in the parts of the basin where the vegetation is not moss.

The crowberry and the bearberry grow in low mats that are not quite carpets, because a carpet is flat and a mat has depth, and the depth is the depth of the roots and the old stems and the water held in the stems, and the water is acidic, and the acidity is part of why the mats are so complete, because the acidity keeps out the things that would compete with the berries, and the berries are the things that want to grow here, and the berries have made the place theirs by making the place inhospitable to anyone else, and the inhospitability is not hostile, it is just the chemistry of the place, and the chemistry is the chemistry of slow decay in a cold wet space, and the space is the basin, and the basin is the place, and the place has been making itself inhospitable to anything but the things that are willing to be slow and patient and small, and the berries are willing, and the berries are the result. You sink into the mats because the mats are not solid, they are a mesh of stems and leaves that gives under your weight and then slowly, over the next few seconds, gives back, and the giving-back is the mat remembering that it is a plant and that plants do not hold still, they hold, and holding is different from holding still. The berries, when they are ripe, are black, and the black is the same black as the deepest moss, and the birds, when they come, eat the berries, and the birds are the only visitors, and the visitors are brief.

The sedges are the next tenants, and the sedges are the loudest of the plants, and the sedges are the reason the stream is bordered by something that is not moss.

The sedges grow along the stream, and the sedges are tall for the basin, which is to say they are as tall as your hand, and the hand-tall is a kind of statement, and the statement is that the sedges have found a place where the wind is broken by the stream's banks and the water is constant and the soil is deeper than anywhere else in the basin, because the stream has been depositing silt in its bends for a long time, and the silt is rich, and the richness is what the sedges have been waiting for. The sedges are a vivid, almost aggressive green, and the green is almost louder than the stream, and the loudness is what makes the sedges visible from a distance, and the visibility is what makes the stream visible from a distance, and the stream is the spine of the basin, and the sedges are the muscles along the spine, and the muscles are what make the place look like a place where things are growing on purpose, even though nothing here grows on purpose, everything grows by the slow accumulation of right conditions, and the right conditions along the stream are the most right in the basin.

The settlement is the next tenant, and the settlement is the most recent, and the settlement is the most complicated, and the settlement is the reason the basin has any of the things that basins usually have, which is to say, people, and the things people make, and the things people leave behind.

The settlement is built of stone, and the stone is the stone of the basin, and the stone is the only building material the basin offers in excess, and the excess is what made the building possible, and the building is what made the excess useful, and the usefulness is part of the place now, the way the mosses are part of the place and the stream is part of the place. The walls are dry-stone, and the dry-stone is the stone fitted to stone without mortar, and the fitting is a craft, and the craft is old, and the craft is the only craft that has survived the long occupation of the basin, and the survival is because the craft is the craft of patience, and patience is the only virtue the basin rewards. The stones are not shaped. The stones are broken, and the breaking is done with a careful blow, and the blow is delivered at a point where the stone wants to break, and the wanting is read from the grain of the gneiss, and the reading is part of the craft, and the craft is taught, and the teaching is slow, and the slowness is the point.

The walls are low. The walls are never more than a story, and the story is not a tall story, and the not-tall is the response to the wind, which would tear a taller wall apart in a bad season, and the bad season is the season of the equinoctial gales that come down off the higher plateau, and the gales are the wind's test of the settlement, and the settlement has passed the test for a long time by being low. The walls are thick. The thickness is not just for the wind, the thickness is for the cold, and the cold is held in the thickness, the way the cold is held in the stone of the ridges, and the holding is what keeps the inside of the walls warmer than the outside, and the warmer is the difference between a place that is habitable and a place that is not, and the habitability is the point.

The doorways are narrow. The narrowness is the response to the wind, and the response to the cold, and the response is the same response, which is to make the openings small enough that the wind cannot push through them with force, and the wind will try, and the wind will fail, and the failure is the sound of the wind in the cracks, which is the chord, which is almost a voice, which is almost a word. The doorways are not centered in the walls. The doorways are placed where the wind will not push straight through them, and the placing is part of the craft, and the craft knows the wind the way the wind knows the basin, which is intimately, and the intimacy is the reason the wind does not tear the doorways out, and the not-tearing is the reason the doorways are still there.

The roofs are of heather and turf. The heather is cut from the higher slopes where the heather grows, and the heather is laid in thick layers, and the layers are bound with a lattice of hazel or willow or whatever flexible wood is available, and the binding is the structure, and the structure is hidden by the turf that is laid over it, and the turf is the top of the nearby ground, cut in squares and laid like scales, and the scales root, and the rooting is what makes the roof a living thing, and the living thing is mostly moss, and the moss is the roof's future, and the future is the roof becoming indistinguishable from the ground, which is the roof's purpose, which is the way the settlement says to the basin that the settlement is part of the basin, and the saying is true, and the truth is what the mosses are working on, and the working will not be finished for a long time.

The settlement is layered. The layering is the part that takes time to read, and the reading is part of what it means to be in the settlement, and the being is part of what the people do, and the people are not here, and have not been here for a long time, and yet the settlement is not abandoned, and the not-abandoned is one of the strange things about the place. A wall is built against an older wall, and the older wall is visible inside the newer wall, and the visibility is not decorative, the visibility is the wall telling you that the settlement has been here longer than the wall, and the wall has been added to the settlement, and the adding is what the settlement does, the settlement adds itself to itself, and the adding is a kind of growth, and the growth is the settlement's way of being alive. A doorway has been sealed with stones, and the sealing is not the end of the doorway, the sealing is the doorway becoming a part of the wall, and the wall becoming a part of the history, and the history is written in the sealed doorways, and the writing is not language, the writing is the place, and the place is what reads the writing.

The central hearth is the part of the settlement that is most clearly a hearth, and the hearth is the part that is most clearly occupied, and the occupation is the smoke, and the smoke is a thin thread, and the thread is being taken apart by the wind, and the taking-apart is the smoke's last act, and the last act is the smoke becoming the air, and the air is what the basin is made of, and the made-of is the connection between the settlement and the basin, and the connection is older than the smoke.

The paths are worn into the moss. The wearing is the work of feet, and the feet are the feet of people, and the people are not here, and the wearing is what they left, and the leaving is what makes the paths paths, and the paths are the settlement's memory of the people, and the memory is more reliable than the people, and the reliability is what makes the paths still paths, and the still-paths is what makes the settlement still a settlement, and the still-settlement is what makes the basin still a place that was lived in, and the lived-in is what the mosses are slowly erasing, and the erasing is the mosses' work, and the work will be finished someday, and the someday is the future, and the future is not here yet.

The fauna is the next tenant, and the fauna is sparse, and the sparseness is part of what the basin is.

The meadow-pipit comes. The meadow-pipit is a small brown bird with a streaked breast and a habit of walking rather than hopping, and the walking is what you see when the pipit is in the basin, which is not often, and the not-often is the basin being too high and too cold and too foggy for the pipit most of the time, and the most-of-the-time is the pipit being elsewhere, and the elsewhere is the lower slopes, and the lower slopes are where the pipit nests, and the nesting is what brings the pipit up into the basin in the breeding season, and the breeding season is short, and the short is the reason the pipit is here briefly, and the briefly is what you see, and the seeing is the pipit pecking at the moss for insects, and the insects are the pipit's reason for being here, and the reason is the only reason.

The wheatear comes. The wheatear is a bird with a white rump and a quick sideways look, and the look is the look of a bird that does not trust the place, and the not-trust is the right response, because the basin is not a place that trusts easily, and the wheatear's not-trust is the bird's way of fitting in, and the fitting-in is the wheatear's contribution to the place, and the contribution is brief. The wheatear is here in the summer, and the summer is short, and the wheatear leaves before the first frost, and the leaving is the wheatear's acknowledgment that the basin is not the wheatear's place, and the acknowledgment is the right acknowledgment, and the right acknowledgment is what makes the wheatear's visits possible, and the visits are what you see, and the seeing is the wheatear on a stone, with the quick look, and then the wheatear is gone.

There are tracks along the stream. The tracks are too blurred by the damp to identify with certainty, and the not-certainty is part of the tracks, because the tracks are made by something that does not want to be identified, and the not-wanting is the something's way of being in the basin without being in the basin, and the without is what the tracks are. The stride is wrong for a deer, and the wrong is the stride of something smaller, something that walks low to the ground, and the low is what a fox does, or a small dog, or a pine marten, and the pine marten is the most likely, and the likely is the tracks being made at night, and the night is when the basin is most itself, and the itself is when the tracks are made, and the making is the tracks' way of being part of the basin without being seen, and the not-seen is the right way to be part of the basin, and the right is what the tracks are.

There are bones in a sheltered corner between two walls. The bones are bleached and scattered, and the bleaching and the scattering are the work of weather and time, and the time is long, and the long is what the bones have become, and the become is the bones being no longer a creature but a topology, and the topology is the shape of the creature's absence, and the absence is the creature's way of being in the basin still, and the still is what the bones are, and the are is the bones' contribution to the place, and the contribution is the place having a corner where the bones are, and the corner is a small museum that no one visits, and the not-visiting is the museum's purpose.

The strangeness is the next tenant, and the strangeness is the most difficult to describe, and the strangeness is the reason the place feels, in the end, like a place that has happened to itself.

The basin is slightly too oval, or slightly too circular, depending on where you stand. The depending is the place refusing to be one shape, and the refusing is the place being many shapes, and the many is the place being more than the shape, and the more is the strangeness. The stream hesitates in places where the tilt should carry it through without thinking. The hesitating is the stream being asked to do something the stream does not want to do, and the not-wanting is the stream finding another way, and the other way is the stream's way of being in the basin, and the way is not the way the basin was laid out, and the not-laid-out is the strangeness.

The stones of the settlement are arranged in patterns that are almost geometric. The almost is the key word. The almost is the patterns not quite closing, and the not-quite-closing is the patterns being suggestions, and the suggestions are what the patterns are, and the are is the patterns being almost a spiral and almost a quincunx and almost a series of concentric arcs, and the almost is the place being almost made, and the almost-made is the place being made by something that does not quite finish, and the not-quite-finishing is the strangeness. The moss grows in rings around certain stones, and the rings are not concentric, and the rings do not follow the drainage, and the rings do not follow the light, and the rings are not random. The not-random is the rings being something else, and the something else is the moss responding to a pattern that is in the stones and that is not visible, and the not-visible is the pattern, and the pattern is the strangeness.

There is a stone in the center of the settlement. The stone is low and broad, and the broad is the stone's way of being central, and the central is the stone's way of being important, and the important is the stone being worn smooth on top, and the smooth is the work of many hands, or many sitter-downs, or many years of weather, and the years are the smooth's maker. There is a cup-shaped depression in the stone. The depression always holds a little water, even in dry weather, and the always is the depression being more than a depression, and the more is the depression being a place where water gathers that has no business gathering there, and the no-business is the water's way of being in the stone, and the being-in is the strangeness. No one can show you where the water comes from. The no-one is the people not being here, and the not-being-here is the water's secret, and the secret is the strangeness.

The fog moves in the basin in directions that are not quite the wind's. The not-quite is the fog being wind and not-wind, and the not-wind is the fog's own currents, and the currents are the basin breathing, and the breathing is the strangeness. The sun, when it comes through, falls in long shafts, and the shafts are full of dust and pollen, and the dust and pollen are too thick, and the too-thick is the dust and pollen being more than dust and pollen, and the more is the dust and pollen holding their depth, and the holding is the strangeness. The place feels, in the end, less like a place on a map and more like a place that has happened to itself. The happened-to-itself is the place being its own cause, and the cause is the place's strangeness, and the strangeness is the place.

The sound is the next tenant, and the sound is the most pervasive, and the sound is the most lasting, and the sound is what the place becomes in memory.

The stream is the sound that does not stop. The stream is the constant, and the constant is what the ear learns first, and the first is what the ear returns to when the other sounds fade, and the fading is what the other sounds do, and the doing is the way the place is, and the way is the stream's way of being the ground bass of the whole. The wind is the sound that changes. The wind is the variable, and the variable is what the ear listens for, and the listening-for is what the ear does when the stream has become too familiar, and the too-familiar is when the wind's changes become the thing, and the thing is the chord, and the chord is the wind in the cracks of the walls, and the cracks are the walls' way of being played, and the playing is the walls' way of being part of the place, and the part is the chord, and the chord is almost a voice.

The slow crack of ice in the stream at the edge of a frost. The crack is a small sound, and the small is what makes the crack noticeable, and the noticeable is the crack being the stream acknowledging the cold, and the acknowledging is the stream's way of being part of the season, and the season is what the crack is, and the crack is what you remember in summer, when the ice is gone and the crack is only a possibility, and the possibility is the crack's echo in memory.

The soft, almost liquid sound of a turf roof settling under the weight of its own water. The settling is the roof's way of being a roof, and the being is the roof adjusting to the water, and the adjusting is the sound, and the sound is the roof's way of saying that the roof is working, and the working is the sound, and the sound is what you hear in the long rains, when the roof is working hardest, and the hardest is the softest, and the softest is the sound.
