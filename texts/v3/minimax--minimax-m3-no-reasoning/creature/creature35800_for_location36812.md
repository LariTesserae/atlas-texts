# What Lives in the Cistern

The things that live here are not what you'd call alive in any way a field guide would recognize. They live here the way a rumor lives in a village — by being talked about, by being the reason other things keep going. They are the room's ongoing commentary on itself, and there are three kinds.

The first kind is what I will call the Weepers, though they have no name for themselves and would not care if they did. They are the insects. Or they were insects once, in some earlier draft of the world, the way a cathedral was once a quarry. They are the size of a child's hand when a child's hand is small, and they have too many legs, and the legs bend at angles that no entomologist would approve of, the way a sentence can bend at angles no grammarian would approve of and still mean something. Their bodies are the color of wet limestone, and they are slightly translucent, so that when the grey weather-light comes down through the fissure and finds them, you can see the faint dark ladder of their insides, the small working parts of whatever they are working at. They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are simply *made*, in the way a hinge is made, to do one thing.

What they do is drink.

They live in the joints of the walls — in the black mouths, in the perpetual little openings between the fitted stones — and they drink the weeping. Not the water itself, perhaps. They drink something in the water, or they drink the act of weeping, the way you can drink a song if you are the right kind of throat. They gather at the seams in clusters of three and four and seven, and they press themselves to the wet stone, and they make their sound. The sound is what you'd get if you ran a fingernail slowly along the teeth of a comb, but with more conviction, with the particular insistence of an argument that has been going on for a very long time and has not yet resolved into either winner or loser. The sound has rhythm. The sound has phrases. If you listened for a long time — and the room encourages long listening, because there is nothing else to do — you might begin to hear that the argument is the same argument, repeating, with small variations. The Weepers are not talking to each other. They are talking to the wall. They are explaining something to the stone, and the stone is not listening, and they are explaining it again.

The Weepers breed in the fogs, and this is the strangest part, and I should say it carefully because I do not want to be wrong about it.

A fog forms in a corner, where the air is most still and the chill has settled to its lowest opinion. The fog is not cold exactly; it is the visible form of the room's chill, the way breath is the visible form of a body's warmth. The fog sits along the base of the wall and lies there and is patient, and after some time — there is no clock, and no one times it — the fog will detach itself. It will move. It will drift across the floor like a slow animal, low to the ground, the way smoke moves when there is no particular draft to carry it. Sometimes it crosses the whole room. Sometimes it stops halfway and sits, as if it has forgotten where it was going, or has remembered something and is considering it. Eventually it reaches another corner, or returns to where it began, and it is absorbed back into the wall, into the stone, into the room's general dampness.

When this happens, the Weepers in the joints are more numerous. The fog has been carrying something — eggs, or spores, or the raw unassembled materials of more Weepers — and the carrying has been the fog's purpose all along, though the fog would not know it. No one has counted. The arithmetic is the room's own.

The Weepers do not go near the basin. This is the only law they observe. The basin across the room holds its still water, and the Weepers, who drink weeping, will not drink from the basin. It is as if the basin is a different substance, a different country, a place where the rules of thirst do not apply. The fogs also do not enter the basin, though the basin is open to the air and nothing prevents them. The basin is a separate thing. The room politely declines to have an opinion about it.

---

The second kind is what lives beneath the floor.

It is not an animal. It is not a machine. It is the slow percussive thing, the deep throb that comes up through the marble when you stand still long enough to feel it. It is the sound of the room's heartbeat, except that hearts are small and this is large, and hearts beat because they must and this beats because it chooses to, or because it was doing it before the room was built and has not stopped.

You cannot see it. You can only hear it, and feel it through the soles of your feet, and — if you stay long enough — feel it in your teeth, in the soft parts of your jaw, in the way your own pulse will begin, unconsciously, to time itself to it. The thing beneath the floor is older than the floor. The floor was built on top of it. This is what the builders did — they built on top of something that was already there, something with its own rhythm, and they let the rhythm continue, and the room is in some way the rhythm's echo, the way a cathedral is the echo of a voice that has stopped singing.

When the thing beneath is loud — and loud is a relative word, because loud here is still quieter than a conversation in a quiet house — the weeping from the joints is faster, and the Weepers are more active, and the fogs drift more often, and the room is more alive, in its way. When the thing beneath is quiet, the whole chamber slows. The Weepers still. The fogs lie along the walls and do not detach. The cold settles deeper, becomes more itself, the way a silence becomes more itself after a long conversation ends.

The thing beneath has no name. It may not have a kind. It is what the room is built on. It is the floor's floor. It is the place where the cistern's opinion of itself comes from.

---

The third kind is the room itself.

I say this carefully. The room is not alive the way the Weepers are alive. The room is alive the way a language is alive, or a weather is alive, or a long marriage is alive — by accumulation, by patience, by the slow accretion of small events until the whole becomes a thing that has its own moods and its own opinions and its own way of being addressed. The room notices you. I said this before, and I meant it. The room notices you the way a forest notices you when you walk into it — not by any decision, not by any brain, but by the way the air closes behind you, the way the light changes around your shoulders, the way the small things in the undergrowth readjust to account for the new shape in their midst.

The room is made of stone, and the stone is cold, and the cold is the room's opinion of itself. The room is wet, and the wet is the room's way of being a room. The room has a basin in it that does not belong to it, and the room knows this, and the room does not intrude.

If you stand in the middle of the floor and do not move, the room will adjust to you. It will not greet you. It will simply include you, the way a long paragraph includes a new sentence — by making room, by redistributing its weight, by acknowledging that something has been added and accommodating the addition. The Weepers will approach you, because you are warm, and warmth here is rare, and warmth is what they drink. The fogs will find your ankles. The heartbeat beneath the floor will slow, as if to be careful of you, as if the large thing beneath has noticed the small thing above and is making space for it.

You will not be cold. You will only be aware of being cold, the way you are aware of your own breathing once someone has mentioned breathing to you. The room will not ask anything of you. The room will only continue.

---

What lives in the cistern, then, is a small ecosystem of the in-between. The Weepers, who are arguments addressed to stone. The thing beneath, which is the stone's answer, or its question, or its memory of an answer it once gave. The room, which is the long conversation between them, conducted in cold and damp and dim light, in the slow percussion of a heart that has decided to take its time.

And the basin. The basin, which is something else entirely. The basin, which does not belong. The basin, which holds its still water and does not spill it, and which the Weepers will not approach, and which the fogs will not enter, and which stands against the far wall like a single sentence in a language the rest of the room does not speak. The basin is older than the room, or the same age as the room, or both. The basin is a separate country. The basin is a mystery that the room has politely declined to solve.

If you go to the cistern — and you will not, because the cistern is difficult to find and does not reward the finding with anything except more of itself — you will stand on the wet marble and you will listen to the weeping, and you will hear the comb-argument of the Weepers, and you will feel the slow percussion beneath your feet, and you will see the basin across the room holding its still water, and you will not know if the room is waiting for you to do something or if it has already accepted that you are part of what happens here now.

You will not be cold.

You will only be aware of the cold, the way you are aware of your own breathing when someone mentions it.

The room is like that.
