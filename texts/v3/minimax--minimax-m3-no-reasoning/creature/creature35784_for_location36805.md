# The Inhabitants of the Stone

What lives here lives by patience. This is the first thing to understand about the plateau — that the creatures who have stayed are the creatures who have agreed to its tempo. Everything that required urgency has gone. Everything that required warmth, or depth, or rain, has gone. What remains is a community arranged around a single, shared virtue: the willingness to be unhurried in a place that does not hurry.

## The Herd

The antelope-shaped ones are the largest bodies, and so they are the first you notice, though you are not meant to. They graze at the edges of vision, scattered across the gentle slopes in a constellation so loose it is almost an arrangement of stones. A hundred of them, perhaps more. Their coats are the exact color of the plateau — that warm, dusty grey, the color of dried blood or long-closed windows — and when they stand still among the tussocks they vanish, and when they move they move like shadows. You cannot tell at first whether the dark shapes on the slope are animals or rocks. This is not camouflage in the way we usually mean it. It is participation. They have allowed themselves to take on the color of the place because they have been here long enough that their color and the place's color have become a single fact.

They are smaller than true antelope. They have thick, almost shaggy coats, ill-suited to speed. They do not run. When something disturbs them — a shadow, a sound, the rare footfall of something larger than themselves — they lift their heads in unison, and then they move, but they move with a deliberateness that is not flight. It is more like a tide adjusting. They resettle themselves, a hundred paces further along the slope, and resume. Their hooves leave marks in the dust, and the dust holds the marks for days, and the marks are part of the place now, another layer in the slow accumulation.

They eat the tussocks. Only the tussocks. Their teeth are worn in a particular way, and their stomachs, you can tell by the way they stand after grazing, are built for the slow digestion of stiff, fibrous, mineral-tasting grass. They do not seem to need water. Perhaps they lick the condensation from the stones in the early morning. You never see them drink. They are creatures who have learned to pull their moisture from the air, the way the lichen does, the way the cushion-plants do.

They mate, you assume, though you do not see it. The young appear in spring, smaller, darker, with legs too long for their bodies, and they grow into the color of the place within a season. The young are quiet. The adults are quiet. The herd makes almost no sound. When they move it is the soft percussion of hooves on stone, and a low, collective breathing, and sometimes, in the late afternoon, a single throat-clearing sound from one of them, almost inaudible, like a stone settling.

They migrate. You would not know to look. But there is a season when the constellation shifts its center of gravity, and the slope they were on a month ago is empty, and the next slope over is full. They are following something — the growth of the grass, perhaps, or the temperature of the stone, or some older instruction that has nothing to do with either. They will come back. They have always come back. The cycle is longer than a human can comfortably observe, but the animals are not human and they have no need to observe it. They are inside it.

## The Lizards

The small one, the length of a finger, lives on the warm slabs. It is not the only one. There are dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, scattered across the plateau's exposed stone. They are the color of the place. They are the color of the place more perfectly than anything else here — when one lies flat on a slab in the noon light it is invisible, and only the slight movement of its breathing gives it away. They have understood the sun better than any other inhabitant. In the early morning they are torpid, slow, dark against the cold stone, and they orient themselves broadside to whatever sliver of warmth is available. By midday they are kinetic, darting across the slabs in pursuit of the small insects that have also emerged into the heat. In the late afternoon they flatten again, and their color drains back into the stone.

They watch with one eye at a time. This is a habit of creatures that have to be aware in two directions at once — up, where the birds are, and across, where the larger predators might be. There are no large predators here, but the habit persists. The lizard's eye is round, golden, with a black pupil that contracts to a slit in the bright light. It does not blink. It watches you with the patience of something that has been watching things for a long time and has found that watching is its primary occupation.

They eat beetles. They eat the smaller insects. They are eaten, occasionally, by the birds, though the birds do not seem to specialize in them. A lizard who has escaped a season has earned its color, and its wariness, and its way of lying flat against the warm stone and becoming, for a few hours, indistinguishable from the place itself.

## The Beetles

The metallic-bronze beetles move in single file. This is the first thing you notice, and it is the only thing you need to notice. They cross the stone in lines so straight they look drawn. A dozen beetles, sometimes more, traveling from one crack to another, from one concavity to the next pool, in a procession that does not vary by a body's width from one hour to the next. They have worn their own paths into the stone. The grooves are faint, almost invisible unless you crouch and let your eye follow them, but they are there. The beetles have been walking these paths longer than the standing stone has been standing. They are the oldest commute on the plateau.

Their shells catch the light. In the even noon sun they flash, briefly, as they cross an open slab — bronze, copper, a color that does not belong to the plateau and that has been allowed to stay anyway, the way a single loud lichen is allowed to stay among the muted greys. They do not fly. Their wings, if they have them, are folded tight under their shells. They walk. They walk and walk and walk, and the only sound they make is the faint tick of their feet on the stone, and even that is so small it is more felt than heard.

What they are doing, exactly, no one knows. They seem to be going somewhere, and then arriving, and then going again. They meet at the pools. They cluster at the edges of the damp, where the stone is greener, and they lower their heads, and they drink. Or eat. Or both. The damp is their pasture, in its way — the films of algae and the small things that live in the films. They farm it. They do not farm it, but they tend it, by moving across it steadily, by distributing themselves, by being present.

## The Centipede-Thing

It flows. There is no other word for it. It flows across the path and is gone. It is the color of dried blood, and it is longer than a centipede ought to be, and it has too many legs, and its motion is not the stop-start motion of a centipede but the continuous, sinuous motion of something that is not quite a snake and not quite an arthropod and not quite anything that has a clean name. It moves the way water moves when it has been poured carefully down a slope — finding the path, taking the path, not hesitating.

It is rare to see. You see one a day, perhaps, if you are watching. It appears from under a stone and crosses a stretch of open ground and disappears under another stone, and the only evidence it was there is the slight parting of dust, and a faint, dry whisper of many feet.

It eats things. You do not see it eat. You find, sometimes, the small emptied husks of beetles, or the soft remains of a lizard that did not move fast enough. The lizards know it. When one is near, the lizards flatten harder against the stone, and their color deepens, and they become, for a few minutes, indistinguishable from the dust itself. The beetles scatter, briefly, and reform. The birds do not eat it, or cannot. It is too fast for the birds, and too low, and too hidden.

What it does when it is not crossing open ground, no one knows. It lives under the stones. It lives in the cracks. It is part of the dark underside of the plateau, the part that does not see the sun, and when it comes up it comes up briefly, and then it goes back down, and the surface seals over its passage as if it had never been.

## The Birds

They are in the air. They are always in the air. They ride the thermals with their long wings held flat, and they do not call, and they circle. A dozen in the air at any moment, sometimes more, sometimes a great many more — a loose spiraling, high above the plateau, drifting on the columns of warm air that rise from the sun-warmed stone. They are not hawks. They are not vultures. They are something else, something with long narrow wings and a tail that opens like a pair of shears, and they hang in the air the way a thought hangs in a mind.

Their silence is the strangest thing about them. You expect, from a bird, a call. You expect a territory. You expect a song. These birds have no song. When they speak — and they do speak, sometimes — it is a single note, repeated, low, almost more felt than heard, a vibration that sits in the chest rather than the ear. It is not a song. It is a confirmation. They are saying, to one another, that they are still there.

They are watching. From their height they can see the entire plateau, or most of it — the herd on the slope, the line of beetles crossing a slab, the lizard flattened in the sun, the dark pools. They see everything. They are the eyes of the place, perhaps, the way the lichen is the skin and the beetles are the circulation and the herd is the memory. They do not strike often. A bird will fold its wings and drop, suddenly, from a great height, and there will be a small explosion of dust, and then the bird will be back in the air, and something will be missing from the ground below. A beetle. A small lizard. A young one from the herd, perhaps, though this is rare. They are not hungry, exactly. They are completing a cycle. They are part of the slow turning of the place.

## The Pool-Things

In the oldest pools, the dark ones, the ones that are the color of weak tea, there is life. You have to kneel to see it. You have to put your face close to the still surface and look in. There are small shapes moving in the water — shapes without obvious names, shapes that are more suggestion than form, that drift at the edges where the algae rims the pool in green and rust. They are not fish. They do not have the architecture of fish. They are something more ancient, more patient, more committed to stillness. They hang in the water the way the birds hang in the air. They exist in suspension.

They are very small. They are transparent, or near to it. They are the color of the water. You would not know they were there except for the slight motion of them, a trembling at the edge of the pool, and the way the dust on the surface moves when they rise to it. They eat the algae. They eat the films. They are eaten by nothing that you can see. They are the deepest inhabitants, in a sense — the ones most committed to the slow rhythms of the place, the ones who have agreed most thoroughly to its timescales.

The pools they live in are very old. The water does not move. It evaporates and is replenished by the condensation each morning, and the small creatures in it live through the cycles of evaporation and replenishment without disturbance. A pool that has dried up entirely is rare. When it happens, the creatures in it are gone. When the pool returns — and the pools do return, the concavities fill again — they return too, or their descendants do, or something like them does, and the pool resumes its quiet tenancy.

## The Lichen

It is not, strictly, an inhabitant. It is more than an inhabitant. It is the slow skin of the plateau, and it moves across the stone at a pace that is not pace at all, that is more like the turning of a page. The mats of it spread in colors that would be loud if there were more of them — the deep orange of one species, the pale grey-green of another, the black of a third, and the white of a fourth, all of them crusty and dry and alive in the way that stone can be alive, which is to say: barely, and for a long time.

It does not have a generation. It does not have a body. It is a community of very small lives arranged in a single continuous surface, and it eats the stone, slowly, and what it eats becomes part of it, and what it becomes is part of the place. The lichen is the closest thing to a native. It was here before the herd. It was here before the beetles. It was here when the mountain was a mountain. It has been here, in some form or another, since the wearing-down began, and it will be here, in some form or another, after everything else has gone.

It does not think. It does not watch. It does not hurry. But it is the patience of the place made visible, made colorful, made something you can see with your eye and almost feel with your hand, if you are willing to kneel on the stone and let your palm rest against it for a while. It is cool in the morning and warm in the afternoon and cool again at dusk, and it is patient, and it is here.

## The Cushion-Plants, and the Stemless Yellow Flower

Between the stones, in the cracks and the small concavities where a little dust has gathered, the cushion-plants sit. They are tight, rounded, hard to the touch. They are the color of the place, or near it — a grey-green that does not draw the eye. They do not flower often, or visibly. They exist as a presence, as a fact. They hold the dust. They hold the moisture. They are the small pockets of soil that the lichen and the grass depend on, and without them the plateau would be a little more bare, and a little less alive.

The grass — the stiff, blue-green tussock — emerges from the cracks in a way that suggests it has been pushing for a long time. It grows in clumps, each clump a small fist of blades, each blade wiry and mineral-tasting. The herd eats it down to the quick in some places, and it grows back, slowly, from the roots. It is the only true pasture on the plateau, and it is rationed out, and the rationing is part of the slow deal the animals have made with the place. They do not take more than grows back.

And the yellow flower. You see it once, between two stones, pushing a single head up with no stem at all, as if the head had decided to be there and the stem had decided not to bother. It is a bright, anachronistic yellow — the only yellow for a hundred paces in any direction — and it nods slightly in the still air, and it is open, and it is facing the sun, and it is the loudest thing on the plateau. It will be there for a few days. Then it will be gone. The place does not notice. The place is patient with its flowers. It lets them come, and it lets them go, and it does not grieve.

## The Builders

They are gone. Whatever smoothed the stone in a direction the wind would not have chosen, whatever dragged the heavy thing or walked on claws across the flat face, whatever set the ring of stones in a circle that is not quite a circle, whatever raised the standing stone and arranged the smaller stones around it in a way that points somewhere or away from somewhere — they are not here. They have been gone long enough that the making and the unmaking have become the same process. The ring sits in its circle. The standing stone stands. The grooves in the stone are worn deeper, now, by the wind, and the edges have softened, and in another thousand seasons the grooves will be invisible, and in another ten thousand the standing stone will have fallen, and the ring will have spread, and the place will have absorbed the trouble entirely.

But the trouble is still here. You can feel it, if you sit inside the ring, in the slightly darker air. You can read it, in the way the shadow of the standing stone lands. The builders made a mark, and the mark is being slowly erased, and the erasing is the mark's final form. The plateau does not remember them. The plateau is what they did. They are part of the residue now, the way the bones of an animal are part of the dust, the way the dust is part of the stone.

The herd grazes among their ruins without noticing. The birds circle over them without comment. The lichen climbs the standing stone without hesitation. The beetles walk their straight lines across the grooves that were made before them, and the grooves are part of the beetle-path now, and the beetle-path is part of the stone, and the stone is part of the plateau, and the plateau is part of whatever the builders were trying to say.

## The Whole, Together

This is the community. It is not a harmony. It is more like a long, slow negotiation that has been going on for so long that the terms have been forgotten and only the negotiation remains. The lichen eats the stone and the beetles eat the lichen and the birds eat the beetles and the herd eats the grass and the grass eats the dust and the dust is the stone and the stone is the plateau and the plateau is, was, will be. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is in excess. The pools evaporate and the moisture comes back. The herd grazes and the grass grows back. The builders made their mark and the wind erases it and the erasing is the mark. It is a closed economy, and it is a quiet one, and it has been running for longer than anything here can remember.

The visitor is not part of it. The visitor is too fast, too warm, too loud. The visitor leaves a footprint in the dust, and the dust holds it for a few days, and then the wind takes it back, and the place closes over the impression as if it had never been. This is not hostility. It is the ordinary business of the plateau. It has made places of things for a very long time, and it will continue to make places of things for a very long time, and the visitor is one of the things, and the visitor will be made a place of, gently, and the plateau will not say so, and the plateau does not need to.

What lives here lives by patience. What lives here has agreed to be unhurried. What lives here is part of a slow turning that does not include the visitor, and that is all right, because the slow turning has been going on for a long time, and it will go on for a long time, and the visitor is only here for now, and now is, on this plateau, a very small thing.
