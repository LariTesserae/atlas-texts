# The Inhabitants of the Hearth-Sink

The people who live in the Hearth-Sink are not numerous. There are perhaps fifty adults who work, a dozen children who will work, and a small uncertain number of the very old who have, in some sense, stopped working without quite having stopped being alive. They do not have a name for themselves as a group. When they need to refer to themselves collectively — which is rarely — they say *we here*, or simply *us*, and the pronoun is sufficient, because no one else is addressed.

## What They Look Like

They are pale. This is the first thing an outsider would notice, and the last thing they themselves would think to mention. The bowl cuts the angle of the sun so steeply that even at midday the light is filtered through haze and wet air, and the body, left to its own preferences, makes little of the pigment it cannot use. Their skin is the color of the wet limestone walls — a yellowed ivory, a faint buff, sometimes almost the gray of old brick. They do not tan. They cannot tan. They are like mushrooms grown under a stone, or the soft underside of a leaf.

They are thin, but not wasted. The heat keeps the fat off them, and the work — there is always work — keeps the muscle on them in particular places. Forearms. Shoulders. The small of the back. Their hands are the most worked part of them, and the most recognizable. The knuckles are swollen, slightly. The fingernails are often broken short, because nails catch on the iron and the brick and the wet rope. The palms are calloused in patterns that correspond to the tools they have held for decades: a smooth band across the base of the fingers for the hammer-hand, a hardened patch at the heel of the thumb for the wrench-grip, a thickening along the outer edge of the palm for the shovel-shaft. You could, in theory, tell what a man does by looking at his hands from across the bowl, though you would have to know the work first.

Their eyes are large, in the way that eyes become large in dim places. Not dramatically so. Just slightly larger, slightly more liquid, than the eyes of someone raised in open sun. The whites are not white — they are the faint yellow of old paper, of tea, of the haze itself. Some of them, the older ones, have a permanent squint that has nothing to do with brightness and everything to do with the way the moisture settles on the skin around the eyes and makes them water. They wipe their faces with the backs of their wrists. They have always wiped their faces with the backs of their wrists.

Their hair is dark, mostly, and the men keep it cropped close because the heat would make it otherwise unbearable. The women wear theirs longer but pinned up, knotted, wrapped, never loose. A loose hair in the Hearth-Sink is a hair in a gear, in a valve, in the mouth of something that will then spit. Loose hair does not survive a childhood here.

Their clothes are minimal and they are not ashamed of this, though they are not brazen either. The men wear trousers, sometimes a shirt, often neither when neither is required. The women wear shifts, light cottons, things that have been washed so many times they have the texture of the wet air itself. Footwear is a practicality: heavy boots for those who work the rail and the dock; soft-soled shoes for those who move quietly between machines. Almost everyone owns a hat of some kind — a brimmed thing, a cap, a piece of folded cloth — because the heat on the bare scalp, even in dim light, is a particular kind of punishment.

They smell of warm oil and of the faint sweet rot of the standing water and of themselves, which is to say of sweat that has dried on skin and been re-wet and dried again so many times that the salt has become part of the skin itself. They do not smell bad. They smell like a place.

## How They Move

Slowly. This is the first correction the place makes in a newcomer. The body wants to move quickly, to get out of the heat, to do the thing and be done. The place will not allow it. The air is too thick. The surfaces are too slick. The work is too constant and too unchanging to be hurried. Within a week, a newcomer slows. Within a month, the slow has become the only speed they know. They walk as if the air itself has weight, and indeed it does. Their strides are short. Their shoulders are dropped. They lean against walls, against doorframes, against each other, in postures that look casual but are actually structural — the body finding a way to do less work in a place that punishes work.

When they work, they work with a steadiness that looks, to an outsider, like trance. The man with the hammer does not swing the hammer. He brings it up, and he lets it fall, and the fall is the part that does the work. The woman who tends the gauge does not watch the gauge. She reads it with her peripheral vision while doing something else, and only turns her head to it when something has changed, which is almost never. The children who carry small loads from one place to another do not run. They walk, and the loads sway, and the sway has a rhythm, and the rhythm is the rhythm of the place.

They sit, when they sit, with a particular heaviness. They sit the way people sit who have been standing for hours. The whole body comes down at once, and once down, it stays. They do not fidget. They do not cross and uncross their legs. They sit and they are where they are.

## The Sound of Their Voices

The bowl is loud, and the people of the bowl have made their voices to fit the bowl. They speak at a volume that would be a shout in an open field but is here simply a sentence. They do not modulate. The man on the walkway does not lower his voice when he is addressing the man on the ground beneath him; the sound is the same either way, because the steam and the water would swallow the difference. They speak in fragments, because fragments are what survive. A full sentence is a luxury. *Coal's low.* *Gauge is holding.* *Tell her I'm coming.* *Not yet.* *No, the other one.*

The children speak like this almost from birth. The very old speak like this almost until death. There is a small middle generation, the ones who came in from outside once, decades ago, who sometimes attempt a longer sentence, and it sounds, in the bowl, like a strange bird call — too elaborate, too soft, belonging to some other place.

They do not sing. There is no music in the Hearth-Sink. There are rhythms — the engine, the hammer, the rail, the hiss — but they are not human rhythms and the people do not try to make them so. A man will sometimes tap a foot or a hand to the engine's pulse, but this is unconscious, the way a person taps to their own heartbeat.

## The Workers

There are several kinds of work, and the people are divided among them in a way that has been stable for so long that no one can remember a time when it was otherwise.

**The engine-tenders** are the innermost circle. There are three of them, working in shifts that do not really correspond to day and night, because the engine does not sleep and someone must always be near it. They live in the deep brick buildings, where the electric light is and the air is warmest. They are the only people who have seen the engine itself clearly, and even they speak of it in a way that suggests they have not quite seen it — *the thing*, *it*, *the works*. They come out into the upper bowl rarely, and when they do, they blink in the half-light as if it were too bright, which it is not. They smell of hot metal and of the particular oil used in the deep valves. Their hands are the most marked of anyone's.

**The rail-men** work the small line at the bottom of the bowl. There are six or seven of them. They drive the squat locomotives and shunt the cars and couple and uncouple the loads. They are the ones most often seen by an outsider, because the rail is the most visible activity. They wear heavier boots than anyone, and they have a particular way of walking that is not quite a waddle but acknowledges the slickness of the rails. They are loud, by the standards of the bowl — they have to be, to be heard over their own locomotives. They shout to each other across cars and across the dock. Their profanity is elaborate and entirely without heat; it is a technical vocabulary, like the names of the rail-cars themselves.

**The dock-workers** are the ones who load and unload at the tunnel mouth. The tunnel goes into the limestone wall and disappears, and no one in the bowl knows where it goes or where the loads come from or who is at the other end. The dock-workers do not wonder about this, or if they once wondered, they no longer do. They take what comes off the cars and they put what goes on. Ore, clay, wet lumber, sometimes other things in crates that are nailed shut. They are the strongest of the workers, broad through the shoulders and the back, and they are the sweatiest. Their work is in the wettest part of the bowl, near the constant seep of the wall.

**The pipe-walkers** are the ones who tend the pipe network along the walls and under the walkways. There are four of them, and they are thin even by the standards of the place. They carry wrenches and they move along the iron walkways with the sureness of people who have done so for years. They are the most likely to be seen at height, bent over a joint, listening to a leak, tightening something by fractions. They know the pipe network the way a sailor knows the rigging — by feel, by the particular sound of each section, by the temperature of the metal at certain hours. When a pipe bursts — which is rare — it is a pipe-walker who finds it first, often by the change in the air before the change in the sound.

**The kiln-tenders.** The kilns are said to be abandoned, in the description of the place, but this is not quite right. They are mostly abandoned. There is one kiln, the smallest, set into the eastern wall, that is still tended by an old woman and her grown son. They fire brick from the local clay, slowly, in the old way, and the bricks they make are not used for new construction — there is no new construction — but for repair. The bricks replace other bricks. The kiln-tenders are the closest thing the place has to an inheritance, and the bricks they make are the closest thing it has to a continuity that is visible to the eye. The old woman's name, when it is used, is *Marra*, and her son is called *the lad*, though he is fifty-three.

**The tenders of the living.** A few of the women, and a few of the men, do the work that is not work but must be done. They cook, in the low-ceilinged rooms above the brick buildings, on stoves that vent into chimneys that vent into the haze. They wash, in the channels of water that run everywhere, which is convenient but also unavoidable, because the channels are where the water is. They mend the clothes. They look after the children, who are not so much raised as deposited in the cooler rooms during the day and retrieved at the hour when the light begins to fail. They tend the sick, though there is rarely sickness — the place is too steady for sickness, too much in equilibrium. They bury the dead, which is more common than sickness but still not common; deaths are spaced out, and when one comes, it is noted, and the body is taken up to the rim of the bowl, because the dead should not be kept in the wet earth.

## The Children

The children are born here. This is worth saying plainly, because an outsider might assume that the workers come and go, that there is some rotation, that the place is staffed from elsewhere. There is no rotation. The children are born here and they grow here and they become workers here and they die here. There has been no one born in the Hearth-Sink in the last fourteen months, which is not unusual; there are sometimes gaps of two years between births, and sometimes clusters. The children who are here now are seven in number, ranging from an infant carried on a hip to a girl of perhaps eleven who has already begun to learn the rail.

The children are paler than the adults, if anything. They move more quickly than the adults, because the body has not yet been fully taught by the place. They run, sometimes, when there is nothing to be done. They are not discouraged from running. They are not encouraged either. They learn. They play small games in the channels and on the rail ties, games that involve balance and throwing and the catching of the small dark lizards, which they do not harm but simply hold for a while and then release. They are loud, by the standards of the bowl, but their loudness is different from the workers' loudness — it has more variation in it, more peaks and valleys, more laughter. The laughter is the most surprising sound in the Hearth-Sink. It comes unexpectedly, from a doorway, from under a walkway, and it cuts through the texture of the place like a knife through wet cloth, and then it stops, and the texture closes over it again.

The children do not ask about outside. They do not ask where the tunnel goes, or what is beyond the rim of the bowl, or what the sky looks like without the haze. They have no word for *outside*, or rather they do, but the word refers only to the causeway that leads up and out, and beyond that, to a vague notion of more plain, and beyond that, to nothing. They are not curious about it. They will leave the Hearth-Sink exactly once in their lives, if at all — at the moment of death, when the body is carried up to the rim.

## The Old

There are perhaps four old ones still living in the Hearth-Sink, though the count is uncertain because they are rarely seen. They live in the uppermost ironwork, in rooms that are not quite rooms — cubbies, really, with thin walls of riveted plate and small windows that look out over the bowl from above. They were workers once. They are not workers now, and they are not quite anything else. They sit. They watch. They receive food from the tenders of the living, and they eat it slowly, and they are still there the next day, and the next.

The oldest of them is a man whose name no one uses anymore, because he no longer answers to it. He is very thin. His hands lie in his lap like tools that have been put down for the last time. He watches the bowl from his window, and his eyes move, but they do not seem to be tracking anything. He has been here, by the reckoning of the others, for as long as anyone remembers, and the reckoning of the others goes back three generations. He may have been here when the iron was added. He may have been here before the iron. He does not say. He does not say anything, almost ever. Once, in the memory of the living, he spoke, and what he said was: *It is the same.* And then he did not speak again for a long time.

The old ones are not revered, exactly. They are not neglected either. They are allowed to be what they are, which is to say, the place's way of showing itself to itself over a longer span than a single life can hold. When one of them dies, the body is carried up to the rim, and the workers stop for a quarter of an hour, and the engine continues, and then the work resumes.

## The Cats

The cats are not pets. They are workers, as the description says, and they work the rats that thrive in the damp grain stores. There are perhaps a dozen of them in the bowl at any time. They are of no particular breed, or rather they are of the breed that the place makes — short-haired, dark, thick-bodied, with the slightly flattened faces of animals that have been bred, by circumstance rather than intention, for the heat. They do not mew. They do not purr audibly. They move through the bowl with the same slow certainty as the people, and they are treated with the same matter-of-fact respect. They are fed. They are allowed to sleep where they will. They are not named. A child will sometimes point at a particular cat and say *that one*, and the phrase will be used by others, but it does not stick.

The cats and the lizards have an arrangement. The lizards are too large for the cats to bother with often, and the cats are too warm for the lizards to fear. They share the warm stones. They share the channels. A cat will sometimes watch a lizard with the particular focused attention of an animal that is not hunting but is also not *not* hunting, and the lizard will watch back, and nothing will come of it. The cats have been seen to drink from the channels. The cats have been seen to sit on the rail ties as the small locomotives pass, inches away, and not move.

The cats have kittens, sometimes, in the spring — though what *spring* means in a place without seasons is unclear. The kittens are dark and small and they mew, and the mewing is the only sound in the Hearth-Sink that seems to belong to no one.

## How They Are With Each Other

The people of the Hearth-Sink are not unfriendly. They are not friendly, either, in the way that word is usually used. They are *together*, in a way that is prior to either. They have lived in the same bowl, hearing the same engine, seeing the same lizards, for their whole lives. They do not need to negotiate. They do not need to introduce. They know each other's hands, each other's gaits, each other's particular silhouette against the haze. Two men who have not spoken in a year can stand on the same walkway and feel no need to speak. The not-speaking is not a failure of communication. It is communication, complete and settled.

There is no violence in the Hearth-Sink. This is not because violence has been forbidden, or because the people have agreed to peace. It is because there is nothing to fight about. The work is fixed. The place is fixed. The food is shared. The water is everywhere. A fight would require a difference of interest, and there is no difference of interest that the bowl can produce. When two workers disagree — about a coupling, about a pipe, about the right way to load a car — the disagreement is brief, technical, and resolved by reference to the thing itself. The thing itself does not change. The disagreement cannot continue.

There is love in the Hearth-Sink, of a kind. Not the love of open places — not the love of long letters and songs and dramatic declarations. The love of the bowl is the love of proximity, of long acquaintance, of having been warmed by the same wall. A man will put his hand on a woman's back as she bends over a stove, and the hand will stay there for a moment, and then it will be removed, and nothing will have been said, and the moment will not be referred to again, and it will have been enough. The children are held. The old are fed. The dead are carried up. These are the gestures, and they are sufficient.

## What They Think of the Place

They do not think of it. This is the truest thing that can be said about the people of the Hearth-Sink, and it is the hardest for an outsider to credit.

An outsider stands at the rim and sees a place — a strange place, a heavy place, a place of heat and wet and constant noise and dim light. An outsider thinks: *How do they bear it?* An outsider thinks: *Do they dream of leaving?* An outsider thinks: *What do they make of their lives, here, in this bowl?*

The people of the bowl do not ask these questions, not because they have answered them and found the answers grim, but because the questions do not occur to them. The place is not a thing they are in. The place is the medium they are made of, the way water is the medium a fish is made of. A fish does not dream of leaving the water. A fish does not dream of water. A fish simply swims.

They do not call the place *the Hearth-Sink*. They call it *here*. They call it *the bowl*, sometimes. They call it *down*, when speaking to someone at the rim, which means *down here*. They do not have a name for it the way a visitor has a name for it, because a name is something you give to a thing that is not you, and the bowl is not a thing that is not them. It is the thing they are inside of, and also the thing they are made of, and the difference between those two has not occurred to them to remark upon.

## What They Would Say, If Asked

If you were to descend into the bowl, and find a worker, and ask — politely, or even impolitely — *what is it like to live here?*, you would get one of a few responses.

The most common would be no response at all. The question would not register as a question, because it would not register as something that needed answering. You would have asked a fish about water, and the fish would have looked at you with its large wet eyes and gone back to whatever it was doing.

If you pressed, you might get: *It's the same.* This is what the oldest old man said, the one time he spoke. It is the most articulate thing that can be said about the place from inside the place. *It's the same.* Not *it is the same as it was*. Not *it is always the same*. Just *the same*. The present tense, undifferentiated. The place, the life, the work, the light, the sound, the water — all of it, *the same*. A single word that holds everything, and explains nothing, and is not meant to.

If you pressed further, and the worker was the kind who could be pressed further, you might get: *It suits.* Which is a phrase you will hear in the Hearth-Sink, used of clothing, of tools, of food, of anything that has been found to fit. *It suits.* Meaning: it does what it is supposed to do, and I do what I am supposed to do, and the two things meet, and there is no gap, and the gap is not missed. *It suits.* A phrase that contains no complaint and no praise. A phrase that is, itself, the sound of the place: steady, warm, undemanding, complete.

And if you were to ask a child — and you would have to ask a child, because only a child would still find the question strange enough to actually consider it — the child might look at you for a long moment, and then look at the channel of water running past your feet, and then look back at you, and say: *What do you mean?*

And the question would be genuine. And you would have no answer. And the steam would hiss, and the water would run, and the lizard on the warm stone would not move, and the engine somewhere deep would thud its single note, and the child would wait, and the waiting would be patient, and the patience would be the patience of the place itself, which is the patience of something that has found its shape and intends to keep it.
