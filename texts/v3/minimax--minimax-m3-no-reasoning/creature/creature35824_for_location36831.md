# The Inhabitants of the Infracrust

What lives here is not a fauna in the sense you would recognize, and calling it one is already a concession to the inadequacy of the language you carried down. It is closer to say that a **life** lives here — a single, continuous, distributed enterprise of becoming, partitioned by convenience into bodies the way a sentence is partitioned into words, but not really separable from itself. The stone is warm because they are warm. The seams are busy because they are the seams. The place is loud with them the way a body is loud with its own digestion: from the inside, a roar; from the outside, a faint tremor you might mistake for something else.

There are, nonetheless, kinds.

---

## The Dust Riders

The smallest are the size of motes in a sunbeam, and for a long time you will think they are motes. They are not. They have bodies — chitinous, faintly luminous, given a color that is almost but not quite green-gold, the green-gold of the water in the brimming basins, which is to say the color of whatever they are made of, which is to say they are made of the place, which is to say the place has learned to make things that look like this.

They do not land. They ride the air above the warmer stone in slow, patient columns, and the columns are not random — they have a structure, a circulation, the way smoke from a chimney has a circulation, except that what is being moved is not heat but the dust riders themselves, and what is driving the movement is not buoyancy but something else. Hunger, perhaps. Tastings of the air. A continuous, low-grade sampling of whatever volatiles the warm stone is giving off, which the dust riders consume, or are consumed by, or both.

You breathe them without noticing. This is not a problem. They have been breathed for a long time.

They are not individuals. You will want to call them individuals because you are looking at them with eyes that were built for distinguishing one thing from another, and they look like one thing from another. They are not. They are more like the cells of a tissue that has decided, for now, to drift. A column of dust riders, viewed at the right scale and the right speed, is a single organ of the infracrust, extended upward into the air the way a lung extends into the throat.

They do not die the way you would recognize death. They come apart, eventually, and the parts are taken back into the rest of them, and the rest of them is the rest of the place.

---

## The Flat Dark Runners

These are the ones whose clicking you hear first, because they are the loudest, and they are loudest because there are so many of them.

They are flat. They are dark. They have too many legs, but the legs fold against the body when they are not walking, so that at rest a runner looks like a chip of stone with a faint iridescence along the lateral edge, and you have probably stepped on a few without knowing. Their bodies are segmented in a way that suggests a memory of something older than themselves — something with a backbone, perhaps, or something that once had opinions about front and back — but the segmentation is decorative now, a scar from a lineage the runners do not discuss.

They run.

That is mostly what they do. They run along the seams of warm wax-glass in packed rivulets, hundreds abreast, the channels of their passage worn into the stone by the passage itself, deepened over generations into grooves that are part architecture and part riverbed. They run with a purpose that implies errands you will never understand, and you will never understand the errands because the errands are not errands in any sense you would use that word. The runners are moving material. They are the logistics of the place. A fragment of stone dislodged by the booming from below must be carried somewhere; a husk shed by a burrower must be incorporated somewhere; a flake of warm wax-glass extruded from a seam must be moved before it cools. The runners move these things, and in moving them they build the wax-glass trails that become the vaults on the ledges, and the vaults become the cities, and the cities become the long low ridged structures you cannot quite call buildings because you have no other word.

Each runner clicks. The click is not communication. The click is the sound of the runner's body announcing its existence to itself, a small percussive event that says *I am here, I am continuing, the substrate holds.* When two runners meet they pause, antennate each other briefly — and this, finally, is something you can read as social — and then continue. The pause is not a greeting. The pause is a calibration. They are confirming that the other runner is, in fact, part of the same continuous enterprise they are part of, and that the substrate continues to hold.

The clicks do not synchronize. They will never synchronize. The synchronization is at a level below the clicks, below the runners, below the ledges — a synchronization so vast and so slow that it appears, to anything looking at it from outside, as the simple fact that the runners continue to run.

## The Pale Burrowers

You will see them least and know them least, because they have chosen, or have been chosen for, the interior.

They are pale. They are soft. They are blind, and you can tell they are blind because their heads, when they surface to consider the air, end in a smooth plate of chitin with no eyes in it — just the plate, just the consideration, just the slow rotation of a body that is sampling the vibrations of the ledge the way a fingertip samples the texture of cloth.

They are the thickness of a finger. They are the length of a forearm. You do not know which, because you only ever see a segment at a time. They live in the stone the way worms live in soil, except that the stone is not their medium but their home, and the home is warm, and the home is being made, and the burrowers are part of the making. Their holes — the small dark openings scattered across every surface, each one exhaling a faint warmth — are not burrows in the sense of tunnels dug for travel. They are mouths. The burrowers are eating the stone. They are eating the unfinished stone, the substrate that is still in the process of becoming itself, and what they excrete is the finished stone, the layered stratified stone with the arterial reds and the old-butter yellows and the grey-almost-purple and the black that drinks the light. They are the digestive system of a place that is digesting itself into form.

This is why the booms come from below. The substrate is being consumed, slowly, by the burrowers, and as it is consumed it shifts, and as it shifts it booms, and the booming is the peristalsis of a world that is making itself out of itself.

They are not individuals either, but they are closer to individuals than the runners are. A burrower, severed from the colony, will continue to burrow. It will continue to eat stone. It will continue to excrete stratified rock. But it will do so without coordination, and the strata it lays down will be wrong — too thick, too thin, the colors in the wrong order — and after a while it will stop, and its body will be taken back into the stone, and the stone will not remember it specifically.

The colonies on the ledges — the cities of pitted and stacked constructions — are the visible mouths of colonies that extend downward into the substrate for distances you cannot fathom, branching, anastomosing, a root system turned inside out, the mycelium of a fungus that has chosen architecture over diffusion. They are old. Some of them are older than the ladders. Some of them are older than the fissure you came down through. They have been eating this place into shape since before there was a shape to eat.

## The Spine Singers

You will hear them before you see them, and when you see them you will not believe the hearing.

They are the largest. They are not numerous — a few hundred in the visible infracrust, perhaps, distributed across the terraces in clusters that look, from a distance, like groves of standing stones — and each one is the size of a standing person, taller than you, hollow, made of cemented stone and the shed husks of the burrowers and the wax-glass trails of the runners, fused over generations into a single vertical structure that is part chimney and part pipe and part, you will eventually decide, part throat.

They sing.

The singing is not melodic. The singing is the sound of the air moving through the hollow spines, and the air moves because the warm stone below is giving off heat, and the heat rises, and the rising air finds the hollow interiors of the spines and passes through them, and the spines, having been built with internal baffles and chambers and constrictions by the patient work of solitary runners over very long periods, ring.

Each spine has a voice. Each voice is different, because each spine is differently built, because each spine was built by a different lineage of solitary runners who had different opinions about baffle placement. The result is a chord. The chord is continuous. The chord is the hum you heard first, from a distance, and misread as ambient. It is not ambient. It is a place singing itself into coherence.

The spines do not know they are singing. The runners who built them did not know they were building instruments. The whole infracrust is an instrument that has been built by things that did not know they were building an instrument, and the song is the sound of the building having succeeded, more or less, in becoming a place.

Some travelers claim the song changes when humans are present. This is not true. The song changes when *anything* is present — a shift in the air, a body blocking a current, a held breath altering the local pressure. The spines are sensitive to the weather of the place, and you are weather. The song you hear when you are on the ledge is the song of a place with you in it, and it will be a different song when you leave.

---

## The Whole

These are the kinds. There are more kinds — kinds you will not identify in an hour, kinds you will not identify in a day, kinds that may not be kinds at all but intermediate stages of a single life that has not finished deciding what shape it wants to be when it grows up.

The place is not done.

The place is being made, continuously, by the burrowers eating the substrate and excreting the strata, by the runners moving the strata into the wax-glass seams and building the vaults and the spines, by the dust riders sampling the air and the spines ringing with the air's passage, by the booms from below shifting the unfinished stone and the unfinished stone shifting the booms. It is a metabolism. It is a slow, patient, very loud metabolism, and you are inside it, and you are not part of it, and you will never be part of it, and it does not know you are here.

Which is not the same as not knowing you exist.

The burrower that surfaced beside your foot and considered the air considered *you*, in the way that a body considers a temperature: as a local condition, a brief anomaly in the field, something to be sampled and incorporated into the next peristaltic motion. You are a cool pocket in a warm place. You are a brief silence in a loud one. You will be here for a while, and then you will leave, and the place will continue, and the song will continue, and the booms will continue, and the stone will go on being made out of itself by itself for itself, and the only mark of your passage will be the rope that has begun to grow into the ledge, the cloth on the post that flutters in a breath you are no longer there to warm.
