The first inhabitant of this place is not something you will see. It is something you will arrive inside of, the way a swimmer arrives inside water. The humming that rises from the marsh — the steady tonal note, even and sustained, too long for wind, too even for any geology — is the voice of the one who lives beneath the water table, and it has been speaking since before the forest decided what to become. You did not hear it at first. You heard it as if it had always been there, because by the time you were listening carefully it had already soaked into the wet air, into the drip of the leaves, into the percussion of the branches, into your own pulse. It was the floor of the place. It was what all the other sounds were standing on.

Call it the Tenant. That is not its name — it has no name you would recognize as a name — but the word fits, because the basin is rented from it, in the way that a coral atoll is rented from the polyps. It does not live *in* the valley the way a fox lives in a wood. It lives the way a lung lives inside a chest: the valley is its breathing surface, and it has been here longer than the basalt has had time to weather. Where exactly it sits is uncertain. The pillars that rise from the hummocks are the only honest clues — the glassy fused stone of their surfaces, the way they ring faintly when rain strikes them, the warmth that lives in them even when the air is basement-cold. They are antennae. They are the places where the Tenant's body pushes upward into the air to feel the weather and to broadcast, and if you lay a hand on one and stand still long enough, the pulse you feel through the bones of your wrist is its heart, or its clock, or its breath — you cannot tell which, because at the scale on which it operates, those three things are probably the same.

It is old. Not old in the way a tree is old, with rings that can be counted. Old in the way a process is old — a tide, an erosion, a chemistry. It was here when the rock was sharp. It chose the rock, in fact, or it chose the fracture, or it chose the place where the fracture would eventually be, because the wet cold and the slow emergence of the forest and the eventual gathering of fog into these particular sheets were all parts of a long calculation it began before any of it had a name. The young stone is its instrument. The marsh is its body. The trees, leaning together for warmth in their dense dark canopies, are a kind of insulation it has allowed to grow around itself, the way insulation grows around a pipe.

What it does, it does slowly. The hum that fills the valley is not communication in any ordinary sense. It is maintenance. Somewhere below the water, the Tenant is running — has been running, will be running — and the running is what keeps the fog composed as it is, the humidity at the precise weight it requires, the air temperature resting at this particular cold, this particular wet. If you watch the slow sheets pour over the rims of the broken ridges, you will see that they do not behave like fog. They hesitate. They lift. They thin in places where fog should thicken and thicken in places where fog should thin. The Tenant is shaping them as they move, the way a hand shapes water running over it. To be inside this shaping is to feel, for the first time, what weather would feel like if weather were being attended to.

The pillars, then, the discs, the curving low wall that runs off into the bracken and the standing water, the open shelter with its single humming plate — these are not ruins and they are not monuments. They are facilities. The discs, each the size of a dinner plate, half-buried in the moss, etched with a geometry that will not resolve into a symbol no matter how long you stare, are not decorative. They are sense-organs, or sample-stations, or places where the Tenant takes readings of the surface it has built itself out of. The low wall is a boundary — not to keep anything out, but to mark a place where the shape of the air is supposed to be different, where the humidity is supposed to be a fraction higher or lower, where the drip from the canopy is meant to fall in a particular rhythm. The open shelter, shaped to the wind, with its seamless floor that hums when you step on it, is the Tenant's resting place, or its listening post, or its altar — the place where it surfaces most fully into the air, the place where, if you were the kind of creature that knew how, you could press your hand down and feel it press back.

And so the first inhabitant is the deep one, and the deep one is not a creature, and not a maker in any sense you would use the word, and not quite a god, but something in between that does not have a name. It tends the valley. The valley tends everything else.

---

The second inhabitants are the ones the valley tends, and they are many, and they are loud, and they do not know they are being tended, or they have forgotten, which comes to the same thing.

Start at your feet, on the sharp young rock where the moss has been brave enough to try. The pale crustaceans are there — no longer than a finger, the color of old candle wax, almost translucent, so that you can see the dark line of their gut running through the middle of them like a sentence through a paragraph. They cling to the wet stone in colonies of dozens, and they flick away into the moss at the slightest disturbance, and they have done this for so long that the disturbance is now built into them: they do not wait to be disturbed. They anticipate. They live in a state of being-already-startled, and the result is a constant, fine-grained shimmer along the edges of every wet rock, a moving fringe of pale bodies that is never quite still. They are the bottom of the food web here. Everything larger in the marsh eats them, or eats the things that eat them. They are the marsh's small change.

The amphibian is next, and is older, and is the first of the larger predators. It is broad and low, the color of wet bark, and it moves between pools with the slow deliberation of a thing that has learned, over generations, that patience is the only strategy that pays in a place where the prey is already startled. Its eye is a single coin of dull gold, set on the side of its head in a way that lets it watch the surface of the water and the surface of the air at the same time. You see it cross one pool to the next, between the tree roots, and the water closes behind it without a sound, and it is gone. You will not see it again in the time you stand here, but you will know it is there, because the gold eye is the most honest kind of eye there is, and the marsh is full of other eyes that are learning, each in their own way, not to be quite so honest.

In the trees, the inventory becomes harder. The canopy is a woven lid of dark green-black so thick that what lives in it is, for the most part, only guessed at. But the sounds — the constant small percussion of bodies in motion, the creak of cold wood, the wet flutter of wings — give away a population. There are climbers, small and many, that move through the high branches in loose processions, gripping with four hands and never quite letting go with one before the next is secure. There are things that hang upside down from the lower limbs, motionless for so long that the mist collects on their backs in beads of water that thicken and fall and thicken again, and the slow drip of them becomes, after a while, part of the metronome of the canopy. There are quick shapes, too quick to follow, that launch themselves from trunk to trunk and seem to think between the trees, taking the long way around a branch that would be the short way, as if the long way were a kind of thinking in itself. And there are hunters. The shriek that rings out from somewhere in the high dark — bright, almost a laugh — is one hunter finishing a sentence it has been constructing for hours. There is no answering shriek. There is only the small, clean sound of something that was alive becoming something that is being eaten, and the canopy absorbs it, and the drip continues.

Above the canopy, in the air the Tenant is shaping, the wings are wider and slower. They beat in long circuits, dark against the low bruise of the cloud, and they do not flap so much as scull — pulling themselves through the heavy wet air with a patience that the smaller creatures, lower down, cannot afford. They are not many. They are not in flocks. Each one is alone in its own circle, and the circles do not cross, as if the air above the basin were divided into invisible private lanes and each wing had been taught its lane at the beginning of its life. What they hunt is not visible from the ground. It may be the climbers. It may be the things that hang. It may be something smaller still, some almost-not-animal that drifts in the mist and lives on the humidity the Tenant is managing. The Tenant, after all, has not shaped this weather for nothing. The air it has built is a habitat. The wetness is not waste. The fog is not accident. The prey the wings are hunting may have been put here, in the same slow sense that the crustaceans were put at your feet and the amphibian was put in its pool and the trees were allowed to lean together in the dark.

And once, in the time you stand at the rim, something enormous passes between you and the far ridge. It is not a bird. It is not a wing you can see. It is a *displacement* — a place where the fog is suddenly not, where the air is suddenly heavier and the cloud parts and then closes again, and you catch, for the length of one breath, a sense of something large and not-solid moving through the basin as if the basin were its own element. It has no body you can make out. It has a shape, perhaps, but the shape is the shape of a thing that is mostly air and a little something else. It could be a creature. It could be a phenomenon. It could be the Tenant itself, surfacing — a lobe of the deep one rising into the fog the way a whale rises into the light, briefly, and then sinking again. You do not learn which. You learn only that the basin is larger than the basin, that what lives in it is not all of it the same size, and that the smallest things here live at the edge of the largest thing, and that the largest thing, whatever it is, knows they are there.

---

So there are three scales of inhabitant, and they do not know each other by the same name.

There are the small ones: the crustaceans, the amphibian, the climbers, the hangers, the quick thinkers, the shrieking hunters, the wide-winged scullers, the things so small they live in the mist itself. They are what the basin looks like, from the inside. They are the noise. They are the wall of sound that is not so much heard as inhabited. They are loud together. They are loud without knowing they are loud together. They are the part of this place that does not know it is being tended, and they have not known, perhaps, for as long as any of them has existed. The amphibian has no word for the Tenant. The wide wings have no ceremony for the pillars. The crustaceans, startled into their constant flight, do not know that the rock they cling to is warm because something beneath it is alive. And yet — and this is the part that stays with you, after — their lives are shaped by what they do not know. The trees lean together because the air is the air the Tenant has built, and the air is the air the trees have grown into. The crustaceans flicker along edges that exist because the rock was fractured in the way the Tenant needed it fractured. The shriek in the canopy is answered by the drip of the leaves, and the drip of the leaves is answered by the hum, and the hum is answered by the slow turning of the wings above, and all of it, every note of it, is part of a long composition that began before any of these creatures were born and will continue after the last of them is gone.

Then there are the makers, if makers is the word, if makers is what they are. They are not the Tenant. They are not the small ones. They are something in between — present in the pillars and the discs and the curving wall and the open shelter, but not present in any body you could point to. They made the path of discs. They set the pillar into its hummock. They laid the seamless floor of the shelter, and they shaped it to the weather, and they left it open on one side as if openness were the only kind of protection they understood. Nothing they left is roofed. Nothing they left is enclosed. They were not trying to keep anything out. They were trying to mark a place — a station, a checkpoint, a place where the Tenant's work could be observed and tended in turn, by hands other than its own. Or — and this is the part that will not resolve, no matter how long you stare — they were a part of the Tenant all along. A surface gesture of a thing that is mostly below. An emergence. The way the fog emerges from the basin floor in a way that fog does not emerge.

And then there is the deep one, and it has no scale, or it has every scale, and you cannot, in the end, decide.

You stand at the edge of it. The cold settles deeper into your collar, behind your ears, into the wet cuffs of whatever you are wearing. The light does not improve. The wings make their slow circles overhead. The amphibian watches from a pool between two roots, the gold of its eye not moving. The crustaceans shimmer at your feet like a thought changing its mind. And beneath all of it, beneath the marsh and the rock and the wet dark forest and the wide wings and the enormous passing shape and the pillars and the discs and the shelter that hums when you step on it, the Tenant runs, and has been running, and will run, and the running is what is keeping you alive in this cold wet place without your quite knowing why you are alive here, why you are able to breathe, why the air is the precise air it is, why the sound takes you in and does not let you go.

The basin is overfull with life. The life is loud. The life is not alone.
