Who lives here

What lives here has worn the place to its shape, and the shape has worn them to theirs, and the two are no longer easily told apart. Let me start again, slower. Let me see what is here.

The microbial sheen first, because it is underneath everything else — that gray-green slick on the wet surfaces, never quite enough to be called growth. It is enough to be called life. It is bacterial, probably, and algal, probably, and it does what such things do: it eats the surface, slowly, and it leaves a film behind. It lives on the wet concrete, in the puddles, on the walls of the empty tanks. It is the color of tarnished copper and the texture of a fingerprint and it is everywhere there is standing water. It does not move. It is the most patient resident of the place. It has been here longer than the rats, longer than the buildings, longer than the fill. It is what grows when nothing is allowed to grow. It is the only plant that has made its peace with the substrate. In a real sense it is the substrate's own skin, the way the surface has learned to feed itself. I think it is the foundation. Everything else is built on it, or out of it.

The mosquitoes. They rise after every rainfall, in clouds so dense you can hear them as a single note. They are the loudest inhabitants. They are the most insistent. They breed in the standing water — every puddle is a nursery, every wet pipe trench, every condensation pool on the flat roof, every slow sheet of water running down the side of a cylinder. They do not live long. A mosquito here lives its whole life in the time between one rain and the next, or in the wet half-hour after the rain stops. They hatch, they swarm, they find a rat or a cat or a person or each other, they lay eggs in the water, they die. The dead ones fall into the puddles and feed the microbial sheen, which feeds the eggs, which feeds the next generation. It is a small, complete, vertical cycle, and it turns over faster than anything else here. They are the fastest thing in the place. They are also the most fragile. A wind kills them. A dry day kills them. They exist in the rain like the rain exists in the clouds — temporarily, abundantly, as a phase.

The rats. They are the most numerous. They are the most seen, if you bother to look. They are under the buildings, in the pipe trenches, in the empty tanks, in the wall cavities, in the bundles of insulation behind the unused facilities. They move in waves at dusk, brown rivers running between the cover of one structure and the next. The largest are the size of small cats. They eat what they find — the industrial waste, the dead of their own kind, the food the workers bring, the grain in the storage sheds, the small things that live in the drainage ditches.

Let me say what a rat is here. A rat here is not a city rat. A city rat navigates alleys and knows the rhythm of bins and lives in the gaps. A rat here navigates a pipe trench the way a city rat navigates a subway — as a corridor, as a territory, as a known thing. It has learned the shape of the place. It knows which joints weep water and which are dry. It knows which tanks have grain in them and which have chemicals and which are empty and which have been empty long enough to nest in. It knows the timing of the workers — when the canteens open, when the grain sheds are restocked, when the trucks park and what falls off the trucks. It has a schedule. It has a route. It has, I think, an inheritance.

They are brown. Some are darker. Their fur is thick because it has to be — the wet would kill a thin-coated animal, would chill it, would mat its fur and rot it. Their fur is dense and water-shedding and slightly greasy, the way an animal's fur gets when the water is permanent. Their tails are scaled and pink and wet. Their ears are small and close to the skull, to keep the water out. They are built like animals that have been here a long time. Their whiskers are tuned to the dark — they navigate the inside of a pipe trench mostly by whisker, mostly by air currents, by the feel of a wall close or far. Their eyes are not useless but they are not primary. They see motion, mostly, and see it well enough. In the dark, in the trenches, in the wall cavities, the eye is a backup sense. The whiskers are the main sense. The nose is the memory.

They breed constantly. There is no season to stop them. The place is warm and wet and the food is always somewhere — in a storage shed, in a canteen, in a pipe trench where a rat before them died and was not eaten in time, in the slow rain of food particles the workers scatter without noticing. They breed faster than the cats can eat them. They breed faster than the hawks can eat them. They are not held in check by predation. They are held in check by their own numbers — by disease, which moves through them in waves the way they move through the pipe trenches; by the slow cannibalism of the starving; by the food running out and then running back in, in pulses, with the canteen cycle and the truck schedule. They are a population that has found its ceiling and presses against it, and the ceiling flexes, and they sometimes crash, and they recover. They crash quiet. A rat dying in a wall cavity is heard by no one. The next generation finds the body.

The rats are old residents. They were here before the buildings were built, in the fill, in the marshes that were filled in. They have been here the whole time. They have watched — if the rat is to watch — the place change around them. They have watched the fill pour in. They have watched the foundations go down. They have watched the buildings rise. They have watched the steam come and the steam stop and the steam come again. They do not care about any of it. The place is the same to them. The buildings are cover. The pipe trenches are corridors. The canteens are food. The workers are large, warm, unthreatening presences that sometimes drop things. The place is what it has always been to the rats: a wet, warm, food-rich, dark sheltering plain. The buildings are not an imposition on the rats. The buildings are an opportunity. The rats moved into the buildings the way mammalian moves into a burrow, with relief and quick occupancy. They are the most adapted residents here. They are the most at home.

The feral cats. They are in loose colonies, between the structures. They are mousers gone back to mousing. They were pets once, probably — brought in to keep the rats out of the canteens, kept on-site, fed on scraps, breeding, and then the people who owned them rotated out, and the cats did not. Or they were not owned at all, and they arrived on a truck, and they stayed, and they bred, and they became something else. They are lean. They are scarred. Their coats are rough and matted in the permanent humidity. Their eyes are wide and reflective. They hunt the rats, mostly — the young rats, the slow rats, the rats that move wrong, the rats that come out into the open at the wrong moment. They are hunted in turn by the hawks, the great horned owls, the ravens. They live in the middle of the food chain. They live short, intense lives. They hunt at dusk. They sleep in the day, in sheltered places — under a truck, in a drainage ditch, in a wall cavity a rat has been evicted from. They are not affectionate. They are not pets. They have become what the place made them: small predators, dark-colored, predators that can live in the wet and the dark and the rat-rich quiet of the place. They do not meow for food. They hunt for food. They have become the cats of an empty industrial plain, and they are very good at it.

The hawks and the owls and the ravens. They are on the higher steel — on the crossbeams of the pipe racks, on the rims of the flare stacks, on the roofs of the operating facilities. They are above the place. They hunt from height. A hawk hits a rat at speed, and the sound is louder than it should be, because the quiet is so complete. The hawk is the apex predator here. The owl is the night hunter. The raven is the scavenger, the opportunist, the one that eats what the hawks drop, what the cats leave, what the rats die of. The raven is also the most intelligent. The raven watches. The raven learns the schedule of the place. The raven knows when the trucks come and when the canteens close and when the rats are most exposed. The raven is, I think, the most aware resident of the place. It knows what is here. It has opinions about what is here. It has a territory. It defends its territory. It will mob a hawk that comes too close. It will mob an owl that hunts too loud. It is the supervisor of the higher air, and it is not gentle.

There are no songbirds. There are no insects you can hear, except the mosquitoes. The place is too wet, too exposed, too empty for songbirds — there is no perch-song territory, no tree, no bush, no hedge. There is no insect chorus because there is no place for insects to chorus from, except the water, and the water has the mosquitoes and nothing else. The place is not silent. The place is depleted. The place has the animals it can sustain. The ones it cannot sustain have left, or they never came.

The microbial sheen, the mosquitoes, the rats, the cats, the hawks and owls and ravens. These are the permanent residents. These are the ones that stay. These are the ones the place supports.

Then, the people. The ones who come and go. They are few. They work the operating facilities and drive the trucks and service the flare stacks. They eat in the canteens and sleep in the dormitories and leave for the city at the end of their rotations. They are here for a shift, a hitch, a contract. They are passing through. They are not residents. They are workers. They are commuters of a strange commute — out to the plain, into the facilities, back to the city. They live in a different time zone from the place. They live in shift time. The dunes they live in are the dunes of rotation: two weeks on, two weeks off, or four on, four off, or six on, six off. They live in the rotation's clock. The place knows a different clock. The place knows the clock of the rain, the clock of the rats, the clock of the rust, the clock of the microbial generation. The two clocks do not align. The people work their shifts and leave and the place does not notice them leave. The rats notice, briefly, because the food is gone. The cats notice, briefly, because the rats become harder to hunt. The ravens notice, briefly, because the canteens close. Then the next rotation arrives, and the food is back, and the rats are bold again, and the ravens are fed again, and the place resumes.

The people are not part of the place. The people are visitors. The place endures their visits the way a wet place endures the rain: it does not care. It absorbs them, it continues. The people are a phase the place goes through, the way the mosquitoes are a phase the rain goes through. They come and they go and the place is what it was.

But the people also leave traces. The food they bring feeds the rats. The canteens feed the cats. The rats consume the workers' waste and the workers' dropped scraps and the workers' grain and the workers' everything. The people feed the ecosystem without intending to. They are part of the food chain whether they know it or not. The rats know it. The rats have always known it. The rats are the most attentive to the table.

There are also, perhaps, a few other things. A snail, perhaps, in the wetter drainage ditches. A small frog, perhaps, in the standing water, calling at night in a thin reedy note that no one hears. A spider, in the unused facilities, building a web across a corner that no one brushes. A small invertebrate, in the microbial sheen, feeding on the bacteria. The place is not as empty as I have made it sound. The place is full of small things. The place is full of small things that feed on smaller things. The place is a vertical column of consumers, transposed onto a horizontal plain. Each thing eats the thing below it and is eaten by the thing above it, and the column goes from the microbial sheen up through the invertebrates up through the rats up through the cats up through the hawks and the ravens, and the column is held together by the rain and the standing water and the permanent wet. Take the wet away and the column collapses. The column is built on the rain. The column is built on the microbial sheen.

And the microbial sheen is built on the surface. And the surface is built on the fill. And the fill is built on the plain. And the plain was, perhaps, a marsh, once. And the marsh was built on something older. And the place has worn itself into its current shape, and the shape is holding, and the column is holding, and the place is what it is.

I think this is the most accurate account of who lives here. The microbial sheen, the invertebrates, the mosquitoes, the rats, the cats, the hawks and owls and the ravens, the few snails, the few spiders, the people who come and go. The whole column. The whole cycle. The whole place.

The place has settled into itself. The place is what it is. The place endures.
