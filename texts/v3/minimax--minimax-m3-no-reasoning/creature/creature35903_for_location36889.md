The question of who lives here is not the same as the question of who is here. The marsh is full of presence that does not arrange itself into the categories a casual census would reach for — things with legs, things with wings, things that swim — and what lives in it lives the way the slag cools: in slow increments, on a timeline that does not acknowledge you.

Begin with the smallest. The insects are the loudest fact of the valley, and they have the character of a fact — continuous, indifferent, slightly oppressive. They are present in such number that the eye cannot separate one from another; the swarm above any given reed bed is less a population than a condition, a localized thickening of the air into something that hums. They live in the wet air the way fish live in water, suspended in it, and their hum is not a sound they are making so much as a sound they are. When one of them, by chance, comes to rest on a sleeve, it has the heft of a small grape. Its body is the color of dried blood. It does not seem to mind you, but it also does not seem to register you; it is too occupied with whatever business an insect of this size and density has, and the business has the look of waiting. They land and lift off and land again, and none of it is for you.

The hum is what you hear first and what you hear last and what, if you stay long enough, becomes the silence you listen past. It is not steady the way a single note is steady; it has a kind of breath in it, a slow ebb and rise, as though the swarm itself is respiring. Some mornings, when the light is grayer and the temperature has dropped a degree, the hum is lower. Some afternoons, when the slag mounds are venting a little extra steam from a crack that has widened, the hum seems to thicken and lift. They are not sensitive to weather so much as they are part of the weather. They are the weather, in the same way the rain would be.

Beneath them, and through them, and visible only when they move, are the pale amphibians. There are several species, or what passes for several species here, though the boundaries between them are not crisp. They are roughly the size and shape of a fat dog with the legs folded away — a smooth ovoid body, slightly flattened on the bottom, a wide mouth that takes up most of the face and closes in a line that is more theoretical than actual. Their skin is the color of skim milk, or of a slug, or of the underside of a mushroom that has just been turned over. When they sit in the shallows, you can see them as a row of pale lumps between the dark stems of the sedge, and they sit very still for what seems like hours, breathing through their flanks in slow pulses. Then, for reasons you cannot read, one of them will move, and the movement is the most unsettling thing about them.

They do not propel themselves so much as they unfold. A body that had seemed merely resting extends along its length, becomes long, slides through the water with the unhurried ripple of a heavy sack being dragged across a wet floor. The wake they leave is wrong: it persists too long, holds its shape too well, as though the water is remembering the passage more carefully than water usually does. A wake behind an amphibian in this marsh will still be visible as a faint depression a minute later, and the next amphibian to pass will sink slightly into the depression the previous one left, as though the water has a memory of where the bodies have been. They do not appear to interact with each other in any way you can name. They do not hunt, or court, or fight. They move. They stop. They breathe. Their pale shapes drift through the channel and through the quieter pools, and the pools remember them.

Above all of this, on the slag ridge, in the lines of willows that have colonized it like a procession of tired penitents, the birds.

It is not certain they are birds. They have wings. They roost. They make sounds. But the sounds are the problem. From a distance, and even from close, the sound is a vocalization that contains the architecture of speech without its content — vowels riding on vowels, the shape of consonants implied by the muscular effort the throat is making, but no consonants delivered. It sounds like someone talking through several thicknesses of water, or through a long pipe, or through the wall of a house from which you are standing a certain distance away. It sounds like a word heard in a dream and half-remembered. When you turn your head toward the source, the sound ceases at once, not gradually but in the way a hand claps shut over a mouth. When you turn away, the sound resumes, sometimes in the same phrase, sometimes slightly further along it, as though the speaker has continued in your absence and has not lost the thread.

What they are saying is a question you cannot answer. Whether they are saying it to you, or whether you are incidental to a longer conversation being held among themselves, or whether they are saying it at all and are instead producing a noise that has the accidental shape of language, you cannot determine. They are brown-gray, slightly larger than a crow, with beaks that are too long for their faces. They watch you. When you look at one, the eye that looks back is dark and round and very calm, with the calmness of something that has decided what you are and is no longer interested in the decision.

The willow trees they roost in are themselves inhabitants in a sense the word *tree* does not quite cover. They are skinny, wind-leaned, gray-blue in their leaf, and they are growing in a single-file procession along the slag ridge as though they were planted there by something that understood the concept of a line but not the concept of a row. Their roots are sunk into the slag itself; they feed, presumably, on whatever the slag offers in the way of minerals and moisture, and they have grown an inch a season for what may be a very long time. They do not appear to die. They do not appear to fruit. They simply continue, very slowly, very patiently, leaning hard against a wind that may or may not be blowing. Some of them have grown right up out of the cooling clinker as if the clinker had been waiting to give birth to them.

Between and beneath and through all of this, in the slow waterways, in the quiet pools, in the brick foundations that stand in shallow water with nothing above them — there are the smaller things. A kind of small crustacean, no longer than a finger, lives in the iron-sweet bacterial films on the slag. It is the color of rust, and it is present in numbers that you do not notice until you look, and then it is present everywhere. A flatworm, pale and ribbon-thin, lives in the seams between the bricks of the foundations, feeding on the film of green that has begun to grow on the mortar. A kind of slow fish — if it is a fish — lives in the deeper central channel, surfacing rarely, and when it does surface it does so without sound or splash, presenting its back for a moment and then withdrawing again, leaving only the slightest dimple in the water to indicate that anything was there.

And then there is what is below.

You cannot see it, and you may never have been meant to. But it is there, and you can feel it through your boots when the marsh gives under you, in the lowest places, where the mud is thinnest: a flat, hard, horizontal plane, a stratum of something that is not quite stone and not quite metal and not quite concrete, that runs all through the valley beneath the water and the sedge and the slag. Whatever it is, it holds the land up. The land holds the water. The water holds the light. The structure of the marsh, in the most literal sense, is a layered sandwich of strange materials, and the bottommost layer is not natural. It is the floor of something, or it was. It extends in every direction you walk to, and the marsh extends only as far as the floor does, and beyond the marsh you cannot go.

Whether anything lives in the floor is a question that begins to feel impolite the longer you are here. Whether anything still operates down there, in the dark, beneath the mud and the clinker and the slow iron water, is something the swamp does not invite you to wonder about. The pipes that run along the higher ground weep their slow contents, and the contents behave in ways that water and oil do not behave. A drop falls correctly. It hits the pool. The surface does not ripple outward; it gathers inward, as though the pool is drinking, and then is still. The contents of the pipes are the contents of the floor, if there is a floor, and the floor, if it is a floor, is alive in some sense that the word *alive* does not cover.

The smoke, when there is smoke, rises from the same places the slag does. There is no fire visible, and there has been no fire for a long time, but some of the cracks in the older mounds exhale a thin gray vapor that smells of something like metal heated too slowly, and at the base of these cracks, where the vapor meets the cooler air, a kind of growth happens — not quite lichen, not quite mineral deposit, a dull orange-rust bloom that is alive in the way lichen is alive, which is to say slowly, and at the boundary. The bloom is inhabited. Something small lives in it, feeding on the chemistry at the crack, and that something is fed upon in turn by something smaller, and the chain continues downward into scales of life that are not visible to you.

What lives here, then, is layered the way the marsh itself is layered. The largest inhabitants — the floor, the pipes, the slow processes below — are not visible, but they make the visible possible. The slag is theirs. The water is theirs. The iron-sweet smell in the air is theirs. The pale amphibians, the brown birds, the rust-colored crustaceans, the clouds of insects — all of these are the bloom on the surface of a longer process, the way a meadow is the bloom on top of a forest floor. They are all, in their different ways, the visible part of something that does not present itself to you and that does not, in any case, have a face. They live here because they are what grows here. They are not the residents of the marsh so much as they are the marsh's way of being seen.

And the birds keep saying their almost-words into the gray, and the amphibians drift through the channel leaving the water to remember them, and the insects hum the hum that becomes the silence you listen past, and the slag cools a little each century, and the willow grows an inch a season, and somewhere below, beneath everything, in the place you cannot see and have not been invited to, something that is not quite a floor and not quite a body continues to hold the marsh up the way a heart holds a body up — without showing itself, without stopping, without complaint, for as long as it is required.
