# Who Lives in the Petal Mires of Verís

The short answer is: almost nothing that would recognize you, and several things that would recognize what you are before you finished recognizing them. The place does not curate its inhabitants. It accumulates them.

## The Engineers of the Coagulate

The tram did not build itself, and someone maintains it, though you will be hard-pressed to meet such a person at any working hour. They are called *the coagulators*, an old word no longer used precisely, and they live almost entirely in the substrate-level causeways — the abandoned stone paths that now lie just beneath the surface of the mires, half-drowned in pale moss. They are a small population, perhaps three hundred, and they have arranged themselves along family lines that have, by now, less to do with genetics than with the inheritance of specific frequencies. A coagulator household is a household tuned to a particular resonance — the one their stretch of causeway was grown to carry — and they keep that resonance alive in their children the way musicians keep a tuning alive, by ear, by long proximity, by the particular patience of people who have decided that their work is finished and is only being maintained.

You will not see them from the tram. The tram window does not look down. But you may, if you ride at the right hour, see one of them emerging from a side-passage onto a lower walkway — a small figure in pale oilskins, carrying something that might be a tool and might be an instrument, moving with the unhurried gait of someone whose shift is several decades long. They are short, broad through the shoulders, and they speak, when they speak, in a register that the tram operators find difficult to record. There is a folk belief among them that the causeway sings to them in their sleep, and that the song is the only reason any of them are still alive.

## The Rowfolk

Above the coagulators, in the inhabited middle levels of the orí, live the bulk of the population — perhaps a hundred and twenty thousand souls, by the last careful count, though the count itself was conducted by people who live in a forest that computes, and so the number is, like most numbers here, an approximation that has been approved by committee. They call themselves *rowfolk*, after the high row, though only a small minority of them actually live in the high row itself. Most live in the *middle row* — the dense threading of platforms, balconies, covered bridges, and suspended rooms that winds around and through the trunks of the orí at heights varying from thirty to two hundred feet.

The rowfolk are slender, longer-limbed than the coagulators, with a tendency toward a particular shade of dark hair that the locals describe as *kite-black* but that is, in fact, simply very dark brown with a faint reddish cast when the light is good. Their eyes are wide-set and adapted to the dim grey-gold of the place; visitors sometimes mistake them for being perpetually startled, but they are merely seeing in the spectrum they were grown to see in. They wear a great deal of layered cloth — looser than you would expect, given the warmth, but the warmth is wet warmth, and wet warmth requires ventilation. Their clothes are mostly undyed. There is no prestige in color here. There is prestige in *quiet*.

A rowfolk childhood is mostly an education in listening. By the age of four or five, a child is expected to be able to identify, by ear, which orí trunk their family is associated with, even if they have been moved to a different part of the city; the trunks each have a slightly different pitch, and a child who cannot pick out their own is considered, gently, to be drifting. By the age of ten, a child is expected to be able to hum in tune with at least one of the kites overhead, and by fifteen, to be able to feel a phase-shift before it clicks. None of this is taught formally. It is learned the way a tree learns the wind: by being in it for a long time.

The rowfolk are not, on the whole, an anxious people. They have a great many preoccupations — the maintenance of the high row, the cultivation of the orí (which is to say, the negotiation with the orí; the orí do not respond to being *told*), the breeding and release of the small green-blue animals, the long-running argument about whether the kites are computing or merely singing — but they are not anxious. Anxiety requires a sense that something could be otherwise. The rowfolk have lived so long inside a system that regulates itself that the question of whether to intervene has, for most of them, simply stopped arising. There is a word for the state: *trame*, which might be translated as *the settled-in-ness of a place that has stopped asking permission of itself*.

There are exceptions. There is a small philosophical movement, perhaps a few thousand strong, who argue that the kites are not computing but are *being computed by* the city, that the city is the actual instrument and the kites are merely the strings. They are called the *inversionists*, mostly as a courtesy, and they meet in the upper platforms during the late afternoon, when the pollen comes down, and they take turns humming phrases that they hope will not be tuned. They have never succeeded. They keep trying. They are not unhappy about this.

## The Upper Dwellers

Higher still — in the wind-catchers, in the open platforms near the kite-tether cables, in the small chambers that have been built into the very tops of the orí — live the *upper dwellers*, a population of perhaps eight thousand, fewer than the rowfolk, and considerably more peculiar. They are the kite-keepers, or rather the kite-accompanyists, since they do not keep the kites in any sense that implies ownership; the kites were here before them and will be here after them, and the upper dwellers have, on the whole, made their peace with this.

An upper dweller is, on average, taller than a rowfolk, and quieter, and slightly more weathered in the face, because they spend more hours in the open air. Their work is to climb — daily, in season, with long poles and a particular kind of soft-headed hammer — to the cable junctions and adjust the tension of the tethers. This is not a maintenance job in any conventional sense. The kites do not need their tethers adjusted. But the kites, like the orí, like the mires, like the city itself, respond to being attended to; they sing slightly differently when someone is in the air with them, slightly more *deliberately*, as if the presence of a watcher matters to them. The upper dwellers understand this. They do not claim to understand it. They simply climb.

There is a guild of upper dwellers, and the guild is old, and the guild is the only institution in Verís that still keeps a written record of its members' names, because the names are needed to ensure that no two adjacent cable-junction workers are kin, because kin-workers, it has been observed, fall into the same humming patterns, and the kites, when they are being worked on by two people who hum alike, do not adjust. They hold. They wait. They are not comfortable. So the upper dwellers are dispersed across the cables according to a long-running rotation that takes, by the most recent count, sixty-three years to complete.

## The Small Green-Blue

And then there are the animals, which are not so much inhabitants as constituents. The small green-blue creatures — which the rowfolk call *vré* in the singular, *vrín* in the plural, though the plurals are mostly academic since you never see just one — live in the holes in the orí trunks and emerge at the three appointed hours to stream across the basin. They are about the size of a human thumb. They have too many wings, or what look like wings; the wings may in fact be modified limbs, the biologists among the rowfolk disagree, and the disagreement has been going on for longer than anyone can remember. They are iridescent in that deceptive way that the place seems to specialize in: you keep wanting to call them black, and they keep refusing.

The vré feed on the condensation that the mires exhale, and on the spores that the orí release in the late afternoon, and on the smaller kites, and on each other. They are not, despite the last fact, in any obvious sense at war with one another. The predation among them is more like the way a fire consumes its fuel: it is what they are doing, and it is what they are, and there is no aggression in it, only appetite. They make a great deal of sound, which is to say they vibrate at frequencies that the tram window does not transmit well, but which a patient observer standing on one of the lower causeways — a coagulator, perhaps, on an evening walk — can hear as a kind of bright static underlying the warmth in the ears.

The vré do not belong to anyone. They are not, on the whole, eatable. They have been tried. The rowfolk have a long and inconclusive history of trying to eat them, and a longer history of deciding not to try again.

## The Orí Themselves

And finally, the orí. Whether the orí count as inhabitants is a question the inversionists have been arguing about for generations, and the answer is not, in the end, the point. The orí are here. They predate the city. They will, almost certainly, postdate the city. They grow at their own rate, which is slow, and they propagate themselves now, having outgrown the rowfolk's attempts to cultivate them, and they are, by every measure available, *listening*.

An orí is not sentient in any way that the rowfolk have been able to determine. It does not respond to being spoken to. It does not respond to being cut, though the rowfolk no longer cut them; they build around them, into them, and where the architecture meets the bark-equivalent, the architecture yields. The orí are not interested in the city. They are interested in the mires, and in the wind, and in the slow upward gradient of light that filters through the engineered upper atmosphere, and they grow toward these things the way a river grows toward the sea. The city is what they happen to be growing through.

This is not a fact that the rowfolk find depressing. It is, in fact, the foundation of a certain kind of contentment they have, and it is what you, sitting at the tram window, may find yourself envying, if you are the kind of person who envies.

## What Is Missing

There are no children playing in the streets, because the streets are the trunks of the orí, and the children are higher up, or inside. There are no markets in the usual sense, because trade is conducted in the middle row, in small rooms, in long quiet conversations, in the exchange of *attunements* — small tuning forks, small lengths of coagulate, small vials of the pale moss from the lower causeways — that are valued not for what they are but for the pitch they carry. There are no temples, because the rowfolk do not distinguish between the system they live inside and the thing that system is for. There are no soldiers. There are no politicians, in the sense that the word is used elsewhere; there is a council of elders, and the council meets twice a year, and the meetings are long, and most of what is decided is decided by listening for whether the kites, that afternoon, are singing in tune.

There are very few visitors. You, on the tram, are one of perhaps a hundred this season. You are not expected. You are not unexpected. You are simply *present*, in a place that has a long tradition of being present itself.
