The workers are the first inhabitants you learn to read, and you learn them slowly, because they do not offer themselves quickly. They come into focus the way a face comes into focus in a poor photograph: a shoulder, the edge of a jaw, the smudge of a hand on a ladle, and then, after a long moment, the whole figure standing in its dim light. There are perhaps twenty of them in the vault at any one time, but the vault is so large and so full of shadow that the twenty could be two, or could be fifty, and you would not be certain until you had stood in one place for a quarter of an hour and let the place teach you its arithmetic.

The man with the missing eyebrows is the easiest to watch because he is the most visibly human. He stands at the central crucible, his leather apron lacquered with a century of small splashes, and the muscles of his forearms move under skin that has taken on the color of old putty. His scalp is bare not from baldness but from the heat; what hair he had is gone the way the hair on a potato is gone after long storage, retreated into the body. When he lifts the ladle, his hands do not shake. When he pours, his wrist does not hesitate. The metal runs white and almost blue, and the light of it throws his face into a relief that is older than his years. He has, you realize after a while, the face of a man who has not been surprised in a long time. Surprise requires an expectation of alternatives, and down here the alternatives have narrowed to one.

He does not speak, and neither do the others, except in the low, mouth-to-ear way of people in libraries or in chapels. When two of them pass each other on the rail cart's track, they incline their heads by a degree, the way monks incline their heads, and continue. There is a courtesy here that is not quite courtesy; it is more like a recognition that each of them is in possession of the same small, hot truth, and that the truth does not need to be discussed. The truth is: we are still doing this. The truth is: the crucible is still hot. The truth is: the next ladle will be poured at the next bell, and the one after that at the bell after that, and so on into a future that has stopped being distinguishable from the past.

Their clothes are not clothes in the way clothes are clothes in the upper city. They are accretions. A worker you pass near the second stack has what appears to be a woolen undershirt, but it has been so soaked in sweat and then dried and then soaked again that it has become a kind of soft armor, holding the shape of his shoulders without reference to what it once was. Another wears a canvas jacket that has been patched so many times it is more patch than jacket, the original fabric surviving in islands no larger than a palm. A woman—you do not see her clearly, only her silhouette against the glow of the third stack—wears a long apron that reaches her ankles, and her boots are wrapped in strips of rubber cut from somewhere, and the rubber has gone grey and lost its elasticity and clings to her calves like the skin of a fruit that has been peeled and allowed to dry. None of these garments have been bought in a long time. They are heirlooms of the body, passed from worker to worker, repaired and re-repaired, and in their layers and patches they record every small disaster and long recovery of the foundry's continuous running.

Their faces are the most human part of them, and also the least. They have the expressions of people who sleep lightly and rise without transition into the day's work, because the day's work is the same as the night's work, and the night's work is the same as the day's. There is a softening around the eyes of the older ones, a particular slackness in the muscles of the cheek, that you recognize from pictures of prisoners or of very old sailors. It is not unhappiness. It is the face of a person for whom the muscles of expression have become mostly vestigial, like the muscles of the human ear. They have not forgotten how to smile, but smiling has become an act of such deliberateness that they save it for the rare occasions when something in the vault deviates from its routine, which is to say, almost never.

One of them, the youngest, has not yet lost the habit of looking up when something moves in the dark above him. He is perhaps twenty-five, and his eyebrows are still intact, though thinned to a kind of pale down, and his skin has not yet fully committed to its putty color. You see him glance once, twice, toward the ceiling, tracking a sound you cannot hear, and you understand that he is still in the process of becoming a worker of this place. In another ten years he will have stopped looking up. He will have learned, as the others have learned, that the sounds above are not his concern, and that the only sounds worth attending to are the sounds that come from the crucible and the stacks and the water. He will have stopped being surprised.

The youngest worker is the one who feeds the creatures.

---

The creatures are fed at no particular time, or rather at a time that is determined by the foundry's needs rather than by the clock, and so the feeding is folded into the larger rhythm of ladle and mold and rail cart. The youngest worker, when his pour is done, takes a small wooden bucket from a niche in the brickwork near the second stack and walks to the edge of the vault where the grates give way to the deeper dark. He does not call. He does not whistle. He tips the bucket, and the offal of whatever has just been poured into the molds—shavings, skimmings, the dull grey froth that forms on the surface of the molten metal as it cools—falls through the gaps in the grate and is received below.

The first to appear are the mice.

They are not mice. They are the descendants of mice, the way a cathedral is the descendant of a chapel, and they have been in the dark so long that the dark has become a kind of element they inhabit rather than endure. They pour out of the gaps in the limestone walls—gaps that were once hairline cracks and have been widened, over decades, by the constant passage of small bodies. They are pale in a way that is almost not a color at all, a pink so faint it is closer to the suggestion of pink, the pink of a wax flower held up to a candle, and their skins are so thin that you can see the slow movement of their interiors, the peristalsis of their digestion, the faint dark line of their spines. Their eyes are closed. Not closed the way eyes are closed in sleep; closed the way eyes are closed in species that have stopped using them, sealed over by a translucent lid that has become, over generations, permanent. They feel the world through their whiskers, and their whiskers are extraordinarily long—longer than their bodies, longer than seems structurally reasonable, branching and re-branching from their muzzles in fine, sensitive fans that they hold forward as they move, reading the air the way a hand reads a surface. They move in a constant, low rustle, a sound like paper being crumpled very slowly, and they smell, when the air brings their scent to you, of something mineral and slightly sweet, like wet stone, like the inside of a cave.

They are the size of mice still, mostly, though their legs have become long out of all proportion to their bodies, almost stilted, so that they move with a careful, deliberate stride, lifting each foot high and setting it down with the precision of a person crossing a stream on stepping stones. They are, you realize after watching them for a while, more leg than body. The bodies have become small packages of intestine and lung, suspended between four long limbs that carry them patiently through the gaps and over the stones and along the grates, and the whiskers lead the way. When they reach the place where the offal has fallen, they do not rush. They arrange themselves around the food with a kind of ceremony, each one finding its place, and then they begin to eat with a slow, grinding motion of their jaw that produces no sound at all.

There are hundreds of them. The walls of the vault, which seemed solid in the dim light, are revealed by their movement to be a kind of honeycomb, a thousand small chambers hollowed out in the limestone, each one just large enough for a small nest of these pale, long-legged creatures, and from each chamber the family emerges at feeding time and returns when the feeding is done. They are not afraid of you. They flow around your feet as you stand at the edge of the grate, parting and re-forming the way water in a stream parts around a stone, and once one of them pauses at your boot and its whiskers reach up and brush the leather, reading you, and then it continues on its way, uninterested, having established that you are not food.

---

The cats come when the mice have eaten.

This is a thing you are told by watching, not by speech. The youngest worker, after the mice have taken their share, returns to his station at the crucible, and the other workers continue their work, and the vault resettles into its dim and amber routine, and after a while—a quarter of an hour, perhaps, perhaps less, time is not reliable down here—the cats begin to appear.

They appear from the same gaps the mice came from, but they fill them differently. A mouse pours through a crack like liquid; a cat arrives as a presence, first a shadow in the gap, then the suggestion of a shoulder, then the head, then the whole long body unfolding into the light of the vault. They are the size of small dogs, most of them, though their proportions are not those of any dog. Their bodies are long and low and heavy, built close to the ground, and their legs are set at angles that suggest a permanent readiness to fold, to crouch, to wait. Their fur is the color of the limestone itself, a pale, mottled grey that shifts in the light from almost white to almost the color of bone, and it lies flat and slick against their bodies, as if they have been oiled. Their eyes are open, and they are a green so pale it is almost yellow, rimmed with a darker green that gives them a kind of permanent half-lid expression, as if they are perpetually on the edge of a blink they will never quite complete.

Their jaws are the wrong shape.

You notice this only gradually, because the cats move with such unhurried patience that you are lulled into watching their bodies before you notice their heads, and when you do notice, the wrongness is already several seconds old. Their skulls are wider than they should be, the bones of the cheek flared out, the lower jaw longer and deeper than the architecture of a cat's jaw has any right to be, and the result is a face that is still recognizably a cat's face—the triangular nose, the long whiskers, the upright ears—but a cat's face as if drawn by someone who had only been told about cats and had tried to remember. The widened jaw accommodates the long-legged mice, which could not be eaten by an ordinary cat's mouth without being taken apart, and so the cats of the foundry have, over the generations, evolved a mouth that can take a mouse in a single motion, the way a heron takes a frog. You do not see them eat. You see them approach the place where the mice have eaten, and you see them lower their great heads, and when they raise them again the offal is gone, and the cats are already retreating to the walls, already half-dissolved into the limestone, their pale fur indistinguishable from the sweating stone.

They do not hunt the mice. The mice are fed first, and the cats are fed after, and the arrangement is so old that the hunting instinct has been bred or trained or simply forgotten out of them. The cats and the mice live in the same walls, sleep in adjacent chambers, breathe the same wet air, and there is no hostility between them, only a kind of inherited patience, a contract written in the long body of the place itself. A cat will sometimes pass a mouse in a gap, and the mouse will not flinch, and the cat will not pause, and they will continue on their separate ways as if the other were a piece of furniture. Once, you see a cat settle itself into a hollow in the wall, and a mouse, a few inches away, settles itself into its own hollow, and they are so close that the cat's flank rises and falls with the mouse's breathing, and neither of them seems to find this remarkable.

The cats do not approach the workers, and the workers do not approach the cats. The youngest worker, who feeds them, sets the bucket down and steps back before the first cat appears, and he does not look at them as they eat. He has not looked at them, you realize, in years. Looking is a kind of acknowledgment, and acknowledgment is a kind of claim, and the workers do not claim the cats. The cats are the foundry's, the way the water is the foundry's and the heat is the foundry's, and the workers are themselves a kind of property, and the boundaries between these possessions are so old and so negotiated that no one thinks to redraw them.

---

The thing by the third stack is the one no one will talk about.

You learn of it the way you learn of any unspoken thing in a closed community: by the shape of the silence around it. The workers, who speak rarely and then only low and close, will not speak of it at all. If you ask one of them, in the cautious, mouth-to-ear way of speaking that is the only way down here, the worker will incline his head by a degree, as if in courtesy, and change the subject, or he will simply not hear you, or he will turn away toward his work with a deliberateness that is itself a kind of answer.

You see it only in glimpses, and only from a distance, because the third stack is at the far end of the vault, where the light is dimmest and the grates have given way almost entirely to the open channel of the drainage. It lives in the corner where the stack meets the wall, in a space that the workers have, over the years, walled off with loose bricks and bits of iron and pieces of broken mold, not to keep it in but to keep the sight of it from being too easy. The wall is halfhearted. It is a suggestion of a barrier, a polite screen, and through its gaps you can see, if you look, the steady points of ember-orange that are its eyes.

The eyes are the only part of it you see clearly. They do not blink. They do not move, except very slowly, the way the sun moves across a wall over the course of an afternoon. They are set in a face that is, when you catch it in the right angle of lantern light, the face of a cat that has been allowed to grow for a very long time, but the proportions have gone past cat, the way a river goes past the shape of its original bed. The skull is wide, wider than the wide skulls of the other cats, and the jaw—God, the jaw—is deep enough to hold a boot, and the fur around it has gone dark with age, not grey but a kind of black that is also brown, the way peat is both black and brown. The body, when you see it move, is the size of a large dog, perhaps larger, and it moves with the same patient, unhurried gait as the other cats, but heavier, slower, as if the air itself is thicker around it.

It is old. You understand this without being told. The other cats are its descendants, or its relatives, or its pack, and they move around it the way planets move around a heavier body, in orbits that take into account its gravity. When it shifts in its corner, the cats in the nearby gaps shift too, and the mice in the deeper chambers go quiet, and the youngest worker, who has been pouring at the crucible, pauses for a quarter of a second before resuming. The thing by the third stack is not the ruler of the vault; it is something older than rulership, something the foundry made before it knew what it was making, and now it is here, and the workers have made their arrangements with it, and the arrangement is silence.

The workers do not feed it directly. Whatever it eats, it eats from the scraps that fall through the grate, or it eats the smaller cats, or it eats the mice, or it eats something else that lives in the deeper dark, something you do not see. It is not hungry, in any way you can read. It is not sleepy. It is not awake, exactly. It is simply present, the way the heat is present and the water is present, and its presence has become part of the texture of the vault, the way the weeping of the limestone and the breathing of the stacks are part of the texture. You stop being afraid of it, after a while, the way you stop being afraid of the heat. It is too large to be afraid of, in the same way that the foundry is too large to be afraid of, and the city above is too large to be afraid of, and the centuries are too large to be afraid of. It is patient, and you learn to be patient around it, and the patience is a kind of peace.

---

The thing in the drainage channel is older.

You see it only once, and only because you kneel at the grate near the second stack and look down into the dark below, and the lantern light, held at the right angle, reaches further than you expected. The drainage channels run under the grates, a system of shallow cuts in the limestone that carry the condensation and the small spills and the slow weeping of the walls down toward whatever the deeper dark is, and in one of these channels, far back, something looks up at you.

Its eyes are not the pale green of the cats or the sealed pink of the mice or the ember-orange of the thing by the third stack. They are yellow, but a yellow so deep it has become almost a separate color, the yellow of sulfur, the yellow of a vein of ore in a cliff face, the yellow of something that has been in the dark for so long that the dark has stained it the way tea stains a cup. The eyes are set in a face you cannot make out, only the suggestion of a face, a low, wide shape pressed against the stone, and the body behind it disappears into the deeper dark where the lantern light does not reach.

It does not move. It watches you, and you watch it, and the water of the channel runs between you in a thin, steady sheet, and after a moment you realize that the eyes are watching you the way the limestone watches you, the way the heat watches you, the way the foundry watches you. They are not curious. They are not afraid. They are simply aware, in the way that very old things are aware, with a kind of attention that has no opinion in it.

You stand up. You do not speak of it. There is nothing to speak of, and no one to speak to, and the unspoken things in this vault are heavier than the spoken things, and have been for a long time.

---

Above all of them—the workers, the mice, the cats, the thing by the third stack, the thing in the drainage—the foundry breathes.

It is not a living thing, exactly, but it has made its arrangements with the living things, and the living things have made their arrangements with it, and the result is a kind of life, or a kind of life-adjacent thing, a slow, hot, wet continuity that has been running since the second decade after Filament was founded and will run, if no one stops it, for a very long time after the city above has forgotten it is there. The stacks breathe their slow, sick exhale. The water runs its low, steady chuckle. The creatures watch from the walls with their many patient eyes. The workers move in their unhurried choreography, ladle and mold and rail cart, ladle and mold and rail cart, their bodies softened and their clothes accreted and their faces gone quiet with the long absence of surprise. The thing by the third stack watches from its corner, and the thing in the drainage watches from below, and the whole vast slow machinery of the buried foundry turns one more degree into the centuries, and the centuries do not mind, and the foundry does not mind, and the inhabitants of the foundry, who are many and various and patient beyond any patience the upper city has ever imagined, do not mind either. They are still doing this. They will be doing this for a while yet.
