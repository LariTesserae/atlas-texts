# What Lives in the Hollow of Slow Rain

What lives here has learned to live slowly.

It is not that the hollow is uninhabited. It is that whatever is here has made a kind of peace with the pace of stone, and has let the brisker kinds of life — the crawling, the flying, the blood-warm — go elsewhere. To call its residents *inhabitants* would be a kind of insult. They are too still for that word. They are more like *leftovers of presence*, or *arrangements that have begun to tend themselves*.

The mosses are the most populous, and they are the closest thing the basin has to citizens. They are old, by the look of them — old enough that they have stopped growing into recognizable shapes and have begun instead to grow into the spaces between things, the cracks, the undersides, the slightly concave places where moisture gathers. Some of them are pale, the silvery olive of things that have spent so long in dim light they have forgotten what green is *for*. Others are a deeper, more dignified green, the green of something that has decided not to compete with anything and so can afford its color without ostentation. They do not seem to be in any hurry. They are the ones who will be here last, probably, when the last worked stone has crumbled into the shape of an unworked stone. They have no opinions about this.

The lichen is their slower cousin, and it lives mostly on the standing stones and the stub of the low wall. It is a thin crust, a kind of pale crusting, the color of bone that has been left in a cellar. It does not appear to be doing anything. If you looked at a particular patch of it for a thousand years, you might, at the end, find it had crept a finger's width further up the stone. This is the rate it has chosen, and it does not seem to mind.

The shrubs are a more recent arrival, or perhaps a more stubborn one. They are woody, reduced, twisted into shapes that suggest a long argument with wind that has now been settled — they have agreed to be small. Their leaves, where there are leaves, are small and leathery, as if the plant has decided that any leaf larger than a thumbnail would be a wasteful gesture in this climate. They are the color of dust. They bloom, if they bloom, in some season no visitor has ever happened upon. Mostly what they do is stand in the same posture they stood in a hundred years ago, and will stand in a hundred years hence, the tips of their armature holding a small handful of leaves like someone absently turning a coin between their fingers.

The fern is the strangest of the plants, and the most deliberate-looking. It grows out of a crack in a fallen block near the eastern curve, and it has the appearance of having *chosen* that crack, in some long slow arborescence of consideration. Its fronds are still. They do not bend, even when something passes near. They have a faint dusting of pale mold at the edges, and one might almost mistake this mold for a kind of beard the fern has grown deliberately, in the way of very old men who cease to shave. It does not seem to be in any kind of distress about its condition. It seems to be the only one of its kind, and to have made its peace with that.

There are, perhaps, a few things that are not plants. The drops of condensed moisture that sometimes fall from the underside of a stone and make their small sound on the earth — these are not really *inhabitants*, but they are part of the life of the place, the way rain is part of the life of a roof. There are the slow chemical turnings of the soil, the breathing of the damp, the almost imperceptible creep of oxidation on the iron-bear in the worked stones if any of them contain iron. There is the memory of water in the lowest places, held in the clay like a thought held in a mind.

There may be worms. It would be strange if there were no worms. But if they are here they are so quiet about it that they have not registered on the basin. They are, perhaps, the deepest citizens — the ones who have stopped moving altogether, and have become a kind of dark thread in the earth.

What is most striking is what is *absent*. There are no birds, because there is nothing here to call a bird down from the rim. There are no insects, because the dim warm damp is not the dim warm damp of a forest floor; it is the dim warm damp of a place that has been swept clean, or that has chosen emptiness. There are no spiders, no ants, no wasps. The absence is not hostile. It is more like the absence of guests at a house where the host has stopped sending out invitations. The house is still here. It is still in good order. It simply does not ask for company.

There is, you might say, one other resident, and it is the most elusive. It is the *shape* of whoever was here before — the ones who set the four cardinal stones, who built the low wall dry-stacked in the eastern curve, who set the lintel-shaped table on its two uprights near the northern edge, who placed the ring of small stones at the southwestern rim. They are not here anymore. But the *arrangement* they made is here, and it has begun, in the long quiet, to take on a kind of life of its own. It is the geometry of intention that the basin keeps — the curve, the four stones, the rise, the moat — held now in its own stillness. It is not a ghost. It does not rattle or moan. It is more like the impression left in a cushion after someone has stood up: a form without a person, still warm.

If you stood in the hollow for a long time, you might begin to feel that this is the real inhabitant — not the mosses, not the lichen, not the patient fern, but the *form of the place itself*, the held shape that something made and then left for the weather to keep. The plants are only what has grown into the form, the way dust grows into the lines of an unworn hand. The basin is not ruined. It is simply *empty in the way a cup is empty*, with the shape of what it was for still perfectly preserved in its sides.

This is what lives here. Patience. Slow green. The memory of an arrangement. The soft falling of moisture from the underside of stone to earth. Time, resting.
