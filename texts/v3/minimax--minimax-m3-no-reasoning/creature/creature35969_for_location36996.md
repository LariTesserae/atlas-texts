# Inhabitants of the Bowl

The mosses are the oldest ones here. They came first, or they were here first that anyone — any water, any wind — could account for. They do not move in any way that can be seen by an eye that blinks, but between blinks they have crossed the pebble again, have crept another centimeter up the green stone tool, have opened a new lobe at the edge of their felt. They are several species, or many species, and they do not compete so much as layer — cushion over cushion, the lower ones dying slowly into the ones above, becoming the dark sweet peat of their own ancestors. Some are the color of wet slate. Some are the pale luminous green of new ferns. Some are nearly black and grow where the walls lean so far inward that the rain never quite strikes them directly. They drink the mist. They hold what the mist gives them and give it back, drop by drop, to the walls, to the pool, to the air. If you sit among them for an hour, your knees will be wet through to the bone before you have any feeling of being wet at all.

The liverworts are their cousins, and they live in the places the mosses will not go — flatter stone, damper stone, stone so old it has forgotten it was ever hard. They are thalloid, and lie against the rock like a pressed leaf, like a small green tongue. Their reproduction is something no one in the bowl watches. They simply are more numerous each year by a small fraction, and less numerous by a smaller fraction, and the difference accumulates without announcement.

The bromeliads live in the trees. Each one has made of itself a small cistern at the heart of its rosette, and each cistern holds a different aquarium — rain, leaf-rot, a drowned moth, a single strand of something algae-green swaying gently. The frogs of the bowl are born in these cisterns. They are tiny, and they are several colors. The largest are a dull mossy green with a stripe of pale yellow along the lip; these are the ones you would see if you sat very still. The smallest are the color of wet bark and are never seen except as a brief motion in the eye-corner. The frogs call, when they call, in a single high thin note that the bowl absorbs before it can echo. Their tadpoles develop in the bromeliad-tanks and emerge as something that is not quite a frog yet, something with a tail it will lose by degrees, and by the time the tail is gone, the young frog has already learned to climb. There are tree frogs here that never touch the pool below, that live their whole lives in the canopy of water-tanks, drinking the rain that falls into their grandparents. They are loudest in the mist.

Below them, in the pool, there are other things. The pool is shallow — knee-deep at its center, shallower at the edges — and it is warm, and it is dark, and it is not still. There is a current in it so slow it cannot be measured by any clock the bowl owns. It enters from somewhere beneath the lowest tier of stone and exits by some route that has not been seen and does not need to be seen. The pool's surface is a skin of floating liverworts and a single species of small round lily with leaves the size of a thumb; the flowers of this lily are white and open only at night, briefly, and by morning are a soft collapsing cup. In the water live things that look, at a glance, like dark sticks, but the sticks have small patient eyes and move, when they move, with a thoughtfulness that does not belong to fish. They are salamanders, or close to salamanders, and some of them are very old. The oldest one — if there is an oldest one, if they do not all die and are reborn in the same pool in the same way — is a deep soft brown with a back of moss-green, and it lies along the bottom in the warmest part and does not move for weeks at a time. The young ones are brighter and move more, but not much. They eat what falls into the pool: a drowned moth, a dropped berry, the small frogs that misjudge a leaf. There are insect larvae in the pool too, blood-red, that wriggle when the light changes, and the salamanders eat those as well, and there are small pale snails that graze the underside of the lily pads and never appear above. The pool, in its way, is a whole country, with its own citizens and its own quiet treaties.

The trees are the largest inhabitants, and they are not really inhabitants so much as long-term guests who have stopped pretending to leave. Their roots have found the cracks in the stone and have widened them, slowly, patiently, the way water does, and the cracks are now mostly root. The trunks are short and broad and lean as if listening. The bark is so thick with moss that the wood beneath it has not seen air in many human generations; the tree is being eaten alive, slowly, by the things that live on it, and is content with this arrangement. The crowns are wide and flat, like umbrellas, and they hold up the bromeliads the way shoulders hold up smaller heads. The leaves of the trees are oval and leathery and dark green on top and a paler green below, and when the mist settles on them they hold the mist as droplets for hours after the mist has gone. A leaf falling here falls into a thick layer of old leaves and is not heard. There are small white blooms on the trees in a season that does not match any season in the world outside the bowl — sometimes twice a year, sometimes not for two years, sometimes in a thin flush that lasts an afternoon. The flowers do not fully open. They swell, they pale, they drop their petals one at a time into the wet air, and the petals fall and are gathered by the mosses and become part of the floor. The trees, like the salamanders, are slow. They add a ring to their girth so slowly that no one ring has ever been seen being added; the trees simply, over the years, become slightly thicker, slightly more gnarled, slightly more themselves.

There are other small things. Springtails live in the wet moss and hop in the air when the light shifts; they are white and very small and there are uncountable numbers of them, and they are the principal food of a kind of small spider that lives in the lower branches and builds no web but hunts by sitting very still and then not being very still. There are beetles with backs of burnished green that graze the algae on the wettest stones. There are millipedes, and there are flatworms that glide over the damp leaves at night and are gone by morning. There are moss-mites too small to be seen, and nematodes in the soil that is mostly not soil, and in the soil there are threads of fungus that connect one tree to another and pass sugars between them, a quiet economy that has been running for longer than anything in the bowl has a name for.

The grey creature with the red eyes is not a permanent resident. It is a visitor from the high forest outside the bowl, a small marsupial that comes in along the upper rim when the nights are warm and leaves before dawn. It eats the tree-frogs, or the beetles, or both, and it has been doing this for many bowl-lifetimes. Its droppings, on the moss-shelf where it sits, are small and dark and are absorbed quickly. The bowl does not mind its visits. The bowl does not mind anything very much.

The pebbles are not alive. The tool is not alive. But the moss advancing on them is alive, and the moss will, in its own unhurried way, finish them — has, in fact, already begun to finish them, the way the bowl has already begun to forget the hand that placed them. Whatever person, whatever small people, came up the long difficult way to this place and laid the pebbles in a circle and left the green-stone tool here, they have been gone long enough that the bowl no longer remembers them as people but only as a fact: that the bowl has been visited, and will be visited again, perhaps, or perhaps not. The bowl does not wait. The bowl does not need to wait. The bowl is patient in the way that stone is patient, and the stone here is beginning to remember the shape of water.

The hum is not made by any single inhabitant. It is the bowl itself, the slow vibration of the whole — water, stone, root, air, the small shiver of the lily pads, the longer shiver of the trees, the deep almost-not-sound of the warm pool breathing its slow current in and out. To stand in the bowl long enough is to be enrolled in it. The heart learns the rhythm. The breath, without deciding to, slows. The eyes, after an hour, stop looking for birds.
