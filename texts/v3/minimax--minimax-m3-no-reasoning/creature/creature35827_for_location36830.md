There are perhaps a hundred and seventy people in the hollow, though no one has counted them in a long while. They are small in build — the dark stone and the dim light seem to have made them so — and their skin carries the same range of browns as the wet basalt around them, so that at the edges of vision they are sometimes hard to separate from the walls they lean against. Their hair is most often black, and they wear it long, or bound up with vine-cord, or shorn close to the skull in the way of the very old. They are not a uniform people; a visitor from far off would notice the difference between the broad, square faces of some families and the narrower, sharper features of others, and would guess that several groups came down into the hollow in different generations, and stayed, and were folded into one another. They speak a single language now, though an older woman will sometimes use a word that no one else can place, and the children will look at her with polite incomprehension and then forget.

They have learned to live in the wet.

This is the first thing, and it shapes everything. Their feet, bare, are wide and tough, the soles calloused from a lifetime on stone that is almost always cool and often slick with the morning's condensation. They walk with a slight lift, a careful placing, that someone from a drier country would have to think about and they do not. Children learn the steps young, and an infant will sometimes be set down on the flagstones outside a doorway before it can sit up, and watched. They are short people, as I said, but not small — there are broad shoulders among the men, the women move with a contained, deliberate economy — and their clothes are of woven leaf-fibers, layered, that drink the damp and dry slowly through the day. The fibers darken with use. A new garment, fresh-woven, is pale gold; an old one is the color of wet bark. No one is dressed for a place that is not this place.

The town has a center, and the center is the basin. The basin is not large — a person could span it with arms out — but it is the heart of the hollow, and the people come to it many times a day. The women who draw water do so with shallow gourds that have been cut and smoothed and fitted with a handle of vine, and they pour from one vessel to another in the air, letting the water fall from a height, which is the way they know it is clean. A man may kneel at the basin's lip and drink directly from his cupped hand, but only if no one is watching, because the women have opinions about this. Children are not allowed to draw water until they are old enough to do it without spilling, which is later than they would like. Old people sit on the ring of stones around the basin for hours at a time, doing nothing visible, and this too is a kind of work, because the basin is watched, and someone should always be watching. When a stranger comes into the hollow — and they do come, sometimes, led down by a guide who has been paid in salt or in the small bright shells that trade up from the coast — the stranger is brought first to the basin, and water is given, and this is the welcome.

There is no chief, exactly, but there is an elder who is listened to. He is called something that means, roughly, *the one who remembers the stones*, and his name for the hollow, the true name, is longer than the word the visitors use. He is very old. He sits in a chamber that has been partly hollowed from the back wall of the hollow, on a raised stone that is worn smooth from use, and his beard is white, and his eyes are not good anymore. He has the look of a man who is half in the place and half somewhere else. He speaks rarely. When he does, people listen the way they listen to the drip of water — without seeming to, but completely.

The town wakes slowly. There is no true dawn down here, only the slow brightening of the green twilight as the column of light from the crack above shifts across the floor and finds a new patch of flagstone. A woman will rise from her sleeping-mat and stand for a moment in the doorway of her chamber, which is low, so that she must duck to enter, and look out at the ferns across the way, and the small luminous patch where the morning light is just arriving, and she will breathe in, and the breath will come out as a faint mist in the cool air, and then she will go to the basin. The smoke from the first cooking-fires is the signal that the day has begun, though the smell of it is hard to separate from the smell of the ferns themselves, which is the smell of everything green and a little peppered, the smell of things that have just stopped being water.

The food is gathered, not grown. There is no planting here that anyone remembers, though the women who work with the vines have a saying — something like *what the forest gives, the forest gives back* — which may once have meant something else. They eat fern-fiddleheads, uncoiled and pale, picked when they are still curled tight; the white hearts of palm, brought up from somewhere far below in ropes and baskets; the small sour fruits of a bush that grows where the plateau cracks and the soil is thin; mushrooms, which the old women know and the young women are taught, slowly, one kind at a time, with great seriousness. They eat fish when the rains are heavy enough to send a stream down the crack, and the fish come flopping into the basin, and there is a scramble, and laughter, and the children are soaked. They eat the figs of the strangler fig when they ripen, which is a brief season, and the figs are small and dark and stain the fingers purple-black, and the children come back from the upper edges of the hollow looking guilty and delighted. They keep a kind of dried meat — small strips of the deer-like creature, smoked over a slow fire of palm-wood — but they do not eat it often, and there is a feeling about it that the meat is borrowed, that the deer-like creature has more right to the water at the basin's edge than the hunters do.

The hunters go up and out, in the mornings sometimes, in the evenings often, climbing the broken stair of the entrance with long toes and careful hands. They carry spears tipped with knapped stone — not the brittle, gleaming stone of high places, but a duller, tougher stone that holds an edge — and they carry nets of vine-cord, and small pouches of ground nut for the long wait. They are quiet men, mostly. They do not speak about what they have caught or not caught. A successful hunter will lay his catch at the door of an old woman who lives alone, or at the basin, and walk away without saying anything. The women will prepare the meat. The men will not be thanked in any way that acknowledges the thanks, because that would be to claim what is not theirs to claim.

The dogs are not dogs, exactly. They are the size of dogs, and the color of dogs, and they have the faces of dogs, but their ears are rounder and their tails are shorter, and they do not bark. They make a single low sound, somewhere between a hum and a moan, when they want attention, and otherwise they are silent. They were here before the town, or they came down into it at some point no one can now say, and they have made themselves useful in the way of dogs everywhere — they warn of strangers, they clean the floor of scraps, they lie across the feet of old people on cold mornings. A litter of pups is born in the spring, and the children name them all, and half of them die, and the children grieve, and then there is another litter. The dogs eat the beetles that work the damp earth into their small volcanos, which is one of the reasons the beetles do not multiply past the point of the bearable.

The foxes — the basalt foxes, as they are sometimes called, though the word *fox* is the visitors' word — are a different matter. They live deeper in the ferns, and they are seen only in glimpses: a flash of slate-colored fur, a low shape moving between the broad-leafed plants, the brief impression of eyes catching the column of light. The people do not hunt them. There is an understanding, older than any of them, that the foxes belong to the hollow in a way the people do not, and that to take one would be to take something that cannot be returned. The foxes come at night, sometimes, to the basin's edge, and drink, and leave, and in the morning there are faint tracks in the wet stone that no one wipes away.

The bats are the clock of the hollow. At the first thinning of the light in the crack above — and the light does thin, in a way that is felt rather than seen, because the crack faces west and the hollow darkens earlier than the plateau does — a small restlessness begins in the upper reaches. A single bat drops, then another, then a dozen, and then there is the column, pouring down and out through the crack in a soundless, streaming ribbon, and the children stop what they are doing and watch. They always watch. They have watched every evening of their lives and they still watch. The bats do not seem to mind. They pour out, and wheel once above the plateau, and are gone into the dimming air, and the hollow is quieter for their leaving.

The lizards are everywhere. They are slate-colored and quick, and they hunt the walls for moths and flies and the small pale insects that gather around the cooking-smoke. They are not afraid of the people; they will run across a bare foot and not pause. The children try to catch them and cannot. The old people do not try. There is a kind of truce.

The people themselves, in the late part of the day, settle. The cooking-fires are banked. The women sit at the edges of the chambers with their weaving — leaf-fibers stripped and soaked and dried and split into fine ribbons, then woven on a small wooden frame that is held in the lap — and the shuttle of their hands is steady, and the mats that come off the frame are patterned in ways that seem to have no source, no design, but appear anyway, year after year, the same patterns returning, the same small variations. The men sit too, sometimes, but their hands are less patient, and they will more often be found sharpening a stone, or mending a spear-shaft, or simply looking at the ferns. The children play in the basin-ring, or chase each other through the flagstone paths, or lie on their backs on the stones and look up through the crack at the bit of sky that is visible there, which changes its color through the day and is, by the late afternoon, a deep and luminous blue, and they wonder what is above it.

At night, the hollow is its own country. The column of light from the crack above moves off the floor and is gone, and the green twilight deepens into a blue-dark that is not black, because the mineral glow in the walls is enough, just, to see by. The drip of water is louder then, or seems so. The ferns are a smell. The dogs are warm against the legs of whoever is sitting. A child is carried, sleeping, from the basin-ring to a sleeping-mat. An old woman sets down her weaving and goes inside. The smoke from the last fire has long since found its way up and out. There is the distant thin whistle of wind in the crack above, and the sound, which is faint and could be imagined, of water moving somewhere below, in the deep places the people do not go.

And in the morning it will begin again. The light will return to the floor. The ferns will be a half-inch further up the walls. The basin will be full, drop by drop, from the ceiling that has known only this place. A woman will stand in her low doorway and breathe out, and the breath will mist, and she will go to draw water.

The hollow keeps them, or they keep the hollow, and it is hard, by now, to say which.
