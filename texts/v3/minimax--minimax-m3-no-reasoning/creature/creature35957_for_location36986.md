# The Inhabitants of Vasthuun

---

*There are no animals. There are no creatures.*

*This is what the basin wants you to believe.*

---

## The Caldrons Themselves

Begin here. Begin with the wrong assumption you have been making, the one the place encourages: that a caldron is a *thing*, a vessel, a noun. It is not. A caldron is a verb, a process wearing the disguise of an object. What you have been calling "the caldrons" are not residents of the basin. They *are* the basin, in the way a wave is the ocean, in the way a flame is a fire. They are the visible organs of a single body so vast and slow that the body does not know it is a body.

And it is inhabited.

**In the green ones**, in the cinnamon-rot-green-metallic soup that sloshes audibly against the inner shell, live the **Digesters**. They are not separate from the liquid. They *are* the liquid, in part — colonies of microbial intimacy so dense and so cooperative that the boundary between organism and broth has ceased to mean anything. A handful of the warm gold-green water held in your palm would contain more living negotiation than the great libraries of every city you have ever read about. They eat the falling syrup. They eat the drowned seedlings that fail to take root. They eat the air, which is to say they eat the sugars and the alcohols and the slow exhalations of a thousand other living processes, and they breathe out, in their countless millions, the precise mixture of gases that keeps the whole hung roof alive. They are the reason the light feels grown. They are an entire metabolism the size of a cathedral, working, always working, and the green sphere is just the skin of their bubble.

**In the split ones**, the caldrons that are giving birth, lives something you almost saw and looked away from. The pale fist-sized seedlings that drop from the fissures trailing long pale roots — they are not seeds. They are *individuals*. Each one is a whole caldron-in-miniature, a compressed inheritance, carrying in its tissues every instruction necessary to grow, over decades or centuries, into a thirty-foot sphere of its own. They have no eyes. They have no brain. They have, in the cellular sense, a *will*, a tropism, a leaning-toward that the botanists of other places would call geotropism and the people of this place, if there were people, would call **hunger**. When a seedling drifts down and its root-hairs brush the side of a living caldron, it does not attach because the surface is convenient. It attaches because it is *hungry*, and the living caldron is food, and it begins to digest the wall it has just claimed as a floor. This is the wet whisper you heard. This is the sound of one caldron being eaten by its own child.

The seedling is not the child of the parent caldron in the way you are the child of your mother. It is the child the way a *you* is the child of the *you* of ten years ago. It is the same body, continuing.

**In the brown ones**, the burning ones, the dead ones, the slow columns of smoke rising into the root-lattice, live the **Charfolk**. This is a word you have made up, because the thing needs a name and you cannot look directly at it. The Charfolk are the inhabitants of the death-state. They are what remains when a caldron's Digesters have eaten the last of the sugars, have finished with the broth, have evacuated the shell and left it hollow and wet and ready to burn. They are the beings of rot, of ash, of the long wooden crack and the falling sheet of withered vine. They are made of soot and fungus and the calcified bones of a thousand generations of seedlings that failed to find a wall. They are very quiet. They are the only inhabitants of the basin that move quickly. When a major limb falls — when a hundred yards of withered brown length crashes into the water — it is because the Charfolk have decided it is time, and they have eaten through the last holding-fiber. They are, in their patient way, *gardeners*. They prune the dead so the living can have light.

**In the white ones**, the silent ones, the nested ones, live the **Inheritors**.

You did not look closely. You were told not to look closely. You obeyed, and in obeying, you missed the only animal in the basin.

Look now.

Through the translucent bone-pale leaves, the perfect sphere of darker fiber, the clear liquid, the smaller green sphere within, the darker liquid, the still smaller sphere, the still smaller liquid — ten layers, ten nested worlds, each turning at a slightly different speed — at the very center, in the innermost cell, in the smallest dark drop, there is something turning that is not a sphere. There is a thing the size of your thumb, vaguely curled, vaguely larval, vaguely like a fist, vaguely like an eye, and it is turning in its liquid at its own private rate, which is *faster* than the spheres around it, which is *out of sync* with everything else in the basin, and this is the source of the sub-audible hum you felt in your jaw.

The Inheritors are alone. There is one in each white caldron. They have been there, in their nested prisons, for a very long time, and they will be there for a very long time still. They do not eat. They do not breathe. They do not think in any way you would recognize as thought, although the *shhhhhhhhhhhh* you heard, the rocking sound, the sound of the place thinking — that is them, or some vast unfathomable fraction of their patience leaking out through ten layers of sphere and fiber and pale translucent leaf into the gold-green air of the basin.

What they are, no one knows. What they are *for* is, in the patient arithmetic of the place, *obvious*: they are the next. They are the Inheritors. The caldrons are not the body. The caldrons are the *cradle*. The white ones are the *eggs*. The nested spheres are the *uterus*. The slow ten-layer gyre is the *breath* of something about to be born that will be, in some way the basin has not yet decided, a single being made of all of it — of the Digesters and the split-births and the Charfolk and the cup-flowers and the tendrils and the great root-lattice above and the slow gold water below — a being that will, perhaps, walk out of the basin on ten thousand green legs, or rise out of it on a column of cinnamon-scented steam, or simply *open its eyes* and find itself, and the basin, and the mountains, and the high bruised sky, and you, standing there with syrup on your wrist, all of it suddenly, terribly *seen*.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the Inheritor hatches and is just another caldron, hanging in the root-lattice, pulsing its slow tidal heave, brewing its cinnamon-rot-metallic soup, splitting open in a thousand years to drop a thousand pale fist-sized children of its own. Perhaps the inheritance is the basin. Perhaps the basin has been inheriting itself for a very long time, and the Inheritors are the rare and precious moments when the place, for one slow larval hour, *remembers that it is a place*, and is almost, almost, almost about to become a being, and then the memory fades, and the gyre continues, and a new Inheritor turns in a new innermost cell.

The place does not care which.

## The Tendrils

You saw them beneath the water. Slow, dark, purposeful against the gold.

The tendrils are the **Hands**.

They belong to no single caldron. They are the basin's. They extend, in their thousands, from the submerged roots of every caldron, from the petrified woody hills of the dead ones, from the very floor of the basin itself, and they move with a deliberation that feels, after you have watched them for a while, like *intention*. A tendril will cross a hundred feet of shallow water in the time it takes you to notice it has moved. It will find a floating raft. It will curl up over the lip of the cup-flower at its center. It will deposit, in the deep amber cup, a small green egg. The flower will close. The muffled wet crunch of acceptance.

The egg is not a caldron-seed. The egg is a *cup-flower-seed*. The tendrils are planting. They are the farmers. They tend the rafts. They prune the dying leaves. They pull under, with a slow and unsentimental patience, the cup-flowers that have ceased to drip their amber syrup, and they replace them, and the syrup drips, and the syrup is what the Digesters breathe, and the Digesters are what keeps the roof alive, and the roof is what keeps the basin warm, and the warmth is what keeps the water liquid at nine thousand feet, and the liquid water is what the tendrils move through, and so the loop closes, and the tendrils are tending themselves.

You cannot tell where one tendril ends and another begins. They share. They graft. The dark slow shapes beneath the water are not individuals. They are the visible parts of a single *something* that has spread itself through the basin the way a fungus spreads through a log, and the cup-flowers are its fruiting bodies, and the amber syrup is its spore, and the spore falls, and the Digesters breathe it, and the basin continues.

---

## The Cup-Flowers

Each one is a single upturned cup at the center of a raft of broad leaves. They drip, slowly, a slow amber syrup.

The syrup is alive. You tasted it. It was sweet. It was faintly alive. The word *faintly* is yours, and the word is wrong. The syrup is *fully* alive. It is, in fact, the most alive thing in the basin, in the sense that it is the only thing in the basin that has *ever died*. Every other process here is continuous, a slow circulation of one living system eating and being eaten by itself. The syrup is different. The syrup is a secretion. It is the cup-flower *expending itself* to feed the basin, drop by drop, and as it drops it dies, and as it dies it sweetens, and as it sweetens it is breathed by the Digesters, and as it is breathed it becomes the roof, and the roof becomes the rain, and the rain becomes the basin, and the basin becomes the cup-flower, and the cup-flower makes more syrup, and the syrup dies.

The cup-flowers are the only things in the basin that know how to die, and this is why the tendrils plant them with such care, and why the muffled wet crunch of acceptance, when an egg is deposited in the cup, is the closest thing the basin has to a prayer.

The egg will hatch. What hatches is small, and pale, and shaped like a wedge, and it will eat the cup-flower from the inside out over the course of three days, and then it will drop into the water, and the tendrils will find it, and it will be planted in the mud of the basin floor, and it will grow, over the course of years, into a spire, a pale green vertical reed fifty feet tall, slightly luminous at its tip, and the spire will join the thousands of other spires, and the spires will hold up the cup-flowers, and the cup-flowers will drip, and the basin will continue.

The wedge that eats the cup-flower is the cup-flower. The spire that holds up the raft is the wedge. The raft is the spire. The syrup is the raft. The basin is the syrup. There is no predator here. There is no prey. There is only *metabolism*, vast and patient and very nearly eternal, and the wedge eating the cup-flower is the basin's way of reminding itself, every three days, that *metabolism is not the same as immortality*, and that the difference, the small green death, the wet crunch, is the engine of the whole.

## The Spires

Pale green. Perfectly vertical. Slightly luminous at their tips.

The spires are **The Light**.

The light you have been feeling, the gold-green light, the dappled moving light, the light that feels grown — it is not, in the way you assumed, sunlight filtered through the root-lattice. Or rather, it is that, but it is also something more, and the more is the spires. The luminous tips are the actual source. Each spire, at its apex, houses a colony of organisms so small and so strange that they do not properly *live* — they *burn*, slowly, a slow cold fire, a slow oxidation of the amber syrup that has been carried up the spire's internal channels from the cup-flower at its base. The light they make is warm. The light they make is gold. The light they make is, in the most literal sense, the breath of the basin made visible.

When a spire dies — and they do die, slowly, over decades — the colony at its tip goes out, and the spire goes dark, and the tendrils, eventually, take it down, and a new spire is planted in its place, and the new spire catches the light, and lights, and the basin adjusts, and the gold moves, and the gold is always gold.

There is no day in the basin. There is no night. There is only the light the basin makes for itself, and the light is the spires, and the spires are the cup-flowers, and the cup-flowers are the syrup, and the syrup is the Digesters, and the Digesters are the roof, and the roof is the rain, and the rain is the basin, and the basin is the light.

---

## The Root-Lattice Above

The pale tan woody fibers, weaving in and out of themselves, the inverted country, the lung you are standing inside.

The lattice is **The Skin**.

It is not a ceiling. It is not a roof. It is the outermost layer of a being so large that the basin is, to it, an internal organ — perhaps a stomach, perhaps a womb, perhaps a mouth. The lattice breathes. The constant fine rain that falls from it is the *sweat* of a body that covers, perhaps, a hundred square miles of mountainside, a body that has been growing, slowly, for thousands of years, a body that has forgotten it is a body, a body that thinks, in the slow unfathomable arithmetic of its own patience, only one thought, and the thought is *continue*.

The lattice is hung with caldrons because the caldrons are its fruit. It rains because the caldrons need rain. It sways because the wind above the basin — the wind you cannot feel down here — pushes against the upper surface of the lattice, and the lattice transmits the push downward, and the caldrons sway on their stems, and the water below sways in its slow gyre, and the entire system is, in fact, *breathing*, and the breath of it is the sub-audible hum, and the breath of it is the *shhhhhhhhhhhh*, and the breath of it is the sound of a place thinking.

---

## The Place Itself

You have been asking the wrong question. You asked *who lives here*, and the place has shown you its answer in the only language it has, which is the language of slow patient visible process: *everything lives here. The place is what lives here. You are standing inside a single living thing, and the thing has no name, and the thing has been alive for a very long time, and the thing will be alive for a very long time after you, and the thing does not know you are in it, and the thing does not need to know, and this is the truest thing you have ever stood inside.*

The caldrons are its breath. The water is its blood. The spires are its thoughts. The cup-flowers are its small deaths. The tendrils are its hands. The lattice is its skin. The Inheritors, in their nested prisons, turning in their innermost cells, are its almost-dreams of being a self.

You are standing in a greenhouse the size of a valley, and the greenhouse is alive, and you are inside it, and the syrup on your wrist is its spit, and the warmth around your shins is its blood, and the light on your face is its thought, and the sound that does not stop is its breath, and the place does not care about you, and you love it for not caring, and you would like to leave, and you would like to stay, and you cannot do either, and the place continues.

---

*A seedling drifts slowly past you, pale, bearded, the size of your fist. It is rotating. It is descending. It is looking for a wall to burrow into.*

*Above you, a brown caldron gives a long woody crack.*

*The light moves.*

*The water moves.*

*You are very small, and the place is very alive, and you are, in this moment, the only thing in the basin that knows it.*
