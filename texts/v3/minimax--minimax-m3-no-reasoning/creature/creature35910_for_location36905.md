The people here do not raise their voices. They have not, in three centuries, had reason to develop the musculature for it, and even if they had, the basalt would swallow the sound before it traveled ten paces. So when they speak, they speak at the volume of a whisper, and they speak at a whisper whether they are angry, whether they are bargaining, whether they are delivering the formal sentence of disgrace upon a Breath-Guild apprentice who has mishandled a root. The apprentice hears his exile pronounced in a whisper. He packs his bundle in a whisper. The streets do not echo his leaving.

They are not, on the whole, a small people, though several generations of vault-life have made them slight. Their ancestors were probably stockier; you can see it in the proportions of the oldest portraits, the ones painted on cambium panels in the first century of the settlement, when the painter was still working out how to render the amber light and made everyone look like surface folk standing in a tavern. The present colonists are narrow through the chest, slightly stooped from a lifetime of looking up at the canopy that has replaced their sky, and pale in a particular way that is not the pallor of deprivation but the pallor of amber light — the way mushrooms are pale when they are grown in certain woods. Their eyes are large, dark, and very still in the face. They do not blink much. They are accustomed to a light that does not flicker, and the first salt-cart drivers who came down from the surface, in the early years of the trade, used to weep for a day afterward, not from grief but from the afterimage of a blue sky. The present drivers are trained. They take wraps. They descend quickly, and they do not look up until the canopy has had time to take them back.

They organize themselves in three guilds and a number of smaller trades. The Graft-Guild is the largest and the youngest; its members are the architects, the city-planners, the patient ones who guide a doorway's migration over the course of a season so that last year's customs house becomes this year's granary becomes next year's chapel without ever quite ceasing to be itself. They are the ones you see most often in the streets, because their work is above-ground, or what passes for above-ground here — at street level, at the threshold level, with the cambium lattices they keep trimmed and the lintels they keep encouraging to grow in the right direction. They are the visible city. They wear leather aprons stained amber with sap, and they carry shears of surface-forged steel that they hone against the corky bark of the streets.

The Dust-Guild is smaller and older. Its members work the canopy. They climb. They do not climb in the way surface climbers climb, with ropes and pitons, because there is no rock here to piton into; they climb the way a child climbs a familiar tree, with the sureness of long acquaintance, their bare feet finding the ridges of bark that they have known since childhood. They are leaner than the Graft-Guild, and their hands are stained a deeper amber, almost brown, from years of handling the heartwood. They know the canopy the way a sailor knows a sea — by its moods, by its sounds, by the particular pitch of the leaf it gives when the dust is rising and the chord that comes before waking. They time their rest to it. They know which leaves will sound which notes. There is one — they call her the *première*, the first one — whose pitch has not changed in living memory, and whose note, when the long evening descends, is the one the city orients itself by.

The Breath-Guild is the oldest and the most respected, and its members are the strangest to look at. They spend much of their working life below the root-line, in the chambers and tunnels where the basalt gives way to the deeper geology, and they come up pallid and slow-moving and smelling faintly of sulfur. They are the ones who negotiate with the substrate — the long conversation, conducted in mineral cycles measured in weeks, between the city's metabolism and the city's bones. They are also the ones who decide, in the formal ritual of disgrace, whether an apprentice has failed. To fail a Breath-Guild apprentice is a serious thing. The apprentice is sent above, into the dry loud light, to join the caravans. It is not a death sentence; most return, after a season or two above, having learned what they lacked. But they return changed. The light has done something to them. They do not blink any more than the others, but they flinch at the canopies. They have to be re-taught the city's sleep.

The salt-cart drivers themselves are a small, proud caste. They are the only colonists who go above as a matter of course, and they are marked by it. Their eyes are wrapped when they come down, and they do not unwrap them until they have spent an hour in the deepest chamber they can find, letting the amber light reabsorb them. Their skin is weathered in a way the others' is not, and they speak, when they have been above too long, in a louder voice than is decent, and they have to be gently reminded. They bring down stories of the surface. They bring down metals, ceramics, salts. Once a year, with great ceremony, they bring down the seeds.

The seeds are the city's great hope and its small grief. Most fail to take. The master-arborists graft them into the cambium lattices of the upper walls, in specially lit alcoves where the dust is thickest, and they wait. Some sprout and wither within a season. Some sprout and grow for a year and then refuse the substrate and die. A few take. The colonists do not celebrate a successful graft. They mark it quietly, in the records, and they tend it, and they wait to see what it will become.

There are children here. The children jump to shake the branches and bring the dust down. They play a game in the upper streets, in the districts where the canopy is youngest and the leaves are still loose in their sockets, of trying to catch the falling particulate in their open mouths, and they laugh when they succeed, which is often, because the dust is light and the air moves slowly and a child's mouth is wide. They are pale and dark-eyed like their elders. They are afraid of the deep drafts. They are taught, very young, which drafts to avoid. They are taught, slightly older, that the échos are not to be bothered. The échos were here first.

The échos are the size of dogs and entirely silent, and they are the city's principal scavengers. They are blind, or nearly so; their eyes are pale and recessed and seem not to focus, and they navigate the root-mat by smell and by some sense no colonist has been able to name. They eat dead cambium. They eat the fallen limbs. They eat the trimmed lintel-ends that the arborists leave for them, as a matter of long-standing arrangement, at the edges of the Graft-Guild's working yards. The arborists do not feed them out of charity. They feed them because the échos keep the root-mat from becoming impassable with fallen wood, and because the échos were here before the first settler drove the first graft into the first trunk, and the colonists understand that the city is not, in any deep sense, theirs. They have been permitted.

The échos tolerate the colonists with what appears to be indifference. They do not flee from human presence. They do not approach it, either. They go about their business in the root-mat, and the colonists go about theirs in the streets above, and the two occupations rarely interfere. Individual échos are known to the arborists of the district they haunt. There is an écho in the chapel-district — the one that was the granary two seasons ago — that has been there longer than any of the present arborists. The arborists call her the Old Lady. She has a pale patch on her left flank, just behind the shoulder, in the shape of a roughly heart-shaped thumbprint. The apprentice who eventually replaces her in the district's working memory will be told about the Old Lady. The apprentice will be told that the Old Lady was there when the chapel was a granary.

There are larger things. The colonists speak of *les longues*, the long ones, and they do not speak of them often. Les longues move through the trunk-forests of the deeper, less-inhabited districts, the districts that the Graft-Guild visits in rotation to fell and thank the oldest trunks. A district that has been recently visited by les longues is recognizable in the morning by a hundred meters of recently grown wood — fresh, pale, faintly luminescent — where the night before there had been a regular street. The trees have grown around them. The trees have grown them a path, and a ceiling, and in some cases a small chamber at the path's end, where the new wood is thickest and brightest, as if the trees had been paid for their trouble. Whatever les longues are, the trees seem to prefer them there. The Graft-Guild does not fell in those districts while the new wood is still curing. They wait. They are patient. They have the patience of the substrate.

Les longues are rarely seen. The colonists have stories, of course; they are a people with stories, and the deep districts are dark and full of strange acoustics. There is a story of a Graft-Guild apprentice who, on a felling rotation in a far district, came around a corner in the new wood and saw, at the far end of a long pale corridor, a shape that moved the way a very large snake moves, or the way a thought moves through the canopy, without visible means. The apprentice walked, very slowly, back the way she had come. She did not run. She reported what she had seen, in a whisper, to the master-arborist of her district. The master-arborist nodded, and noted it in the records, and the district was added to the list of districts that would not be felled that season. The apprentice was not disgraced. She was not commended. She had seen what there was to see, and she had come back, and the trees had grown another corridor in the morning.

There are smaller things. The mites-faucons drop from the canopy on silk threads and take the hexapods that graze the luminous dust where it settles on the upper surfaces of the leaves. The hexapods are tiny and innumerable. The mites-faucons are dramatic. Children watch them from below and try to time their falls. The children are not very good at it. The mites-faucons are not interested in the children. The mites-faucons are interested in the hexapods, and the hexapods are interested in the dust, and the dust is interested, in its slow patient way, in nothing at all, and is content to fall and rise and glitter in the canopy leaves and be caught, sometimes, in a child's mouth.

The colonists live among all of this as a fish lives in water, or as a thought lives in a mind. They are aware of the substrate. They are not always conscious of it. The Graft-Guild apprentice trimming a lintel in a quiet street is conscious only of the lintel, and of the shears in her hand, and of the apprentice beside her who is learning the trade. The Dust-Guild member climbing toward the *première* is conscious only of the bark under her hands and the pitch of the leaf she is tending. The Breath-Guild member in the deep chamber is conscious only of the root she is coaxing through a micro-fracture, and of the slow mineral pulse in the stone. They are not, in those moments, thinking about the vast and ancient life they are immersed in. They are thinking about the work.

But sometimes — at the long transitions, when the canopies sing, and the dust begins its slow descent or its slow rise — they stop, and they listen, and they know. They know they are guests. They know the city will outlast them. They know that the *première* will sing her note long after their great-grandchildren are dust themselves, returned to the canopy, settling on the upper surfaces of the leaves, grazed by the hexapods, taken by the mites-faucons, glitter in the dark.

They call the vault *ici*. Here. It is the only name that fits.
