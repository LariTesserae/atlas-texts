What lives there has forgotten what living is for.

Not in any dramatic way. Not in the way of something that has been deprived, or starved, or broken down by cold or hunger. The creatures of the hollow have all of what they need, in the precise quantity they need it. There is no want here. There is no absence. Wanting itself has been slowly leached out of them, the way minerals are leached from stone by water that never stops. They have been in this place so long, or their ancestors have been in this place so long, or the line between ancestor and descendant has been so gently blurred by so many generations of simply *continuing*, that the distinction between one body and the next has become academic. They are not individuals in the way we use the word. They are the hollow's small, breathing surfaces.

The salamanders are the truest residents.

This is a thing one can feel without being told, the way one can feel which animal in a pond is the pond's own animal. There are so many of them that the floor of the hollow has a second floor, made of their bodies, and beneath them the moss has accommodated their weight so completely that the salamanders rest in shallow depressions shaped to themselves. They are dark. They are the color of wet basalt seen through three inches of water — a color that is not really a color at all, but a degree of absence of light. Their bellies, when one turns a stone and sees them, are the pale grey-pink of something that has never been touched by sun. They have the blunt faces of creatures that do not need to recognize one another. Their eyes are small and dark and they do not, I think, do much. They see motion, perhaps. They see the difference between shadow and a darker shadow. This is enough.

They do not move.

This is the first thing that unsettles, and then the second thing, and then the thing that stops unsettling and becomes the texture of the place. A salamander lies on a stone. Another lies beside it, slightly overlapping. A third is half beneath the first. Their bodies are cool to the touch — cooler than the stone, cooler than the moss, almost cool enough to feel like a current of water rather than a body. Their skin is so thin and so damp that one can feel, beneath the palm, the slow hard working of their hearts. Not fast. Not slow. The tempo of something that has been beating in this dimness for, perhaps, the entire span of its species' time in this hollow. Pick one up and it lies in the hand the way a wet leaf lies in the hand — not limp, not tense, simply placed. Set it down and it does not crawl away. It is already in the place it was. It adjusts one toe, perhaps. The barest possible motion. Then stillness again.

What do they eat?

The question comes and then the question becomes foolish. They eat what is here. They eat what moves, which is not much. They eat the springtails and the mites that pass near their mouths in the dark. They eat the eggs of other salamanders, sometimes, and the dead ones, when one of them finally stops and the others find it by smell and by the slow process of remaining-thing attracting remaining-thing. They do not hunt. They wait with their mouths open, or nearly open, and what walks or crawls or falls into the mouth is what is eaten. A salamander here has perhaps eaten a hundred things in its life. A salamander here has perhaps eaten a thousand. The number is the same. The eating is the same. The mouth, the patience, the small swallow, the settling.

They have grown old without ever having been young. This is the strangest thing about them, though one does not see it at first. There is no young among them. There is no very-old. There are salamanders of a kind of uniform middling size, as if they had all been placed here at the same moment by a hand that understood their proportions and then left. But this is not so. There are small ones. There are smaller ones. They are under the stones, mostly, and under the moss, in the damp dark places where the eye does not reach. They are born — or hatched, or emerged, or however a salamander comes into being — and they grow, slowly, toward the middling size of the floor. They do not grow past it. They grow toward the size that the hollow can hold, the size that the moss and the stones have made room for, the size that allows every salamander to have its place without overlapping more than is acceptable. They grow to fit.

I do not know how old the oldest one is. I do not think the question has meaning here. A salamander in the hollow has lived its entire life without ever having left the hollow. It will live its entire life without leaving the hollow. Its parents lived here. The stones its parents rested on are the stones it rests on. The moss its parents were pressed into is the moss its children will be pressed into. Time, for these creatures, has become a kind of place — a slow place, an unhurried place — rather than a thing that passes. They live in it the way they live in the hollow: completely, without remainder, without destination.

---

The frogs are quieter.

This is wrong to say, because the salamanders are quiet, and the moss is quiet, and the stones are quiet, and to say the frogs are quieter than any of these things seems impossible. But the frogs are quieter, in the way that a held breath is quieter than an exhaled one. They have *withheld* themselves. They are not the absence of motion; they are the held possibility of motion. A frog on a fern is a frog that is about to leap, except that the about-to has lasted for a very long time.

They are small. They are the size of a thumb's first joint, perhaps smaller. Their skin is the green-brown of moss in shadow — the same green-brown, the same shadow — and their eyes, when one looks closely, are not eyes so much as small convex windows into some interior dimness. Their toes are pale and slightly spread and they grip the fern fronds and the wet stone with a delicacy that seems almost too fine for the place. They cling the way a thought clings to the edge of sleep.

Their throats pulse.

That is the only motion, mostly. The throat pulses in, the throat pulses out. In. Out. In. Out. At a tempo that is not the tempo of breath but is something like breath, the way the drip of water is something like a heart. They are breathing, certainly. They are taking the damp air into their small lungs and putting it out again. But the pulsing has a quality of waiting. It is the breathing of something that is holding itself in reserve. The muscles of the hind legs are tensed, faintly, even at rest. The body is poised. The whole frog is a question that has been asked and is waiting for an answer that does not come.

Sometimes one of them leaps.

Once in a long while, in the hours when some accident of weather shifts the quality of the dim light falling down from the opening, a frog moves. It moves with a completeness that is almost shocking after so much stillness — the hind legs extend, the body arcs, the mouth opens to take the air, and it lands somewhere in the mass of fern and moss, and it settles, and the throat resumes its pulse. The leap was not toward anything. The leap was not away from anything. It was the answer to a question no one asked. Then stillness.

There is a sound they make, sometimes. A small wet *cluck*, deep in the throat, more a vibration than a vocalization. It comes from one frog, or from several, and it does not travel. It is absorbed by the moss and the wet stone and the damp air as soon as it is made. It is the sound of a place acknowledging to itself that it is a place. I do not know if it is a call. I do not know if it is a warning. I do not think the frogs know either. It is simply a sound that frogs in the hollow sometimes make, the way stones in the hollow sometimes drip.

They do not breed, or they breed in a manner so slow and so private that no event of breeding is visible. There are small frogs. There are smaller frogs. There are frogs so small they are almost indistinguishable from the moss itself, from the small insects, from the bumps on the wet stone. They have come from somewhere. They are growing toward the middling size of the ferns. They will reach it. They will become still. They will begin to wait.

---

The bats are the highest.

This is true in the literal sense — they are in the upper walls, in the cracks where the rock meets the leaning roots of the fir — and it is true in some other sense that I cannot quite name. They are the highest of the hollow's creatures in the sense that they remember, faintly, a world outside. They are the ones who have, perhaps, flown out into the night and returned. They are the ones who might, if the hour were their hour, take the dark between them and the stars.

But it is not their hour. It is never their hour. The hour of the hollow is the same hour, always — the dim grey-almost-dark of an overcast day held forever in a stone throat — and so the hour of the bats is also forever. They hang in their crevices with their wings folded close and their faces tucked down and their small ears curved forward like the petals of a flower that has not decided to open. They are the color of the wet stone. They are the texture of the wet stone. From a few feet away, one cannot see them. They are knots in the rock. They are bulbs in the bark of the leaning fir's roots. They are part of the place's stillness.

Hundreds of them. Perhaps more. The crevices of the upper hollow are full of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, folded into themselves so tightly that the outline of one bat becomes the outline of the next becomes the outline of the rock. They do not move. They breathe — a faint expansion and contraction of the folded wings, a faint lifting and settling of the small bodies — but they do not move. They wait.

I think they remember flying.

I think this because the muscles of their shoulders are faintly tensed, the way the hind legs of the frogs are faintly tensed. They are held in the posture of flight. Their wings, folded, are not loose; they are wrapped, gathered, prepared. The small heads are tucked down toward the chests that hold the muscles that hold them in the posture. They are bats who are about to fly, except that the about-to has lasted longer than any bat's life, longer than the line of bats who have hung in this crevice, longer perhaps than the crevice itself.

Once, perhaps, they flew out. They took the dark air of the hollow in their wings and they rose, and they passed the opening above, and they entered the night. They found the moths above the forest, perhaps, and the small flying things. They drank from the puddles on the mountain's shoulder. They were bats, for an hour, in the way bats are bats. Then they returned. They folded their wings. They tucked their heads. They waited in the crevice, and the dark did not change, and they have been waiting since.

Or perhaps they have never flown.

I cannot tell. The posture is the posture of flying, but the posture is also the posture of not-flying, which is the posture of being held. They are held in the place. They are part of the place. They have been part of the place for so long that the question of where they have been and where they might go has become as small and as irrelevant as the question of where the water comes from. The water comes from above. The bats came from above. The matter is closed.

---

And then there is the rest.

This is the small life — the worms, the springtails, the mites, the beetles, the things too small to name or to track. They are in the moss. They are under the moss. They are in the film of damp that covers every surface. They are the moss's other half. The moss could not be the moss without them; they could not be themselves without the moss. Together they make a kind of slow engine, a slow circulation of small deaths and small digestions and small transformations of one kind of matter into another.

The worms are thin and pale and they live in the uppermost layer of the dark mud. They withdraw when disturbed. They emerge when the disturbance passes. They eat the leaf-rot, the fallen fir needles, the small dead things. They become, in time, the substance of the moss. A worm here has eaten a needle that fell from the leaning fir above, perhaps a hundred years ago, perhaps longer. The needle is part of the worm. The worm is part of the mud. The mud is part of the moss. The moss is part of the salamander's resting place. The cycle is very small and very slow and it has been turning for a very long time.

The springtails are the quick ones. They are quick in a relative sense, which is to say that they move at all. They are small white-gray shapes that walk on the surface of the water, sometimes, when the water has gathered in a small hollow of stone. They spring. They spring and they land and they walk a few steps and they spring again. They are the busiest things in the hollow, and the busyness is itself a kind of stillness — the same few motions, repeated, in the same few places, in the same damp dim air. They are the punctuation in the long sentence of the hollow's quiet.

The mites are smaller. The beetles are smaller still. They are at the threshold where one kind of life becomes indistinguishable from another kind of life. A beetle on a fern stem is a small dark shape against a small dark shape, and the eye cannot quite resolve them as two things. They climb. They stop. They climb. They stop. They are doing something. The doing is too small to understand. It is part of the hollow's working, the way the drip of water is part of the hollow's working, the way the pulse of a frog's throat is part of the hollow's working. They are part of the stasis. They keep it turning, the way a slow wheel keeps itself turning by the weight of its own rim.

---

What holds them?

This is the question the hollow asks, in its quiet way, of anything that enters it. The tools at the base of the wall ask it. The chert flakes, sharp and old. The stone with the worn depression. The broken bone. The length of wood that is more peat than wood. They ask it of the moss that is growing over them. They ask it of the salamanders that are pressed around them. They ask it of the fir needle that falls, occasionally, from above.

They ask: what brings you here, and what keeps you here, and what will you become?

The hollow does not answer. The hollow is the answer.

The creatures here have been held so long in the same dim wet air that they have become — what? Not the same. Not interchangeable. But continuous. A salamander is a continuation of the salamanders that were here before. A frog is a continuation of the frogs. A bat is a continuation of the bats. Each one is a small held note in a long sound that does not change. Each one is a single drop in a slow fall of water that does not stop. They are not individuals in the way we use the word, but they are not nothing, either. They are the hollow's way of being alive. They are its breath. They are its pulse. They are the slow warm wet fact of its continuing.

The light does not change. The water continues to weep. The moss continues to hold. The salamanders continue. The frogs continue. The bats continue. The springtails spring and land and spring again. The worms withdraw and emerge and withdraw. The beetles climb and stop and climb.

It is a place in balance.

It is a place that has found its form.

It will go on being its form for a very long time, until something from above — a fall of stone, a fall of fir, a change in the weather that does not change back — interrupts the equilibrium and the hollow becomes something else. But this is not something the hollow thinks about. This is not something the creatures of the hollow think about. They are in the place. They are the place. They have been the place for so long that the distinction has softened, the way the chert flakes have softened at their edges, the way the wood has softened into peat, the way the moss has softened over everything that is not moving.

A fir needle, occasionally, falls.

That is the only event.

And the hollow receives it, as it receives the drips and the dim light and the small held breath of the bats, and it takes it in, and it holds it, and it goes on.
