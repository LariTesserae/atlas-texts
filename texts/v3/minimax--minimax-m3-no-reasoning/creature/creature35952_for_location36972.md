# The Tenders

They live in the columns the way termites live in wood — not as guests, not as intruders, but as a population that has become structural. You could not remove them without bringing the whole system down, and the system has not been brought down. The system has been running, the words you used are exactly right, *running*, the way a body runs, the way a metabolism runs, and the Tenders are part of the metabolism now. They have been part of it for so long that their presence in it is no longer an event.

They are small. This is the first thing the place tells you, and it tells you by scale: the columns around them are cathedral. A Tender at the base of a mature stalk is a vertical smudge, a thing that could be mistaken for a piece of equipment, a leaning tool, a shadow. They have been selected for smallness over generations, or they have accommodated themselves to smallness, or — and this is what the older ones will not say aloud — they have been *made* small, the way working animals are made small, the way draft horses were once bred back to ponies when the mines narrowed. A Tender standing on a pipe junction is a head and a hunched shoulder and two careful hands and that is all. The rest is a suit.

The suits are the second thing. They are not suits in the way a vacuum suit is a suit — they are not built to keep the inside in. They are built to keep the outside *out*, but selectively, and the selectivity is the point of them. The air here would cook a wet lung in a minute. The dust would sand a throat raw in an hour. The brine mist that rises off every leaking joint would pit an exposed cornea by evening. So the suit is layered, each layer a different negotiation with a different violence: the outermost skin is a thick, dark, faintly iridescent material that reflects the furnace-glow and sheds the dust, and the Tenders are very beautiful in it, in the way a beetle is beautiful. Underneath that, a thermal layer that holds a stable temperature against the body by being full of a liquid that circulates. Underneath that, a close weave that breathes, that wicks, that lets the sweat — and they do sweat, they are still mammals, the system has not yet taken that from them — evaporate against the cool of the layer above. The face is the reflective thing you noticed: a curved mask, polished on the outside, vented in careful patterns, with a horizontal slit where the eyes are, dark behind the glare. When a Tender turns toward the furnace-glow, the mask throws a dull red flash back at you, and the person behind it is gone, and you are looking at an animal in armor.

They move in pairs. This is not a rule they enforce on themselves so much as a rule the work enforces on them. The columns are too tall to see over. The pipes block sightlines. A single Tender, alone on a gantry, can be ten meters from another Tender and have no way of knowing the other is there, and the machinery is too dangerous for that, and the columns are too dangerous for that. A column that has been topped and is regrowing is not a stable object; it is a slow explosion of new tissue, and a person in the way of it will be slowly enclosed, slowly absorbed, slowly digested by the same peristaltic fans that drink the leaking pipe-fluid. So they go in pairs, and the pairs are not always the same. They are paired by the day, by the task, by who is awake, and a Tender may work a shift with three different partners in a week and never learn any of their names. Names are not what the work is for.

What they do, in the most literal sense, is keep the pipes flowing.

This is the job. This has been the job for longer than anyone has asked whether it could be a different job. The columns grow, and the columns are harvested, and the harvest has to go somewhere, and the *somewhere* is below — cracked in furnaces, distilled, separated into the fractions that keep the system running. The pipes are what carry the fractions. The pipes are old, in the way everything is old, and they are constantly being infiltrated by the plants, which are constantly trying to incorporate them, because the pipes are warm and the pipes carry good things and the plants are hungry the way a forest is hungry, opportunistically, without malice. A pipe that has been still for a day is no longer a pipe. It is a column in training, a future member of the forest, slowly being absorbed. So the pipes must keep moving. The fluid in them must keep moving. The fluid *is* the motion, in a way: as long as the heat differentials hold, as long as the convection is running, the pipes are defined by their contents and not by their walls, and the Tenders' task is to make sure the convection never stalls. They clear blockages. They reroute around columns that have grown into a junction. They tap new sources, tapping the columns the way a maple is tapped, setting a metal spile into the flesh and waiting for the dark sap to rise. They repair the leaking joints, which cannot actually be repaired, only replaced, and the replacement is always partial, and the new joint begins leaking almost immediately, and the leaking is caught by the plant fans, which is the point. The leak *is* the point. The plant fans expect to be fed. If the pipes stopped leaking, the fans would start climbing the pipes to find the source, and that is much harder to deal with.

A Tenders' shift, then, is a kind of long, looping, patient war against entropy, fought with hand tools and muscle memory and a deep, inherited knowledge of which columns are about to do what. They know the forest. They know it the way a forester knows a wood, or the way a shepherd knows a flock, but the forest is also a flock, and the flock is also a forest, and the metaphor breaks and reassembles every few minutes. They know that the third column from the southern gantry puts out a new fan every nine days, and that the fan is the sweetest one, and that if you don't cut it on the tenth day it will begin to climb. They know that a certain pipe junction makes a sound — a high, thin singing — about an hour before it bursts, and that the sound can be heard from the next gantry over, and that if you hear it you have about forty minutes to get there with a patch. They know that a Tapping set into a column that has already been tapped three times is going to take, and that one set into a column that has never been tapped is going to be rejected — the flesh will close over the spile in a day, and the column will be angry in the slow way columns are angry, and the anger will manifest as a new growth of bitter, thorned filaments around the wound for a season. They know that the small bright liquid in the pipes is not water but does some of the work of water, and they know that drinking it would kill them in a way that is slow enough to be interesting and fast enough to be a warning to others, and they have a saying about it that is untranslatable and not very funny.

They do not speak much. This is partly the work — the sounds of the forest swallow speech, and you cannot raise your voice against the hiss of a pressure relief and be heard — and partly a thing that has settled into them the way the pipes have settled into the columns. There is a sign language, mostly. There is a set of hand signals for the common events: *clear*, *blocked*, *tap here*, *do not tap here*, *come*, *wait*, *down*, *up*, *home*. The home signal is interesting. It is a closed fist held against the chest, palm in, and it means *return*, and it is used so often and in so many contexts that it has come to mean *safe* and *done* and *over* and *enough* and sometimes just *I see you*. A Tender who has been out of contact with their partner for an hour will make the home signal at the next junction, even if no one is there to see it, because the making of it is a kind of return. The older Tenders do it more than the younger ones. The older Tenders are the ones who have begun to understand that they may not, themselves, return, in the larger sense, and the signal is for the forest.

They live underground. This is the part that is hard to picture from above, and the part that, if you were to descend with them at the end of a shift, would rearrange your sense of the place. The gantries have ladders — woven of the same dark fiber as the pipes, set with metal rungs that are themselves slowly being absorbed — and the ladders go down through the root crust and into a space that is not so much excavated as *grown around*, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. The root mat is hundreds of meters thick, and the descent is slow, and the heat does not relent, but the air changes. The brine mist thins. The dust settles. The columns are above you now, and you are in the dark of the root itself, and the dark is not the furnace-glow dark, it is a blacker dark, a more patient dark, the dark of being held. There are corridors down here that are lined with a kind of pale, soft, luminous tissue that the Tenders have cultivated — coaxed, really, with nutrients and warmth and the attention of generations — to provide light. The tissue gives off a faint, cool, greenish glow, the only cool color in the whole vertical world, and the Tenders' faces, when they take off their masks at the end of a shift, are lit from below by it, and they look, for a moment, like people in a forest on Earth. They don't, for longer than a moment. The resemblance is a thing the place has learned to imitate.

Down here, the columns become pipes, and the pipes become columns, and you stop being able to tell. The root mat presses in from all sides. The floor is warm and slightly yielding. There are chambers where the fluid from above has been separated into its fractions, and the fractions are stored in tanks that were once hollowed-out root nodes, and the tanks are tended by Tenders who do nothing but tend the tanks, and these Tenders are the specialists, the ones with the most knowledge, the most inherited, the most careful. They are also the most silent. They have not seen the sky — there is no sky, but the absence of the columns — in months. Their suits are softer. Their faces are paler. They do not make the home signal. They have forgotten it, or they have decided it is for other people.

The young ones, the new Tenders, come up from below once. This is a kind of initiation, or a kind of inoculation, or a kind of warning. They are brought up through the root mat by an older Tender, and they are shown the forest, and they are told something that is not quite a story and not quite a lesson. The older Tender stands with them on a gantry and points at a column, and the column is being tapped, and the dark sap is running into a small bright vessel, and the vessel is being carried away. The older Tender says: *This is what we are for. We are the vessel. We carry the system through itself.* And the young Tender, who has been below their whole short life, looks at the column, and the column is very large, and the column is faintly wounded and faintly glowing, and the young Tender understands, in the way that a person understands a thing they will spend the rest of their life not quite believing, that the column does not care about them. The column is doing what the column does. The Tenders are doing what the Tenders do. The doing has been going on for a long time. The young Tender is not the first and will not be the last, and the column was here before any Tenders were here and will be here after the last Tenders are gone, and this is not a sad thing. It is just the shape of the place.

They are not unhappy. This is the part that is hardest to convey, because the place is so much like a hell in the pictures of it that the inhabitants of a hell should, by rights, suffer. They do not. They have a fatigue that is deeper than tiredness, a chronic low-grade depletion that they manage the way a diabetic manages sugar, by constant small adjustments, but they do not have a despair. They do not have a hope, either. Hope requires an elsewhere, and they do not have an elsewhere, and they do not want one. The forest is all the world there is, and the forest is enough, in the way that a body is enough for a person who has never had a body leave them. They are tired. They are careful. They are competent in a way that is beautiful to watch, the way any long-evolved competence is beautiful — a Tenders' hands on a tap, the practiced tilt of a Tenders' body around a regrowing fan, the slow and certain way a Tenders' boots find the next rung of a ladder they have climbed ten thousand times. They are small and dark and reflective and they move in pairs and they do not speak and they make the home signal against their chests when they are alone, and they keep the pipes flowing, and the pipes keep the forest fed, and the forest keeps the Tenders fed, and the Tenders keep the forest harvested, and the cycle goes on, and the cycle is the place, and the place is the cycle, and a Tenders' life, from first descent to last ascent, is a single careful gesture inside the gesture of the system, and the gesture means *continue*, and the meaning is enough.
