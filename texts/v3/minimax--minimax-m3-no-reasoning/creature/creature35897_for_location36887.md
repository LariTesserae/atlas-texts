# Those Who Dwell in the Crockery Valley

The menders come down from the high country with the first drying of the mist, and they come down looking like their grandfathers, and their grandfathers came down looking like theirs. They are a small people, narrow in the shoulder and careful in the foot, the way people become careful when the ground they walk on has opinions about how it is walked on. Their boots are wrapped three times over with broad strips of undyed linen, and the linen is changed out at the rim of the basin where the mender's path ends and the sherd-floors begin; you can see the old wrappings hung on the posts of the gatehouse like the cast skins of reptiles, grey and stiff and faintly patterned with whatever they walked through on the way up. Underneath the wrappings they wear a single layer, also linen, also undyed, because dye in the basin does not behave. The body of a mender, after a season in the valley, takes on the color of the place: the pale yellows stain the knuckles, the celadons the inner arms, the oxbloods the face and neck if a mender has worked without his cloth over the lower face. They come up out of the basin looking like old porcelain that has been used, and they keep that color for the rest of their lives, which is part of why the high-country people can pick a mender out at fifty paces.

They carry their tools in long bags across the back, and the tools are strange and ordinary at the same time. There are hammers with heads of antler and heads of lead and heads of something older that the menders do not name, only point to when you ask, and the heads are used for different kinds of breaks, and a mender knows from the sound of the tap whether the break is the kind that wants antler or the kind that wants lead. There are tongs of bone and tongs of bronze, and the bronze ones are never used in the basin itself, only at the workshops on the high ground; the bone ones are for picking through the wet middens where metal would stir up whatever it is that lives in the green-black water and dislikes being stirred. There are pots of paste, kept sealed in oiled skin, and the paste is made from the dust of the basin itself, ground and re-mixed with something the menders will not say the name of, and the paste is what they use to make two halves of a broken thing believe, again, that they were ever one thing. There are brushes, and there are awls, and there are small hand-bellows of lacquered wood, and there is, in the bag of every mender of any standing, a single small mirror, polished but not silvered, made of a black stone that comes from somewhere far to the west, and the mirror is used to see the inside of a piece without reaching into it, because some insides are not meant to be reached into. A mender who has come down for his first season carries all of these in a bag made by his mother. A mender on his tenth season carries only the hammer he has chosen and the mirror and the paste, and he has replaced everything else with his hands.

They do not speak much in the basin. This is partly because of what the bowl does to sound, and partly because speaking is a kind of breaking, and the menders are people whose whole life is the putting-together of breaks. When they do speak, they speak low, and they speak in a register that is almost but not quite a hum, and the words they use are mostly the names of pieces: *foot, lip, handle, well, shoulder, throat, belly, base, flange, spout*. They call out across the flats sometimes, two menders working at a distance from each other, and they call out in those words, and the words carry across the shallow water and lose nothing, and the answer comes back just as whole. They have a word for the particular sound a piece makes when it is the right piece, a word that has no translation and that sounds, when they say it, like a small bell struck once and damped at once, and they will sometimes stand very still in the middle of a fan of fresh breakage with a piece in the hand and say the word to themselves, very quietly, and the basin will not repeat it back, which is how you know the word is true.

The menders live in camps that move with the season. In the high summer, when the mist thins enough to let a kind of light through that is not quite light but is at least brighter than the rest, the camps are set on the slopes above the flood line, on the natural shelves of sherd that have not yet begun to slide. In the deeper dark, when the cloud settles all the way to the floor and the standing water is a single grey sheet that moves without wind, the camps are higher still, up near the rim, and the menders sleep on pallets of woven reed with a piece of something heavy across the chest, because in the deepest dark the basin does something to the chest of a sleeper, something that has to do with the breathing and the ringing and the way the two together try to set up a sympathy, and the weight on the chest is to break the sympathy before it can begin. A mender who sleeps without the weight wakes up humming, and a mender who wakes up humming does not go back to the basin the next day, and a mender who does not go back does not go back again. The menders do not speak of this, either. They are not a people who speak much of the things that matter most.

They age in a particular way. They come down young, in their late teens or early twenties, brown and clear-eyed from the high pastures, and they go up grey. Not the grey of hair, though that too; the grey of the eye, the grey of the skin, the grey that the basin puts on a person the way it puts the yellow on the knuckles and the green on the arms. A mender at fifty looks like a piece of pale celadon that has been handled too much: the surface of him is still bright in places, but the brightness is worn through to something cooler underneath. They live long enough, mostly, though not as long as the high-country people, and they die in their beds in the workshops, usually in winter, usually with a piece in the hand, and the piece is set on the chest and buried with them, which is the only piece that ever leaves the basin for good. The menders do not have cemeteries. They have shelves.

There are not many of them. A working season in the basin takes perhaps forty people, and of those forty perhaps ten are menders of any true skill, and of those ten perhaps two or three have the ear for it, the thing that lets a mender hear which piece wants which other piece, and of those two or three perhaps one in a generation has the eye that can see the inside of a piece in the black mirror without cracking the piece open to look. The menders know who these ones are. They do not announce them. They let them come down and go up and come down again, and they wait, and when the waiting is done, the one with the eye is given the bag with the antler hammer and the mirror and the paste, and from that season on he is the one who decides which shelves receive which pieces, and the others defer to him without speaking of the deferring, because speaking of it would be a kind of breaking.

The shard-fish are the most numerous things in the basin after the sherds themselves. They are not fish. The menders know this and the basin knows this and the fish themselves, if they know anything, know it too. They are shaped like fish, the long oval body and the forked fin and the small hard eye, and they are the size of a mender's hand from wrist to fingertip, and they move in the shallow water in loose shoals that turn together in flashes of color that match whatever stratum of breakage they are swimming over. A shoal over a band of blue-and-white is a shoal of blue-and-white. A shoal that has wandered out over the oxbloods is a shoal of dark red. The menders do not know why. They have theories, which they will share if pressed, and the theories involve the glaze dissolving into the water and the fish taking on the color the way a cloth takes on a dye, but the theories do not explain why the fish that have lived for years over a mixed stratum are not a mixed color but a moving patchwork, each fish a single color, and the shoal as a whole a kind of swimming mosaic that rearranges itself as the fish turn. The fish feed on whatever the water dissolves from the sherd-faces; you can see them nosing at the edges of midden-heaps where the water has crept up and the glaze is going soft. They eat, in other words, the memory of cups. They do not seem to grow. The menders do not know how long they live. They do not know if they breed, or if they simply appear, the way sherds appear when a slump brings up a fresh layer. The menders catch them, in the shallows, with small hand-nets of bone and undyed linen, and they eat them in the camps on the slopes, fried in their own oil in a pan of beaten copper, and they taste, depending on what color the fish was, of one thing or another: the blue-and-white taste of river-water and clay, the oxblood of iron and something faintly sweet, the celadon of cold green tea, the yellow of a kind of grain they do not grow in the high country and cannot name. The menders consider the shard-fish good eating. They consider it a kind of mercy, to eat the fish and let the fish go back to the water as memory and muscle. They do not consider it further than that.

The rim-birds are the only things in the basin with wings. They nest in the cracks of the outer wall, in the high places where the strata have been exposed and have begun to weather into proper stone, and they come in over the rim at dawn, when the cloud is at its thinnest and the basin is at its lightest, and they drop down into the wet middens and they feed on whatever is small enough to be fed upon. They are dark birds, the color of the wet slate of the sky, and they are silent in flight and loud at the nest, and the sound they make at the nest is not a song but a kind of rattle, as if the bones in their throat were small sherds knocking together. The menders like the rim-birds for the rattle, which they say is the only honest sound in the basin, because it is the sound of breakage, and the menders are people who understand breakage. The rim-birds are afraid of the menders, and they will not come down to a midden where a mender is working, but they will come down to a midden where a mender has just left, and they will pick through the disturbed sherds for whatever the mender turned over and did not take, and they will eat it, and they will fly back up to the cracks and rattle for an hour, and the rattle carries down into the bowl and mixes with the ringing of the ground. The menders consider this a fair exchange. The rim-birds get the leavings. The menders get the rattle. The basin gets to keep its silence, which is the only thing in it that is truly whole.

The things in the deep water are not named. The menders do not speak of them at all, and the basin does not give them up to the eye. What the menders know of them is the sound they make, which is a slow displacement and a soft knock, and the knowledge that the knock is the knock of a body of size pushing against a submerged pile of sherds and not the knock of a current or a slump. The menders know that the deep pools are the oldest water in the basin, the water that has wept down through the layers for longer than the menders have been coming down, and that the water is green-black because of what has dissolved into it from the lowest strata, the strata that have begun to forget their shapes, the strata that hum when you press your ear to them. The menders know that the things in the deep water are not shard-fish, because the shard-fish stay in the shallows, and they are not rim-birds, because the rim-birds do not go into the water at all. They know that the things move slowly and live long, because the knocks come from the same directions season after season, and the directions do not change, which would not be the case if what was making the knocks was small or short-lived. They know that the things do not like to be stirred, because the menders who have gone into the deep pools to fish—only twice in the memory of the menders, and the second time only because the first mender did not come back—have come back without the fish they went in for and without the net they went in with, and with a look on their face that the other menders have learned not to ask about. The menders do not fish in the deep pools. The basin does not need to be told this twice.

There is one more thing in the basin, and it is the oldest and the quietest and the most uncertain. It is the yellow cup on the high shelf near the eastern rim. The cup is whole. It is the only whole piece in the basin that the menders did not make whole, and it has been on the shelf longer than anyone alive remembers, and no one has drunk from it, and no one will. The menders do not know what it is for. They have theories, which they will share, and the theories are many and they do not agree. One theory is that the cup is the first piece that was ever broken, the original break, and that the basin is the cup spread out across the floor and the shelves are the places where pieces of the cup are set down to be remembered, and the menders are the people who go out among the pieces and find the ones that fit the cup, and the work of mending is the work of putting the cup back together, piece by piece, season by season, and the basin will be here until the cup is whole again, and then the basin will not be here. Another theory is that the cup is what the basin is for, that the basin was built to hold the cup, and the cup was set on the shelf and the basin was filled with sherds to keep the cup safe, and the menders are the people who walk among the sherds and make sure the sherds do not climb the shelves. The menders consider these theories roughly equal. They do not act on either of them. They do not take the cup down. They do not drink from it. They do not speak of it except in the low hum they use for the names of pieces, and even then they do not say its name, because they do not know its name, and a thing without a name is a thing that cannot be broken, and a thing that cannot be broken is a thing that does not need to be mended, and a mender who does not need to mend does not know what to do with himself in a basin of broken things.

The yellow cup sits on the shelf. The menders come and go. The shard-fish turn in their mosaic shoals. The rim-birds rattle in the cracks of the outer wall. The things in the deep water knock softly against the sherds of the bottom. The mist weeps onto the sherd-floors and runs into the green-black pools. The basin rings, faintly, the way it has always rung, the way it will ring after the menders have gone up into the hills and not come down again, and after the people in the hills have stopped sending menders down, and after the high country itself has gone quiet and the paths to the rim have been lost in the tall grass. The basin will ring for a long time. The basin is mostly silence, under the ringing. The basin is mostly whole, under the breaking. The yellow cup sits on the shelf and gives back its faint sourceless glow, and the glow is the color of itself, and the color of itself is the color of something that has never been used and has been waiting, all this time, without knowing what it is waiting for.
