# Who Lives There

The first thing to understand is that the population of the Reservoir is not the population one sees. One sees three thousand, perhaps, going about the day's business on the terraced slopes — and this is true, but it is the smaller truth. The larger truth is that the city is inhabited by a great and patient attention, and the people are merely the places where that attention has come to a focus.

They are a slender people, the visible ones, with the long bones of people who have lived for many centuries in low light and have arranged their skeletons to suit. Their skin carries the same blue-grey undertone as the buildings, not as pigment exactly but as a kind of *agreement* — as if they had been standing too long in the doorways of structures that exhale. They dress in layers of a fabric I have not been able to name, something between felt and slate, that moves when the humidity shifts and lies perfectly still when the air is dry. They wear no ornament except, in some cases, a small device hung at the throat — a tuning element, I think, that vibrates in sympathy with the city's harmonic and lets its wearer know when the chord is about to change. The engineers wear them prominently. The others wear them tucked away.

They are not unfriendly, but their courtesy has the particular quality of people who have very little to protect and therefore no reason to refuse you anything. They will answer questions. They will offer food. They will walk you to the edge of a graded slope of attention and show you how to step onto it. What they will not do, or cannot do, is *explain*. There is a sense in which explanation would be a form of disrespect to whatever it is they live inside of, and they are sparing of disrespect.

The antecedents — the reflected population — are quieter and, I think, older in a way that has nothing to do with years. They go about their business in the mirrored city with the unhurried efficiency of people performing a task they have performed so often that the performance has become its own reward. Some of them I have watched for an entire afternoon, doing the same small things — drawing water, mending a chime, adjusting a leaf of the negotiated vegetation — and the next afternoon they are at it again, in the same postures, at the same seams. The locals do not look at them directly. The locals do not look away from them either. They occupy a third mode of regard, something like peripheral fellowship, and when I have asked about them the answer has always been the same: *they are not ghosts, they are antecedents, and the distinction matters in the building of municipal policy.* I have chosen, as I say, not to press this.

The engineers are a different matter. They are fewer — perhaps two dozen in total, perhaps fewer — and they are the only people here who possess, in any obvious sense, a *project*. They dress in a uniform of sorts: heavier layers, darker, the fabric stiffened with some mineral finish that makes it clink softly when they walk. Their instruments are numerous and various — long rods, shallow bowls, coils of wire that sing when the air is right — and they carry them with the casual competence of surgeons. They go to the warm seams in the basalt and they listen. They make small notations in a script I cannot read, though I notice it is the same script that grows, very faintly, on the inside of certain of the inverted cisterns beneath the plazas, as if the cisterns are keeping their own records and the engineers are merely the messengers.

What the engineers are listening for, I have not been told. What I have observed is that when they return from a seam, they consult among themselves in a manner that resembles, more than anything, the way musicians tune to one another before a piece. There is a settling. There is a nod. And then the city's harmonic shifts by some amount too small for me to measure, and the next morning the chimes ring a quarter-tone higher, and the birds adjust their schedules, and the antecedents in the reflected city pause, briefly, in their mending and their drawing of water, as though they had felt the change in their own mirror.

The fish — the silver, eyeless, not-entirely-fish — are not discussed. They surface and they go down again. They feed on the mist, which suggests a mouth, which suggests a need, but the locals do not fish for them and do not eat them and will change subject when you do. I think the fish are a different order of resident, or a different resident in a different order; I think they belong to the river and the river belongs to them in a way that has not yet been spoken aloud.

The birds are spoken of with affection. They are the only creatures here that the locals will praise without reservation. They are small and dark and quick, and they nest exclusively in the chimes, which are tuned, it turns out, *for* them — the chimes are not for the householders' pleasure but for the birds' tenancy, and the householders have accepted this as they have accepted the plants: as a form of co-residence that requires no formal arrangement. The birds sing, in their way, and the chimes ring, in theirs, and the two together make the town's only honest clock. The engineers consult the chimes the way sailors used to consult the stars.

And the plants — the broad-leafed, almost-banana, almost-fern, almost-succulent tenants who pay their rent in humidity — are, I think, the eldest inhabitants of all. They were here before the city, in some form. The buildings are no more than the more articulate parts of a continuous substance that began as vegetation and ended as stone, or began as stone and ended as vegetation, or has never quite decided. The plants climb the walls because they are the walls, in some older sense. The householders accept them because they are householders *by their leave*.

I have not decided what the people of the Reservoir are, in the end. They are courteous. They are unhelpful on the central question. They live inside a process they do not discuss. They have made their peace with antecedents, with engineer-musicians, with fish that may not be fish, with birds that own the chimes, with plants that own the walls, with a basalt that is still, beneath them all, *thinking itself into a new articulation*. They are the most patient people I have ever lived among, and the most opaque. They have offered me food that tastes like a memory of food, and they have suggested I walk the slopes and listen to the chimes and not attend too carefully to the warm seam at the top of the valley, which is not, they assure me, doing what it appears to be doing.

I am learning which parts to believe in. I have not yet learned them all.
