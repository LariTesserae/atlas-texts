The lizards are the first ones you would notice, if you noticed anything, and most people don't. They are so flat, so pale, so committed to being stone that they pass beneath notice the way a small coin on a sidewalk passes beneath notice. They lie along the warmer seams of the ledges — the places where the sun has been working all morning — and they are themselves, by midmorning, almost too warm to touch. A finger laid against one's back would feel the rock's heat and the lizard's heat as a single thing, indistinguishable. Their camouflage is not a trick. It is a vocation.

They move, when they move, in quick economical darts. A fly has landed two body-lengths away; the lizard has decided, and is there. They eat the flies, and small moths that come in on the evening air, and the termites that wander too far from the mounds. They are themselves eaten, occasionally, by the vultures — though the vultures are not, on the whole, hunters. The lizards do not seem to know this. They live as if no one is watching them. Most of the time, no one is.

Their eyes are their only color. When a lizard lifts its head and looks at you, you see two drops of amber, almost golden, set in the pale stone of its face. It looks at you for a long moment, considering. Then it lowers its head again and resumes being rock. It has decided you are not a fly.

The termites are the architects. They have built across the shelves a series of structures that would be impressive if anyone were here to be impressed — mounds of cemented sand as tall as a man, oriented east-northeast to catch the morning sun. Each mound is a small fortress of regulated temperature, the colony within maintaining a climate suitable for the cultivation of the fungus on which they feed. From a distance the mounds look like loaves of pale bread set out to bake. Up close they have a faint sweet rot to them, the smell of the fungus gardens.

The termites themselves you almost never see. They work inside, in the dark, in the warm. The mound's surface is too hot, too dry, too exposed. But you hear them — a low hum that comes through the cement when you put your ear to it, especially at dawn when the colony is warming. They hum in chorus. The hum is not quite a sound; it is more like a vibration you feel in the jaw. It is the sound of a great many small things all doing their job.

The mounds are warm to the touch at dawn because the sun has been heating the cement all morning. By dusk they are cool, having radiated their heat back into the thinning air. They are, in their way, a kind of breathing — in with the morning warmth, out with the evening cool. The colony inside, in its pitch dark, lives in a slow steady temperature of its own making. They have made a small, stable world, and they sit inside it and hum.

The antelope appear at dawn. They are no bigger than dogs, with the leggy lankiness of creatures built for running across broken ground, and their coats are the same dust-gray as the rock. You do not see them so much as catch them — a flicker at the corner of the eye, a sense that something was standing there and is not standing there now. They stand in loose herds on the upper ledges, silhouetted against the bright sky, and at that distance they could be mistaken for cairns.

They are not cairns. They move.

Their hooves make a sound you would not expect from so small an animal: a sharp castanet percussion against the dry stone, audible at a hundred meters. When a herd lifts and moves across a shelf, the sound precedes them like music from an unseen band. They are drinking from the channels when the channels run, which is for an hour or two in the morning. The rest of the day they lie up in the shade of the north faces, chewing, ruminating, conserving. They are perfectly adapted to the throbs. They can go a week between real drinks. They have nowhere else to go and no reason to go there.

You might think, watching them, that they are the largest animals on the place. You would be almost right. The vultures are larger, in their way, but the vultures do not really inhabit. They pass through, or they turn above. The antelope are the largest things that live here.

The vultures of the upper air are always present. Three, four, a dozen at a time, broad-winged, bone-white, riding the thermals in slow circles above the highest ledges. They are not, as you would guess, hunting. They are not searching. They are waiting, perhaps, for the next throb to bring something dead, or for the small grey antelope to falter, or for the termites to fail in their maintenance of the mounds. None of these things is likely. The vultures wait anyway.

Or perhaps they are not waiting for anything. Perhaps they are simply what the wind does when it learns to glide. There is a sense in which they are not creatures but a feature of the atmosphere, like the wind itself in feathered form. They turn and turn and turn, and the turning is its own purpose.

They make, when they make any sound at all, a thin dry hiss, like a sail in a faint breeze, or like air escaping from a small cracked vessel. It is not a call. It is the sound of their feathers against the air, or of their breath through some internal chamber. It is the sound of a thing in motion.

They roost, when they roost, on the very edges of the upper shelves, where the rock drops away. From there they can simply step into the air and be gone. They are, in the way of the place, perpetual transients. The lizards stay. The antelope stay. The termites stay. The vultures only seem to stay.

There are others. Small things, furtive things — a kind of gerbil that lives in the cairn-strewn places and eats the seeds of the scrub; a fox-sized predator, rarer still, its coat the color of shadow, that hunts the gerbils and the young antelope; a great variety of spiders that build webs in the channels during the wet hours and dismantle them when the channels dry. There are birds, but only a few species, and they are mostly silent. There are, somewhere, snakes, but you see them less often than you see their sloughed skins dried against the rock. The place supports more life than it looks like it supports. It supports life the way a thin soil supports a forest: not generously, but completely.

Then there is the smoke.

A long low structure on the third ledge, with thick walls of dressed stone, no windows on the west face, narrow slits on the others. Its roof is loaded with the same pale rock as its surroundings, so that from above it looks like nothing. From below it looks like a kind of bunker, or a low and patient animal. Someone is in there, or was. On certain days a thin smoke rises from a vent in the roof and disperses into the bright air; on other days there is no smoke and no sign.

You cannot tell, from the outside, how many are within. The building is long enough for many, or for one with room to spare. Whoever lives there is sparse. Whoever lives there has decided that the place is worth a structure but not worth a village. They have built a wall thick enough to hold off the afternoon sun and the western storms. They have cut slits to admit the morning light, sparingly. They have not, on the whole, made a claim. They have made a foothold.

You might, walking past, see a figure on the roof, balancing another pale stone on the heap of pale stones. Or you might not. You might see, at the cairn nearest the building, a small offering: a bone, a sprig of dried herb, a feather. You might assume the building dweller tends the cairns. You might be right. You might be wrong. The cairns are everywhere, balanced with a care that suggests devotion, and they never fall. Someone is, somewhere, maintaining them.

Or something.

The cairns are the most numerous structure on the place. There are thousands of them, perhaps — three stones balanced on a fourth is the commonest form, but variations abound. They are set in places that look, to the eye, arbitrary, but they are not arbitrary. They are set where someone stood, at some point, and felt the place was worth marking. They are set where the light falls a certain way, or where the wind has carved a small singing fluted stone, or where a throb-watered patch of moss blooms sudden green. They are a kind of writing. They are a kind of gratitude.

They are maintained. They never fall. Wind, antelope, thermal expansion — none of these have, in your observation, knocked one down. They are reset by hands that may be there and may not. They are the most communal feature of the place you can see, even though you see no community. They are a community's footprint without the community, the way a snail's trail is a snail's footprint without the snail.

And then there are the things that do not belong.

The spire stands on the uppermost shelf, alone, on a flat expanse of rock that has been swept bare of cairns and of scrub. It is slender, twenty meters tall, perfectly vertical, perfectly smooth, and the color of a piece of the night that has forgotten to leave. It reflects the bright sky as if it were a sheet of still water standing on its end. When you approach it, you feel something in your inner ear, a small pull, as if the gravity around it were very slightly different. Dust motes near its base curve in their descent, drift sideways for a moment, then resume. The spire hums at a frequency you cannot locate.

You cannot hear the hum. You know it is there because you feel it, faintly, in your sternum, in the roots of your teeth. It has been there longer than the cairns, perhaps longer than the building. Whatever civilization set it here is gone, or never lived here in any conventional sense. The spire is not maintained. It does not need maintenance. It stands.

It is, in some sense, the oldest inhabitant of the place.

The ring is smaller and stranger. Five meters across, set into the stone, dark metal, no visible mechanism. Some days the air inside it shimmers, as if heat were rising from it, even when the surrounding stone is cool. If you stand inside it, you feel nothing. If you jump inside it, you land a fraction of a second later than you should. The ring has bent time, slightly, locally, into a shape that no longer perfectly agrees with the time outside.

It is not clear whether the ring is alive. It is not clear whether "alive" is the right question. It is, like the spire, settled into the place as if the rock had accepted it. The lichen has begun to grow on its outer rim. A small antelope has, you would swear, slept against it on a cold morning. The vultures turn above it without comment. It is part of the place now. It is, you might say, the place's strangest inhabitant.

Or perhaps these things are not inhabitants at all. Perhaps they are features, like the channels and the yardangs, like the fluted stones that sing in the afternoon thermals. Perhaps the rock grows them as it grows the moss. Perhaps they are a kind of mineral life — slow, patient, present.

The rock itself, in any case, is an inhabitant.

It ticks at night as it cools, a slow deep ticking that you can hear if you are very still, and you can hear it always if you live here long enough. It absorbs the day's sun and breathes it out into the small hours. It has been here longer than the concept of erosion. It has been here, by your estimate, since before the spire. It will be here after everything else. It is the ground of the place, the medium in which everything else is set.

It is not, of course, sentient. But it is present. And presence, here, is the only currency.

The dew is also an inhabitant, of a kind. It gathers each morning on every surface, darkening the stones, making wet footprints behind a man walking at dawn. It is the daily visitor, the most reliable presence. It is the water the place has, when it has no water.

The light is an inhabitant. It pours across the stone from the slightly-too-large sun and behaves oddly — shadows falling a half-breath behind the objects casting them, as if the photon stream keeps having to catch up. At midday the place looks carved from white paper. The light is the painter. It is what makes the lichens reveal their orange and the mosses their emerald. Without it, the place would be uniformly pale.

The wind is an inhabitant. It speaks softly, mostly in sibilants and long exhalations. It carries the mixed scents of the scrub — the resinous and the peppery and the mineral — and weaves them into the shifting, unplaceable perfume that hangs over a stand of scrub like an invisible architecture. It moves through the standing stones, in the place where the wind is funneled through a natural gap, and makes a low resonance, almost subsonic, that you feel in your sternum. It is the voice of the place.

You leave Ulúrath over the western shelf, into the thin bright air, and the place settles back into its long patience. The lizards resume their stillness. The little antelope lift their gray heads and consider, then lower them again. The vultures turn in their slow circles. The building smoke, on the day you leave, is rising. The cairns hold their balance. The spire stands. The ring shimmers, or doesn't.

The rock ticks as it cools.

The dew gathers.

The morning is already half over.
