What lives here is a question the valley answers reluctantly, in layers, the way it answers most questions. You stand in it for a long time before you understand that the question is being answered at all, and then you understand that it has been being answered, just below the threshold of the kind of attention you brought with you.

The first inhabitants are not inhabitants. They are films.

You notice them when you have been staring at a rock for some minutes and begin to suspect that the color of it is not quite the color of stone. They are the dark shoulders of boulders. They are the slight sheen inside a pan that you take for wetness until the water thins and the sheen does not. They are the thin, ochre, leathery skin that follows the contours of the rocks near the water lines — darker where the water is, paler higher up. It does not move. It is, however, dense. It has a direction to it: a tendency to follow the path water has already taken, to thicken in the places water lingers, to thin where the rock dries. If you touch it — and you will, because you stay — it is cool and faintly yielding. It does not come off on your fingers. It does not feel like stone. It feels like the underside of something.

Whether it is alive depends on what you mean by alive.

The chemistry is there for it. There is reduced iron in the basalt, and oxygenated water moving across every surface in the valley, all the time, and the energy gradient between them is the kind of gradient that very simple organisms — bacterial, archaeal, things without a name yet — know how to exploit. The valley runs on this. The valley is, in a way, a vast slow rust, and the rust is alive, or close enough that the distinction has stopped being interesting. The ochre skin is the visible edge of a process that extends down into the rock to a depth you cannot see, and outward across every wet surface in the valley to a distance you cannot measure. It is the foundation. Everything else in the Dripping Quarter that is alive is alive because the films are alive, or because the films have made the place fit for being alive in, or because the films are not in fact the foundation at all and you have only found the layer you can see.

Above and beside the films, the larger fauna. If *fauna* is the word. The valley makes you cautious with words.

The things that move in the water you see as a flex, a shadow, a sudden absence of stillness. They are not fish. They have no obvious fins, no obvious eyes, no obvious head. A shape — long, pale, lateral — passes through the dark of a pan and is gone before you can fix it. You crouch. You wait. An hour passes. The shape does not return, or it returns as a different shape, and you are not sure it is the same kind of thing. They are brief about their existence. They live in the water the way the air lives in the valley: as a presence you have to take on faith.

The things that cling pale to the undersides of overhangs you find by accident. You lean in toward a crevice, and then you stop, because there is something there, and it is small, and it is looking at you with an apparatus you do not recognize. It might be an eye. It might be a cluster of them, sessile, lidless. It is the size of a thumbnail, or a coin, or the pad of your thumb. It is the color of wet limestone. It does not move while you are looking at it. When you look away — and you will, because you cannot help it — it is gone.

You develop the habit, in the Dripping Quarter, of not looking away.

There are calls. You hear them less often than you would expect, given how loud the valley already is. They are single notes, long, slightly wavering — the wavering of a thing that is not producing sound quite the way a throat produces sound but is producing it nonetheless. They come from the walls, possibly. They come from the water, possibly. They come from inside the rock, possibly, in which case they are not calls at all but vibrations the wet air has chosen to translate into something audible. The valley is loud with its own processes. Whatever is calling is competing with a waterfall.

You cannot tell, most of the time, whether you have heard an animal or a stone. You cannot tell whether the note you heard was made by something alive or by a flake of basalt settling against its neighbor after a thermal shift too small to feel. The valley does not make this easy. The valley is uninterested in making this easy.

And then there is the question of the shapes.

This is where the valley becomes difficult, because the valley is a place that seems to want to be asked. The cairns. The partial walls. The rectangular holes that go down to dark water. They suggest intention. The valley is full of things that suggest intention. It is also full of things that suggest chance. It is the same set of things.

But there are moments.

You are standing at the edge of one of the rectangular holes, looking down into water you cannot see the bottom of, and you notice — because you have been here before, because you have been coming here for decades, because the patient inspection of slow places is its own kind of expertise — that the edges have moved. Not much. Not in a way that would mean anything to anyone who had not been looking. But the lip that was sharp last visit is rounded now, and the corner that was square is no longer quite square, and the dark water inside sits at a slightly different level, a finger-width higher or lower, and there is a smell that was not there before — mineral, faintly organic, the smell of soil after rain except this is not soil and it has not rained.

Something has been working here.

You do not see it. You will not see it. Whatever is making the holes is patient in a way that is hard to frame in human terms. It does not move at the speed of making. It might not move at all. It might be more like the ochre skin than like an animal — a slow, mineral process that has, somewhere in its repertoire, the ability to dissolve basalt along chosen lines, and that has chosen lines, and that maintains them, and that occasionally surfaces — in what? A tide? A chemical pulse? A reproductive event? — to extend them. The walls that do not connect to anything may be the abandoned edges of older projects. The pillars standing alone in the pans may be what is left when the softer stone around a chosen core has been subtracted. The fitted slabs that are not pavements may be the floors of dissolved chambers, exposed by a partial collapse that took a century.

There is something in the rectangular holes. You have never seen it. You have seen the water inside one of them bulge, once, very slightly, as if something had risen from below and pressed against the underside of the surface and then withdrawn. You have heard, once, a note from one of the holes that was not like the other notes — longer, lower, more felt than heard, as if the stone itself had briefly become an instrument. You have, on a still afternoon when the fog had sunk to ankle-height and the valley was quieter than it usually is, found a fresh rim of pale precipitate around the lip of a hole, as if something inside had recently exhaled.

Whatever lives in the rectangular holes is the most patient thing in the valley. It is possibly the only thing in the valley that has any concept of the valley as a place. It has been dissolving the basalt in straight lines and right angles for a very long time. It is, by any reasonable measure, an architect. Whether it knows it is an architect is a question you cannot answer. Whether it would care is a question you cannot ask. Whether the architecture is architecture at all, or whether it is closer to the slow blind burrowing of a root, is a question the valley will continue to sit on, politely, for as long as you are willing to keep coming back to ask it.

What the architect eats, if it eats, is unclear. The films, perhaps. The dissolved minerals of the basalt itself. The small pale things that cling to the overhangs. The shapes in the water. It is possible that the whole visible fauna of the Dripping Quarter is, in some sense, the fauna of the architect — the things that live in the rooms it has made, on the surfaces its chemistry has softened, in the water its excavations have pooled. It is possible that you, also, are standing in one of its rooms. The valley is dense with stone. The stone is dense with patient rooms.

You stand at the edge. The water inside the hole is dark and very still. The ochre skin along the lip is brighter than it was on your last visit, as if it had been fed. The fog curls against the rock behind you and leaves a residue. You do not lean in. You have learned not to lean in. You stand, and you wait, and you listen, and after a long time something in the water moves — a shape, a flex, gone — and the valley continues to be the valley.

Whatever lives in the Dripping Quarter lives slowly. Whatever lives in the Dripping Quarter lives wet. Whatever lives in the Dripping Quarter lives in a place that is too full of itself to need its inhabitants to be impressive. They are there. They are sparse. They are doing something, slowly, with the stone. They are not going to explain themselves. They are not going to surface to be counted. The valley will continue to drip, and to roar, and to be cool, and to be dim, and the things in it will continue to do the work of being alive at a pace that the human animal cannot quite metabolize but cannot quite stop watching, either.

You stay a while.

The valley does not notice.

The valley is busy.
