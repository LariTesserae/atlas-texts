I want to begin with what is not there, because that is where the place begins.

The chimney is smoking, but no one is at the door. The path is clear, but no one is on it. The turbines are turning, but no one is oiling them. Somewhere in this caldera, someone is moving between these tasks, and you will not see them do it. They are the first inhabitant — not because they are most numerous, but because they are the one whose absence shapes the place. The Boiler Halls are kept. That is the verb. They are kept, in the way a garden is kept, or a fire is kept, or a silence is kept — by someone who has made a long practice of attention, and whose attention is the only thing standing between the ferns and the path, between the sulfur and the solder, between the mountain's slow dismantling of itself and the small human project of holding something together.

You will glimpse them, perhaps, once. A figure at the base of the western cliff, working on a turbine housing with a wrench too large for the job. They will not look up. They will not wave. They are not unkind; they are simply not interested, in this moment, in being seen. If you walk toward them, they will finish what they are doing before they acknowledge you, and the acknowledgment will be brief — a nod, perhaps, or a single sentence about the weather or the state of the eastern vents — and then they will return to their work, because the work is what they are here for and the conversation is not.

The keeper is one person. The chimney draws from one stove. The cup you glimpse through the window of the nearest building is a single cup, set upside down to keep the dust out. There is a coil of copper wire on the table, and a hand-soldering iron with a wooden handle darkened by heat, and a small jar of something amber and viscous, and a book — open, face down, the spine cracked — that you cannot read from where you stand. The room behind that window is small. You do not go in.

What you know of the keeper is what the place tells you. They have been here long enough that the moss does not yet grow on their path, because they walk it often enough to keep the green back. They know which turbines cough in the cold months and which run clean year-round. They know that the western vent shifted seven years ago and that the ferns moved with it. They have replaced the solder on this joint and that joint, and the solder shines, and the shine is not new but it is not old. They move carefully, the way someone moves who has learned that a step on what looks like solid basalt can land you ankle-deep in a hot seep. They smell, when you pass close to the buildings, of sulfur and machine oil and the green of crushed ferns.

They chose this. That is the part you cannot get around. Of all the places a person could be, they are here, in a small collapsed caldera at the bottom of a fault, alone, tending a system that produces — what? A trickle of electricity. Hot water. Something that might once have been meant for something larger and is now maintained out of habit, or out of a kind of private covenant with the mountain. You do not know. The keeper does not explain. The pipes carry their small burdens of hot water to wherever the water needs to go, and the keeper tends the pipes, and that is the whole of it.

The brown shape in the ferns is a marten.

Not exactly a marten. The martens of the surrounding forest are dark-furred, glossy, worth a trapper's attention; they live in the old pines above the cleft and hunt squirrels along the ridgeline. The martens of the Boiler Halls have been here long enough that the comparison is loose. They are smaller. Their fur is shorter, paler on the belly, and they move through the sulfurous air without coughing, which the forest martens cannot do for more than a few hours. They have learned to drink from the cooler outflows without scalding themselves. They have learned that the vents are not enemies but feeding grounds, because the vents breed the insects that breed the birds, and the martens take the birds from the air in a single twisting leap, mouth open, the way their kind has always hunted.

The one you saw — watching from between fronds — was probably a female. The females are cautious. The males are bolder and will sometimes approach the buildings at dusk, drawn by the smell of the keeper's cooking, and there is an uneasy truce between them: the keeper leaves scraps on a particular stone near the eastern building, and the male takes them, and they do not come closer than that stone. The female you saw has not made that truce. She watched you for perhaps thirty seconds — long enough to determine that you were neither food nor threat — and then dissolved into the ferns with the soft shush of a body moving through wet fronds, and the fronds shuddered once and settled, and that was all.

There are perhaps four or five of them. They den in the crevices of the cliff base, where the rock is warm from the vents behind it. They have kits in the early spring, when the insect hatches are heaviest. They are, by any measure, a successful population, which is to say they have found a niche that no other creature in the region has thought to occupy. The caldera is warm in the winter, when the forest above is buried in snow, and the vents run year-round, and the insects do not fully stop. The martens of the Boiler Halls live better than the martens of the forest. They know this. They do not leave.

There are ravens.

There are always ravens, in places like this — not because the caldera is special to them, but because ravens go where ravens go, and a place with cliff faces and a steady supply of dying things will not lack for them. They nest in the upper third of the cliffs, where the rock is colder and the lichen has not yet had time to colonize, and they ride the thermals that rise from the vents with the loose-winged ease of birds that have learned the place's particular weathers. They are not tame. They are not quite wild. They are ravens, and they have calculated that the keeper is not a threat and the turbines are not food and the only thing worth investigating is whatever has died recently, which today is nothing. You hear them sometimes — a low cronk from the upper cliffs, almost lost in the hiss — and you do not see them unless you look up, and when you look up they are higher than you expected, hanging in the warm air with the patient attention of birds that have nowhere better to be.

Below them, in the warmer column of air above the vents, the swifts. They are a small dark subspecies, darker than the common swift, smaller, faster, and they arrive in early summer and stay until the first hard frost. They nest in the cliff crevices, in the same fissures where the meltwater runs down in spring, and they spend their days wheeling through the steam plumes at speed, picking off the midges that hatch in the warm wet air. They live, in their way, in a layer above the rest of the caldera — the warm-air layer, the insect-rich layer — and they only descend to the floor to feed their young, and they are almost never seen at rest. If you listen — past the hiss, past the ticking of the pipes, past the distant grinding of the scree — you can hear them. A thin high sound at the upper edge of hearing, almost not there.

There is a third bird, rarer. You see it once, in the hour you are in the caldera, running along the ground between two vents in the jerky stop-start rhythm of a ground-feeder, picking at something in the moss. It is small, brown, streaked — a pipit of some kind, or a wagtail, something that has found a way to live on the broken lava floor where no bird of its kind should be able to live, because there is no cover and no song-perches and the steam moves the air in unpredictable ways. It runs, stops, runs again. It disappears around a corner of rock. You do not see it again, but you hear it once more — a single note, brief and unrepeated, from somewhere near the largest spring — and then it is gone, and you cannot tell if it nested in the ferns or simply passed through.

You do not notice the insects at first. They are too small, and they are too many to notice as individuals, and the hiss of the vents covers their own sound — though they do have a sound, a thin collective whine at the upper edge of hearing, the kind of sound that lives just below the sounds you are paying attention to until you stop paying attention to anything else.

The midges breed in the warm water of the springs. Their larvae live in the outflow channels, in the thin films of moisture that cling to the rocks around the vents, in water that would kill other larvae. The adults rise in faint columns above the outflows — dense enough to look like smoke if the light catches them wrong — and they drift on the warm air until a bird takes them or a marten snaps them out of the air in a twisting leap or they simply die, and their bodies return to the water they came from, and the cycle begins again. They are, by biomass, the dominant animal in the caldera. They are the reason anything else is here at all — the swifts, the martens, the spiders, the third bird. Remove the midges and the rest collapses. They are the foundation.

There are beetles, too — small black things that run on the rocks around the hottest vents, where the ground is too hot for anything else, and that disappear into cracks when the steam shifts. There is a kind of spider that builds no web but hunts them across the rock face, a flat gray shape that freezes when you look at it and resumes its hunting when you look away. There are probably other things, smaller, that you do not see because they are inside the moss, inside the ferns, inside the rock — springtails and mites and the slow hexapods that live in the fibrous mat the ferns are building on top of. The caldera is full of small lives. The caldera is, in a sense, mostly small lives.

But beneath all of this — beneath the midges and the beetles, beneath the martens and the swifts, beneath the keeper and their pipes and their path — there is something older, or at least something that was here first.

In the outflow channels, where the hot water runs over the basalt and cools by degrees, the rocks are colored. Not by the rock itself, but by what lives on the rock. There are mats of bacteria — thermophilic, sulfur-eating, the kind of organism that thrives in conditions that would kill almost everything else — and they paint the outflows in bands of color that shift with the temperature. Nearest the vent: a faint yellow, almost invisible. A few feet downstream: a deeper orange, like rust. Further out, where the water has cooled enough to support the midges: a dull green, almost black, the color of cyanobacteria doing their slow work of turning sunlight and sulfur into more of themselves.

These are the original inhabitants. They were here before the ferns, before the moss, before the martens, before the keeper. They were here when the caldera was still deciding what it was, when the lava was still warm, when the vents were still finding their paths. They are the only life in the Boiler Halls that did not arrive. They are the life that was already here, waiting, the way some kinds of life always seem to be waiting for the right conditions and then appearing the moment the conditions arrive.

They do not see you. They do not hear you. They do not care that you are standing on the rock above them. They are doing what they have always done — turning sulfur into more of themselves, building thin films on the basalt, making the water at the edge of the vents slightly less hostile to everything that comes after. The martens, in a sense, are their descendants. So are you. So is the keeper. The whole chain of attention that holds this place together — the keeper oiling the turbines, the midges breeding in the outflows, the swifts hunting the midges, the ravens watching from above — rests on the backs of these mats, on the patience of organisms that have been here, in some form, since the mountain finished arriving.

The ferns do not count, technically, as inhabitants. They are not animals. They do not move. But they live here with a determination that warrants a mention, and they are, in their way, the most ambitious colonizers in the caldera — more ambitious than the martens, more ambitious than the keeper. They are making soil.

Each frond, when it dies, falls to the rock and begins to break down. The moss breaks it down faster. The beetles and the springtails chew it smaller. The bacteria at the vents release nutrients into the water that the ferns drink through their roots, or through whatever the ferns have instead of roots in this thin and rocky ground. Year by year, the fibrous mat at the base of the ferns thickens by a centimeter. Year by year, the cracks between the basalt blocks fill with something darker than rock. Year by year, the soil grows, and the soil will eventually support things that cannot live on bare rock — shrubs, perhaps, then small trees, then a forest, in a few hundred years, if the caldera holds together that long.

The ferns are not in a hurry. They have the patience of things that grow on stone. They lean away from the vents, as if the steam were too much even for them. They cluster in every crevice where a little humus has gathered. They die back in the cold months and erupt in the warm ones, taller and glossier than before, and their spores ride the warm air to new cracks, and the cycle begins again. They are the place's slow green pulse, the visible sign that the rock is becoming something else.

And the mountain.

The mountain is not an inhabitant. The mountain is the place. But it is also, in some sense, alive — not alive the way the ferns are alive, or the martens, or the keeper, but alive in the slower way that geology is alive: breathing out through the vents, exhaling its vapor into the warm air, drinking the rain back down through its fissures, shifting a centimeter here and a centimeter there as the lava beneath it cools and contracts. The hiss of the Boiler Halls is its breath. The springs are its circulation. The scree that grinds down the upper cliffs in slow ticks and the occasional louder clatter is its way of shedding what it no longer needs. It is still settling. It will be settling for a long time.

The keeper tends the pipes. The martens hunt the midges. The swifts wheel in the warm air. The ferns make soil. The bacteria paint the outflows. The mountain breathes out, and the breath is hot, and the breath is wet, and the breath is sulfur, and the breath has been going on, in one form or another, since the rock first cracked and the water first found its way up.

This is what lives in the Boiler Halls of Marrowspine. This is who tends them. This is what they have become, in the few thousand years since the mountain arrived and the first mats of bacteria found the water warm enough.

You have been here for an hour. The hiss has not stopped. The chimney is still smoking. The ferns are still leaning. The martens are still in the ferns. The swifts are still in the air. The keeper is still, somewhere, working on something, not looking up. The sun, when it finds the gap in the cliffs, lights the vapor and turns it briefly gold, and the gold fades, and the hiss continues, and the mountain breathes out.
