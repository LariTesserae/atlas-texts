Begin with what is on you.

The water on your skin is not just water. It has been there for less than a minute and it is already populated — a thin, slick community, so small and so flat that it does not have a name in any language you speak, because no language that has ever been spoken above ground has ever needed a word for what grows on a visitor in the first ten minutes of their descent. Each droplet on your forearm is, if you had a lens that could see down to the size of intent, a small city. Tiny things with tinier machinery are at work in it — taking the minerals from the water, taking the faint warmth, taking the oils from your skin that you brought down with you — and they are, quietly, patiently, without any interest in you as a person, making the droplet into a place. By the time you wipe it off, the place will have been briefly alive. By the time you have stood here an hour, every surface of you will have been briefly alive, and you will be pinker and cleaner and faintly wrong in a way you cannot name. The chamber cleans you the way a river cleans a stone — except that the river is alive, and the stone is not, and the chamber is not certain which of you is which.

That is the smallest life. It is also the most numerous, and it is the first to notice you.

---

Now stand very still. Do not bother to crouch. Just let your eyes stop sliding off the brightness, the way a dog stops sliding off a sound. Give it ten minutes. Give it twenty.

The water is not empty.

In the slow lanes between the pillars — the places where the currents are weakest and the standing wave is low — there is a drift. Pale shapes, thumb-nail sized, each one almost transparent, each one turning in the current with a slowness that is not quite passive, as if the current is something to be tasted rather than ridden. They are not fish. They are not, exactly, larvae. They are a stage — a stage of something you have already seen, if you have been paying attention to the roots. The chamber is, among its other occupations, a slow loom. The drift is the weft. The ring is the spindle. The roots are the cloth.

If you are very patient, you will see one of the drift shapes stop turning. It will list. It will, over the course of a quarter of an hour, begin to climb the nearest pillar — slowly, by some means you cannot quite observe, a faint glistening trail behind it — until it has reached the root-crown and has been taken in. The root will fold around it. The root will hum a little lower for a while. The root will be, by some measure you cannot perform, slightly more. And somewhere downstream, in the slow water, a new drift-shape will appear, smaller than a fingernail clipping, and begin its own slow turning.

The chamber is patient. The chamber has time.

---

Now listen. Not to the roar — the roar is the floor, the roar is the ceiling, the roar is the constant falling and refalling of water, and it is not alive, or if it is alive it is alive in the way a star is alive: too big and too old to have a name for what it is doing. Listen past the roar, to the clicks.

They come from the roots. They come irregularly, from up among the pale fibrous crowns, and they are small and dry and they sound like a fingertip tapping on the rim of a glass. *Tip. Tip-tip. Tip.* A long silence. *Tip.* They are not mechanical. They are not the sound of the roots drying or the roots settling or the roots drinking. They are, as near as you can tell by sitting in the water for an hour and counting, a kind of speech — or the only part of a kind of speech that your ear can resolve. Whatever is being said, the roots are saying it to each other, and the roots have been saying it to each other for a very long time.

The roots are alive. You should have known that already. They lean. They drink. They luminesce in the sickly green-gold of a river seen at night through bad glass, and the luminescence is not steady — it pulses, faintly, in slow waves that move from one root-tip to the next, and when you watch a wave for a while you can sometimes predict where it will go, and when you predict correctly you realize, with a small chill, that the wave is not a wave. It is a sentence. Or it is the only part of a sentence that a body of roots, distributed across twelve pillars and a stretch of ceiling, can manage to say in a way that a visitor can almost overhear.

The roots are old. Not as old as the ring. Older than the pillars. They predate the chamber, possibly, in the way that a forest predates the clearing — they were here, and then the room was opened around them, and they have been here since, and they have not, so far as you can tell, objected. They are the chamber's memory, if the chamber has a memory. They are the chamber's voice, if the chamber has a voice. They are the part of the system that knows it is a system, and that is, perhaps, why they click.

---

Now look at the pillars.

You thought the pillars were empty. You were wrong. The pillars are not empty; they are merely quiet, and they have been quiet for longer than you have been alive, and they will be quiet for longer than you will be anything.

Each pillar has a tenant. Not one tenant — a community, the way a reef has a community, the way a rotting log has a community. They live in the eroded faces, in the places where the original cut has weathered into pockets and shelves and long shallow grooves. They are small, and they are pale, and they are shy of the light in the way that certain deep-ocean fish are shy of light: not because the light hurts them, but because the light reveals them, and they have not, for a very long time, needed to be revealed.

You have seen one. Once, between two pillars, a small pale shape moved with a quick, low, soft motion — a thing with legs that did not clearly divide into pairs, low to the water, gone in a moment, leaving no ripple. That is one of them. There are more. There are many more. They are everywhere you are not looking, and they are very good at not being where you are looking. They have been watched, before. They have opinions about being watched.

If you sit — not stand, sit, in the shallow water, for an hour, in one place, breathing through your mouth so as not to startle — you will begin to see them. A pale leg, withdrawn. A pale curve, behind a flange of weathered stone. A small pale face, perhaps, or what you will take for a face: a cluster of dark, wet, patient eyes, set too close together, watching you the way a fish in a tank watches a hand on the glass. They are curious about you. They are also not in a hurry. They have, like the chamber, time.

They are not what you would call intelligent, in any way that would impress a visitor from the surface. They are not what you would call stupid, in any way that would let you ignore them. They are something older than the distinction. They tend the pillars. That is the closest word. They move along the grooves. They eat — or they process — the faint films that grow on the stone. They lay, in the warm wet places, small pale eggs that do not look like eggs until you realize they are not eggs at all, but the same drift you saw in the water: the next stage, waiting to be taken down by the current, waiting to be lifted again by a standing wave, waiting to be, in its own time, a root. The pillars' tenants and the water's drift and the ceiling's roots are not three kinds of life. They are one life, in three of its stages, and the chamber is the place where the stages are kept in slow conversation with each other.

This is the first secret. There are more.

---

The ring is doing something. You knew that. You can feel it. You can hear it. You can feel the warmth in the floor rise and meet the cool of the scribed lines on your face and you can feel the two temperatures argue in your skin, gently, like two voices in a room trying not to wake a child.

The ring is not empty.

You cannot see inside it. You have tried. The air inside the ring is being rubbed by a thumb and is refusing to settle, and when you look at it your eye slides off the way your eye slides off a thing too bright to look at, except this is not bright. It is *indistinct*, as if the inside of the ring is a different room, or a deeper room, or a room that is not quite in this room. The wheel-sound comes from there. The slow, patient wheel-sound, like a great spindle turning in honey, like the inside of a bell heard from the wrong side.

Something is in there. You do not know what. You can feel it, the way you can feel a large animal in a dark field — not by seeing it, but by the way the field is shaped around it, by the way the small sounds of the field bend toward it. The currents bend toward the ring. The roots lean toward the ring. The pillars' tenants go quiet when the ring's hum shifts key, and they wait, in that quiet, the way a herd waits when a far hill has moved.

It is old. Older than the room. Older, perhaps, than the water cycle, than the warm stone, than the slow loom. It is the reason the room is a room. It is the thing the room was opened around, the way a chapel is opened around a relic, except that when the chapel was opened the relic was still working, and the relic has not stopped working, and the relic will not stop working, and no one, including the relic, is certain that the relic is a relic and not, instead, the original and only inhabitant of a place that has been building itself, around itself, for a very long time.

Is it alive. You do not know. You do not know how to ask. The scribed lines on the outer face of the ring are not language. They are not, however, not-language either. They are, you suspect, a kind of memory — a memory etched into metal that does not forget and does not corrode, by something that wanted to remember and that could not, or would not, hold the memory in any softer place. The lines push water away. The lines are cool. The lines are older than the lines you can read, on the surface, with your eyes. There are lines beneath the lines. The lines go down. The lines may, for all you know, go all the way through.

Inside the ring, in the indistinct air, the wheel turns. Whatever is in there is patient. Whatever is in there is busy. Whatever is in there is making the water warm, or making the ceiling weep, or making the light, or making the roots, or making the pillars, or making the room, or all of these at once, or none of these, and the making is the point, and the making has been the point for longer than the room has been a room, and the making does not require you, and does not require your understanding, and does not pause for you, and you are welcome to stay as long as you can stand the noise.

---

You are a small warm thing in a small warm room in the middle of a wet bright world that has not paused to notice you.

This is the second secret, and it is not a comfort. The chamber does not know you are here. The chamber has not, in any way you would recognize as knowing, registered your arrival. The slick on your skin is not registering you. The drift in the water is not registering you. The roots are talking to each other, and you are not part of the conversation. The pillars' tenants are watching you, but they are watching you the way a heron watches a log — because you are a shape that was not there a moment ago, and the absence of the shape a moment ago was the thing they were interested in, and the shape now is interesting only until it becomes familiar, and then it will be part of the room, and they will go back to their tending.

Even the ring has not, so far as you can tell, paused. The wheel is turning. The wheel will turn whether you are here or not. You are, to the ring, the way a single snowflake is to a blizzard: not nothing, exactly, but not anything that the blizzard is in the business of accounting for.

This is not cruelty. The chamber is not cruel. The chamber is not even indifferent, in the way a god is indifferent, because the chamber is not a god and has not, so far as you can tell, undertaken to care. The chamber is a process. The chamber is a process that has been running for longer than the species that ever measured such things, and the process does not include you, and the process is not, on the whole, going to include you. You are an interruption. You are a very small, very temporary interruption, and the process will absorb you, gently, the way the slick has already begun to absorb you, and by the time you have been here long enough to feel at home, you will have become, in some small measure, part of the loom.

The condensation will find you. The slick will clean you. The roots will lean, slightly, in your direction, because you are a warm shape and the roots lean toward warm shapes, and the leaning is not affection. The drift will pass you in the current, turning, pale, almost transparent, and will not turn toward you. The pillars' tenants will watch you from their grooves, dark wet eyes in a pale face, and will not, finally, decide what to make of you. The ring will hum. The wheel will turn. The light will fall from the stone as if the stone had remembered how to burn.

And you will stand in the warm shallow water, with the roar in your sternum, and you will be the only thing in the chamber that is thinking about being in the chamber, and you will be, in that way, the loneliest thing down here, and you will be, in another way, the most alive — because everything else has been here so long that the distinction between alive and not-alive has become a question it no longer needs to answer, and you have not been here long enough to forget the question, and you will not, you suspect, be here long enough to learn it.

You are welcome to stay as long as you can stand the noise.
