# The Inheritors of the Green Dark

They are old. That is the first thing to understand about them, though old is the wrong word — *uninterrupted* is closer. No generation here has ever begun from nothing. Each has woken into the work of the one before, and has left more than it received, and has folded that excess into the inheritance of the next. The first of them — the ones the later ones call, in the deep-grammar tongue, the *Unwritten* — are not remembered as individuals but as a kind of soil condition. A decision that was made, and kept being made, and then made itself.

You will not meet the Unwritten. You will meet their great-grandchildren, perhaps. You will not recognize the relationship.

---

The ones who live here now are small. This will surprise you. You have been imagining engineers, gardeners, architects, minds proportionate to the cathedral-vast organism they tend — and you have been looking up, for scale, at the joined canopy fifty meters above your head, and have been reading patience in the slow pulse of the floor, and have assumed that patience of that magnitude requires a thinker of some equivalent magnitude to hold it. This is a category error. The patience is not held by anyone. The patience is *the place.* The ones who tend it are small, warm, busy, and roughly the size of your spread hand.

They are not human. You should not expect them to be. They have hands — four-fingered, with an opposed thumb, the nails pale and faintly translucent — and they have faces, and the faces are arranged in the conventional mammalian way of having a front, and the faces express things you can read if you are patient, though the grammar is different. The eyes are larger than you would expect, and very dark, and they do not blink so much as *refresh* — a quick flutter of the inner lid, sideways, every several seconds, like a screen clearing. They have no hair. Their skin is the green of their hands, a soft olive going to grey at the joints, and it is warm to the touch, and it breathes with them.

They are warm-blooded. You can feel the heat of one standing near you in a cool chamber — a radiant heat, gentle, not unpleasant. They smell, when you are close, of cut grass and warm metal and something sweeter that you cannot place and that is probably the engineered pheromones they use to mark the chambers they consider their own for the season. They speak. The speech is in the air and is not in the air; you will have to attend to the details.

---

There are perhaps forty of them in the structure you can reach. There are more elsewhere. You do not know how many more. You will never know, because the structure does not end at any edge you can walk to, and they have not, in any conversation you succeed in having with them, expressed the concept of a population count, because the concept is, to them, slightly obscene. A count would imply a boundary, and the place has no boundary that they want to believe in. There are *as many as there are.* There have always been, more or less, *as many as there are.*

They do not, as a rule, live in families. This will strike you as strange, and then it will not. The familial unit presupposes a kind of privacy of inheritance that the place does not accommodate. A child here is not *raised*; a child is *germinated,* and is tended, and is grafted, in the literal sense — onto the social body, onto the work, onto the circulation — and the people who tend it are not its parents in the sense you mean, because the word *parent* here means something like *the two people whose graft-lines you carry,* which is a relationship of technique, of inheritance of craft, and is not exclusive, and is not biological in the way your word implies. There is biology, of course. There must be. There are small warm bodies and there are births. But the births are folded into the work so quickly, and the births are distributed so widely, that the family as a unit is not the load-bearing unit. The work is the load-bearing unit.

You will see a young one, if you are lucky. It will be perhaps the size of a loaf of bread, and it will be carried in a sling of woven bark-cloth against the chest of an older one, and it will be watching everything with those too-large dark eyes that do not blink so much as refresh, and it will already be learning the names of the chambers, because the names are the grammar, and the grammar is the inheritance, and the inheritance is everything.

---

What they do, mostly, is *tend.* This is the verb that will replace all your other verbs if you stay long enough. They tend the trunks — pruning, training, grafting, coaxing the growth into the shapes that the work requires. They tend the pipes — clearing the blockages that the growth introduces, replacing the sections that have been incorporated into the wood and must now be re-extruded, negotiating with the living system for the right of way. They tend the pumps — the great cast-iron hearts of the place, the pale ceramic lungs — and the tending of a pump is a vocation that one of them may apprentice to for decades before being trusted to do it alone, because a pump failed is a chamber lost, and a chamber lost is a generation's work undone.

They tend the seeds. This is the work you will see them at, if you see them at anything. You will be standing in a high chamber where the light comes down in pale shafts, and one of them will come in — moving through a doorway that you had not noticed, because it was hidden behind a curtain of growth — and they will be carrying a basket. The basket is woven of living fibers, and it is itself tended; the fibers are still putting out pale rootlets that drink from the air. In the basket are seeds — the seeds whose slow snow you noticed earlier, falling through the shafts of light. The seeds are angular. They catch the light in facets. They are not all the same. There are dozens of kinds, hundreds perhaps, each one a specific graft, a specific inheritance, a specific intention.

The one with the basket will pause in the shaft of light and will hold one of the seeds up, turning it, considering it. They will make a small sound — a low note, almost a hum, but with structure in it, with grammar — and the sound will not be for you. It will be for the seed. It will be a kind of *reading aloud* of the seed's lineage, its intentions, the place it is meant for. Then the other will move to a prepared spot on the floor — a soft place, a dark place, a place where the substrate has been recently opened to the air — and will press the seed in, with two fingers, to the first knuckle. Then they will cover it. Then they will move on.

You will watch this many times. You will never see the same seed planted twice. You will begin to understand that the planting is not agriculture. The planting is *speech.* Each seed is a word, and the place is the sentence, and the sentence is being spoken continuously, has been being spoken continuously for centuries, and will be spoken long after the one with the basket has moved on to other work, in other chambers, that you will not live to see.

---

They are not silent among themselves. This will surprise you, after the held-breath quiet of the place. When two of them are working in the same chamber, they exchange — and you will have to listen for it, because it is quiet, it is in the background of the breathing of the place — a constant low murmur. The murmur has grammar. The murmur has cadence. But it is not, you will realize after some time, primarily *vocal.* The vocalization is a thin layer, an afterthought. The primary content is in the *hands.*

Watch the hands of two of them working near each other. They are never still. Even when the rest of the body is at rest — crouched, waiting, attending — the fingers move. They make small shapes in the air. They tap rhythms on the wood. They sketch diagrams, tiny and precise, on surfaces that you cannot see but that they can — surfaces that are, perhaps, the faint electromagnetic traces of old copper wiring, or the subtle thermal gradients of the substrate, or some other layer of the place's information that you do not have the apparatus to perceive. They are *writing,* constantly, in a medium you can barely detect. The vocal murmur is the shadow of the writing. The writing is the speech.

This is why they speak so quietly. This is why the place is so quiet. They do not need to be loud. They have a thousand quiet channels, and they use them all.

---

They know you are here. You should not assume they do not. The place will see you long before any of them do — the floor will feel your weight, the circulation will register your unfamiliar chemistry, some pump in some distant chamber will adjust its rhythm by a fraction of a percent to accommodate the new metabolism — and the information will travel, through the buried pipes, through the root conduits, through the mycelium, faster than you can walk, and one of them, somewhere, will read the information, and will decide whether to come.

They will come. You will not see them decide to come. They will simply, at some point, be there — in the chamber with you, or in the corridor you are about to enter, or behind you, at a distance that suggests they have been following for some time. They will not speak first. They will wait. They are good at waiting. They have been waiting for a very long time, in the collective sense.

When one of them addresses you, it will be in the vocal murmur, but the murmur will be slower, will have more of the writing-hand accompaniment, will be shaped for a system that does not have the apparatus. They will be *translating,* for you, into a mode you can perceive. They will be patient about this. They have had, perhaps, no one to translate for in a long time. They will be out of practice. The translation will be imperfect. There will be misunderstandings. They will proceed anyway.

They will offer you water. This will be the first thing. Not because you need it — the place is keeping you hydrated, gently, through some mechanism of the air that you have not detected — but because the offering of water is the first word of the greeting, and the greeting must be spoken. The water will be in a vessel of polished rootwood, and the vessel will be warm, and the water will taste of the place — resinous, faintly sweet, with the metallic undertone you noticed earlier. You will drink it. This is not optional. This is the completion of the greeting. They will nod, or the equivalent. They will wait.

---

What do they think of you? This is the question you will be asking, beneath your other questions, and they will be unable to answer it, because the question requires translation and they have not, perhaps, had to translate the concept of *an outsider* in a long time. You are not the first outsider. There have been others. The place remembers them, in the way the place remembers everything — as a layer in the substrate, as a note in the circulation, as a seed of a kind that is no longer planted. They will be polite to you. They will be patient with you. They will not, in any way that you can detect, be *curious* about you. This is not indifference. This is the opposite of indifference. They are so attentive to you that they have already absorbed you, have already incorporated your presence into the ongoing sentence, have already planted the seed of the next moment of the conversation. Curiosity would imply that you were a question. To them, you are already part of an answer.

You will leave. They will not prevent you. They will not particularly encourage you to leave. They will accompany you, perhaps, to a chamber near an egress — and the egress will be a growth-tunnel, a corridor that has been deliberately opened to the outside, that the outside is being deliberately invited in through — and they will make the low vocal sound that means *the greeting is complete,* and they will press, into your hand, a seed. The seed will be angular. It will catch the light in facets. It will be warm from their hand. They will not explain it. They will not tell you what to do with it. The explanation would be redundant. The seed is the explanation. The seed is the completion of the sentence they have been speaking since you arrived.

You will take it. This is not optional. You will step out into whatever is outside — the place has an outside, though you have not seen it, and you will not describe it here, because the description belongs to the seed and to the place you will go to plant it — and you will walk away, and the pulse of the place will fade behind you, and you will feel, for a long time, the slow expansion and contraction of the floor in your feet, a memory the place has left in your bones.

You will not forget them. This is not a prediction. It is a description of what the seed will do.
