# What Lives in the Relay Vault

The granite lives here.

Not the granite as a noun, as a *thing*, but the granite as a verb — the slow, patient grammatical tense of stone. The lichen is the granite's skin, and the lichen does not so much live *on* the rock as *is* the rock at a finer resolution, the rock having learned to differentiate itself into acid yellows and deep, watching greens. The granite is awake. The granite has been awake since 1923, or 1841, or 1987. The contradiction of dates does not trouble it. The granite has time.

The turbine lives here too, but only in the way a heart lives in a chest — without opinion, without witness, without the capacity to know that it is doing anything at all. It turns. It turns because water falls on it, and water falls on it because the cold contracts the air and pulls the mist down through the grille, and the mist is gathered, and the gathered water falls, and the falling water turns the pale disc that you would see if you knelt and pressed your face to the iron. The turbine is not a thing with intentions. It is the mechanical equivalent of a heartbeat: a function that continues because continuing is what it *is*. To ask whether it is hydroelectric or pneumatic is to ask whether a heart is electrical or muscular. Both. Neither. The distinction is for engineers, and the engineers are not here.

The lichen, though. The lichen is here.

It is the only organism in the vault that can rightly be called an organism, and even that word strains. The lichen is a coalition — fungus and alga bound in a slow embrace, neither fully alive without the other, and the particular colonies on these walls have been negotiating with this air and this wet and this cold for a long time. They are not the same lichen that was seeded. The seeded lichen was a template; what you see now is what the template *became* when it was left alone with itself for a century or two, allowed to drift from the original yellow toward grays and toward that deep, almost-black green in the upper corners. The lichen does not grow so much as *translate* — taking the inputs of fog and temperature and the faint sulfur of the air and rendering them in color. It is a living instrument panel. It is also, in its own slow and partial way, *watching*.

The lichen does not have eyes. But the acid yellow of the colonies — the pale, sour, lemon-bright spots — does something that eyes do. It *faces*. When you enter the vault, when you sit on the outcrop near the wall, the lichen seems to orient toward you the way a sunflower orients toward the sun. This is a trick of perception, or a trick of the lichen. It may simply be that your body heat, your exhalations, your ambient CO2 shift the microclimate at your edge of the chamber, and the lichen responds to the shift by altering its chemistry, and the chemistry alters the color, and the color *looks at you*. The operators in their low room know which colors mean what. You, up here, only know that the lichen attends.

The cold lives here, and the cold is the closest thing the vault has to a citizen.

Four degrees Celsius is the temperature at which water is at its densest, a small thermodynamic coincidence that the engineers — whoever they were, whenever they were — understood and exploited. The cold here is not the cold of a winter night, which is a cold that comes and goes. This is a cold that has been *held*. It is held by the glacial water pumped up from below, by the elevation, by the wetness of the air, by the granite itself, which holds a temperature the way a bank holds a deposit. The cold is the contract. Everything else — the turbine, the pipes, the lichen, the dated plate — is a clause in the agreement that the cold enforces.

If you stay long enough in the vault, the cold will begin to live *in* you. It will move from the air into your clothing and then through your clothing into your skin and then past your skin into the blood. Your fingers will go white where you brush the copper pipes and will not come back for ten minutes. Your breath will almost plume, almost, and the almost-ness will be the thing you remember — the way the cold stops just short of the visible, the way it insists without evidence. By the time you leave, you will have carried some of the cold out with you, lodged in the marrow of your bones, and it will take hours to dissipate. The vault will have *changed* you. The vault will have left a deposit.

The sound lives here.

The sound is not a sound in the ordinary sense. It does not have a source you can point to. It has a *throat* — the grille, the dark throat below — but it does not come from the throat so much as it *fills* the chamber, the way water fills a bowl. It is omnipresent and granular and low. It is the sound of something being turned at a scale you cannot see, the slow rotation of a pale disc the size of a planet, or the size of a coin, depending on where you imagine it. The sound does not have edges because it has been *running* since the vault was built, since 1923 or 1841 or 1987, and it has worn smooth the boundaries of itself. There is no attack. There is no decay. There is only the continuous *being* of the sound, the way there is only the continuous being of the cold.

The sound is what you adapt to, or fail to. At first your body fights it. Your jaw clenches. Your shoulders rise. Your inner ear sends the small bones of itself into confusion. Then, if you sit long enough, the body gives up the fight and accepts the sound as a new ground state — a *baseline*. The sound becomes the silence, and what was silence becomes a thin, anemic thing. When you eventually leave the vault, the outside world will sound wrong to you. The world outside will sound *thin*. You will miss the granular thunder. You will miss it the way a person who has lived by the sea misses the sea.

The fog lives here, and the fog is the most social inhabitant.

The fog is what moves. Everything else — the granite, the lichen, the pipes, the dated plate, the iron door — is fixed. The fog moves at knee height, sometimes at waist height, sometimes so low you forget it is there. The fog is the only part of the vault that *visits* you. It drifts toward your outcrop and pools around your feet and dampens the cuffs of your trousers and tastes, when you breathe it, of copper and cold water and the faintest mineral bite. The fog is also the medium of communication. The operators in their low room signal through the fog — a sigh of colder air from a hidden register, a shift in the sulfur content, a thinning or thickening of the mist. The fog is the alphabet the operators write in. The fog is the mail.

You do not see the operators. You may never see the operators. But the fog carries their messages, and the lichen reads the messages, and the lichen answers in its slow color-language. The operators and the lichen have been corresponding for a hundred years, perhaps longer. You are, in your small way, walking into the middle of a conversation you cannot overhear.

What lives in the Relay Vault is *patience*.

The lichen is patient. The granite is patient. The cold is patient. The turbine is patient. The fog is patient. The sound is patient. The dated plate with its contradictory years and its engineered tongue is patient. The iron door is patient. The spiral stair behind the iron door is patient. The relay room and the valve gallery and the long descent to where the water begins are all patient.

And if you stay long enough, sitting on your outcrop near the wall, letting the sound press against you until your body stops trying to fight it, something in you will become patient too. Something in you will understand that the vault was not built to *contain* anything, but to *endure* something. The vault is a holding pattern. The vault is a place where time is kept at a low temperature so that it does not spoil. Something is being relayed down there, through the iron door, down the spiral stair, through the relay room and the valve gallery and the long descent. You do not know what is being relayed. You may never know. But it is being relayed patiently, in the cold, in the fog, in the sound, and the lichen is watching, and the lichen is not objecting, and the operators are turning their small levers in their low room, and the turbine is turning, and the water is falling, and the water is gathered, and the water is sent down.

The sound will not stop.

You do not have to go down.
