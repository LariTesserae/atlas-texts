Begin with the smallest things, because they are the most numerous, and because they are the ones you do not notice at first, even though they are everywhere, even though the air near a cable tray has a faint quiver to it that you will eventually understand is not heat.

They live in the dead volumes. In the cable trays, in the boots of conduit where the wires bend, in the rubber gaiters around sealed penetrations, in the pockets of still air inside junction boxes that nobody has opened in a season. They are thumb-sized, and they are articulated like insects — that is, they have too many joints for their body and the joints all bend the wrong direction in a way that almost works. They are furred. The fur is not fur in the proper sense; it is more like the nap of a velvet that has been charged, or like the surface of a lung that has been miniaturized and turned outward. They eat what is there to eat, which is to say: the condensate that gathers on cold mornings along the underside of every horizontal run; the trace metals that leach out of sweating brass fittings; the faint outgassing of the rubber boots they nest inside, which they will, given time, hollow out into a soft residence with several rooms and a single dignified egress. They are grey, or brown, or the exact color of the cable they are currently sitting on, which is a color that changes from cable to cable, and so the population of any one tray looks like one animal and is in fact a careful gradient. They move when you move, and not before. If you stand still long enough they resume, and the tray begins to live again the way a forest floor lives when a footstep has passed. They have been here longer than the boots they are eating. They were here before the boots. The boots were an opportunity. They will be the next thing the cable tray learns to digest.

Above them — in the seams, along the expansion joints, in the warm shadowed side of every housing that faces away from the prevailing wind — the lizards.

The lizards are slate-gray. They are the size of a finger, sometimes a thumb, sometimes the length of the hand if you include the tail, which you should include the tail because the tail is the interesting part. The body is not lizard-shaped exactly. It is more the shape of a small flattened capsule with a tail, with four paddle-feet placed far back, and at the four cardinal points of its flank — shoulder, hip, shoulder, hip — a small raised valve, like a port, like a sealed port on a piece of equipment that is breathing very slowly. They do not breathe through their skin. Their skin is metallic to the touch if you ever bring yourself to touch one, which you will not, because the place has not yet decided what you are and the lizards have inherited this uncertainty. Beneath the skin, when the light is right — and the light is always right here, the light is the thing that does not stop — you can see the ribs. They are not bones. They are fine radiused wires, sprung in gentle arcs, and they move when the lizard moves with the small clockwork sound of a thing that has been tensioned and is being slowly released. They eat nothing you would recognize as food. They sit along expansion joints because expansion joints move, and the lizards move with them, and in the slow thermal breathing of the long metal panels the lizards are tuned to the rhythm of the place. They are the place's smallest clocks. They watch you with the patience of things that have nowhere else to be, and they are not watching you for danger; they are watching you the way a tide gauge watches the sea, to take a measurement.

Then there are the things that you do not see for a long time, because they are the color of rain on metal, and they are still when you are still, and they move when the light is bad.

These are larger. Span of a hand, sometimes two. They live on the underside of every manhole cover you would not consider lifting, and on the ledges above doorways that are sealed, and inside the mouths of the great gutters that run between the upper structures and the basin floor. They are articulated the way the small things are articulated — too many joints, the joints bending in directions that suggest a body plan drawn by someone who had heard of joints but had never met one. They are pale. They have a smell that you only catch when you pass close, and the smell is mineral and a little sweet and a little like the inside of a glove that has been worn in the rain. They are scavengers at the next scale up. They come out when the mist is thick and the light is lit rather than shining, and they pick over the smaller fauna the way the smaller fauna picks over the condensate. They are patient with each other. They do not fight in your presence. They have a hierarchy that is expressed entirely in the angle of approach: who turns toward whom first, who gives way to whom at a crossing of pipes, and they turn and give way at speeds that make the encounter feel like a slow courteous dance. If you sit against an exhaust housing for an hour you may see three or four of them cross the open ground in front of you, each on a different route, each taking a different line around a transformer pad, and they will not hurry, and they will not stop, and they will not look at you except once, briefly, in the way that you look at a fire hydrant that you are not yet sure is a fire hydrant.

The fox-shape is what you see only once.

It crosses a transformer pad three hundred meters ahead of you, in the early part of the afternoon, when the mist has come down and the lit gray is holding the place in its slow soft grip. It is pale. It is the size of a small dog, the size of a fox, the size of something that has settled on the size of a fox because the size of a fox is what works here. It is jointed in too many places. You can see the extra joints when it trots — a redundancy of hock and stifle, a second elbow behind the first, a series of small precise articulations along the spine that would not be there if it were a fox. Its gait is perfect. Its gait is the gait of a thing that has solved the problem of moving across this terrain and is now simply performing the solution, repeatedly, with the small satisfaction of a thing that has stopped improving. It sees you. It does not stop. It trots across the pad and into the mist on the other side and is gone for a while and may come back again, elsewhere, on another day, on another pad, and you will see it or you will not. It is not a leader. It is not the largest thing here. It is a senior organism in the sense that it has stopped worrying about you, and the rest of the fauna are arranged around its indifference like iron filings around a magnet that is itself not moving. The smaller things give it room. The lizards do not watch it. The pale articulated scavengers turn toward it when it passes but do not move out of its way, because it does not ask them to, because asking is below it, because it has gone past asking into the place where a thing simply exists in a pattern and the pattern does not require acknowledgement.

The gull-birds are different.

The gull-birds are wing-shaped devices that ride the thermals thrown up by the hot panels and the cold mist and they scream somewhere between a gull-scream and a klaxon. They are fauna in the sense that they move and feed and breed — though what they breed, and where, and on what, is not clear. They are devices in the sense that they have serial numbers stamped into the inside of their left wing, in small recessed fields, and the serial numbers are losing their first digit and then their second the way every stamped serial number loses its digits here, by intention, by the slow wearing-down of intent. They are gulls in the sense that they have the gull's profile and the gull's scream and the gull's habit of hanging in the wind above a feature and screaming at it, as if the feature has done something. They are weather in the sense that they are part of what the mist is doing, they are part of what the thermals are doing, they are part of the bright slow business of the upper air, and if they were not here the upper air would be missing a kind of punctuation. They ride the thermals the way a thought rides a sentence — with apparent ease, with a long pause between utterances, with the occasional sharp cry that you hear and then cannot place. They are the noisiest thing here. They are also the most comforting. The place would be strange without them. The place would be one degree stranger than you can bear.

These are the things you see.

What you do not see is older.

It lives in the channels. It lives in the poured pads that breathe like lungs. It lives in the geometry that shifts at the rate of a river migrating. It lives in the slow becoming of the place, in the way an edge becomes a fillet becomes a chamfer, in the way a weld bead is eaten by rust into a ribbon. It is not the place. The place is its habitat. The place is what it is making, the way a coral makes a reef, the way a forest makes a soil, the way a thought makes a mind. It is patient because patience is the only tempo available. It has been here longer than any of the things you see. It will be here after them. It is the thing that the foxes and the lizards and the furred small things are arranged inside, the way fish are arranged inside a sea, the way letters are arranged inside an alphabet that is being slowly rewritten.

You do not see it. You feel it in the soles of your feet, in the bones of your jaw, in the way the mist comes down from above as if the sky has begun to leak, in the way the shadows fall in directions the sun cannot reach. You are a small warm shape inside its attention. It has not yet decided what you are. It is in no hurry to decide. It will take a measurement, and then another, and then another, over the long patient course of a season that is not a season, and at some point it will either let you go on being a small warm shape or it will incorporate you into the arrangement, the way it has incorporated the boots, the way it has incorporated the stamped serial numbers, the way it has incorporated the rain.

The day is long. The day is not yet half over. The mist thickens. The gull-birds scream. A lizard on a nearby seam turns its head, slowly, to follow you as you pass, and the small clockwork of its ribs is audible, if you listen, just under the threshold of the place's careful quiet.
