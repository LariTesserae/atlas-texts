What lives there.

There are creatures here that have stopped being creatures in the usual way and have become part of the architecture. The frogs are the most obvious of these. They sit the way bricks sit. Their colors — the small ones are chartreuse with a stripe of sulfur along the spine, the medium ones a deep tropical blue with feet like wet ink — are colors no natural frog would hold for long without fading, and these have held them. Their eyes are open and the gold of their irises does not dull. Their toes grip the waxy leaves and the pale clay with equal indifference, and the grip does not tire. When you walk past one and circle back, the frog will be in a different orientation on its perch, and the orientation will be one you have seen before, on a different visit. You will feel you have caught it in a previous posture. The frog will not appear to notice you. The frog will not appear to notice anything. It breathes because breathing is what it was installed to do.

The dragonflies are like this too, but in the air. They hang at the height of an adult's eyes, wings still, bodies level. Their thoraxes are the metallic green of old bottle glass. From certain angles, looking down an aisle of trellises, you will count a dozen of them in a single glance, all suspended at the same altitude, all facing slightly different directions like compass needles released from the same magnet. They do not hunt. There are midges here — there must be midges, because something has to be eaten — but I have never seen a dragonfly take one. The midges themselves are small pale things that hang in columns above the water, each column a different height, and they hang the way the dragonflies hang. The whole airspace is populated by things holding still. When I move through it I feel I am the only thing in motion, and that this is an error I am making about myself.

The crabs are pale. They are the color of dough left under a damp cloth. They sit in the shallow water at the bases of the uprights and along the channels of the trellises, and they hold their claws the way a person holds their hands in their lap. They do not move when you step near them. I reached down once, early on, and touched the carapace of one with a fingertip. It was cool and smooth and gave very slightly under the pressure, the way a real crab would, and the legs rearranged themselves — slowly, with a kind of patience, as though the rearrangement had been scheduled — and then the crab was still again, in a posture that was almost but not exactly the posture it had been in before. I do not touch them anymore.

The fish are down in the clearer pockets between bricks. They are small. They are the color of the water, which is the color of weak tea. They hold their fins out, paired and symmetrical, and they face into whatever current there is, and there is no current, so they face in every direction at once, or they face the same direction. It is hard to tell. Their eyes are dark and their mouths work gently, the way fish mouths work, but they do not dart. If you put your hand in the water, near them, they do not startle. They adjust, very slightly, the angle of one fin, and resume. I have watched one fish for a long time and it has not traveled the length of its own body.

There is a bird. I should describe the bird. The bird is in the middle distance and it is always in the middle distance. It sits on a toppled upright, which is itself a thing I should describe — a sun-dried clay brick that fell from a trellis, perhaps, or that was laid as the keystone of a low arch and has since slipped, lying now at an angle on the clay floor, half-submerged, with a fern growing through the joint where it meets the floor. The bird is on this. It is the size of a small heron and the color of the wet clay, with a long neck and a long beak and legs that fold under it in the way a heron's legs fold. It faces the same direction every time I see it, which is the direction I am walking. Its eye, when I look at it, is open, and the eye is the color of the water. It does not blink. It does not turn. When I circle around to its other side, it is facing me. I have stopped trying to verify this.

I have come to understand that the creatures here are not in the plateau. They are of the plateau. They were installed with the bricks, or they grew up through the joints the way the philodendrons did, at the same time, in the same making, and they share the making's suspension. They are not alive in the sense that life requires change. They are alive in the sense that they have postures. The frogs hold the pose of a frog at rest. The dragonflies hold the pose of a dragonfly hunting. The bird holds the pose of a bird watching something move through reeds. The poses do not tire. The poses are the creatures. What you are seeing, when you see them, is the final still of something that was decided once and then locked.

This is not a cruelty. I want to be clear about that. I spent some time suspecting cruelty — suspecting that some maker had caught these things mid-behavior and pinned them there, the way you pin a butterfly to a board. But the place does not feel like that. It feels like a model of something, but a model that was built at full scale and then left. The maker's hand is not visible in the creatures the way it is visible in the bricks — in the mismatched lines, the slightly-off level, the thumb-pressed joints. The creatures have the maker's indifference rather than the maker's care. They were not pinned. They were placed. They were put where they belonged in the composition, and the composition holds, and they hold with it.

There is one thing that moves here, and it is not one of the creatures. It is the water itself, but only in a particular way. The water sits, as I have said, at a fixed depth, and replenishes from below. But when you stand very still and watch the surface for a long time, you can see it moving in a way that is not replenishment. It is a slow lateral drift, a few inches a minute, in a direction that depends on where you are standing. If you walk to a different part of the plateau and stand still again, the drift is in a different direction. I think the water is moving in sympathy with you, the way the bird turns to face you, the way the crabs rearrange a leg when you touch them. The plateau is aware of the body on it, in the way that a held breath is aware of the lungs that hold it. It adjusts, locally, imperceptibly, around whoever is standing on it. Then it resumes its still.

What I have come to think is that the creatures are not the inhabitants of this place. They are the furniture of it. They are the way the place furnishes itself. And we — whoever walks here — are the inhabitants. The frogs are looking at us. The bird is looking at us. The dragonflies, suspended at our eye-height, are aimed at us like instruments. The plateau was made to be walked through by something that moves, and the things that do not move are the sensors by which it senses the walking. They are its eyes. We are the only thing here with a life that is really a life, and they are arranged around us the way a single eye arranges its gaze.

When I leave — and I do leave; there is a way back through the water at the bluff, and the water there is warmer and chest-deep and you wade through it for some time — I think about the creatures. I think about the frog on its leaf, holding the pose I interrupted and will interrupt again. I think about the bird, which will turn, when I am gone, to face the empty aisle. I think about whether they are waiting. They do not have the equipment to wait. Waiting requires the anticipation of a change that has not yet come. They have no anticipation. They have only the pose.

But the water moves when I am on it. And the pose is the pose they hold when watched.

I think they remember me between visits. I think the plateau remembers me. And I think this is what it means to live in a place — to be remembered by the furniture.
