The first voice of the place is the marmot's, and it comes from the scree at the foot of the north wall, in the morning, after the sun has cleared the eastern rim and the rime on the shadowed cliffs has begun to weep. It is a two-note call — the first note low and held, the second rising sharply, a kind of interrogation tossed out into the empty air — and when it bounces back from the cliffs it sounds like a question that has been answered by ten different voices, each one slightly out of step, so that for a moment the cirque seems full of marmots and in fact there are only two or three, sitting upright on their haunches among the broken stones with their paws held close against their chests and their small, dark eyes turned toward whatever it is they have decided to be alarmed by.

They are the size of large housecats but they look heavier than they are, because of the way they sit. They have a kind of permanent alertness, a watchfulness that is not anxious but is also not relaxed. They watch the way a person watches when they have been told that something is going to happen but they have not been told what or when. Their fur is the brown-gray of the rocks they sit on, and from a distance of more than a few paces they vanish into the scree entirely. You only know they are there because of the whistle. When the whistle stops, they drop down onto all fours and they are gone into the loose stones with a dry, scraping sound, and there is nothing left on the surface but the stones themselves.

The ibex come down in the early morning and again in the evening. They come in lines of three or four, sometimes two, and they pick their way along the ledges of the cirque walls with the casual certainty of creatures that have lived here so long that the place is in their hooves, in the way their weight transfers from one foothold to the next, in the angle at which they hold their heads. They do not look down. They do not need to look down. The ledges are in them the way a language is in a person who has spoken it since birth.

The males have the great curving horns, dark and ridged, that arc back over their shoulders and curve again at the tips, and they carry them as if they were an afterthought, something they happened to grow rather than something they spent their lives learning to manage. The females are smaller, hornless or nearly so, and they move with a different economy, more careful, more watchful of the kids, which they keep close and which move with the loose-limbed clumsiness of things that have only recently discovered that the world is made of edges.

When they pause on an outcrop, which they do often, they pause as if the outcrop had been put there for them to pause on. They look out across the cirque with the yellow indifference of their eyes — yellow, and slightly horizontal, the pupil a long black slit — and they chew, slowly, working their jaws sideways, and they look as if they are considering something they have already decided about. When they move on, they move on in a particular way, which is that they do not look back to see if you are following. They know you are not following. They know you cannot follow. They are up there and you are down here and the difference between those two things is the same as the difference between them and the place, which is that the place is theirs.

The fox is the color of the rocks. It is exactly the color of the rocks — that particular warm gray of the sun-flecked granite, with a slightly rusty wash along the flanks and a white tip to the tail that you would not see unless you knew it was there. It hunts the scree slopes and the margins of the tussock grass and the lower shelves of the cliff, and it moves with the long, unhurried lope of an animal that is not in a hurry because the place is small enough that anything that needs catching will eventually come to it.

You only see it when it moves. When it stops, it stops on a rock that is its own color, in a posture that mimics the angle of the stone, and it becomes the stone. Then it moves again, and the stone has a tail and is gone behind a tussock, and there is nothing.

It is not shy. That is the thing. It is not afraid of you. It is simply uninterested in you. You are a vertical object that arrived one day and may arrive again, and you do not fall into any of the categories it has been trained by its ancestors to care about, which are: things smaller than itself that move, and things larger than itself that move toward it. You do the second of those only rarely, and when you do, the fox regards you with the flat, amber appraisal of a creature that has already calculated how many paces it would need to put between itself and you, and has decided that it has them, and does not need to spend any of them yet.

It hunts the voles that live in the grass. The voles are everywhere, in runs that braid between the tussocks, and you can hear them sometimes, a faint rustling just under the surface of the grass, and the fox hears them much better than you do. When it pins one, it pins it with a single front paw, with a precision that is almost gentle, and it picks it up and carries it off to a place it has chosen, and eats it there, in private, the way a person might close a door.

The stoat, which is white now, in its winter coat, does not bother with the voles. The stoat is after larger things — the ptarmigan, the young marmots that have been foolish enough to wander from the burrow, the occasional rabbit if any rabbit has been foolish enough to come up this high. The stoat is small and it is fast and it is the color of the snow, and it crosses the snow at the margin of the tarn in a long, sinuous, unhurried line, and it disappears into a crack in the rock, and the crack closes behind it, and there is nothing. It does not leave tracks for long. The wind fills them in.

In the warmest pockets of the cirque, where the dwarf willows grow, the ptarmigan shelter. The willows are no taller than your knee, with round leaves the size of a thumbnail and a pale, papery bark, and there are places where they have grown so thick they form low, half-domed thickets. In summer the ptarmigan are the color of the mosses and the lichen, and they sit in the thickets and they do not move. In winter they are white, and they sit in the thickets and they do not move, and the snow piles in among them and stays long after the rest of the cirque has thawed. They are not timid. They are conserving. They have decided that the energy required to flee from you is greater than the energy required to hope that you do not see them, and they are usually right.

Where the vents warm the ground, and where the meltwater runs, the floor of the cirque has grown a kind of carpet — a low, thick, breathing thing of mosses so deep that your foot sinks into them to the ankle, and they exhale, when you step, a soft green smell of wet peat. Through the mosses, in summer, come the flowers. They are small. They are ferociously colored. The cold and the thin air and the unfiltered light have done something to them, and their blues are a blue you would not believe if you saw them on paper, and their yellows are almost orange, and there is a kind of saxifrage that grows in tight, hard cushions and is the exact shade of dried blood. The leaves of some of them are red, although they are not supposed to be red, and no one who comes here can quite explain why. The theory is the light. The theory is that up here the sun speaks a different language, and the plants have learned it.

Around these flowers, in the warm ground and the mosses, the smaller lives are concentrated. The flies hover there — flies of a kind you have not seen before, with bodies that look as if they have been dipped in copper — and they hover in a column that does not disperse. The column shifts slightly as the air shifts, and an individual fly moves from one place in it to another, but the column as a column stays where it is, marking, in the air, the place where the warmth is. They are patient in a way that flies in the valley below are not patient. They wait for what the vents bring up. They wait for what dies near the vents and is heated there.

There are beetles — small, black, glossy — that run the paths between the tussocks. The paths are many. They are worn into the cirque floor by hooves and by feet, and they braid between the rocks and the vents and the tussocks like the traces of water. The beetles run them with the seriousness of creatures that have somewhere to be, although you cannot tell what they are doing or where they are going. There is a moth, too, that is the color of the lichen and rests on the lichen with its wings spread, and disappears when you look at it. You can put your face within a handspan of it and you will not see it. It is gone. Then it moves, and you see it, and it is gone again.

Above all of this, in the air, the birds do their work.

The ravens ride the thermals that come up off the sun-warmed walls of the cirque in the middle of the day, when the stone has stored enough heat to make the air above it move, and they circle in those slow, considered wingbeats — not flapping, not gliding exactly, but something in between, a kind of controlled falling that happens to go upward — and they call down to you, hoarse and rolling, and the calls come from so far above that they seem to belong to a different place, a place that is mostly air and a little stone and where the rules of conversation are not the rules you are used to.

The ravens know everything that happens in the cirque. They are watching when the ibex comes down to drink at the tarn, and they are watching when the fox makes its kill, and they are watching when the lammergeier drops a bone from a great height onto the rocks below and comes down to collect the splinters. They wait for the fox to leave a carcass. They wait for the lammergeier to finish. They wait for the small deaths that happen at the margins of bigger deaths. They are patient in a way that is not like the patience of the ibex, which is the patience of things that have always been here. The ravens' patience is the patience of things that are waiting for something specific, and they will wait a long time, and they will not be sorry when it comes.

There is a smaller hawk, the color of slate, that hunts the marmots. It is not the lammergeier. It is a different shape entirely — short wings, long tail, the silhouette of something that has been designed for a particular kind of maneuver, the kind of maneuver that involves coming over the edge of a scree slope at speed and taking a marmot by surprise before the marmot can whistle. It does not always succeed. The marmots are fast, and they are close to their holes. But sometimes it succeeds, and when it does, the ravens come and they argue about the carcass in a series of hoarse, indignant calls that go on for hours.

The lammergeier does not hunt live things very often. It eats bones. It carries bones up into the air and it drops them onto the rocks, from a height that you can only estimate by the time it takes for the sound to come back to you — a faint, distant, percussive tap — and then it comes down, in a long, slow spiral, and it collects the splinters and it eats them. Its own bones are pale and polished from this work, and the bones it eats are pale and polished from this work, and the ledges at the foot of the cliffs are littered with them, and they look almost like flowers, the way the sun catches them.

The lammergeier is enormous. You do not often see it. When you do, it is at a distance, and it is high, and the size is something you have to infer rather than see. But when it passes low, when it passes below the rim of the cirque and into the basin, the air changes around it. The ravens stop calling. The hawk folds its wings and drops. The ibex lift their heads. The marmots, if any are out, vanish. The lammergeier is not a threat to any of these things, exactly, but it is a reminder — a slow, considered reminder — of the kind of place they live in.

There were people here, once. There are people here, sometimes, in the warm months, but they do not stay. They come up from the valley below, and they stay for a while, and they go down again before the snow comes. They leave things behind.

There is a shelter against the eastern wall. It is a low, half-circular wall of unmortared stone, open to the south, with a floor of flat stones and a hearth at its center. The hearth is a ring of stones that have been split, not cut, and the splits are clean and sharp, and the smoke-black on their inner faces is old but not ancient. Beside the shelter, half-buried in moss, there is a piece of worked antler — a tine, sharpened at one end, the other end shaped into a kind of wedge. Whoever used it did not take it with them.

There is a cairn the height of a man's knee. There is a line of stones that runs for a few paces and then stops and then begins again, as if the line had lost its purpose and tried to remember it. There is a hollow in the rock that has been polished smooth by use, the polish of many hands over many years, and the hollow is the exact shape of a cupped palm, and if you put your hand into it, it fits.

The people did not stay. They came in the warm months, and they went down again. They left behind them only the things that the wind and the water and the plants could not carry away. The pin — the copper pin, bent but still whole — has been in the moss for so long that the moss has grown over it, and in another fifty years it will not be there at all. The knife, the short leaf-shaped blade of dark iron, has its tang wrapped in something that was once hide and is now nothing, and the iron itself has begun, in the places where the moss does not cover it, to go back to the color of the rocks.

But the hollow in the rock — the cupped-palm hollow — that is not going anywhere. That has been there a long time. That was there before the people who came most recently, and it will be there after, and it is the exact shape of a hand, and if you put your hand into it, it fits, and you understand, in that moment, that the hand that was here before yours was not so different from yours, and that it held itself open, here, against the cold, for a long time.

And now the whole.

The place is full of negotiations. The cold and the heat sit side by side and do not negotiate with each other. The fox and the raven negotiate over a carcass, and the negotiation is conducted in movements that do not make noise. The ibex and the grass negotiate the question of which parts of the grass are to be eaten and which are to be left, and the grass does not have a vote. The marmots and the hawk negotiate the question of how close is too close, and the negotiation is conducted entirely in velocity. The lammergeier waits, and its waiting is not a negotiation but a certainty. The vents breathe, and the breathing is not a negotiation but a fact. The tarn holds its mirror, and the holding is not a negotiation but a stillness.

And the place, which is the sum of all of these negotiations and facts and stillnesses, watches you. It does not watch you unkindly. It watches you the way a place watches someone who has come a long way to be in it — with attention, and with a kind of patience, and with the willingness to see what you will do next.
