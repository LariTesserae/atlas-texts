What lives here is not a creature. It is an arrangement.

It is the water, first, that lives here. The water is the longest tenant and the most thorough. It arrived in some year that did not matter and it has been paying rent ever since, filling the chamber to the brim of its own geometry, lapping at the ragged ceiling-edge where the men who cut the stone left off. The water does not feed. It does not breed. It does not behave as a body behaves. It only *is*, in the way that weather is, in the way that a held chord is. Where it moves — and it moves so little that to call it movement is to be generous — it is in conversation with the limestone walls and the air above, trading fractions of a degree, trading dissolved gases, negotiating the small difference between a deep pool in a quarry and a cup of water left on a table. It has done this for a long time. It will do it for a long time yet. It is patient the way an ocean is patient, only smaller.

On the metal things, there is a skin.

The paint that was once green on the motor housing has gone the way paint goes when there is no one to mind it. What was sealed is now flaking, in long strips the shape of fingernails, peeling back from the cooling fins to show the iron beneath, which is itself going the way iron goes. The rust here is not the bright orange rust of a freshly cut pipe; it is the dark, almost plum-brown rust of long acquaintance with water, of iron that has been giving up its iron for years and is no longer in a hurry about it. This rust is layered. If you lifted a flake it would come away in three or four thinner flakes beneath, and beneath those still more, and at the bottom of them all would be a soft orange powder that would disperse in your fingers like cocoa. The rust has climbed up the brackets that hold the pipes to the wall. It has eaten the brackets at the waterline, where the air touches the water touches the metal, and left the brackets above the waterline a different color from those below. There is a tide-mark, here, but it is not a tide-mark of water. It is a tide-mark of oxygen, of wetness, of the slow exchange between what is wet and what is not. The rust lives along that line, like a moss, like a lichen, like a thing that has chosen the boundary.

On the cooling fins of the pump, there is growth.

It is thin. It is the kind of growth that does not call attention to itself, because it has not been given the chance. There is a film of green on the metal — not the green of healthy algae in a sunny pond, but the dim, almost olive green of algae that has learned to work with what arrives. A few strands of something darker, hair-fine, hang from the fins and wave in currents that are too slight to feel. They wave because the water moves; the water moves because the air above moves; the air moves because the day is happening, somewhere, in a place that is not here. These strands are the closest thing in the chamber to a creature. They are the most alive. They do not eat the iron. They only rest against it, and gather a little light, and grow as much as a thing can grow in a place this dim, and then they grow no more.

On the floor, there is silt.

The silt is the digested. It is everything that was once something finer — a flake of paint, a spore, a particle of limestone grit, a fleck of rust so small it would float — that has settled, over the slow accumulation of seasons, into a soft, pale, marl-like layer on the concrete plinths and the stone floor. The silt is a few inches deep around the bases of the machines. It is, in its way, the most actively growing thing here, in that it is always very slightly deeper than it was, and it is composed of the things above it. It is a slow mouth at the bottom of the room, taking the chamber into itself, particle by particle. A wrench left here, in a hundred years, would be a humped shape under the silt, the silt the color of the wrench.

In the silt, there is nothing moving.

This is the strange fact of the place. There are no fish, because fish have not found the way down. There are no insect larvae, because insects do not lay their eggs in water without a way in from above, and the way in is a vertical hole in the ground that is, by some accident of geology or design, too small or too deep or too uninviting to be discovered by the usual colonizers. There are no snails grazing the walls, no worms threading the silt, no small crustaceans picking over the rust. The water is not dead — it is moving, after its fashion, and it holds dissolved oxygen, and it would feed something if something could arrive. But nothing has. The chamber is a kind of sealed envelope in this respect. The door is open — the opening in the ceiling is open, the air is open, the water is open to the rain — and yet nothing has used the door. The chamber is a place that life has walked past. A room left in a hotel that no traveler has checked into, with the bed made.

The machines do not live, but they behave as if they were living.

The hanging pipe, the one at the tired angle, breathes. As the day warms above and the air in the pipe warms with it, the metal expands; as the evening cools and the air cools, it contracts. The pipe, suspended at one end, cannot expand evenly, and so it moves — a creak, a groan, a faint, almost sub-audible tick. The pipe has a day, in this sense. It has a morning and an evening. It speaks once, or twice, or not at all, depending on the weather above and the temperature of the water and the small geometry of how it is wedged against its neighbor. The pipe is a clock, but a clock that no one is reading, that strikes at intervals no one has scheduled.

The dials on the control panel are stopped, but they are stopped in particular places. One says a number. Another says another. A third has fallen to zero, or was at zero when the current stopped, and sits there with its needle pointing at the floor of its face like a compass that has lost the north. These are not living things, but they are records. They are the place's memory of what it was, frozen mid-sentence. The control panel is the only thing in the chamber that has not been allowed to forget. Everything else has been permitted to become its new self, the slow self, the underwater self. The panel insists on the old self. The needle at 4.2 bar is a small act of remembrance, performed over and over by the inaction of a spring.

The light, too, has a kind of life.

It comes through the opening in the ceiling and passes down through the water, and as it does it is bent, scattered, dispersed into the soft green of the place. The light is older when it reaches the floor than when it entered. It has given up some of its blue. It has been filtered of its red. It is a temperate light, a softened light, the light of a room with curtains. The bright lattice on the floor  the caustic net of refracted sun, cast by the moving surface above — shifts and breathes and never repeats. It is the most active thing in the chamber. It crawls over the silt, over the plinth, over the rust, over the open flange of the pump. It is the only thing that does not settle. Everything else has come to rest. The light is still arriving, still departing, still on its way somewhere.

What lives here, then, is the state of being after living.

The rust is the slow fire of iron, going on for years. The silt is the slow eating of the room by itself. The growth on the fins is the slow answering of algae to whatever scarce invitation the water extends. The creak of the pipe is the slow breathing of a thing that has no lungs. The dials are the slow forgetting of a thing that will not quite forget. The light is the slow traveling of something that started far away and ends here, for a moment, on a patch of silt in a flooded chamber in an old quarry, before it is gone.

It is not a thriving place. It is not even a place. It is a register, an account, a ledger in which the entries are the slow withdrawals of oxygen from iron, of light from the sun, of seconds from the cooling of metal, of the falling of one drop from the lip of something overhead into the water, where it lands with a single note, and is quiet, and then is quiet, and then is quiet again.
