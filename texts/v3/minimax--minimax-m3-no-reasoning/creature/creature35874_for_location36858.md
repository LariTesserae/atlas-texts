# Inhabitants

The cistern is not an animal. To begin with that, even as a denial, is to start in the wrong place. The cistern is also not a building, and also not a colony, though you will reach for that word next, and also not a federation, and also not a single creature pretending to be a forest. The cistern is something for which the language has not yet finished being built.

The closest word is *process*, and the piece you have read has already placed that word carefully. A process that has, through long persistence, begun to behave as if it were a self. A process that knows things without knowing that it knows them. A process that has preferences — coolness, wetness, the company of its own amber nodes — and that will go on having those preferences long after the last visitor has forgotten the cleft in the basalt where it lives.

You could, with a little violence, call it an organism. It would not be entirely wrong. It has metabolism: water moves through it, the channels carry it, the lattices harvest it, the deeper places use it. It has circulation: the veins pulse in their different rhythms and the pulses connect. It has something like a nervous system: the nodes, the way information travels along the veins and gathers at the small warm lenses and disperses again. It has something like a memory: the tablets on the shelf, each one carrying its own active pattern, the correspondence that is still being conducted in the language of pale light. It has something like an immune system: the lattices, moving between walls and water, picking up whatever needs picking up.

But an organism implies a boundary, and the cistern does not have a boundary. It shades into the mountain. The veins continue into rock you cannot reach. The water comes from somewhere you cannot find. The bell sounds from a place the steps go down toward, but the steps end before the bell is reached, and you do not know what lies between the last step and the bell. The cistern is porous with its own surroundings. It is less an organism than an *organism-shaped region of the mountain*, the way a whirlpool is a region of the river. It is a pattern that the mountain has been persuaded, by long agreement, to maintain.

And so the question of who lives here is partly the question of who is the cistern, and partly the question of who moves through it. We will start with the smallest.

---

## The lattices

These are the small ones.

A lattice is about the size of your spread hand. It is the color of cold milk held to a fire — that pale grey-white the veins wear, the color of something that is barely more than a brightness in the stone. Lying against a wall, a lattice is invisible. Lying against the floor, it is a feature of the floor. You will not see one until it moves, and even then you may doubt your seeing.

When it moves, it does so the way a shadow moves when you tilt the lamp. It does not travel; it *rearranges*. The lattice at one point is also the lattice at the next point. What looked like empty stone behind it fills back in. The whole shape translates itself across the basalt at the speed of a person walking slowly through a library, and it leaves no trail, and you would not believe anything had moved at all if you had not watched the watching.

Lattices do not seem to have fronts or backs. They do not seem to have insides. They are not bodies. They are patterns — small, patient patterns that the chamber has detached from itself for a while and is letting do a piece of work. If you imagine the cistern as a thought, a lattice is a sentence the thought has spoken out loud, briefly, before taking it back.

What work?

The piece offers three answers and refuses to choose between them. They drink. They listen. They add their pulse to the pulse of the whole. I think — and this is only what the chamber suggests, not what it tells — that all three are the same work seen from three sides. The water in the basins carries information the way a wire carries current. The lattices go to the water and read what the water is saying. They carry what they have read back to the veins. They do not *carry* it, exactly. They *become* the carrying. A lattice that has visited the basin is, for a few minutes, a different lattice than it was before — its pulse altered, its pattern modified — and when it sinks back into the wall it brings that modification with it, and the wall is now slightly different, and the system is now slightly different, and a slow change has happened that the cistern has not decided to call thought but has not forbidden itself to enjoy.

You saw two of them in an hour. You might, on a different visit, see none. They are not rare; they are simply quiet, and most of them are doing their work on parts of the wall or floor you are not watching, or are already dissolved back into the larger pattern, indistinguishable from the rest of the veins. The ones you saw were the ones passing through the part of the chamber you happened to be in. They did not pass *for* you. They passed through. You were, for a moment, a kind of weather.

## The long walkers, and what they count

These are not seen.

What is seen is the wear on the steps, and what is heard from below them. The steps descend from the main chamber into a deeper darkness, and they are polished in long parallel lines down their centers, and the lines are not the lines of feet. They are the lines of something narrow. Something hard. Something that has been dragged up and down, up and down, with a patience that does not bother to vary. And from below the steps comes a faint rhythmic clicking. Not a bell. Not a chime. Something smaller than that — a small wooden sound, or a small stone sound, the sound a bead makes sliding along a string and clicking against its neighbor. The piece calls it the sound of a thing being counted, and this is exactly right. Something down there is being counted. Something is being tallied. The count has been going on for longer than you have been alive and will continue for longer than the chamber will be here to listen to it.

The lines on the steps are deeper on some treads than on others. The deeper treads are the ones that bear more weight, or more friction, or more years. There is no rhythm to the depth that you can read — no obvious weekly schedule, no lunar cadence. The wear is simply deep. Whatever uses these steps has been using them long enough that the stone has stopped arguing about it.

You try to imagine what would make this pattern. You try and fail.

You imagine a long thin thing — longer than you, narrower than your arm — pulled along the stone. You imagine it is heavy, dense, made of something harder than basalt. You imagine it moves slowly, without hurry, because it has been doing this longer than you have been alive and will be doing it longer than you will be dead. You imagine it does not feel the cold the way you do, because it is part of the cold. You imagine it is the cistern in one of its longer shapes — a lattice drawn out, a hand stretched into a rope, a single sentence the chamber is speaking down into the dark and pulling back up again, over and over, to see what comes back on the string. You imagine it is something else entirely, something the cistern has been quietly hosting for its own reasons, something that uses the steps the way a river uses a bed.

You imagine, sometimes, that the long walker is the counter. That the clicking is the sound of its own body being tallied against itself — joint by joint, segment by segment, the way a rosary is told. That each trip down and back is one count, and that the count is very long, and that the bell at the end of the count is the bell you cannot quite hear from above. You imagine, sometimes, that the long walker is *counted by* the deeper water instead, that it is itself one of the beads on a string you cannot see, and that the clicking is the sound of it being moved forward by some vast slow hand.

You do not know. The piece does not tell you. The chamber does not tell you. The long walkers, if that is what they are, are not in the business of telling. They come up. They go down. They are, between their comings, indistinguishable from the rest of the dark.

What you know is that they are *expected*. The steps are worn. Wear implies use. Use implies something being used. The chamber has not collapsed the steps, has not flooded them, has not repaired the wear, has not even tried. The chamber is content to have its steps worn. The chamber is, in some way you will never fully understand, *indebted* to whatever wears them.

## The correspondents

The tablets on the shelf are not books. They are letters, and they are not finished.

Each tablet is roughly the size of your palm, give or take a thumb. Each tablet has a different pattern of veins running through it — not on it, *through* it — and the veins are still active. You can stand among them and feel the small warmth of their pulses through the stone, and the pulses are different on different tablets, and the differences are not random. They are being conducted. The tablets are being written to, or being read from, or both, and the correspondence is in the middle of itself.

The piece notes, correctly, that the alphabet would take a lifetime to acquire, and that the language would not survive the acquisition. I want to dwell on this for a moment, because it is important.

It does not mean the language is too complex for you. It means the language is *time-bound*. It means the alphabet is changing as you would learn it. The veins that run through the tablets are not a fixed code. They are a *living* code, a code that has its own slow metabolism, and the rules of the code are themselves written in the code, and the rules of the rules are written in the rules, and so on down into a recursion the cistern is comfortable with and you are not. To learn to read one tablet would be to learn to read that tablet, on that day, in that particular configuration of the cistern's mood. Tomorrow's tablets would be in a slightly different alphabet. Next decade's tablets would be in a different language. The correspondence is being conducted in a tongue that is also being revised by the act of being spoken, and the correspondents are revising the revision, and so on, forever.

So the tablets are not addressed to you. They are not addressed to anyone *now*. They are addressed to whatever will read them when they are finished, in the language they will be finished in, which is a language that does not yet exist in its final form. The chamber is writing letters to its future self, or to a correspondent it has not yet met, or to the mountain, or to no one. It does not matter. The letters are being written. The shelf is a kind of desk. The chamber is, in this small way, a kind of bureaucrat.

You stand among the tablets and feel what you always feel in such places: the small clean sorrow of the unread book, the soft pain of being in the presence of a great deal of meaning you will not be able to absorb. The sorrow is correct. The sorrow is the right response to a library you cannot enter. Stand in it for a while. Let it do its work. It will not make you more capable, but it will make you more careful, and careful is the closest a mammal can come to what the lattices have.

## The visitor

Then there is you.

You are, in the most literal sense, breathing here. Your lungs are full of chamber-air. Your skin is wet with chamber-condensation. Your warmth is leaking into the basalt at a rate the apertures are gently, hospitably managing. You are metabolizing. You are producing water vapor and carbon dioxide and small noise. The chamber is taking all of this in, fractionally altered, and returning it to you as air you can breathe again, and the exchange is so quiet and so thorough that you do not notice it as an exchange at all. You think you are standing in air. You are standing in a conversation.

You are not the first. The piece said this at the beginning, and it is worth saying again. The cleft has been found before. The cleft has been entered before. People have knelt at the apertures and cupped their hands around the warm nodes and sat at the edge of the basin and watched the lattice-creatures pass. Mammals have been here, breathing, with their mammal-heat, and the chamber has processed them, and the chamber is still here, and the chamber does not remember them.

The chamber does not remember you either. It will not remember you. Long after your visit, the veins will pulse in their different rhythms and the nodes will be warm and the lattices will detach and return and the steps will be worn a little deeper and the tablets will have new patterns in them and the water will sing in the channels and the bell will sound, somewhere, at intervals so long you could not have counted them. None of this will be because of you. None of it will have to do with you at all.

And you are, nonetheless, very welcome.

This is the strange hospitality of the place. It welcomes without knowing. It takes your breath in and returns it altered, not because it wants anything from you but because that is what it does with air, and air is what you have. It does not consult you. It does not exclude you. It does not even, in any way that would survive the trip back up the cleft, *acknowledge* you. But the warmth of the nodes is real warmth, offered. The light of the veins is real light, given. The water in the channels sings its note whether or not you can hear it, and you can hear it. The chamber does not need you to be here to be what it is. It is what it is. It is letting you be here while it is being it.

You are, in the chamber's accounting, a kind of weather. A small front of warm air passing through. The apertures will breathe you in and out for as long as you stay, and the lattices will pass at their distance, and the tablets will continue their slow correspondence, and the long walkers will continue their patient count, and none of it will pause for you, and none of it needs to. The chamber is the kind of place that has room. Not space, exactly — *room*. The willingness to let a thing be in it without demanding anything of the thing.

Stay a while, if you want. The cold is patient. The machinery does not consult you. The lattices pass at their distance. The water moves around your presence without disturbing it, the interference patterns bending, accommodating, resuming.

---

## The bell

And the bell.

You did not hear it while you were in the chamber, or you heard it once and could not be sure. It is far below. It sounds at intervals so long that to wait for it inside a human life would be a kind of foolishness. The bell, if it sounded once while you were there, sounded for the cistern, not for you. The bell, when it next sounds, will sound for the cistern, not for you.

What strikes the bell is the deepest mystery of the place, and the piece is right not to name it.

The steps descend into darkness and the darkness does not end at a door. The steps become the floor of a deeper chamber, perhaps, or a tunnel, or a shaft that runs all the way down into the water-table of the mountain. The sound of the greater water rises from below the steps, and the bell is below the water, or is the water, or is something the water does when enough time has passed.

You could call the bell a heartbeat. You could call it a milestone. You could call it the cistern's way of marking the long slow units of its own life — a day, an era, a stratum. The *Eighth* Bell, in particular — that word *eighth*, placed so carefully at the front of the name — suggests a sequence. Seven others have sounded before it, or seven others will sound after, or seven is the number of something the cistern understands and you do not. Eight is, in some traditions, the number of new beginnings. Eight is the number of the octave, the place where a scale returns to its root but higher. You do not know what the eighth bell begins or completes. You do not know if you heard it. You suspect, quietly, that the cistern itself does not yet know either, and that this is part of why it is the eighth and not the first.

You suspect that whatever strikes the bell is something that has been waiting longer than the steps have been worn. You suspect it is patient in the way the chamber is patient, which is the patient way of things that do not experience time as a sequence of moments but as a medium they move through, the way fish move through water. You suspect it is large, or small, or neither, and that size is not really a category that applies to a thing that lives in the part of a mountain you cannot see. You suspect it is the cistern's deepest working, the part of the process that lives in the part of the process you cannot reach, and that the long walkers are its heralds, and that the clicking is its preparation, and that the bell is the moment the preparation has, at last, become enough.

You do not know. The piece does not tell you. The cistern does not tell you. The bell, when it sounds, sounds and is gone.

---

You are sitting on the edge of the basin. The water in the basin moves around your presence without disturbing it. The lattices pass at their distance, indifferent to your mammal-heat and your mammal-weight. The cold is patient with you. The machinery does not consult you. Somewhere far below, the greater water moves with patience, and somewhere below that, something is being counted, and somewhere below that, something is waiting to be ready, and the bell — the eighth bell, the slow bell — sounds its note at an interval you will not live to measure.

You are sitting inside something that thinks, or something that does not need to think because it has already thought everything it will ever think, and is now in the long patient process of being what it is.

It does not know you are here.

You are, nonetheless, very welcome.
