# What Lives There

A garden left half-made and learning how to be wilderness, kept by creatures who have never been hurried and do not know how.

## The Gardeners Who Are Not Here

The place speaks of a maker, and the maker is absent. You can feel them in the spacing of things — in the ferns set at intervals precise enough to read as intention, in the ponds whose stone curbs still hover just beneath the moss like bones beneath skin, in the places where young trees were staked and the stakes have been swallowed by the thickening of the bark. Whoever they were, they worked to a plan. They laid out ribs of stone across a raw high place and decided where the green should go and where the water should be allowed to gather, and then — they stepped back, or were pulled away, or simply reached the edge of their intention and stopped.

The edge is still sharp. You can walk to it. The flagstones end, and after that, the ribs of stone rise out of the grey clay in long pale lines, bare, unplanted, waiting for someone to make a decision about them that is not coming. The makers are not absent the way dead people are absent. They are absent the way an interrupted sentence is absent — the grammar still tense with what was about to be said.

What they wanted is legible. What they did not finish is also legible. The two together make the place.

## The Moss

The moss is the closest thing to a citizen. It is everywhere, a continuous green skin stretched over every horizontal surface, every stone lip, every exposed root. It gives under your foot the way a lung gives under a hand — slowly, with a kind of held patience — and it closes behind you when you step off, and you cannot find your footprint an hour later. It does not hurry because it has nowhere to hurry toward. It is doing what it has always done, which is to spread, and it has been doing it here since the moment the first fern was set down and someone walked away.

It has outlasted the stakes. It will outlast the trunks it climbs. It is patient in a way that patience does not usually look like — not tense, not waiting, just present, in the way that a color is present, in the way that a temperature is present. If you sat down on it and stayed, it would grow over you in time. It does not intend this. It would simply not notice.

## The Ferns

The ferns are the most obviously arranged thing in the garden, and also the most obviously alive. They sit at their intervals like punctuation — each one uncurling in the same slow choreography, each new frond furred with the same bronze down that catches the sourceless light. They were set out by someone, and they have been doing as they liked ever since, and what they liked, it turns out, was to become enormous.

Some of them are the size of doors. Some are larger. Their crosiers still rise in the morning of their unfurling, tight brown fists opening into long ladders of green that hang downward in the wet air. They do not flower. They do not seed visibly. They simply continue to uncurl, year after year, at a speed below the threshold of a watching eye. If you returned in a hundred years, the one you saw today would be only fractionally larger. If you returned in a thousand, you would notice.

They are the slowest firework in the world, and they have been set off already, and no one is watching.

## The Trees

The trees are old even when they are young. They have the layered branching of something that has had a long time to decide what shape to be, and they grip the new stone with roots that look like hands — like the hands of the makers, perhaps, finally at rest, finally allowed to hold something without letting go.

They lean. Slowly. You cannot see it. A degree in a thousand years, the place says, and you believe it, because when you stand beneath one and look up, you can feel the slight off-axis pull of it, the faint and ancient tilt of a trunk that has been thickening for longer than the moss has been growing on its bark. They creak as they adjust — a low groan that travels down into the stone and up into the wet air — and the sound is so deep and so continuous that you stop hearing it after a while and start feeling it in your sternum instead.

They do not hurry. They have made their peace with outliving the ground they stand on, which is new, which is unfinished, which will outlast them anyway.

## The Orchids

The orchids live on bark, as orchids do, but here they live on bark that is itself climbing toward the light, and so they ride upward at the speed of bark. They are pale, fleshy, the color of a mouth. They smell of wet dust and faint rot and something else that is not quite a smell — more like a memory of sweetness, the way a word heard through water arrives at you already half-dissolved.

They do not attract anything. There are no insects here to pollinate them, and the place does not seem to mind. They bloom anyway, in their slow unvisited way, and the petals fall onto the moss and are eaten by the moss and become part of the moss, which becomes part of the air, which becomes part of the next petal. The orchids are not unhappy about this. They are orchids.

## The Frog

The frog is the size of a small melon and has been here for years. It sits in the exact center of a tiny pool on a wet stone, watching you with copper eyes that do not blink and are not afraid and are not tame. It is just present, in the way a stone is present, in the way the water is present. It has made its peace with you standing there. It has made its peace with you leaving. It will be here after.

It does not move. It does not need to move. It has eaten, or it has not eaten. It has mated, or it has not mated. Either way, it is here, and the pool is here, and the stone is here, and the moss is creeping toward the edge of the stone at a speed that makes glaciers look impulsive. It is the most complete thing in the garden.

## The Lizard

The lizard is on a fern. It is the exact temperature of the fern. It is slate-colored, which is the color of the stone under the moss, which is the color of the bark of the trees, which is the color of the light. If you looked away and looked back you would not be sure it had ever been there. It does not perform being an animal. It is simply the next sentence in a paragraph that is mostly stone and plant and water, and it has been written in the same ink.

## The Falling Feathers

Something lives in the canopy that you do not see. You only know it is there because, every so often, a slow falling feather comes down through all the layers — barred and grey, too large for any bird you can name — and lands on your shoulder or in your palm, and is warm, and is gone if you look at it directly.

It is not a bird. Or it is not only a bird. It is something with feathers, and it lives so far up in the layered green that to see it you would have to climb, and the place does not encourage climbing. It drops a feather, and the feather falls for a long time, and lands, and you hold it, and you think about what it is, and by the time you have thought about it, the canopy has closed over the spot it fell from and there is no proof of anything.

## The Many-Legged Things

In the wet moss, things with too many legs move too fast to be looked at. You see them only as a displacement — a slight movement in the surface of the moss that closes over behind them as they go down — and then they are gone, into the dark wet world beneath the green skin where the stone is and the roots drink and nothing is watched.

They are the only fast things here. They are fast the way a spark is fast — a brief bright disturbance in a long stillness. They do their small urgent business and they go back into the ground and the moss closes over the hole they made and forgets them, and they are content to be forgotten. They are the secret keepers of the garden. They know what the roots know. They are not telling.

## The Air

The air is the slowest and largest animal in the place. It moves. It does not seem to move, but if you stand very still for a long time you can feel it shifting around you, the way you can feel a tide if you stand in one place long enough. It is full of water vapor. Every breath you take is like drinking. It tastes of iron, and of crushed green stems, and of something almost sweet that does not have a name.

It is also the sound. The constant low roar that you feel in your sternum — the wind through a conifer forest that is not wind, the breathing of the canopy at a frequency below sight — that is the air, working. It carries water up to the leaves and water down from the leaves and the smell of orchids from where the orchids are to where you are. It is the slowest circulatory system in the world. It is also the patient one.

## The Makers, Still

And the makers are here, in a way. They are here in the spacing of the ferns and the curve of the pond edges and the stake-swallowing of the trees. They are here in the unfinished edge where the flagstones end and the raw stone begins. They are here as a shape that was almost completed and then was not, and the garden is still being completed by everything in it, slowly, in their absence, along the lines they laid down.

You stand on the threshold between the made thing and the unmade thing and the moss gives under your weight and the frog has not blinked and somewhere a feather is falling.
