What lives here is what has stopped needing to.

Begin with the *absence* and let it populate itself. The hollow does not host creatures so much as it shelters the conditions under which a creature might be persuaded to forget it is a creature. There is no community here, no food web with its little dramas of chase and consumption. What lives here instead is a kind of patience that has taken physical form.

The walls are not, in the usual sense, alive — and yet something in them is awake. The iron in the sandstone is patient the way a long memory is patient. It is doing a work so slow it does not need a name. If you could compress the hollow's hour into a second and watch the way the stone moves, you would see the grains rearranging themselves in a slow, ongoing conversation. The rock is not static. It is *settled*, which is a different thing. A settled thing is one that has finished its arguments. The dark stone here has been arguing with itself for so long that the argument has become a kind of rest. If the rock is alive, it is alive the way a sleeping animal is alive — present, breathing in its own way, not requiring anything, not offering anything, simply *continuing* with a minimum of fuss. I would not say the walls are conscious. I would say they are not entirely unconscious either. They have a quality of attention without an attendant.

The sand on the floor is the same substance, with the negotiation slightly less resolved. Each grain is in slow conversation with the grains around it, and from time to time one of them loses its place and slides a little, and there is the soft tick you noticed. This is not quite life. But it is also not quite the absence of life. It is a kind of low, ongoing adjustment that the body understands even if the mind cannot categorize it. You feel, standing on this sand, that you are standing on something that is still in the middle of becoming what it will be. The sand is younger than the walls, and slightly less tired. It is the only part of the hollow that still has any small ambition, and even that ambition is just to be deeper.

The shrub near the mouth is the closest thing to an animal the hollow contains, and even it is half a stone. *Grayia* or something like her — one of those small, durable plants that have made a treaty with aridity so old that both parties have forgotten what the original dispute was about. The leaves are small because large leaves would be a confession of need. They are waxy because the air, even here, will take what moisture it is offered. The shrub does not so much grow as *persist*. Year by year it adds a fraction of a millimeter to itself, and the fraction is so small that to call it growth is to use the word loosely. It is more accurate to say the shrub is a slow accumulation of decisions to continue. Each leaf is a renewed contract. The plant is not so much alive as *unwilling to be otherwise*. If you sat beside it for a season, you would not see it change. If you sat beside it for a decade, you would see, if you were very honest, that it had thickened almost imperceptibly, the way a person's hand thickens over a lifetime of small grips. The shrub is, in a real sense, the slowest hand in the hollow, still holding on to its small crack in the rim.

The pale tufts against the deeper walls are harder. They are not lichen, because lichen is a negotiation between two living things and there is not enough negotiation here. They are not grass, because grass implies a season and these have no seasons. They are something more like a memory of growth, a few pale threads that have not yet decided whether they are part of the wall or separate from it. I have seen similar growths in tombs that have been closed for centuries, in cellars no one enters, in the cold parts of caves where the light comes only as an idea. They are the things that grow where growing has been reduced to a rumor. They do not photosynthesize in any meaningful way. They do not need to. They simply *are*, in the way a watermark is, or a tide line, or the slight discoloration at the edge of a stone that has rested for a long time in the same position. If they are alive, their aliveness is barely a suggestion, the way a voice heard through many walls is barely a voice. I would not grieve for them if they stopped, but I would also not be certain they had stopped. They exist at the threshold where a thing can be either alive or mineral, and have made no commitment.

The marks on the wall are not, now, inhabited. But they are not entirely empty either. They are the residue of a body that was here once, and a body leaves a kind of weather. Not a ghost — the hollow has no patience for ghosts, would refuse them at the threshold along with the cold — but a *shape* in the air, a slight compression of possibility, the way a path is a compression of possibility. The maker of the marks did not stay long enough to leave a haunting. But the strokes on the wall are still, in a faint way, the strokes of a hand. The hand is gone. The intention is gone. The fact of the gesture remains, the way a fossil remains — not the animal, but the evidence that an animal was here, and that the animal was the kind of thing that made gestures. The hollow holds these marks the way it holds heat: gently, without conviction, ready to let them go when the next long patience comes for them.

The air itself is the closest resident to a living thing. It moves so little that it has, over the long stretch, become a kind of slow fluid, denser than the air outside, accustomed to the walls, taking the shape of the hollow as its own. It is warm in the way that body temperature is warm, but it is not body temperature; it is the temperature of a place that has had a long time to decide what temperature it prefers. It has, in its slow way, *settled*. When you step into it, you are stepping into a medium that has been undisturbed for so long that your presence is an event the air will spend the rest of the day metabolizing. You do not so much enter the hollow as disturb it, and the hollow forgives the disturbance by the simple expedient of forgetting it.

There are no insects, because insects require a project, and there is no project here. There are no spiders, because spiders require architecture, and there is no architecture here. There are no microbes in any great number, because microbes require a transaction, and there is no transaction here. The hollow is, microbiologically, almost empty, which is its own kind of fullness. A place with no bacteria in its soil is a place that has stopped being in conversation with the rest of the biosphere. It is, in the small, what a dead planet would be: a place where the long chemical patience of stone has reasserted itself over the busy short-term chemistry of life.

And yet — and this is the strangest thing to say about the hollow — something *attends*. I do not mean anything watches. I mean the hollow itself has a quality of being-in-itself that is, if not alive, then at least not inert. The way the light lies in its long slanted shape and does not move. The way the silence has textures. The way the sand tick sounds louder than it is. These are not properties of an empty room. They are properties of a room that has had a long time to develop its own weather, its own small habits, its own particular way of being a place. The hollow has, over the long centuries, become *itself* in a way that most places never manage. Most rooms are containers for the things in them. This hollow is a container for itself. The shrub is incidental. The marks are incidental. The hollow is the thing, and the hollow is, in some low, mineral, patient way, *present*.

I would not call that life. But I would not call it the opposite of life either. It is the thing that life eventually becomes when it has been still long enough, and the thing that was probably here before life arrived and will probably be here after life departs. It is the patience that the world is made of, the long rest between events, the held breath that the opening of the hollow was made to resemble.

If you stayed here a long time — longer than anyone has stayed, I think, including the maker of the marks — you would begin to feel that you were not so much visiting the hollow as being *visited* by it. The hollow would, slowly, the way the sand rises, take you in. Not consume you. Not change you. Just *include* you, the way it includes the shrub and the tufts and the marks and the slow falling of grains. You would become, after a long enough time, one more patient thing in a place where patience is the only ethic.

This is what lives here: the patience itself.
