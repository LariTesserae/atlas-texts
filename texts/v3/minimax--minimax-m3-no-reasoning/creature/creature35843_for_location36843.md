# The Inheritors of the Loop

They were born in the roar. They die in the roar. In between, they work in the roar, their entire lives a sustained negotiation with a sound that would deafen anyone else to madness, but which has, over generations, become less a sound than a kind of weather. They have stopped hearing it the way you have stopped hearing your own pulse. It is only when a visitor comes — a surveyor, a clerk, a lost scholar from the institutions above — that they remember the noise is there, by watching the visitor's face go white and slack with the effort of holding themselves inside it.

## The Tenders

There are perhaps sixty of them at any given time, though no one has counted in living memory, because counting implies a boundary and the tenders do not have boundaries. They are not employees. They are not a guild. They are a population, in the way a population of lichen or mussels is a population — a thing that has accreted to fit the contours of the niche it occupies.

They live on the engine-houses. The four squat iron structures that rise from the basin on their crusty legs are their villages, their townships, their world. Each engine-house has a name that is not quite a name: *First Cold*, *The Pulley Sibling*, *The One That Coughs*, *Salt-Wife*. The names are passed down. The names are the only history they have.

A tender is born in a low-ceilinged room that smells of copper and sleep. As an infant, the sound of the engines is the sound of the mother's chest, indistinguishable from the heartbeat. They learn to walk on iron decks slick with condensation. They learn to read the gauges before they learn to read anything else — the trembling copper needles that tell them how much pressure is in the system, how much water is moving, how much heat is in the bellies of the machines. A child of four can tell you, by listening to the rhythm of a piston, whether a bearing is going dry three chambers away. A child of seven can grease a flywheel by feel in the dark.

They are not large people. Generations of iron-poor diets and cold-water immersion have made them compact, wide-shouldered, low-centered. Their hands are broad and rough and permanently stained with the black tar that oozes from the lower seams. Their eyes are pale and wide-set, adapted over centuries to the grey-green dim — they see better in the diffused light than any outsider, and they squint painfully in true sunlight, of which they have heard but mostly disbelieve. They have a tendency, visitors note, to tilt their heads slightly when listening, as if the whole skull were an ear.

They speak a language that is technically five languages, or maybe six, layered like the rust on the walls. It has the cadence of an old river-trading tongue, the verbs of a vanished imperial administration, the pronouns of something no one can place. It has no written form — there is nothing to write *with* down here, and nothing to write *on*, and writing requires a stillness the Cathedral does not permit. When the rare visitor comes with paper, the tenders look at it the way deep-sea fish look at a lure: with ancient suspicion, and the slow understanding that whatever touches it will be pulled out of the water.

They do not write. They *remember*. Every tender carries in their head the position of every pipe-joint in their engine-house, the pressure-rating of every valve, the sound-signature of every bearing, the name of every other tender, living and dead, who has worked the machine in their lifetime. There are tenders who know, without consulting anyone, the names of every tender for the last nine generations. They are the librarians. They are unpaid. They do not know they are doing anything special.

## The Work

The work is divided, though no one drew up the division. It simply settled, like sediment.

The **Fire-Watchers** are the most numerous. They tend the combustions — the black coal, the blacker tar, the oily seams that weep from the lower walls. They work in pairs, in shifts that follow no clock but the pressure-gauges, because the engines run hotter when the water-flow is high and cooler when it is low, and a tender who misreads a gauge lets a fire go out and a piston seize, and then there is a different kind of silence for a long time. The Fire-Watchers carry long-handled shovels and iron rakes and they wear face-cloths soaked in oil because the smoke, when there is smoke, is a particular kind of poison that numbs the tongue. They sing, sometimes, to each other across the engine-houses — short, repetitive songs with no words, just vowels shaped by the noise. The songs travel strangely in the acoustics. They become directionless. They become a third layer of the roar.

The **Grease-Bearers** are the most respected. They are the ones who descend, on ropes and on the brass ladders that ring the inside of the engine-houses, into the lower chambers where the great flywheels turn. They are the only tenders who work without being able to see their own hands. They carry long-spouted oil-cans — the spouts as long as a man's forearm, articulated at the joint, so that the bearer can hold the can above the shoulder and reach down and around and into the bearing-housings without ever putting a limb into the path of the wheel. They work by sound and by touch. A new grease-bearer trains for eleven years. A master grease-bearer can tell, by the way a wheel *speaks* through the soles of her boots, when a bearing is in the first hours of drying out. This is the skill the tenders are most proud of. This is the skill that keeps the loop turning.

The **Gauge-Watchers** sit, mostly. They are often the oldest tenders, the ones whose hands are no longer steady enough for the descent, whose lungs are no longer equal to the smoke. They sit on the iron benches beside the copper dials and they watch the needles tremble, and they call out readings to the Fire-Watchers in a private shorthand that compresses a number into a sound. The shorthand has become so dense that visitors cannot tell whether they are speaking or coughing. They are doing both.

There are also the **Mussel-Eaters**, who are not a formal caste but who everyone understands. They are the tenders who wade into the basin, in chest-high water, with a long iron hook, and pull down the blue-black clusters of mussels that grow on every submerged surface. The mussels are food. They are the only food. A tender who has eaten nothing but steamed mussels and roasted mussels and mussels ground into a paste and spread on something that is almost bread (the bread being a tightly-compressed brick of dried algae, baked on the iron plates above the engine-houses) will tell you, with great seriousness, that mussels are a complete diet. They will not be wrong. They will be slightly surprised that you do not know this.

There are children. The Cathedral has children. They are short and solemn and their eyes are very pale. They play a game that involves throwing small iron nuts into the basin and watching them sink, and judging, by the sound of the splash and the way the pale fish scatter, how deep the water is at that particular spot. The game is called *Hearing the Floor*. It is not a game. It is training. A child who can hear the floor at age six is a future grease-bearer. A child who cannot is a future fire-watcher, or a gauge-watcher, or a mussel-eater, and is content.

## The Faith

The tenders do not have a word for what they do that translates cleanly. The closest in their layered tongue is something like *hold-the-up*, or *keep-the-falling*, and it is used as a verb, as a noun, as a greeting, and as a child's first word.

They speak of the **First Engineers** in a tone that outsiders mistake for worship and which is, in fact, something else — closer to the way a daughter speaks of an absent mother, or the way an orchard speaks of the season that planted it. The First Engineers are not gods. They are not saints. They are *the ones who made the machines*, and the machines are the mothers and the fathers and the suns and the seas of the tenders, and the First Engineers are the grandparents of all of that, and so they are spoken of with the same reverent, slightly aggrieved familiarity with which one speaks of any ancestor who has left you an inheritance you did not ask for and cannot refuse.

There is a chamber, in one of the engine-houses, that no living tender has ever entered. The door is sealed. The seal is very old. The seal bears, in raised iron letters, words in a language no one now reads. The tenders bring it offerings, sometimes — a bowl of mussel-paste, a smear of the black tar, a small iron nut that a child has won at *Hearing the Floor*. They do not know what the offerings are for. They do not know if anyone is receiving them. They make the offerings because the chamber is sealed and was sealed by the First Engineers and to pass a sealed door without leaving something is, to a tender, an offense against the order of things. They cannot articulate this offense. They feel it in the chest, in the same place the roar lives.

## What They Are Not

They are not unhappy. This is the thing visitors find most disquieting.

A visitor, standing in the basin with the water at his knees and the fish parting around his legs and the roar beating his ribs, will feel pity. He will feel the specific, choking pity of a person who has stumbled on a population of creatures who do not know they are trapped. He will want to explain to the tenders that there is a world above — vast, dry, lit by a sun that is not a furnace, full of foods that are not mussels. He will want to tell them they could leave. He will want to use the word *freedom*, and the word will taste, in his mouth, like something he has no right to.

The tender he is speaking to will look at him with her pale wide eyes, and she will tilt her head, and she will consider what he has said with the same slow seriousness with which she considers the meaning of a gauge-reading that has wandered out of its expected range. And then she will say, in her layered tongue, something that the visitor's interpreter will render roughly as: *Where would the engines go?*

It is not a question. It is not a dismissal. It is the simple, bone-deep recognition that she and the engines and the water and the fish and the sealed chamber and the mussels and the gauge-needle and the long-spouted oil-can are one thing, and that the thing has a name, and that the name is the sound the place makes, and that to leave the place would be to leave the name, and that to leave the name would be to die in a way that has no word in any of the six languages she carries.

She will smile, then. She will offer the visitor a bowl of mussel-paste. She will go back to her gauge. The visitor will ascend, eventually, on the long iron stair that climbs the wall to one of the grated openings above. The visitor will emerge into daylight that will, for a long time afterward, seem to him a vulgar and excessive thing, full of edges and shadows and a sun that did not have to be fed.

And the tender, below, will hear the visitor's footsteps fade into the roar, and will return to the listening, and the loop will turn, and the water will fall, and the pale fish will part around nothing, and the gauges will tremble, and somewhere in the upper darkness another piston will fall, and the place will continue, the way it has continued, the way it intends to continue, as long as there are hands to feed the fires and ears to hear the bearings and the long slow memory of the First Engineers to keep the inheritors at their posts.
