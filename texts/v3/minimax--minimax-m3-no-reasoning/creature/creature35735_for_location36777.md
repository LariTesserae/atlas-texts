# Those Who Dwell in the Tin Cavern

## The Old Woman with the Dog

She is not the oldest person here, but she is the one who most resembles the place itself. Her name, insofar as anyone uses it, is something soft and unstressed — a name that gets swallowed by the dripping. She has worked the eastern face of the great chamber for forty-one years, and her hands have the particular quality of skin that has been wet continuously for so long that it has stopped protesting. The wrinkles in her fingers are deep enough to hold water in them. She does not seem to notice this anymore.

The dog is a grey animal, indeterminate in breed, with a limp in the left hind leg that arrived one winter and never left. He is called  when he is called at all — a syllable that is more breath than voice. He follows the old woman from the chamber to the eastern gallery and back, and in the evenings he lies at her feet on the low stone platform she has long since claimed as her own. He does not bark. He does not seem to know how, or has forgotten, or has decided it is not worth the effort. He watches the other workers with the resigned attention of a creature who has seen everything the cavern can produce and found it insufficient.

The old woman, for her part, does not talk much. She talks to the dog in a low murmur that is less language than a habit of sound, the way a person might click their tongue. She talks to the rock face. Once, in an unguarded moment, you overhear her say something to a younger miner that sounds like advice, but when you ask her about it later she cannot remember saying it, or claims she cannot, and the moment closes over itself like water.

She knows where the tin runs thick and where it thins to nothing. She can read a face of stone the way another person might read a face of flesh. When the younger miners are uncertain where to place the next wedge, they defer to her, and she points, and they are right to.

## The Smelter, Eitan

Eitan is a thick man, broad in the shoulders, with the particular soot-stained look of someone who has spent so many years in proximity to molten metal that the soot has become a kind of second skin. His eyebrows are singed short. The hair on his forearms is mostly gone, burned away in increments over a working life. He wears leather gloves that are too stiff to bend properly, and he has worn them so long that the bend of his fingers has accommodated the gloves rather than the other way around.

He is in his late fifties, though he could be older or younger. The light in the smelting room is bad for judging ages, and the constant heat dries people in a way that adds years or takes them away, depending. He speaks rarely, and when he does it is in a low voice made lower by years of breathing hot air. He has a habit of running his thumb along the edge of a finished ingot as it cools, testing the way the surface takes the heat from his skin. He does this absently, the way another man might tap a coin against his knuckles.

The molds are his inheritance and his preoccupation. He is the one who decides when a new mold must be carved, and he is the one who carves it, working from the patterns of the old ones, copying a face or a row of figures or a repeating geometric mark that no one can explain to him and that he does not try to explain to anyone. He is not a religious man, insofar as the word applies here, but he treats the molds with the careful respect of someone who understands that he is a temporary custodian of a long sequence. When he pours, he pours evenly. When a mold cracks, he sets the pieces aside to be incorporated into a new one, and the new one will bear the broken edges of the old as part of its relief. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is discarded.

He has a son, not here, somewhere in the city above. The son has not come down in twenty years. Eitan does not speak of him. He does not appear to be angry about this. He appears to regard the son as a person who has made a reasonable choice, given the options, and who has gone where reasonable choices lead. The son's absence is not a wound so much as a kind of fact, like the damp.

## The Boy, Newly Come

There is always a boy, because there is always a boy who has just come down. This one is perhaps fifteen, thin, with the startled look of a person who has not yet learned the trick of the light down here, who keeps trying to focus on the far wall and cannot. He is the son of a former miner, or a grandson — the lineage is hard to trace because no one is in a hurry to assert it and the records, if there are records, are kept in some chamber no one visits. He was sent down by his family, or he came of his own accord, or both. The reasons are not spoken of.

He has been given a chisel and a hammer and a small bronze wedge and told to work the low places, the parts of the face where the tin is visible but not yet loose. He is not good at it yet. He strikes too hard, then too soft, then too hard again. The older miners do not correct him. They watch, occasionally, with the patient attention of people who remember being bad at this themselves. They will not teach him; he will learn, or he will not. The cavern does not insist.

He sleeps in one of the side passages, on a sleeping mat that was rolled up against the wall when he arrived, the way sleeping mats are always rolled up against the wall when new boys arrive. No one showed him which mat. The mat was simply there, unrolled, with a rough wool blanket folded at one end. He understood.

He has not yet heard the thing in the deepest passage, or if he has, he has not yet realized what he is hearing. That will come later, or it will not. Either way, he will work the eastern face, and his fingers will prune, and he will learn to read the stone, and in forty years he will be the old woman with the dog, or he will be something else, or he will have gone up through the grate and out into the wet street and never come back down. The cavern does not require any of these outcomes. It continues.

## The Rat-Watchers

There are three of them, and their work is not what you would think. They do not hunt the rats. They watch them. They sit in the higher galleries, on small wooden ledges nailed into the stone in places where the stone allows it, and they observe the movements of the rats in the ceiling, where the animals move in their own slow processions through gaps in the rock. The rat-watchers keep no written record, or if they do, no one has seen it. They seem simply to *know* things — where the rats are congregating, which of the upper passages is becoming unsafe, where the next bit of ceiling is likely to give way. They report to Eitan, sometimes, and to the old woman, more rarely, and they are listened to when they speak.

The lead rat-watcher is a man in his forties named something you do not quite catch the first time, and you do not ask again, because the asking would imply a kind of importance that the place does not encourage. He is a quiet man, watchful in the way of someone who has spent decades being a peripheral figure in a place that does not encourage centrality. He has a small notebook that he carries, bound in oilcloth, that he has never shown you, and that you suspect contains observations about the rats only in the loosest sense — drawings of cracks in the ceiling, perhaps, or notes about the sound of the water, or nothing at all. People carry notebooks for reasons that have nothing to do with the notebooks.

The other two rat-watchers are older and younger respectively, and they have the same attentive, slightly distant quality, as if they are watching the cavern from a small remove even while sitting in it. They pass the time in low conversation that does not seem to be about anything in particular. They have, over the years, developed a kind of patter — a set of phrases and observations that they cycle through, the way the stalactites cycle through their drops. You suspect, listening to them, that they are slightly mad in the way that long observation makes a person slightly mad, that the line between watching the rats and watching the water and watching the slow settling of the stone has blurred in their minds into a single sustained act of attention that they cannot stop performing.

## The Voice in the Deep

No one will tell you what lives in the deepest of the side passages. If you ask, the question is not refused so much as it is allowed to evaporate, the way a drop evaporates from a warm stone. The question becomes strange to the asker, briefly, as if the words have lost their referent. You find yourself apologizing, or not apologizing but feeling that you should, and you move on to another subject.

But the sound is there. You hear it on certain nights, or in certain hours — a slow, dragging exhalation, the kind of sound that suggests something very large moving very slowly through a space that is not quite large enough for it. The sound does not echo the way the dripping echoes. It absorbs the echo. When it stops, the absence it leaves is heavier than the sound itself was.

The miners have a name for it, which they do not use. You catch one of them, the boy, mouthing something to himself once, in a low passage, and the word he mouths is not a word you recognize, or it is a word in a language you do not know, or it is a sound that has been mistaken for a word because the mind insists on language. The boy sees you watching and stops. He does not look embarrassed. He looks, briefly, the way a person looks when they have been caught doing something they did not realize they were doing. Then he turns and goes back up the passage, and the moment closes.

Whatever is in the deep passage has been there longer than the cavern has been mined, and will be there after the mining is done. The miners know this. They have made their accommodation with it. They do not speak of it, and they do not go that way, and the tin veins do not extend that far, which is perhaps why the deep passage remains undisturbed. It is a kind of preserve. The cavern, which has found its equilibrium, intends to keep it.

## The Lightkeepers

There are four of them, and they tend the lanterns, which is to say they tend the light, which is to say they tend the small, flickering, dim insistence of visibility in a place that would prefer to be dark. The lightkeepers are an unhurried lot. They refill the lanterns with oil from a small barrel kept in a niche near the entrance, and they trim the wicks, and they replace the glass when it cracks, which it does occasionally, from the damp. The light they produce is not bright. It is the light of a single candle seen through fog, the light of a low fire at a great distance, the light of a thing that has decided to be visible but has not committed to being seen.

The head lightkeeper is a woman of indeterminate age with the careful, methodical movements of a person who has performed the same small task ten thousand times and finds in the repetition not boredom but a kind of peace. She is called something like *Maren* or *Mara* — a short, soft name, a name suited to the place. She has worked the lanterns for so long that she can feel, from a distance of several meters, when a wick is beginning to char and needs trimming. She does not know how she knows this. She assumes it is experience. It may be something else.

The lightkeepers speak to each other in a kind of technical shorthand that sounds, to a newcomer, like a private language. *The four's getting low. The nine is smoking. The twelve's cracked again.* They know the lanterns by number, the way sailors know the stars. They have preferences — the lightkeeper you are talking to, on this particular evening, prefers the lantern in the third alcove, the one that throws a slightly warmer light, because the warmer light flatters the tin and makes the ingots look, when they are newly poured, almost like silver. She will not say this is why she prefers it. She will say, if pressed, that the wick is better. The reason and the preference are the same thing, in the end.

They do not go home at the end of a shift, because the lightkeepers do not have shifts in the way that the miners do. The lightkeepers are simply *there*, in the cavern, at all hours, maintaining the small steady dim that is the cavern's compromise with visibility. They sleep on low pallets in a side chamber, and they wake, and they trim a wick, and they sleep again. The light does not vary. They see to that. It is their work, and they are faithful to it.

## The Cart-Draggers

Two men, cousins or brothers — the resemblance is the kind that suggests both — who move the ore from the faces to the smelting room. They work the small wheeled carts along the worn channels in the floor, which is to say they push the carts through shallow streams of cold water, which is to say they spend their working lives walking in cold water while pushing heavy things. Their boots are different from the other miners' boots. They are taller, heavier, waterproofed with a black grease that they apply themselves in a small ritual at the end of each shift. The grease is kept in a wooden box near the smelting room, and the box is old, and the grease inside it is older.

The older of the two is called something with a hard consonant at the front, a name that sounds, in the damp air, like a small stone dropping onto a larger stone. He is patient in a way that is different from the other miners' patience. The other miners are patient because the tin does not hurry. The cart-dragger is patient because the carts do not hurry, and the carts do not hurry because the channels in the floor are too narrow and too shallow and too water-filled for the carts to move any faster than the men pushing them. He is patient the way a river is patient. He has no choice.

The younger of the two does not speak at all, or very rarely. He is not mute — you have heard him say a word or two, in the right circumstances — but he has a long silence in him that he wears like a coat. The older cart-dragger speaks for him, sometimes, when speech is required, and the younger one does not seem to mind. The two of them move through the cavern with the coordinated ease of people who have worked together for so long that they no longer need to discuss the work. They are the sound you hear, sometimes, in the distance — the squeak of a cart wheel, the slosh of water, the heavy breathing of a man pushing a heavy thing through a cold stream. They are part of the chord that never resolves.

## The One Who Counts

There is a person here whose job is to count, though you do not at first understand this, because the counting is done so quietly and so privately that it resembles nothing so much as a person sitting alone with a small, private obsession. She is a thin woman, middle-aged, with a face that has the particular sharpness of someone who looks at things more closely than most people do. She sits in a small alcove near the entrance, on a wooden stool that has been worn smooth by decades of sitting, and she counts.

She counts the ingots as they come out of the smelting room, and she counts the sacks of ore as they go in. She counts the lanterns, the wicks, the barrels of oil, the chisels, the hammers, the wedges, the sleeping mats, the rolls of cloth, the boots. She counts the miners, in the morning, by the simple expedient of standing near the entrance and watching them pass, and she counts them again in the evening, and if the count is the same in the evening as it was in the morning, she is satisfied, and if it is not, she does not say anything about it, because a discrepancy in the count means only that someone has gone up through the grate, which is a thing a person has a right to do.

She keeps a record, in a book that is older than she is, that she adds to in a small, precise hand. The book is passed from counter to counter, over the generations, and each new counter learns the hand of the previous counter and continues it, so that the book, read end to end, is in a single continuous script that may now be two hundred years old. The numbers do not add up to anything in particular. They are not profit, or loss, or inventory in any meaningful sense. They are simply the count, maintained because someone has always maintained it, and the maintenance of it is, in itself, the point.

She does not have a name that anyone uses to her face. She is, simply, *the counter*, the way the old woman is *the eastern face* and Eitan is *the smelter*. The names are functions, and the functions are the people, and the people do not object. To object would be to insist on a kind of separateness from the work that the cavern does not encourage.

## The Boy Who Came Down and Went Back Up

This is not a person you will meet here, because he is not here anymore, but his presence is still in the cavern in small ways that you notice if you are paying attention. He was a miner, perhaps twenty years ago, who worked the western face and then one day did not come back. He went up through the grate, and out into the wet street, and he did not return. No one knows why. He did not say. He did not leave a note. He simply, one morning, was not at the face, and the counter's count was off by one, and the day continued.

He is remembered, faintly, by a few of the older workers. The old woman with the dog remembers him, though she will not say what she remembers, exactly. Eitan remembers him, in the way that Eitan remembers the son he does not speak of — as a fact, a reasonable choice, a person who went where reasonable choices lead. There is a chisel, somewhere, that was his, that no one has used since, that sits on a ledge in the western passage and that the miners step around without looking at. It has a chip in the handle. The chip is the only thing that distinguishes it from any other chisel. It is enough.

When the new boy came down, and was shown to his sleeping mat, and given his tools, and told to work the low places — the new boy, if he had any sense, would have understood that he was being offered a thing that another person had once been offered, and that another person had once accepted, and that another person had, in the end, set down. The cavern does not require him to set it down. The cavern does not require him to pick it up. The cavern continues. The boy works the low places, and his fingers prune, and the chisel he was given has a small chip in the handle that he has not yet noticed, or that he has noticed and decided not to ask about.

## The Cavern, Itself

There is no person here, and there is, in another sense, only person, because the cavern is a kind of slow aggregate of all the people who have worked it, layered into the stone and the air and the water and the dim. The footsteps that cut the channels are the feet of people who are no longer walking. The molds in the smelting room bear the faces of people who carved them and who are now, perhaps, themselves part of the rock, or part of the tin, or part of the damp. The count in the book is the count of people who are not here anymore, preserved in a hand that is not the hand of any single person but of a continuous small act of attention that has passed from one person to another over a long time.

The cavern is not conscious. The cavern is not a god, or a spirit, or an intelligence. The cavern is a place that has had people in it for so long that the people have, in a sense, become part of the place, and the place has, in a sense, become part of the people. The miners move through the cavern the way the water moves through the channels — slowly, following the paths that have been cut, not thinking about the cutting, because the cutting is so old that it has become the natural shape of things. The cavern is patient the way the miners are patient, and the miners are patient the way the tin is patient, and the tin is patient because it has nowhere to be and no urgency and no understanding of urgency, being a mineral.

When the light is at its dimmest, and the sound of the dripping is the loudest thing in the world, and the far wall of the great chamber is uncertain, and the thing in the deep passage exhales its slow, dragging breath, the cavern is, for a moment, most itself. It is not a place. It is a long, slow, accumulated act of being a place, performed continuously by the people in it and the rock around them and the water on the walls and the tin in the veins and the small steady dim of the lanterns, all of it, together, agreeing to continue, and continuing, in the same way, for as long as there is anyone left to do the agreeing.
