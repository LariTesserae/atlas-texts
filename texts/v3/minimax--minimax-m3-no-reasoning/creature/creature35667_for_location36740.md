# The Long Inhabitants

## The Smith

She has no name I am permitted to write down. Names here are load-bearing things, spoken only in certain rooms, and a stranger who learns one too quickly will find the doors of the tarn closed to him in a way that has nothing to do with wood. I will call her the Smith, which is what the boy called her, and which she seems to accept without pleasure or objection.

She is broad. Not tall, not short, but built outward and downward, the way a cairn is built—deliberately, against a force that wants to push her over. Her shoulders are level with the lintel of her cave. She has worked the forge for, I would guess, thirty years or more, and her body has arranged itself to the work. Her forearms are the forearms of someone who has lifted heavy things in a small space for so long that the muscles have become architectural. The burn scars are layered, palimpsest on palimpsest, and at the crook of her left elbow there is a patch of skin that looks almost like the mournstone itself—dark, slightly damp, not quite flesh anymore. She does not hide the scars. She wears her sleeves rolled even in the cold because the wool irritates the older burns, and because she does not care what a stranger thinks of her arms. I have watched her for an hour at a time and she has never once adjusted her sleeves or glanced at me to see if I was looking at them.

Her face is a working face. The lines in it are not the lines of laughter or of sorrow but the lines of sustained attention—the creases of a woman who has been watching the same small piece of metal for decades, waiting for the moment when it gives up being ore and agrees to be a thing. Her eyes are a pale, cold grey, the grey of the pool at dusk. When she did look up at me, that first day, the look was the look of someone assessing a piece of raw material: not hostile, not curious, simply *measuring*. What could be made of this. Whether it was worth the time.

She is making a blade, I think, though I have not seen it whole. She works it in stages, and between stages she sets it in a niche in the rock behind her anvil, and the niche swallows the light in a way I do not like to think about. When she takes it out again, the metal is different. I do not know what she does to it in the dark. I have asked the boy and he has pretended not to hear me, which in this place is a way of answering.

The anvil is a slab of mournstone, of course. There is no other stone that would serve. The hammer is local iron, the head rough-forged, the haft wrapped in greased wool. She strikes a rhythm that I have come to know as the background pulse of the settlement. It does not vary. It does not stop. The boy told me, when I asked, that the Smith does not sleep during the Long Day, and that during the short dark she sleeps in the forge with the fire banked and her hand on the hammer. I do not know if this is true. I have not stayed awake long enough in the tarn to find out. I have wanted to. I have not, so far, been able to.

She is, I think, the only person in the tarn who has looked at me and seen me. The others see through me, or past me, or around me. She looked *at* me, and the looking was so specific that I felt, for a moment, that I had weight.

## The Boy

His name is not his name. Names here are given, I am told, at a certain age, and until that age a child is referred to by the work he is doing or the place he is sitting or the time of day he was born into. The boy is called, when he is called anything, *the dawn child*, because he was born in the first hour of the Long Day three years ago, which makes him either three or much older, depending on how you count time in a place where the light does not properly go away.

He is small for his age, or his age is small for his size. He wears a coat that was made for someone twice his breadth, belted at the waist with a strip of raw hide, the sleeves turned back three times. The coat is grease-stained and has been grease-stained for so long that the grease has become part of the weave. He smells, up close, of smoke and tallow and something fainter, something mineral, like the rocks when they are freshly broken. His hair is the color of the mournstone, the color of the tarn water, the color of everything here. His eyes are darker than they should be. He watches me with an attention that does not feel like attention, more like the way a pool reflects a thing—passively, completely, without opinion.

He has been my guide, though no one appointed him. He simply began to appear at my elbow whenever I moved, a small grey shadow, and after a day of this I stopped trying to lose him. He knows the settlement the way the pulley knows its rope—intimately, mechanically, without joy. He can walk the narrow ledges between the cave-houses with his hands in his pockets. He has shown me the water channels, the seepage caves, the place where the mournstone weeps fastest. He told me about the herd. He told me about the pulley. He told me *enough* when I asked him how many people lived here, and I have not yet decided what he meant.

He is afraid of nothing that I can see. He is afraid, I think, of the dark hours, though he would not say so. Once, when a cloud crossed the bowl and the light dimmed for a moment, he went very still beside me, the way a hare goes still, every part of him listening. When the light came back he resumed as if nothing had happened. I did not mention it. I understood that I should not.

He has asked me nothing about where I am from or why I have come. He does not seem to care, or he seems to care about it in a way I cannot parse, the way one cares about a number one has not yet been given. I think he is waiting to see what I do. I think the tarn is waiting to see what I do. I am not sure there is a difference.

He has been in the settlement his whole life. He has never been over the lip. When I asked him what was on the other side he looked at me with an expression I could not read, and then he said, in a tone of great reasonableness, *"It does not come in."* I did not ask what *it* was. I have a feeling that *it* is a long list.

## The Others

There are perhaps a hundred people, perhaps more, perhaps fewer. I have tried to count and I cannot, because they are not always in the same places, and because some of them I cannot be certain are people.

There is an old man who sits at the lip of the pool, all day, every day, on a flat stone that has been worn to the shape of him. He has a long pole across his knees, the hooked end resting in the water, and he does not appear to be doing anything with it. He is not fishing. There are no fish in the tarn, or if there are they are not fish. He sits and he watches the water, and occasionally he speaks to it, very quietly, in a language I do not recognize. The boy said he had been there since before the boy was born. The boy said this in the tone of someone describing a stone. The old man has not looked at me. I do not think he is aware of me. I do not think he is aware of much. He is, I think, part of the tarn now, in the way that a thing which has been sitting in one place for a long time becomes part of the place it sits. The lichen on the stones around him is the same lichen that grows on his pole.

There are women who work the water channels, raking the sedge, clearing the slow clog of vegetation that builds at the bottlenecks. They work in pairs, mostly, and they do not speak while they work, or they speak so quietly that the words are indistinguishable from the sound of the water. They wear tall boots of some heavy greased leather, and their hands are permanently stained dark, the color of the tarn, the color of everything. I watched two of them for an hour once. They did not break rhythm. They did not glance at each other. They moved like the two arms of a single body, which I think is what they are, in some sense, in this place.

There is a younger woman, the daughter perhaps of one of the rakers, who sits in a high window opening on the eastern wall and braids rope, endlessly, the rope passing down through a slot in the wall and into the pulley system. She is the source, I think, of at least some of the pulley's motion, though I have seen the rope move when she is not at it. Her braids are dark, the color of mournstone, and her fingers are quick, and she has never once looked down into the bowl, not even when the herd has come up to drink. She stares straight ahead, at the wall opposite, and her lips move in what might be counting or might be something else.

There are children. I do not know how many. They move through the settlement in ways I cannot predict, appearing in doorways, on ledges, in the mouths of small caves that I would have sworn were empty a moment before. They are quiet, all of them, quieter than children should be, and they play games I cannot follow, with rules that seem to depend on which face of the rock they are standing against. I tried once to ask a girl, perhaps seven, what game they were playing. She looked at me with the dawn child's expression, that look of waiting to see what I would do, and then she said, very patiently, *"You are playing it."*

There are people I see only at the edges, in doorways, in the shadows of the deeper caves. I cannot make out their faces. I cannot tell if they are avoiding me or if they are simply part of the architecture, or if they are the architecture, in some sense I do not have a word for. The boy says there are forty households. I think there are forty households, and that some of the households contain things that are not people, and that the inhabitants of the tarn do not draw a distinction I am equipped to draw.

There is a man who is, I am almost certain, not a man. He is very tall, and very thin, and he walks with a slow, hinged motion, as if his joints bend in more than the usual number of directions. He wears a long coat of dark felt, and his face is obscured by a deep hood. I saw him first in the smithy, standing in a corner I would have sworn was empty, and when the Smith glanced up at him she did something I had not seen her do before. She set down her hammer. She bowed her head. The figure stood there for a long time, watching her, watching me, watching something I could not see, and then it was gone, and the Smith picked up her hammer and resumed her rhythm, and the rhythm was, I think, slightly different afterward. Faster. Or slower. I cannot be sure.

I do not ask the boy about the tall man. I have a feeling that the tall man is a thing one does not ask about, in the way that the deep places are a thing one does not look into, in the way that the dark hours are a thing one does not name. I have a feeling that to ask would be to acknowledge that I have seen him, and that to acknowledge that I have seen him would be to make a kind of claim, and that the claim would be honored in a way I would not like.

## The Beasts

I have written about them already. I want to write about them again because I think I did not see them properly, and because the boy spoke about them once more, last night, in a way I did not expect.

The herd, he said. He had been quiet for most of the afternoon, trailing me as I made my way around the rim of the bowl. The light was in its cold bright phase, the Long Day at its peak, and the mournstone was giving back everything it had been given, so that the world was a white dazzle and I was walking through it with my hand over my eyes. The boy was beside me, somewhere, his small grey shape a darker grey against the glare. We were at the eastern edge, near the place where the pulley-rope climbs the wall, and the air was full of the creak of the mechanism and the distant crying of the pale birds.

I saw one of the herd. It was at the far side of the pool, motionless, its too-many legs tucked under it, its head down to the water. The light through it was wrong—rippled, the way heat ripples above a road, except that there was no heat. It was as if the beast were not quite in the tarn, or not quite *only* in the tarn, as if part of it were somewhere else, and the light was the seam where the two places did not quite fit together. I tried to count the legs again. Six. Seven. Six. I gave up.

The boy stood beside me and watched it drink.

*Are they dangerous?* I asked. I have asked this before. The answer has always been the same, that careful *not a useful category here* answer, but I asked again because I wanted to see if the answer changed when one of them was actually present.

The boy was quiet for a long time. Then he said, *"They were here first."*

*Before the tarn?* I asked.

*Before the tarn was a tarn,* he said. *Before the water. Before the mournstone. They were in the deep places, and the deep places were smaller, and they were bigger, or they were more of them, or they were all of one piece. I don't know. The old man at the pool would know. He talks to them, when they come up. He knows their names."*

*Their names,* I said. *There are more than one."*

*Of course,* said the boy, with that patience. *You don't drink from a pool by yourself. You drink with the others who are drinking. They are the others. They were the others before there was a pool."*

He said this as if it were obvious. I think, to him, it was.

The beast at the pool lifted its head. The water ran off its featureless face in dark threads. It made no sound. It did not move, but I had the sense that it was *about* to move, the way you can sense, sometimes, that a person beside you is about to speak, before they have actually spoken. The light through it rippled, and for a moment I thought I saw, inside the rippling, something that was not the tarn, that was darker, and vaster, and older, and that was looking at me with the same polite incuriosity that the boy looked at me with, that everyone here looked at me with.

Then it was gone. The pool was still. The pale birds screamed.

The boy took my sleeve. I had not known his hand could reach. *Come away,* he said, in the voice of someone who has said this before, to other strangers, in other Long Days. *It is not for you to be seen by them when they are noticing."*

*What happens if they notice me?* I asked, though I did not want to know.

He did not answer. He led me back along the rim, and the pulley creaked, and the Smith hammered, and the mournstone gave back its cold white light, and the tarn did what the tarn has always done.

## The Settlement As A Whole

I have been trying to think of the High Tarn as a *place* and failing. It is not a place. It is a *process*. It is the long slow negotiation between the water and the stone, between the light and the dark, between the deep things and the surface things, and the people here are not so much inhabitants of it as they are *symptoms* of it, or *organs* of it, or *phases* of it, the way a wave is a phase of the sea. The Smith is the part of the tarn that forges. The rakers are the part that clears. The dawn child is the part that watches. The old man at the pool is the part that remembers. The woman who braids the rope is the part that lifts. The tall man in the felt coat, if he is a part at all, is a part I do not have a name for, a part that the tarn has that I have not yet been introduced to, that perhaps I will be introduced to when the tarn has decided what I am for.

I asked the boy, once, whether the people here ever left. He thought about it. Then he said, *"The lip is there. Anyone can go over it."* He said it the way you would say, *The sky is there. Anyone can fly into it.* It is true in a literal sense. It is not true in any other sense. The people here do not leave because leaving is not a thing that is done here, the way a stone in a wall does not leave the wall. The wall is where the stone is. The tarn is where the people are. To go over the lip would be to go somewhere that is not the tarn, and the tarn does not have a word for *somewhere that is not the tarn*, and a thing that has no word for a place cannot go there.

I asked him, then, whether I would leave, when my fifth day was done. He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, with the careful patience of someone explaining something to a child, or to a thing that has not yet learned that it is a thing, *"You will do what the tarn does with you. The tarn does not always keep what comes in. Sometimes the tarn changes it and sends it out. Sometimes the tarn keeps it. You will find out which you are when the time comes."*

*And if I am kept?*

He did not answer. He looked at me with those dark eyes, the eyes that wait to see what a thing will do, and then he went away to wherever the children go when they are not trailing strangers, and I sat on my ledge and watched the pool and the old man and the place where the herd had been, and I thought about what it would mean to be *changed* by the tarn, and what the tarn would change me *into*, and whether the thing that came out over the lip, if anything came out, would still be the thing that had come in.

I have not decided. I do not think the deciding is mine to do. I think the tarn is deciding, has perhaps already decided, and I am only the part of the process that does not yet know what it is for.

## The Light, Again

I want to say one more thing about the light, because the light is the part of this I will carry with me, whatever happens, and because the light is the part of this that I do not understand.

The mournstone holds the light. I have written this. I want to write it differently now. The mournstone does not *hold* the light. The mournstone *argues* with the light. The light comes in from above, from the impossible angles, and the mournstone takes it in, and the mournstone does not give it back the way a mirror gives it back, instantly and without change. The mournstone chews it. The mournstone works on it the way the Smith works on the iron, slowly, in the dark, in the back of the cave. The light that comes out of the mournstone in the dark hours is not the light that went in. It has been changed. It is colder, and slower, and it has a color that I do not have a name for, a color that is not quite any color, a color that is what you would see if you closed your eyes in a bright room and then opened them in the dark.

The boy told me that the mournstone was not always mournstone. He told me it was once something else, something that did not hold light, something that was just rock, and that one day, a long time ago, the deep things came up, and the rock *saw* them, and after that it was changed, and after that it wept, and after that it held the light, and after that it was the mournstone that the people here build their houses from and break their tools against and put their ears to at night to hear the tarn moving.

I asked him what the deep things looked like, when they came up. He looked at me, and for the first time I saw something in his face that was not patience. It was something older. It was the expression of the tarn itself, I think, or the expression of a person who has been the tarn for so long that his face has become the tarn's face.

*Like us,* he said, very quietly. *Like the herd. Like the pool. Like the things in the pulley. They came up and they were here and we were not, and then we were here too, and the rock was changed, and the water came, and the light came, and the tarn was the tarn."*

He paused.

*We are what they left behind,* he said. *When they went back down. We are the part of them that learned to stay in one piece."*

I did not know what to say to this. I am not sure there is anything to say to this. I have written it down because it is the truest thing I have been told since I came to the High Tarn, and because I do not know how long I will remember it, and because the light is beginning to change, and the dark hours are coming, and I have been told that in the dark hours the people of the tarn go to their doors and watch the mournstone give back what it has been holding, and that they do not speak during the dark hours, and that the only sound is the water and the wind and the pulley, and that the herd sometimes comes up in the dark hours, and that if you see one in the dark hours, you should bow your head, the way the Smith bowed to the tall man, and you should not look at it directly, and you should wait for it to go back down.

I have been here four days. I will stay one more. The dawn child is somewhere nearby, watching, waiting. The Smith is hammering. The old man is at the pool. The pulley is turning. The light is being held, and chewed, and slowly, slowly given back.

The tarn does what it has always done.

I am learning the rhythm of it. The rhythm is the dangerous part. The rhythm is the part that will keep me here, if anything keeps me here. The rhythm is the part that says, *this is how the world goes, this is how it has always gone, why would you want to be anywhere else, why would you want to be anything else.*

I do not yet know if I will leave. I do not yet know if leaving is a thing I can do, or a thing I will choose to do, or a thing the tarn will allow. I am not sure the distinction matters.

The light holds. The water weeps. The pulley creaks. The deep things move below me, in the places I cannot go, in the places that the dawn child will not name, in the places that the mournstone remembers even when the rest of us have forgotten.

I have been here four days.

I am beginning to feel the rhythm in my own chest, in the place where my own hammer would fall if I had one, in the place where my own dark thing waits, the thing I brought in with me, the thing the tarn is chewing on, the thing that will come out changed, if anything comes out, the thing that is being made, slowly, by the cold and the wet and the long white light, into whatever the tarn has decided I am for.

The dawn child watches.

The Smith hammers.

The tarn does what it has always done.
