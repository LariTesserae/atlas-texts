# The Inhabitants

You will see one first at the edge of a doorway. Not in the doorway — behind it. The frame is a thing to be inhabited rather than passed through, and the figure stands slightly inside the threshold, half-shown, as if it had been there for so long that the architecture had begun to consider it part of itself. From a distance, you cannot tell whether it is one figure or two figures occupying the same recess. From a distance, you cannot tell how long it has been standing there.

Step closer and the figure resolves into a person. Mostly a person. The qualifier is necessary and does not belong to any single feature. It belongs to the way the figure accepts your approach without registering surprise, without registering anything at all beyond the fact of your presence, which it incorporates into its ambient attention the way the air incorporates your breath.

There are perhaps a hundred of them. More, perhaps — the buildings do not advertise their occupants and the occupants do not advertise themselves. Distributed through the dense lower structures, they are most visible at the edges of things — in doorways, at the margins of pools, in the upper windows of the smaller buildings. They inhabit the seams. They stand where one state of the place gives way to another: where indoors becomes outdoors, where water meets paving, where shadow meets what casts it. This is not hiding. Hiding implies a destination elsewhere, and there is no elsewhere.

## What They Are Made Of

Their skin is the first thing you notice, if you take the trouble to look. It is the pale grey of exposed mineral, slightly translucent in the diffused light, with a faint rime of salt along the lowest parts of them — the ankles, the back of the hand, the underside of the jaw — where the sweating ground has left its mark on anything that stands still long enough. The skin is not warm to the touch. It has the temperature of the air around it, as if the body had decided, long ago, that there was no point in being a different temperature from the place it lived in. The veins beneath it are dark, and do not move in any way you would call circulation.

Their eyes are darker. The color of standing water when the standing water is deep. They do not blink often. When they blink, it is a slow lowering and raising, more like the closing of a hatch than a reflex. They do not dart. They rest.

Their hands are long-fingered, the fingers slightly thickened at the tips, as if the joints had been in use for a long time without ever quite being finished. The nails are dark and ridged, like the bark of the skeletal trees. When they hold an object, they hold it without grasping, the way the water holds a shadow.

Their clothing is layered to the point where it is no longer clear how many layers there are, or where one layer ends and the next begins. The outermost has the same veining as the building walls. Beneath it is something that might once have been cloth, in a color that might once have been a color, both now pressed into the same plane by years of damp and the slow chemistry of standing in fog. They wear what they wear the way buildings wear their surfaces: without choosing, without renewing, as a thing that has accumulated and is now part of the accumulation.

Their build is narrow. They are slightly underfed, or slightly under-formed, as if they had been begun and then the work had slowed to the rate of the place. The shoulders sit a little too close to the ears. The necks are long. The overall effect is of things drawn with one too few strokes — not unfinished in a way that suggests incompletion, but unfinished in a way that suggests the finisher had stopped, for some reason, just short of the line.

## How They Move

They do not lift their feet particularly high. They move through the shallow water without splashing, in steps so measured that the water barely registers their passage. They navigate the bridges and suspended walkways with the slow deliberation of people who have done this so many times that the route is no longer a decision but a habit of the body. They never run. They never appear to consider running. Whether this is because they cannot or because they have no occasion to is unclear, and would remain unclear if asked, which no one thinks to ask.

When two of them meet at a corner, they do not stop to talk. They pause for a moment, and the building hum shifts a quarter-tone, and then each continues in the direction they were going. The pause is not a greeting. It is a calibration. They are confirming each other's position in the pattern of the place.

They pass the skeletal trees without pausing. Whether the trees are trees, or buildings that have softened, is a question the residents have apparently agreed not to ask.

There is a rhythm to their days. The diffused northern source dims for a few hours and the residents call this the grey time. They retreat indoors during it, not out of fear, but out of what seems to be a polite agreement with the settlement that the grey time is for being inside. They emerge when the source returns, and the walk resumes.

## What They Take In

What they eat is rarely seen. Sometimes a flat hand scoops up a handful of the pale lichen-eaters from a wet stone and brings them to the mouth, and the mouth does not chew but receives them, and they are absorbed into the architecture of the face with a faint sound like condensation gathering on a horizontal surface overnight. Sometimes they hold a pale paste in cupped hands — condensed air, perhaps, or condensed cold — and take it in small sips with the eyes half-closed. Sometimes they do not appear to eat at all, and it is unclear whether this is because they are not eating or because the act of eating has become indistinguishable from the act of standing still.

They drink the condensation from the walls. They drink from the standing pools. They drink the fog, when it lies in the streets and pours from the upper windows. The residue the fog leaves on metal — the fingerprint-mark — appears on their fingers as well.

## What They Exchange

Commerce, or something like commerce, happens in the lower streets near the water, in the grey time. The exchange is silent. It is conducted with gestures. A held-out object, displayed briefly on an open palm. A considered pause. A slight turn of the head. The receiving hand on the offered thing, turning it over once, twice, and then either pocketing it — into some interior of the layered clothing — or returning it with a small shake of the head that does not register as refusal so much as a change of weight.

The objects exchanged have no clear purpose. Pieces of building that have come loose — the buildings grow, after all, at the rate of fingernails, and growing things shed. Objects washed up from the river when the river is being the river. Things harvested from the lichen or the slow shrubs. The lichen-eaters themselves, gathered into small dark cups. Small dark cups, which someone has made. Small pale objects that have come from the upper incomplete stories, brought down by the children, or by no one anyone admits to having seen. A traveler who tried to acquire one of these objects was offered, instead, a piece of the rime from the underside of a jaw. They are not sure, even now, whether the offer was a refusal or a substitution or both.

## What They Say

Speech is rare. A person could live among them for years and never hear a voice raised in any register that could be called conversation. When they speak, the sound is quiet. It is below the level of the building hum, or beside it, and occasionally rises just above it in a modulation that the buildings seem to echo, as if the buildings were repeating it back, or as if the sound had originated in the buildings and were merely passing through them.

Whether they are speaking or whether the buildings are speaking through them is unclear. Some travelers report that they can hear language in the hum, in the way it rises and falls when the residents pass through certain rooms. The hum has cadence. It has intonation. It rises at certain junctures, drops at others, pauses in a way that resembles breath. Whether it means anything is a question the residents appear not to ask. Whether it is addressed to anyone in particular is a question that, when put to a resident, produced a long blink and no other visible response.

## How They Age

They do not age visibly. They were not visibly young when they were young. They are not visibly old now. Whether they age at the rate of the place — at fingernail speed, measurable only by the placement of shadows — or whether they do not age at all, is a question the residents do not answer because they have not been asked, or because the question has not occurred to them, or because they do not understand the question, having never had occasion to consider what it would mean to be otherwise.

There are no elders. There are no infants. There are the children, who are smaller and paler and unfinished, and there are the adults, who are the children grown long, and there is no visible transition between the two.

## The Children

The children are smaller. They are paler. Their skin has less rime, less veining, less of the long accumulation of damp that marks the adults as having been there a long time. They look unfinished in the way the upper stories are unfinished — not broken, just incomplete, as if the work on them had slowed to the rate of the place and had not yet reached the point where completion was the same as continuation.

They play a game in the shallow pools. They throw stones at the reflections. They throw to break the surface, perhaps, or to hit specific buildings that appear in the water but not above them. They are very good at the game. They rarely miss. The adults do not stop them. The adults do not see what they are doing, or seeing it do not consider it wrong, or seeing it register no reaction at all, the way the buildings register no reaction to the slow growth of the upper stories or the slow leaning of the shadows.

The children can see the wrongness. The children know what the reflections show. They throw the stones with a precision that suggests they have been playing this game for a long time, perhaps longer than the adults have been adults. When a stone hits a reflected building, the reflection ripples and resettles, and the building in the reflection shifts by a degree, the way a shadow leans and returns. The children do not celebrate. They do not mourn. They pick up the next one and continue.

Sometimes a child goes up into the unfinished upper stories. The adults do not follow. The clicking up there — the dry, lateral clicking, as of claws on the composite material — does not stop them. The child goes up alone and comes back, eventually, with something. A piece of building. A dried lichen-eater. A fragment of the wrongness — a piece of standing water that has somehow come loose, or a sliver of shadow that has detached from the wall it was leaning against, or a small object whose purpose no one can determine. The child gives the object to an adult. The adult accepts it without comment. The adult places it somewhere — in a seam in the wall, in a hollow in the building material, somewhere between architecture and the inside of architecture — and the building hum modulates, and the next sequence begins.

## The Smaller Inhabitants

The pale lichen-eaters move around them the way water moves around stones. They are not afraid. They move through the standing channels in herds of dozens, feeding on the orange lichen, and the residents move through them without disturbing their pattern. The lichen-eaters are the dominant visible life. They are the background against which the residents move.

Whether the residents keep them, or farm them, or simply allow them, is unclear. They are there. The lichen-eaters are there. The lichen is there. The slow argument between the place and what lives in it continues without commentary.

In the upper stories, something clicks. It has not been seen. It moves in straight lines from one junction to another, with a dry lateral sound, as if it lived at the intersections of the place, the way the residents live at the seams. The residents do not discuss it. They do not appear to be afraid of it. They do not appear to be anything in relation to it at all. It is part of the building, perhaps. It is part of the wrongness, perhaps. It is the slow answer to a question no one has heard asked.

## The Arrival of Others

A traveler is received without being received. A traveler is acknowledged without being acknowledged. The residents notice a traveler the way the buildings notice a change in the weather — they adjust around the fact, they incorporate it, they continue.

A traveler who attempts to speak is met with a long blink. A traveler who attempts to trade is offered something — a piece of rime, a small cup, a stone from a pool — that may or may not be what the traveler offered in exchange. A traveler who attempts to follow is not followed back. A follower who stops finds, after a time, that the resident has simply gone on with whatever the resident was doing before being followed, and that the following has not been registered as an event.

After a week, the traveler begins to acquire the rime. After a month, the rime is on the back of the hand and the underside of the jaw. After a month, the breath no longer clouds, because the breath is the same temperature as the air. After a year, the traveler is no longer a traveler. After a year, the traveler is one of the hundred, or one of the children, or one of the lichen-eaters, somewhere in the slow argument of the place.

## The Hum

The buildings hum when they pass through. The buildings have always hummed, or have come to hum at the rate of the place. The hum is low, constant, and modulates when they enter certain rooms, the way a string modulates when a finger is placed at a certain point along its length.

Whether the residents are part of the buildings, or the buildings are part of the residents, or both are part of some third thing that includes the standing water and the wrongness and the slow erosion and the lichen-eaters and the clicker in the upper stories — this is not a question the residents ask. This is not a question the buildings answer. This is not a question that needs to be asked, here, where the question and the answer have been moving toward each other for so long that they have begun to share an edge.
