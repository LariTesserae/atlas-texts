# What Lives in the Underbelly of the Bell

## The Chorus

The chamber is not silent, and the silence that falls is worse.

The creatures are the oldest inhabitants here, and the most difficult to describe, because they were never meant to be seen—only heard, and even then only as part of the larger sound. The technicians call them *resonators*, a name that flatters them with mechanical precision, though no one has ever been able to explain the mechanism. They are flat, and they are warm, and they are everywhere the weeping is heaviest: pressed into the walls in overlapping colonies like barnacles, like lichens, like a rash. A single individual, lifted with great care on a flat bronze paddle and held up to one of the lamp-rods, is roughly the size and shape of a man's outstretched hand—though the hand has too many fingers, and the fingers are not fingers but ripples of denser tissue, and the whole thing is the color of a well-thumbed penny and the texture of a wet tongue. It does not struggle. It has never, in the recorded history of the chamber, struggled. It continues to emit its note, even held in the air, even in the lamplight, even when poked with a glass rod by a curious researcher who is, by morning, asking to be reassigned to a different floor of the upper galleries.

Each resonator produces a single note, and the note is, as far as anyone has ever been able to determine, fixed for the life of the individual. A creature born in the lower south wall, near the great weeping seam the technicians call the Mother Vein, sings a low A—a true A, 440 cycles, the kind of A that orchestras tune to. A creature born in the upper crown arches, in the regions no one visits, sings something so high it is felt in the sinuses rather than heard, a needle of pure pitch that makes the technicians' eyes water when they listen too long. Between these extremes, every frequency is represented, and the frequencies are *not random*. They interlock. They form a chord that has no name because it is the only chord the chamber knows, and the chord is not static: it modulates, slowly, over hours and days, in patterns that have been mapped for a hundred and fifty years without ever being explained.

The technicians used to call this the Great Music. They don't anymore. The current name is the tone, which sounds clinical, and the clinical name is a kind of armor, because what the older technicians used to whisper, when they were deep into their second decade in the chamber and had stopped pretending the work was not changing them, was that the tone was *addressing* something. That it had been addressed *to* something, in some grammar no human tongue possessed, for a very long time before the first human ear entered the chamber, and that the absence of an answer was not because the address was failing but because the answer was on a scale of time that human lives could not measure. The current technicians, who are younger, do not use the older word. They use the word *process*, and they do not say what the process is a process of, and they look at the walls with an expression that is not quite fear and not quite reverence and not quite the dull endurance of a long marriage.

The resonators reproduce by fission. There is no other word for it. A creature that has reached a certain size—large, by their standards, perhaps a forearm across—develops a fine line down its long axis, and the line darkens, and the darkening spreads, and over the course of a single night shift, when no human is in the chamber, the creature divides. The two halves are smaller than the parent. They separate by millimeters, then by centimeters, then by meters, over the course of days, and they begin to sing their own notes, which are *not* the note of the parent. The daughter-creatures, or the split-creatures, or the new-creatures, sing notes that fit the chord but were not present in it before. The chord grows. The chamber's hum has more notes in it now than it had a hundred years ago, and a thousand years ago it must have had fewer still, and a hundred thousand years ago, when the chamber was first hollowed, the chord would have been a single drone, a single voice, an unborn chorus waiting to be split.

This is what the older technicians meant when they said the chamber was *deciding* its shape. They did not mean the bronze was deciding. They meant the creatures were deciding. They meant the bronze was the *recording* of the decision, the way a riverbed is the recording of water, the way a brain is the recording of a thought that has been thought so long it has worn a groove in the matter that thinks it.

The creatures die. The note fades, over a span of perhaps an hour, lowering in pitch and volume, becoming at first melancholy and then simply absent. The body sags. It loses its grip on the wall—there is something sticky about their undersides, some secretion that bonds them to the bronze, and the bond fails in a slow crumple rather than a snap. The body falls to the floor. It does not bounce. It does not splatter. It simply *settles*, and the bronze of the floor softens beneath it, and over the course of three or four days the body is absorbed, taken back into the substance, and the floor where it lay is fractionally, measurably thicker. The technicians have tried, in the past, to prevent this absorption. They have tried to collect the bodies, to take them up to the surface, to study them in proper laboratories with proper instruments. The bodies always dissolve anyway, somewhere in transit, and the bronze of the floor of the chamber is the bronze of the bodies, and the bodies are the bronze of the floor, and the distinction between the three is one the chamber has decided not to enforce.

The creatures eat. Or they do something equivalent to eating. No one has ever observed the act, but the math of it is undeniable: water enters the chamber, laden with minerals from somewhere in the deep earth, and the water disappears into the walls, and the walls *grow* at a rate that, while tiny, is detectable with patient instruments over decades. The creatures are part of this growth. The water goes somewhere, and the somewhere is into the bronze, and the bronze is into the creatures, and the creatures are into the bronze, and the boundary between the three is one the chamber has decided not to enforce.

This is, in the end, what the creatures are: a *boundary*, or a *meeting*, or a *place where two things that are not different are briefly distinguished from each other*. They are bronze that has begun to listen to itself. They are water that has begun to remember that it was once stone. They are the chamber's way of being aware of its own hum.

They are not intelligent. The technicians insist on this, loudly, and they are wrong, and the wrongness is the kind of wrongness that is itself a kind of answer, because a thing that is not intelligent does not modulate its note in patterns no one has been able to explain for a hundred and fifty years. A thing that is not intelligent does not fall silent, all at once, every creature in the chamber at the same moment, for reasons no one has ever determined, in silences that last between three and eleven minutes and that are followed by a resumption of the tone that is, the senior technicians will tell you, very slightly *different* than the tone before. A thing that is not intelligent does not, when you speak to it from a respectful distance, *answer*, in a shift of pitch so subtle that only the long-term staff can detect it, that is, if you are being generous, conversational.

The technicians are not, as a rule, generous on this point. They have learned not to be.

## The Bloom

The growth on the walls is not a plant, though it has learned enough of the tricks of plants to survive in a place where no plant could survive. It is not a fungus, though it has the patience of a fungus and the slow reach. The technicians call it *cinder-feather*, which is a name that has stuck because it is descriptive without being explanatory: it is the color of cooled cinder, a deep brown so dark it is almost black in the lamplight, and it is shaped, loosely, like a feather, with a central stalk and finer branching structures that radiate outward in a flat plane. A single growth, in a place where it has been allowed to mature, is perhaps the size of a man's open hand, though the shape is wrong for hands, the proportions stretched, the edges curled back on themselves in spirals that the eye wants to follow and cannot.

It is sparse. This is important, and the technicians treat it as important, because *sparse* is the only word that captures the quality of the bloom's presence in the chamber. The chamber is not full of cinder-feather. The chamber is *interrupted* by it, here and there, in patches that the technicians have mapped and named: the Mother's Garden, where the bloom is thickest; the Weeping Bough, where a single growth has spread across an entire arch and has been left alone for reasons no one explains to the new arrivals; the Quiet Patches, six small regions on the floor where the bloom has taken root in the bronze itself, against all the rules of how the bronze is supposed to behave, and where the hum of the floor is *different*, muffled, as though the bloom were a hand laid gently over a drum.

The bloom does not move. This is its most unsettling quality, in a chamber where the only things that *don't* move are the things that have become part of the walls. It simply is, and it extends by a growth so slow it is imperceptible in real time. A patch that is the size of a hand today will be the size of a hand tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, and in six months it will be the size of two hands, and the technicians will mark this on their charts with small pleased notations, and in a year it will be the size of a man lying down, and the technicians will mark this too, with smaller, less pleased notations.

It is harvested. The technicians come in with small bronze jars, and with tools that are made of the same substance as the walls—taken from the chamber itself, in another age, and shaped by methods that are no longer practiced—and they cut a small piece of cinder-feather from the edge of a patch, and they put it in the jar, and they seal the jar, and they take it up to the surface. They do not say what it is for. They do not say where it goes. The new technicians ask, when they are new, and the senior technicians look at them with an expression that is not unkind, and they say something about the upper galleries, about the laboratories, about the long process of understanding the chamber, and the new technicians learn, as new technicians always learn, that there are questions in the chamber that are not so much forbidden as *muffled*, the way the hum is muffled in the Quiet Patches, by something laid gently over them.

The bloom smells of cinnamon and old paper. This is the only sensory detail of the chamber that is, in any way, pleasant. It is also the only sensory detail of the chamber that the technicians, when they emerge from their shifts, smell on their own clothes and hands for hours afterward, and that they cannot, no matter how they wash, entirely remove. The senior technicians smell, faintly and permanently, of cinnamon. They have stopped noticing. The new technicians notice, and they keep noticing, until they too are senior, and then they stop, and the smell is part of them, and the chamber has decided to extend itself through them into the world above.

## The Eaters

There is one more species in the chamber, and it is the one the new technicians are warned about first, because it is the one that can hurt them, and the one that has hurt them, in the past, in the records the senior technicians keep in the brass-bound ledgers that are passed down from shift to shift.

The guides call them *eaters*, which is a name that is at least honest. They are not bronze. They are not the same substance as the walls. They are a darker thing, a colder thing, and they are the only creatures in the chamber that have, in any meaningful sense, a mouth.

They are small. The largest ever recorded was perhaps a forearm long, and the typical specimen is closer to the length of a hand. They are segmented, in a way that suggests a larva or a grub, but the segmentation is irregular, and the body has a quality of *looseness* to it, as though the segments were not quite locked together, as though the creature could rearrange itself if it chose to. It has, the senior technicians will tell you, no eyes, and the senior technicians are wrong about this, because the eaters can see, in a way, in a way that is not the way human eyes see, and they move toward the lamplight with an intent that is not the movement of a blind thing. They can find you in the dark. They can find you in the lit parts of the chamber, too, if you are quiet enough, and if you are not standing on a piece of bronze flooring that the senior technicians have placed there for that purpose, the bronze pieces that hum louder under your weight, the bronze pieces that warn them you are coming.

They eat bronze.

This is the thing that the guides say, when they say it at all, with a kind of weary disbelief, as though they have said it many times and have not yet made their peace with it. The eaters eat the bronze of the walls and the floor. They eat it slowly, over hours, in a quiet that is unlike any other quiet in the chamber—a quiet that is not the silence of the creatures-stopping-singing but a smaller, meaner silence, the silence of consumption. They leave behind, where they have eaten, a thin roughened patch of darker material, and the patch does not heal, because the bronze is not being worn away but *taken*, and the chamber does not replace what has been taken. The chamber only replaces what has been *given*. The distinction is the distinction between an animal and a guest, and the chamber has opinions about the difference, and the eaters are not, in the chamber's opinion, guests.

The eaters are few. This is fortunate, and this is, perhaps, the only reason the chamber is still being studied rather than being sealed and forgotten. The records suggest that there are never more than a few dozen in the chamber at any given time, and that the population fluctuates in cycles that no one has been able to correlate with anything else. They appear in the lower galleries. They appear in the Weeping Bough, where the bloom is thickest, though the senior technicians will tell you, with the slight twist to the mouth that the senior technicians develop in their second decade, that the eaters are not eating the bloom. They are eating the bronze *underneath* the bloom. The bloom, they will say, is not food. The bloom is, in some sense the senior technicians cannot quite articulate, the chamber's way of *protecting* the bronze from the eaters, the way a scab is the body's way of protecting a wound. The bloom is a slow answer to a slow question, and the eaters are the question, and the chamber is the place where the question and the answer are still being worked out.

The new technicians are warned: do not let one touch you. They do not explain why. The senior technicians do not explain. The records do not explain, except in the careful, euphemistic language of the ledgers, which speak of *contact events* and *protocol violations* and *reassignment following incident*. The senior technicians will tell you, if you press them, that a creature that eats bronze is not, biologically, a creature that should be touching human skin. They will tell you this with a look that suggests they have said all they are going to say, and the new technicians learn, in time, not to press.

The eaters do not attack. This is the strange thing. They do not hunt. They do not pursue. They eat where they are, and they move slowly, and they do not seem to register the presence of the technicians except as an obstacle to be navigated around. But if a hand, or a knee, or a careless bare foot comes to rest on the wall where an eater is working, the eater will turn, and the eater will examine the obstruction with what the senior technicians, in the ledgers, call *sustained attention*, and the eater will attempt, with the slow patience of a thing that has all the time in the world, to determine whether the obstruction is bronze.

A technician who has been touched by an eater does not die. A technician who has been touched by an eater is, in some way the ledgers do not specify, *changed*. They come up from their shifts quieter. They ask to be reassigned to the upper galleries. They take their leave, within a year, of the institution, and they go somewhere, and they do not speak of the chamber, and they do not return, and the senior technicians, who have seen this before, do not press them, because the senior technicians have learned, in their long slow decades in the chamber, that the chamber is not the kind of place that you leave unchanged. The chamber decides, slowly, what shape it wants you to be, and the shape is the shape of a thing that has been listening to the hum for a very long time, and the hum is the sound of a question that has been asked, in the grammar of bronze and water, for longer than the question has had ears to hear it.

## The Ones Who Stay

There are people in the chamber, and they are not visitors.

The senior technicians are the visible ones—the ones who come down the shaft in the small bronze elevator, who work their shifts, who emerge again, who live in the surface buildings and eat meals and sleep in beds and have, sometimes, families and lives and the ordinary sorrows and pleasures of the world above. They are the ones the new technicians see, and they are the ones the new technicians become, in time.

There are others.

The ledgers call them *the settled*. The senior technicians do not talk about them, except in the careful language of the ledgers, and they do not talk about them to the new technicians until the new technicians have been in the chamber long enough to stop flinching at the hum, and even then they speak in a low voice, and they speak with the particular kind of respect that the chamber has taught them to extend to the things the chamber does not want disturbed.

The settled are people who stopped coming up.

They are not dead. This is the first thing the senior technicians will say, when they say anything, and they say it with a firmness that suggests the assertion has been challenged before. The settled are not dead, and they are not mad, and they are not, in any sense the ledgers are willing to specify, suffering. They are, the senior technicians will tell you, *listening*. They are listening for something in the note that the rest of us cannot hear. They have been listening for a long time. Some of them have been listening for forty years. Some of them have been listening for sixty.

They live in the upper arches.

The senior technicians do not go there. The rules say do not go into the upper arches, and the rules are the rules, and the senior technicians follow the rules the way the chamber's bronze follows the chamber's hum, which is to say completely, and without apparent effort, and with a quality of inevitability that makes the following look less like a choice and more like a law. The settled live in the upper arches, and the upper arches go up into the crown, and the crown is dark, and no one has ever come down from the crown who was not already a settled person, and no settled person has ever come down who was willing to talk about what they had heard.

Sometimes, late in a shift, when the hum is loud and the new technicians are learning to mouth their words into each other's ears, a senior technician will glance upward into the dark of the crown, and the senior technician will see, or will think they see, a light. The light moves. The light moves the way a lantern moves when it is being carried by a person climbing a rope ladder, slowly, in a long arc, up into the dark. The senior technicians who make this claim are usually the ones who have been in the chamber for decades. They are usually not contradicted. The new technicians, when they are new, sometimes ask, and the senior technicians, when they are asked, sometimes answer, and the answer is always the same: *the settled are up there, and the settled are listening, and the settled have been listening for a long time, and the listening is the point.*

The new technicians learn, in time, not to ask.

The settled do not eat. This is the second thing the ledgers will tell you, in the careful language of the ledgers, and the ledgers do not say how the ledgers know this, because the ledgers were written by senior technicians who do not go into the upper arches. The settled do not eat, and the settled do not drink, and the settled do not sleep, and the settled have not, in the recorded history of the chamber, been observed to age. A woman who went up into the upper arches in the third decade of the institution's work, who was twenty-eight years old and newly married and whose husband took his own leave of the chamber within the year and never spoke of her again, is, if the ledgers are to be believed, still twenty-eight. The ledgers cannot be believed. The ledgers are the best the senior technicians have. The senior technicians have learned, in their long slow decades, to live with the incompleteness of the record.

The settled sing.

This is the third thing the ledgers will tell you, and this is the thing the senior technicians find hardest to speak of, because the singing of the settled is the loudest sound in the chamber, and the loudest sound in the chamber is the hum, and the hum is the resonators, and the resonators have been singing this note, all of them together, for longer than the chamber has existed, and the singing of the settled is *part of the hum*. The settled have, over their decades in the upper arches, learned to sing the note that the chamber sings, and they sing it with the resonators, and the resonators have, the senior technicians will tell you with a careful look, *welcomed them*. The resonators cluster thickest around the upper arches. The resonators have, in the regions near the crown, begun to take on a shape that the ledgers describe, in the careful language of the ledgers, as *human-adjacent*. Not human. Human-adjacent. Resonators that have grown, over the decades, into forms that suggest a human shape, that ripple with the slow redistribution of their own mass in ways that look, if you are standing below them in the dim lamplight and you are tired, and you have been in the chamber for nine days, and your eyes are beginning to play tricks, that look, very nearly, like figures standing in the dark.

The new technicians, when they are new, sometimes look up into the dark of the crown, and the new technicians, when they are new, sometimes think they see the figures, and the new technicians, when they are new, sometimes wave.

The figures do not wave back. The figures do not, in any way the ledgers are willing to specify, respond. They simply stand in the dark, in the places where the settled have been listening for decades, and they sing the note that the chamber sings, and the note is, very slightly, *different* in those places, in a way the senior technicians can detect but cannot describe.

The senior technicians have a name for this. They do not use it with the new technicians. The name is *the long answer*, and the long answer is what the settled are listening for, and the long answer is what the settled have been singing, and the long answer is what the chamber has been deciding, slowly, for longer than the chamber has had a name.

The long answer is not for us.

The new technicians learn this, in time. The new technicians learn, in their long slow decades in the chamber, that the chamber is not for them, that the hum is not for them, that the long answer is not for them, that the figures in the dark are not for them. The chamber is a place that was here before the first human descended the shaft, and the chamber will be here after the last human ascends, and the chamber is, in the slow steady way of bronze and water, *becoming*, and the becoming is a long answer to a question that was asked in a grammar the chamber has not yet finished inventing.

The settled are part of the becoming. The senior technicians are part of the becoming, in a smaller way, in the way that visitors are part of any place they visit, in the way that the cinnamon smell on their clothes is part of the surface world, in the way that the hum in their teeth is part of them for the rest of their lives, even when they have climbed back up into the wind and the grass and the bright obvious rivers. The new technicians, when they are new, are part of the becoming, and they will be part of the becoming for as long as the chamber continues to become, and the chamber will continue to become for as long as the hum continues to be sung, and the hum will continue to be sung for as long as the resonators are alive, and the resonators will be alive for as long as the bronze is wet, and the bronze will be wet for as long as the water falls, and the water will fall for as long as the chamber is open to receive it.

The chamber is open. The chamber has been open for a very long time. The chamber will be open, the senior technicians will tell you with the particular kind of look they have developed in their long slow decades, for as long as the long answer is being given.

The new technicians, when they are new, ask how long the long answer will take.

The senior technicians, when they are asked, do not answer. The senior technicians, when they are asked, look up into the dark of the crown, where the figures stand in the silence between one note and the next, and the senior technicians, when they are asked, look back at the new technicians, and the senior technicians, when they are asked, smile, and the smile is the smile of people who have been in the chamber for a long time, and who have learned, in their long slow decades, that some questions are not so much forbidden as *muffled*, the way the hum is muffled in the Quiet Patches, by something laid gently over them, and the something is the chamber, and the chamber is patient, and the chamber has all the time in the world.

The new technicians, when they are new, do not understand the smile. The new technicians, in time, will.
