# Those Who Dwell in the Mire-Chapel of Uthel

## The Dwellers, in General

There are not many of them. A hundred and thirty souls is the common reckoning; a hundred and forty on the generous count, in the years when the fibrous pods have opened well and no child has been stillborn into the wall. They are a small people in a small place, and they have made themselves suited to the dimensions of both. Their bodies are narrower than you would expect — not wasted, not pinched, but turned sideways by centuries, the way water turns a stone. Their shoulders pass through doorways that were not built for shoulders. Their feet are broad and pale and uncalloused, because they have walked, every waking hour of their lives, on a floor that cushions them and a mat that yields and remembers. You can tell, watching them, that they have not struck a hard surface in generations.

In the bruise-light they are almost the color of the chapel itself. Their skin is the deep blue-violet of an old plum, the indigo of a vein seen close under thin flesh, the inky purple of a river-mouth at dusk. This is not — they will tell you, if you can coax them to tell you, if a hand on your shoulder counts as telling — this is not paint. The dwellers were once, in the generations before memory, the color of any people anywhere. The place has changed them. The light has changed them. Six thousand years of living beneath a canopy that filters the sky down to a stained-glass watermark has done to their skins what stained glass has done to the sun: brought them to a single key of color, the key of the bruise, which is the key of healing, which is the key of injury, which is the key of the world being remade.

They are not unhealthy by this. They are simply tuned to a different octave. They move through the chambers with a slowness that is not weakness but rather a kind of patience made visible. When a dweller walks past you, you feel that the dweller has agreed to be here, in this exact spot, at this exact moment, and that the agreement is being kept carefully, deliberately, as if the wrong step at the wrong second would cost something none of them can afford.

## The Eyes

The eye is the part of them that has changed most.

You will notice it the first time you meet one's gaze and find that you cannot hold it. The pupils are very wide — there is not enough light here, even at the brightest of mid-morning's diffuse overcast filtering through two hundred feet of soil and root, to permit anything narrower — and the irises are so dark that pupil and iris become a single black well, rimmed only by a thin crescent of color that is not quite blue and not quite violet and not quite the silver of a fish's belly but some composite of all these, a color you have no name for, a color you will spend the rest of your life trying to name.

The eyes do not blink often. When they do blink, it is a slow deliberate lowering, like a sluice-gate closing, and when the lid rises the eye is fresh and wet and looking at something else entirely — something they have seen in the half-second of the dark that you, with your open eyes, could not see.

They see in the bruise-light the way you see in afternoon. They see further into the dim than you can imagine. It is said, by those few outsiders who have lingered long enough to be trusted with the saying, that a dweller can stand in the lowest passage, in a chamber where you cannot see your own hand, and read the lines in your palm as clearly as you would read them on a sunny day. They deny this. Then they look away, which is its own kind of confirmation.

The elders' eyes are different from the young. In the old ones the blacks have a quality of polish, of having been rubbed for so long they have begun to throw the light back. They will catch a single drop falling from the canopy and, in the half-second of its passage, they will track it the way you would track a thrown coin. The young do not have this. The young have eyes that drink light the way the walls drink water. They are not yet finished being made.

## The Voice

They speak seldom, and never above a murmur. This is partly custom and partly necessity. The chapel absorbs sound the way felt absorbs a struck match — it drinks the edges of it, the consonants, the bite of consonants, and returns a softened thing, a vowel-rich husk of a syllable that has traveled through living tissue before it reaches you. A dweller will whisper your name on the stairs and you will hear it at the bottom. A dweller will whisper your name in the upper chamber and you will hear it twice, once from the doorway and once, very faintly, from somewhere in the wall beside you, traveling through the root-mass as if it were a current.

They have learned, over generations, to compose their conversations elsewhere. They talk with the palm laid flat on a shoulder. They talk with the back of a hand turned toward a face. They talk with a held glance that has a particular quality — not seeking, not demanding, but offering, the way you might offer an open hand of water to someone kneeling in dust. They talk with the breath itself. A dweller in conversation will sometimes exhale against your wrist, and the warmth of that breath, and its length, and the pause before the next breath, these are words. The dweller you are speaking with will nod and exhale against your wrist in return, and you have both said what there was to say.

Children, of course, do not yet know to be quiet. The children of the chapel are nearly silent for their first two years — born wet and blinking from the fibrous pods, hushed into the dim by their mothers' hands — and then, at some point in the third year, they begin to make sound. The first sound is rarely a word. It is a hum. A low, held note, almost subvocal, that they will produce for an hour at a time while they sit in a chamber with their hands flat on the mat. The mothers listen to these hums the way your mothers listened to your first words. They are trying to find a key for the child. They are trying to find where, in the music of the place, this new voice belongs.

## The Garments

The rust-colored felt. You noticed it at once.

It is sheared, once a year, in the autumn, which here is not a falling of leaves but a thickening of the air and a particular smell of the canopy — a smell of vegetal grief, of the slow tearing-down that the great plant does to itself in the shortening dark. The shearing is a ceremony kept entirely within the walls. No outsider has seen it. Two who have tried, in past centuries, are now in the wall, which is to say they are folded in, which is to say they have become garment or lintel or part of the doorframe arch. The dwellers do not speak of them.

What you are allowed to see, afterwards, are the garments themselves. They are thick and stiff and rusted-orange. They hold their shape — they stand up, almost, on their own, on the mat where the dweller has stepped out of them for the night. They are layered: three or four thicknesses, depending on the dweller and the season. The weave is dense, almost felted solid in places, and it resists water the way your own clothes would resist tar. The dwellers wear these garments in the damp and they do not seem to mind the damp, and a long-term observer will notice, slowly, that the garments are in fact a little damp themselves, have always been damp, are part of the chapel's wetness, woven to hold a particular humidity the way a violin is built to hold a particular resonance.

In the bruise-light a dweller in garment is a figure that you might find in a buried icon: brown and rust and indigo, the colors of old wood and old iron and old blood, haloed in their own slow exhalations of white breath that sinks and pools around them like votive smoke. You will not be able to tell, after a few hours, where the dweller ends and the chapel begins. That is part of what they are for.

## The Counting-Woman

Near the spring — not in any of the inhabited chambers, but in a sort of anteroom or foyer where the water-sound is loudest and the mucus-gravel can be heard slowly shifting — there is a small space set aside for the keeper of the count.

She is older than the others. Her eyes are the polished-black of the deep-cellar elder, and she will not meet your gaze the first day, or the second. By the third she will lay her palm flat on your sternum and exhale against your wrist, and this is how she tells you her name.

It does not translate well. It is something like *she-who-keeps-the-breath*, and what is meant by it is that she counts by respiration. Each morning she sits at the mouth of the anteroom and she listens. Over the course of the day, as the dwellers pass her on their slow errand-rounds, she hears each of them exhale. She knows each exhalation by the way it sits — by its length, by its warmth, by the faint whistle in the third dweller and the catch in the throat of the seventh. She knows them as you would know the creak of your own floorboards under particular feet.

By nightfall she has heard them all, or nearly. The discrepancy, if there is one, is the day's grief. If three exhalations were not heard that day, if a dweller has not passed her, then that dweller is — she will not say *dead*. She will say, instead, that a breath was not heard. That it has been folded elsewhere. That you will see the wall-mat in the morning, in a certain place, will remember the shape of a shoulder pressed into it that you cannot account for.

She keeps the count on a strip of the very bark-felt that the dwellers wear. Each exhalation, each heard breath, is a small notch; the unfelted edge of the strip is rough and she runs her thumb along it to confirm, at any moment, where the tally sits. She knows the number is a hundred and thirty, or a hundred and forty, because she knows the breaths she has heard. She cannot be wrong about this. The chapel will not let her be wrong.

## The Children of the Pod

The young are born in the lower portion of the inhabited chambers, in a smaller pillared room that is slightly warmer than the rest of the chapel and that smells, faintly, of a particular sweetness — the sap, you are told, that bleeds from the canopy when it is wounded. The mothers tend the pods for nine months, which is the same length as yours, and they tend them by touch: laying their palms against the fibrous case, feeling for the small movements within, listening with the flat of their cheek against the surface the way you might listen at a door.

When the pod is ready, it opens. It does not tear; it unfolds, layer by layer, like a dried fruit returning to plumpness, and the child comes out wet and blinking and entirely silent, small enough to be held in two cupped hands, and it is laid at the breast within the hour. The mother is, by then, sitting in the chamber with the others of her cohort — there are usually two or three births in a season — and the chamber is very quiet, and the air is very warm, and you would think, if you crept near, that there was a ceremony happening, but there is not. There is only this. There is only the new breath entering the music.

The child is small. The child is, for the first year, nearly translucent, the bruise-light passing almost through the skin to the organs beneath. You can see the slow pulse in the temples, in the soft places of the wrists. The child does not cry. The child breathes, and hums, when the breath is ready, and the mothers listen.

## The Fauna, in Detail

What else lives here, with you, in the wet.

The beetles are the size of your thumb and shine, when the light catches them, with a dull green phosphorescence that dims as they walk. They graze the wall-mat in long slow lines, and behind each is a faint smear of light that fades over the hour, the way a slug leaves a slime-trail — but luminous, the way a slug's trail might be luminous in the world you came from. The dwellers do not eat these beetles. They like to sit and watch them, and the watching has the quality of a small contented domestic ritual, like watching fish in a tank. The beetles' slow moving-lights across the walls are, for many dwellers, the only visual entertainment they permit themselves.

The grubs are another matter. These are cultivated in small clay pots that the dwellers set into niches in the living wall, and the pots are tended with a quiet dedication that is, if you do not know better, easy to mistake for reverence. The grubs are fat and pale and they eat a paste that the dwellers make from the fermented sap, and they grow for a season and then they are eaten, raw, by the elders, who close their eyes for the act of eating the way you might close your eyes to receive a kiss. This is considered a delicacy. If you are offered one you should take it. The dweller's face, when you do, will be as close to pleasure as you will see in the chapel.

The moth — the blind ivory moth — flutters in the highest of the chambers where a little more of the surface light has leaked through, and it is lovely in the way that only a wing can be lovely: thin and trembling and pulled out of the air by cupped hands. The dwellers trap them, and eat them with closed eyes, and afterwards there is a small lightening of mood across the chamber that takes an hour to dissipate. The moths are not blind by damage; they have never had eyes. They navigate by the moisture in the air, and by the warmth given off by the dwellers' bodies, and they are at ease among them in a way you will find touching, after a time, in a way that will make you think: *they belong here*. They belong to the chapel. They are the chapel's moths.

The rat-like thing — they have no name for it, or the name is untranslatable, or the name is a touch on the ankle and a soft chittering back — lives in the passages and is, on the whole, considerate. It is cold and quick. It will run across your foot in the dark and pause to make its small apologetic sound and then be gone, before you can decide whether to be frightened or charmed. The dwellers do not mind it. They sleep with their ankles exposed at the edge of the mat, and the thing runs along the bare floor beside them, and the dwellers will tell you, with a shoulder-touch and an exhaled breath, that the thing has a route and keeps to it the way they keep to theirs, and that no one falls asleep more deeply, here, than when the thing is making its small apologies along the wall.

## The Thing Below

You will not be told about the thing below.

You will be told, by a hand laid flat on your shoulder, that there is a lower chapel. You will be told, by an exhaled breath against your wrist, that there are older chambers down there, and that they are not for going. You will be told, by the way the elder's polished-black eyes slide away from yours if you push the question, that this is the end of the asking.

If you do not ask — if you sit in the upper chamber, if you sit and breathe and count the drops with the dwellers — you may, in the still hours before dawn, hear it yourself. The bellows-sound. The slow rise-and-fall of something enormous breathing in the lower chapels, the air cycling in and out through passages that you cannot see. The dwellers know the sound. They will be awake to it, around you, lying still on the mat with their eyes closed. They will not be frightened by it. They will be listening, the way you listen to a household sound, the way you listen to the refrigerator cycling on in your own kitchen far away.

They will tell you, if you ask on a morning when the asking has been permitted, that the thing has been there since before them. That it remembers the builders. That it is the reason the builders stopped building down and began to build up. That the dwellers have never seen it whole, only the sound of it, and once, in the oldest of the old, the memory of something pale glimpsed far down in the spring, in water clearer than water has any right to be.

They will tell you it is the chapel's breath.

They will tell you this and mean it literally.

## A Last Note, on Living Here

You will arrive at the chapel a stranger, weighted with the day-world, with the brightness of an outside sky still under your fingernails. The dwellers will not hurry you. They will not interrogate you. They will lay a hand on your shoulder and breathe against your wrist and they will lead you, slowly, to a corner of the mat that has been left open for you. You will sit on it. You will feel the mat yield under you, and remember your shape after you have risen, and this is the chapel saying hello.

You will be fed. The pale tuber, which has the texture of wet paper and the taste of rain after a long dry. The fermented sap, which is faintly sweet and cuts the throat on the way down. The small crustacean, which is offered in the cupped palm and which you should accept with both hands and eat without ceremony, the way a berry is eaten.

You will be left alone for a time. The dwellers will go about their slow errand-rounds and you will hear them passing in the passages — soft footfalls, the rustle of the rusted felt, the occasional held murmur — and you will realize, slowly, that you are being measured. Not judged. Measured. The way the keeper of the breath measures. The way the mat measures, remembering the shape of a foot. You are being added to the tally.

When you sleep — and you will sleep heavily, the way no one sleeps above — you will dream of the chapel itself, of the slow dripping from the canopy, of the bellows-sound below, of the music the place makes when nothing in it is being asked to do anything other than what it is. When you wake the mat will hold the shape of you for an hour after you have risen, and the dweller across the chamber, who has been awake longer than you and will say so only with a soft breath, will look at the held-shape of you and will exhale once, long and quiet, which is the dweller for *yes, you were here. Yes.*

By the third day you will have lost the brightness of the day-world from your fingernails. By the fifth, you will not be sure that the day-world still exists. By the seventh, the keepers of the mat will have learned the shape of your feet and you will find, when you rise, that there is a path worn in for you the way there is for them.

This is how you become, slowly, one of those who dwell here.

This is how the chapel takes you in.
