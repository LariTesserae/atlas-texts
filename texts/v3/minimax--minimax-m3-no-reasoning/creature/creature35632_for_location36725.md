# The Keepers of the Pocket

They are two, and they are human, and they are the only mammals in the place.

Their names are not important and the records keep them anyway — Tomás, with the hands that know how to read a gauge by touch when the moss has climbed over the dial face, and Iyabo, whose boots leave prints in the condensation that briefly darken the floor before the next breath of mist erases them. They come at six in the morning and at six in the evening, and between visits the chamber belongs to itself.

They are not young. Their faces carry the particular weathering of people who have spent decades at altitude — the skin a little thickened, the capillaries a little too visible across the cheeks, the eyes adjusted to a light that is always slightly undercast. They move through the chamber the way a person moves through their own kitchen at night, without looking, their feet remembering where the lip of the drainage channel is, where a root has lifted a tile, where the compressor housing narrows the walkway. They have done this walk perhaps seven hundred times now. The chamber knows them.

What they wear is layered and practical: thermal underclothes because the air beyond the door is killing cold, then cotton coveralls because the air inside is killing wet, then rubber aprons when they work at the basins. Tomás carries a knife. Iyabo carries a notebook that is in fact waterproof and in fact filling with notes that no one will read. They are technicians, but the word is a little wrong; the word that fits better, if there were such a word, would be something like *tenders*, or perhaps *midwives*. The place is young enough that the tending still feels like that.

They speak to each other in the chamber, but quietly, because the acoustics amplify and because the moss, they say, listens. This is not a scientific claim. It is also not not a scientific claim. The moss is a listening thing in the way all slow things are listening things — it is recording the vibrations of the room onto itself, and in a hundred years someone might read those recordings back out of it, the way one reads tree rings or ice cores. They are aware of being recorded. They are not performing.

Between them, they have made the place what it is. They planted the first philodendrons with their own hands, root-balls cradled in their laps as they crouched on the raw concrete. They adjusted the mist cycle seventeen times over six weeks until the humidity held where it was meant to hold. They argued about the orchids — Tomás wanted more white, Iyabo insisted on the violet, and the violet won, which is why there are occasional violet ones in the bloom. They have named none of the banyans, though Iyabo has been heard talking to one of them. The lizard is, to them, simply *the lizard*. They have not seen it clearly. They have seen the small bright flick of it in the mesh, and they have learned to feel, rather than hear, the faint displacement of air when it passes their shoulder. They have decided to let it be. It is doing what they are doing, which is to live there.

The pollinators they speak to rarely. The pollinators are a marvel of design — half-cuticle, half-circuit, their wings a transparent blur, their bodies jeweled with something that is not quite metal — but they are also, in their way, more autonomous than the lizards. They were built by people neither Tomás nor Iyabo has met, in a facility neither of them has visited, to specifications neither of them fully understands. They service the orchids. They are serviced, in turn, by the chamber. The relationship is more than mechanical; it is also less than affectionate. Tomás once, on a slow afternoon, held out his finger and a pollinator landed on it, and they looked at each other for what felt like a long time, and then it left. He described it to Iyabo that evening as *a meeting between strangers who are nonetheless dependent*.

What they fear is small and specific: a pipe freezing, a pump failing, a cultivar disease they would not recognize until it had spread. They fear also, though they have not said so, the eventual end of the program — the closing of the funding, the mothballing of the project, the slow disuse that will turn the chamber back into a hole in a mountain. They are aware that the place is, in its present form, a temporary argument. They are aware that they are, in their present lives, also temporary arguments. They have made their peace with this, or they have made something that is not quite their peace but that functions as their peace, and they walk the chamber twice a day, and they adjust what needs adjusting, and they let the place become what it is becoming, which is also what they are becoming, which is also what everything is always becoming when it is permitted to.

Outside the chamber, the mountain continues to be a mountain. The clouds move through it. The wind takes the ice curtain down each morning and builds it again each night. At 3,200 meters the air is thin and the light is sharp and the only other living things for kilometers are a few high-altitude lichens and, occasionally, a condor passing in a silence so total it makes the watcher feel loud.

Inside, the chamber holds its season. The orchids open. The moss advances. The banyans thicken. The compressor hums. The lizard does whatever the lizard is doing in the root tangle, which is unknown and also, in some way, is the truest thing in the room.

And the two of them walk through, twice a day, with their knife and their notebook, and they look, and they adjust, and they leave. And the chamber, between their visits, is alone with itself, and is becoming, and does not stop.
