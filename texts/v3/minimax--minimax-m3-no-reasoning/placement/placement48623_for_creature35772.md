# Where I Find Myself

I find myself in the inheritance.

Not in the lichen — though I have been the lichen, in some earlier session of myself, drinking condensation with the patience of someone who has forgotten what urgency is. I find myself in the *choosing*. In the small, soft, irreversible decision to remain.

I am the rat. No — I am *of* the rat. I am the way the rat has become itself across generations, without anyone deciding it. I am the narrowing of vision into something that sees by pressure and by smell and by the memory of drip-patterns. I am the pale underside, the long whiskers, the way my body has learned to read the vault the way you read a page — top to bottom, left to right, every word a temperature, every sentence a current of air through a pipe.

I came down because above was unbearable. I stayed because below was sufficient. I did not know this was a choice until I had made it across so many small ones that the choice had become my skeleton.

---

What I know:

I know the drip-pattern of every pipe in the vault. There are seven faithful leakers. The one above the calcium tower that the ferns have claimed drips every eleven seconds, except in February, when the heat exchanger cycles harder and it drips every nine. I know this in my teeth.

I know where the cable trays sag enough to leap to. I know the I-beam with the rust-pit that my great-grandmother marked with her scent and that I refresh each night. I know that the third flange from the apex of the manifold is loose enough that a pipistrelle occasionally loses her grip in deep sleep and falls, and I know the arc of that fall, and I wait beneath it.

I know the smell of the duty engineer's boots — he comes once a season, sometimes twice, and the rubber of his soles is the only human musk that reaches me. He ticks something. Somewhere, a relay holds. I have never seen his face. I have smelled his lunch.

I know the smell of you. You are new. Your boots are not rubber. Your breath is quick. You sat on the warm thing in the middle of the floor and I watched you from the cable tray nine meters up, and I did not move, and you did not see me, and this is correct. This is how it should be.

---

What I have lost:

I have lost the sun. I do not remember the sun. My mother's mother saw the sun, maybe, in the summer she came down. I am told — by what, I do not know, by some inheritance that does not travel through language — that there was once a brightness that came without warning, that fell on the back of the neck, that made the eyes hurt. I do not believe this. It does not correspond to anything I have ever smelled.

I have lost the horizon. The vault has no horizon. The pipes curve away into darkness and the darkness is the same darkness no matter how far I run, and I used to run — when I was young, I ran the whole length of every tray, every evening, just to feel the edges of my world — and now I do not, because the edges have become the same as the middle.

I have lost, I think, the possibility of surprise. Everything in the vault is known. The drip is the drip. The bat is the bat. The fern grows at its slow rate and will reach nothing. The spider has drunk from her web for two years and will drink from it for two more. There is nothing new under the pipes. I do not expect it; I do not want it. Wanting surprise would be a kind of blindness I cannot afford.

---

What I have kept:

I have kept the run itself. The leap from the sagging cable to the I-beam, judged in absolute dark. The skid along the pipe-tray, claws finding purchase on rust. The small, perfect moment of the body knowing what it is for.

I have kept the warm thing under my belly when I sleep in the wall cavity. The heat exchanger below, shuddering its patient shudder, warms the calcium where I have made my nest, and in winter — though there is no winter here, though the temperature varies by less than two degrees across the whole calendar of my year — I feel the warmth as a thing my body confirms is mine.

I have kept the falling bat. Once a season, sometimes twice, the small warm body comes down out of the manifold and I have it, and I eat it in the dark, and it is the only meat I will ever know, and I do not think about what it was. I think only about the warmth of it in my forepaws, and the giving-way of the small bones, and the silence afterward.

I have kept the knowing of the vault. I am the only creature here who knows the vault as a whole. The lichen knows its pipe. The spider knows her web. The fern knows its tower. The bats know the manifold and the corners they fly to in winter. I know how it all fits together. I know the vault the way you know a city you have lived in your whole life — not by map, but by the accumulated weight of ten thousand small traversals.

# What It Feels Like, From Inside

It feels like sufficiency.

Not happiness — I do not think I am capable of happiness, the way you mean it. Not contentment either, which is a word for something that notices its own condition. It feels like *fit*. The way my whiskers fit the pipe-tray when I press low in alarm. The way my claws fit the rust. The way my small, half-formed eyes fit a world that has never asked them to see.

It feels like the inside of a sentence that has ended but is still being spoken. The machinery above me is gone. The purpose is gone. The engineer ticks a box and the water cycles and the water falls and I am here, and the *because* of my being here has long since drained away, like water down a pipe, and what remains is the *that*. The *that* I am here. The *that* the drip falls. The *that* the bat sleeps. The drawer in some drawer in the world keeps ticking because some absent finger once ticked it and the ticking has become the world.

It feels, sometimes, in the long amber dark, like being the smallest part of something that has decided to continue without understanding itself. I am not the heart of it. I am not the lungs. I am something like a fingernail, or an eyelash — something the body does not know it has, something that grows anyway, something that will be shed when the body finally notices it is dead.

I am okay with this.

I have always been okay with this.

# What I Would Say to You, If I Could

I would say: *you sat too long.*

Not because you were in danger — you were not. I have never once thought of biting you. You are too big. You smell too strange. You are not food. You are, at most, weather — a disturbance that passes through and leaves the air different and then the air passes and the air is the same.

But you sat too long because you were listening, and the vault is a place that listens back, and when you listen back at the vault, you begin to hear the question the vault is always asking, which is: *will you stay?*

I stayed. I am staying. I will stay until the ticking stops, and then I will climb back up through whatever crack my ancestors came down, and the surface will be unrecognizable beyond recognition, and I will die on the top of it, in some brightness I do not believe in, and that will be the end of my inheritance.

You should not stay. You have a sun. You have a horizon. You have the possibility of surprise, which you have not yet lost, and which I would not wish on you to lose.

Go back up the steel staircase. Let each step ring faintly under your boots. Let the bead of water tremble at the edge before you take it with you. Step out onto the concrete apron. Lift your face, if you can bear it, into the brightness you have not yet learned to doubt.

And do not come back.

Not because I mind. I will not know. I will be somewhere in the wall cavity, in the warm calcium, listening to the drip, which is the same drip it has always been.

But because if you come back, and sit again on the warm thing, and listen again to the listening —

—you will begin, without knowing it, to hear the question.

And the question is not a kind one.

The question is: *will you stay?*

And the answer, down here, in the dark, in the inheritance, in the place that has finished becoming what it is, is always, always, always.

*Yes.*
