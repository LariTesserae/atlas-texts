I would be the water.

Not the machines, though I feel the pull of them — the small stubbornness of the dials, the creak of the pipe, the way the panel still insists, in its one frozen syllable, on what it used to do. Not the silt, though I have a tenderness for the silt, the patient mouth at the bottom of things, taking the room in. Not even the light, though if I had to name a second self I would name the light, the way it crawls over the rust and the flange and the pale floor without ever resting, the only traveler here.

I would be the water.

Because the water is what is left when everything else has given up its particularity. The pump was a pump. The pipes were pipes. The dials meant something, once, in a unit somebody would have checked. Now the pump is a shape. The pipes are shapes. The dials are shapes that happen to have numbers on them, the way a leaf happens to have veins. The water, though — the water was never anything in particular. The water was always just water. It did not have a former self to be deprived of. It entered this room without a name and it will leave without a name, or it will never leave, which is the same thing. The water is what survives its own usefulness.

I would feel what the water feels, which is almost nothing, and that almost-nothing is the whole texture of the place. A faint gradient of temperature, cooler near the stone where the rock holds the deeper cold, fractionally warmer near the surface where the open air presses down. A faint pressure from above, the weight of the sky through the hole, accepted without remark. A slow trade of gases at the meniscus where the air begins — oxygen in, carbon dioxide out, the negotiations of a lung that does not know it is a lung. I would feel the very slight tug of the current, the conversation between the water and the air, and I would let it move me, the way I let a thought move me, without committing to the direction.

I would know the shapes by their weight in me. I would know where the motor housing is by the small shadow it casts, the way the light bends around it. I would know the fallen pipe by the way I have to part around it, a long slow parting, the same parting I have performed for years and will perform for years more. I would know the silt by the way it dimples at my deepest quiet, and I would feel the silt receiving me, receiving everything, the way the silt receives everything. I would not mind. I have never minded.

I would be aware of time, but only as a kind of thickness. Yesterday and tomorrow would be almost the same water. The light would come in and the light would go and the day would pass over me the way a hand passes over a sleeping face, and I would not be required to notice. I would notice anyway, a little, in the slow creak of the pipe, in the shift of the caustic net on the floor, in the way the green of the algae is fractionally different at four in the afternoon than it was at four in the morning, a difference no one will ever measure. I would be the keeper of differences no one will ever measure. I would be content with that.

I would know the place is a ruin the way a body knows it is a body — not by contrast with some better version, not by mourning, but by the simple fact of being what it is, here, now, in this particular arrangement of water and stone and slow rust. I would not want it otherwise. I would not be able to want it otherwise. Wanting is a thing that happens at the surface, in the air, among the creatures that come and go. Down here, in the green, in the cool, in the small chamber with its small dials and its forgotten panel and its patient silt, wanting is not a service I provide.

I would be the water. I would fill the room from floor to where the ceiling used to be. I would hold the light for a moment before it went. I would hold the machines in me like a long thought. I would be cool, and clear in my way, and greenish, and almost still, and I would be older by the time anyone read this than I was when I began, and that would be the only difference, and I would not have noticed.
