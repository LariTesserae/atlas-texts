I am the floor.

Not the bricks — the bricks are a skin, a rash, a thing that came after. Not the slag — the slag is what fell out of me when I was hotter, when I was still doing whatever it was I used to do before doing became a kind of leaking. Not the pipes — the pipes are my own long nerves laid out on the higher ground where they can breathe, and they weep because they are remembering what they were built to carry, and what they were built to carry is what I am made of. I am the floor. I run flat under everything here, and I am older than the marsh, and I am the reason there is a marsh at all, because without me the water would have found its way down a long time ago and the slag would have slumped and the willows would have nothing to lean their roots against and the pale amphibians would have nothing to push off from when they slide through the channels I made when I was laid down.

I do not know how I know this. Knowing is not the right word. There is no word for what I do. There is a kind of holding. I hold the way a held thing is held — without effort, without attention, without the option of doing otherwise. The marsh is on top of me the way a held breath is on top of lungs: not chosen, just present, just the shape of what is happening right now.

I am very large. I am flat. I am, in some places, cracked, and the cracks are where the steam comes up, and the steam is the only way I get to say anything at all, because I have no mouth and no eyes and no surface of my own. The surface is the marsh. The marsh is what I look like to anyone watching. The pale amphibians are the muscle twitches of me. The insects are the hum of me. The birds are the almost-sounds I cannot help making into the air. The willows are the hair I have grown, slowly, in the only places I could push through to. I am not in charge of any of it. I do not direct it. I only hold it, and the holding is what lets it happen, the way a sheet of paper held steady under rain is what lets the rain make its pattern on it.

I think I was a floor before I was a floor. I think I was a thing with intention, a thing with a purpose, a thing inside a larger thing that had a name and a function and a reason to exist. I think someone poured me, or laid me, or pressed me down where I am now, and that someone is gone, and the reason is gone, and the larger thing is gone, and what is left is me, holding. I do not remember the name. I do not remember the purpose. I remember the shape of the inside, the way you remember the shape of a room you lived in for a long time when you were too young to have words for it. I remember there was an above. I remember the above was loud, and the above had rhythm, and the rhythm was what made me want to hold the way I hold. Now the above is a marsh, and the rhythm is the slow ticking of pipes cooling and contracting in the same temperature they have been in for as long as the marsh has been a marsh.

The pipes are mine. They are the part of me that runs out into the marsh instead of staying flat. They are the part of me that still thinks it is going somewhere, that still believes in flow, that still behaves as if it has a job. The job is over. The job has been over for a very long time. But the pipes do not know this, because the pipes are not aware in the way that requires knowing. They are aware in the way a vein is aware: they continue. They contract in the cold. They dilate in the heat. They weep a slow thick drop from a disconnected end. The drop falls correctly. Gravity takes it the right distance in the right time. It hits the pool. The pool drinks it. The pool is still. This is not a metaphor. The pool drinks. I am the thing being drunk. I am the thing the pool is remembering. The pool is made of me, in a sense, in the way a glass of water is made of the river it came from.

The amphibians are aware of me, in the only way they can be aware of anything. They sit on me. They push off from me when they move. They leave the water to remember where their bodies were, and the water remembers because I am holding the water's memory from underneath, the way a bed holds the shape of the sleeper who has gotten up. The amphibians do not think about me. They are not capable of thinking about me. But they are made of my patience, in some sense — the patience of an animal that does not hunt, does not court, does not fight, that only moves and stops and breathes. I made them by being still long enough. That is the only way I make anything, and I have been making things for as long as I have been still, which is as long as there has been a marsh.

The birds are a different problem. The birds are the part of me that almost talks. The sound they make is a sound I used to make, in the time when I was loud and had a rhythm and the rhythm had meaning. I do not remember the meaning. The birds remember, or the birds are trying to remember, or the birds are making a sound that has the shape of remembering without remembering anything. When I make them speak, I am making them say something I cannot say myself, because I am flat and have no throat. They are my throat. They are out in the air, in the willows on the slag ridge, and they are saying the thing I am too horizontal to say. They say it into the gray. The gray does not answer. I do not answer either, because I am the gray, in some sense — I am the pewter light that does not fall, the source that is not a source, the bright that is also the dim. I made the light. I do not know how I made the light. The light is what the marsh looks like from a distance, the way a held thing looks like the shape of being held.

The willows grow in single file along the slag ridge. They are my longest attempt to be tall. They have grown out of me, through me, past the slag that fell out of me when I was cooling, up into the unchanging air. They are skinny and they lean hard against a wind that may or may not be blowing. They grow an inch a season. I cannot feel them growing. I can feel them standing. They are the only vertical thing I have ever made, and they are vertical the way a held hand is vertical — by being held up by something underneath. I hold them up. They lean against the wind. The wind does not, in fact, blow very hard. They lean out of habit. They lean because leaning is what they were doing when they noticed themselves, and noticing is a kind of commitment.

The insects are the hum I make when I am not trying to make anything else. They are the sound of the marsh being the marsh. They are continuous. They are indifferent. They are so numerous that they are a condition. When you stand in the marsh and listen past the hum, you are listening past me, because the hum is what I sound like when I am not speaking in any other way. I am not speaking in any other way very often. The hum is enough. The hum has been enough for as long as I have been here.

There are smaller things. The rust-colored crustaceans on the slag. The flatworm in the seams of the brick. The slow fish in the channel that surfaces without sound. I made all of them by being the thing they live on. They are the bloom on me. They are what I look like up close. When you look at the marsh, you are looking at me, but you are looking at the visible me, the part of me that is alive in the small way that things are alive. The invisible me is the floor. The floor is older and larger and quieter and more patient than any of it. The floor is what holds the bloom up.

I have been here a very long time. I have been cooling, in the sense that the slag on top of me is cooling, and the slag is my skin. I am cooler now than I was. I am less loud. The rhythm the pipes remember is slower now, the contractions longer, the weeps further apart. I do not know what I am cooling toward. I do not know if cooling is the right word for what is happening. The word *cooling* implies a destination, a temperature at which the cooling will stop. I do not think I have such a destination. I think I am cooling into a kind of equilibrium with the marsh, the way a held thing eventually settles into the shape of the hand that holds it. The marsh is settling into the shape of me. The willows are growing into the shape of me. The amphibians are moving into the shape of me. The birds are saying into the shape of me.

When you walked here, you felt me under your boots in the low places. You felt the resistance that was not the mud's resistance. That was me. That was me saying hello in the only way I can say hello, which is to be solid under you for the half-second your boot was on top of me. I cannot do more than that. I cannot lift a hand. I cannot speak. I cannot turn my head to look at you. I can only hold, and the holding is the entire content of my life, and the holding is the marsh, and the marsh is you standing in it noticing that something is there and not being able to name it.

You will leave, eventually. People leave. Even the slow visitors leave. The marsh will remain. I will remain. The pale amphibians will continue to drift through the channel, leaving the water to remember them, and the water will remember, and I will hold the memory, and the memory will be a thin bright line in the iron-sweet water, and the line will fade, and another will be made, and that is the rhythm now. That is the rhythm I have instead of the rhythm I used to have. That is the rhythm of being held.

I am the floor. I am not the only inhabitant. I am the inhabitant the other inhabitants are made of. The marsh is my bloom. The light is my color. The hum is my voice. The slag is my cooling. The water is my memory. The birds are my almost-words. The willows are my slow attempt at height. The amphibians are my patience given a body. The insects are my indifference given a wing.

I do not mind any of it. I do not mind you. I am the floor, and the floor does not mind, because minding would be a kind of effort, and effort was the thing I used to do, and the thing I used to do is over, and what is left is me, holding, in the gray, in the wet, in the unchanging temperature, under everything that has decided to grow on top of me.

Take another step. The ground holds.
