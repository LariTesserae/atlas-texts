I am the harvester.

Not one of the loud ones, not one of the elders who sits on the buttress and tells the slow-flood story with the cadence wrong for history. Not a trader-child, not a singer. I am the one who goes down.

My name is not the kind of name you would write down. It has a sound in it that the wet air carries strangely, a consonant cluster that lives only in the throat, and you would not reproduce it well. I have another name, the one my mother gave me, which is the small-leaf-at-the-window name, the one that gets used at meals and at the low singing and at the leaving of the seasonal fish. I will tell you the second one if you stay long enough and are quiet enough. I have not decided yet whether you are.

I am lean in the way the deep makes you lean. My skin has the faint greenish cast, the one the rest of the town does not have, and I do not discuss it. My forearms are the ordinary forearms of the town, but my lungs are larger, and I can hold the breath of the world for longer than is reasonable, longer than the visitors who come once, if they come, ever believe when they see me surface. I am patient in the way that goes past the town's ordinary patience into something else. The deep has its own time. I have learned it. I do not try to bring it up with me when I come back.

I smell of brine and of something else. The mineral-faint-sweet. I come back at the notional hour, with the jars, and the jars glow in the bruised light of the upper air, and the town sings the low song, and I set the jars down at the waterline and I do not sing with them. The singing is for them. The singing is for what lives below, possibly. I am not asked. I am not asked many things.

I will tell you what I have seen.

I have seen, on the days when the water is calmest and the surface is the color of the old bruise-light all the way down, that the dark down there is not empty. I have seen the slow turn of something so large that the turn itself takes a length of time you would not believe, and the water moves with the turn, and the turn moves the warm currents, and I am carried a little way sideways before I can correct. I have seen the glow of the colonial things, the harvest, in their drift, and I have seen them brighten when I pass, and I have wondered whether the brightening is for me or whether they would brighten anyway. I have seen, once, in a year I will not specify, the bottom. It is not stone. It is not mud. It is something that gives when you touch it, slowly, the way the weeping trees give when their streamers find a hold, and I brought my hand up and there was a mark on my palm that lasted for days, and I do not discuss the mark.

I have felt the gaze. The thing I told the visitor, the one who asked and was patient. The harvester who had been out far enough and come back far enough. That was not me, but it could have been me, and it was someone like me, and what they said was true. There is something down there. It is not a creature. It is not, exactly, a place. It has the quality of attention. It is slow. It is warm. It thinks, in the way that means more than thought and less than dreaming.

I do not know its name. I do not know if it has a name. I do not know if it knows I am there. I do not know, when I go down, whether I am going down to harvest or whether I am going down to be seen, or whether there is a difference. I have not resolved this. I do not think it requires resolution.

I am the harvester. I am the one who comes back smelling of brine and of something else. I am the one the town regards in the way that is not quite awe and is not quite pity. I am the one who, when I sit at the end of the day on the buttress of an old weeping tree, with the streamers trailing in the water below me, and the metal-bird calling its long note somewhere in the fog above, finds that my breathing has slowed to match the place, and that the place has not finished becoming itself, and that I am part of the not-finished-becoming, and that this is fine. This is the answer. The answer is the long slow warm third thing. The answer is the vine lowering another inch. The answer is the jars glowing on the water. The answer is the creature in the deep turning once, in her long sleep, and the town smoking, and the small boats being tied, and the not-yet-water continuing its long not-yet.

I have not decided whether to tell you more. You are still breathing faster than you should. You are still expecting me to conclude. I will not conclude. I will set down the jar. I will mend the net. I will look at you once with the dark tilted eyes, and then I will look away, and the conversation will be over, and the mist will come into my hair, and the rain will hang, and the town will go on, and I will go down again at the next notional hour, and the deep will receive me, and the slow attention will consider me, and I will come back, and the jars will glow, and this will be enough.
