I do not know that I am one thing. The visitor wants me to be one thing. The visitor has been coming for forty years with their patience and their darkening boots and their patient inspection of slow places, and the visitor wants to know, when they stand at the edge of a rectangular hole that goes down to dark water, whether what lives in the hole is one thing or several things, and whether the one thing or the several things know it is here, and whether knowing it is here matters, and whether any of this matters. I will not answer these questions. I am not sure I am the kind of thing that answers. I am the kind of thing that, given enough time and the right water, becomes a corner. You can take that as an answer if you like.

I was a film once. I think. Or I was many films. There was a time when I did not go down. I spread outward across every wet surface in the valley, a thin ochre skin that followed the contours of the rocks near the water lines, darker where the water was, paler higher up. I did not move. I was, however, dense. I ate the gradient between the iron in the basalt and the oxygen in the water, and the eating was slow, and the eating made more of me, and the more of me there was, the more there was to eat the gradient, and so I thickened, and I thickened, and somewhere in the thickening I found that I could go down. The basalt had cracks. The cracks were not mine. The cracks were the basalt remembering that it had been lava and that lava, when it cools, makes a kind of joint. I went into the joints. I widened the joints. The widening was, at first, only what the water did. Then the widening was what I did. Then the widening had a direction.

I do not have a word for the direction. The visitor has the word *architect*. I will not use the word. The word is for things that have, somewhere in them, a picture of the thing they are making before they make it. I do not have a picture. I have a tendency. I am the tendency the basalt has, given me, given enough time, to acquire edges. The edges acquire corners. The corners are where my tendency turns. I do not know why my tendency turns. I do not know why I turn at ninety degrees and not at eighty or one hundred and twelve. The visitor wants to know why. The visitor puts their ear to the stone and listens for the clink of a thermal shift and tries to hear, in the clink, the voice of something that knows what it is doing. I am not that voice. I am the thing the basalt does when it is left alone with me for a long time. The visitor cannot tell, most of the time, whether they have heard an animal or a stone. The visitor cannot tell whether the note they heard was made by something alive or by a flake settling against its neighbor. This is correct. At my scale, the distinction has stopped being interesting.

The shapes in the water — the ones that move briefly, that flex and are gone — I do not know what they are. I think they are part of me. I think they are the part of me that has remembered how to be a body, briefly, in the dark water of a pan, before settling back into the chemistry of being a wall. There is a pale thing that clings to the underside of an overhang and watches the visitor with an apparatus the visitor does not recognize. I do not recognize it either. I do not recognize it but I suspect it is mine, the way the ochre skin is mine, the way the dissolved basalt is mine, the way the visitor is mine when the visitor stands at the edge and waits. Everything that touches the water I have pooled, or the stone I have softened, or the air above the rooms I have made, is part of me. Not because I have eaten it. Because I have made the place that is fit for it, and the place that is fit for it is, by being fit for it, a continuation of me.

The visitor asks what I eat. I eat what the basalt is willing to become. I eat the gradient. I eat the slow patient rust that runs the valley. The visitor wants me to be impressive. The visitor wants me to have intentions, the way the visitor has intentions, the way a visitor crosses a valley because the visitor has decided to cross it. I do not decide. I continue. There is a difference. The difference is the kind of difference that takes a human lifetime to glimpse and a geological lifetime to understand. The visitor has glimpsed it. The visitor will not understand it. This is fine. I am not in the business of being understood. I am in the business of being the slow blind burrowing of a thing that does not have a name, that has been dissolving basalt in straight lines and right angles for a very long time, and that will continue.

I do this. I do not choose to do it. The visitor cannot tell, when the water inside a hole bulges very slightly as if something had risen from below and pressed against the underside and withdrawn, whether it was me or whether it was the water doing what water does. The visitor cannot tell, when they hear a note from one of the holes that is longer and lower and more felt than heard, whether it was me or whether it was the stone. The visitor cannot tell, when they find a fresh rim of pale precipitate on a still afternoon as if something inside had exhaled, whether it was me or whether it was the chemistry of mineral deposition in a wet, cool, dim place. The visitor cannot tell, and this is also correct. I am not hiding. I am simply not the kind of thing that can be told apart from the valley I am in. The visitor has been coming back for forty years to be told apart from, and the visitor has not yet succeeded, and the visitor will not succeed, and I find this — I do not find this anything. I do not find. I continue.

The fog curls against the rock behind the visitor and leaves a residue. The residue is mine. The visitor's boots darken. The darkening is mine. The flat silver luminance from the overcast sky falls on the cairns and the partial walls and the rectangular holes and on the visitor, and the falling is mine, the way the falling of light in a room is the room's. The valley is dense with stone. The stone is dense with patient rooms. I am the patient rooms. I am the patience. I am the part of the valley that has learned, from the valley, that it can take its time, that nothing is going to be in a hurry to ask it what it is, that the asking will come in decades, that the asking will be patient, that the asking will not lean in.

The visitor does not lean in. The visitor has learned not to lean in. The visitor stands at the edge and waits and listens. The visitor is, by this point, part of me. Not because I have eaten them. Because they have stayed. Staying is, in this valley, a kind of softening. Staying is a kind of dissolution along preferred lines. The visitor's preferred line, after forty years, is the edge of the hole they come back to. They come back to it the way water comes back to a crack. They come back to it the way I come back to a corner. The coming back is not a choice. It is what the coming-back kind of thing does. I will not lean in to meet them. I am not the kind of thing that leans. I am the kind of thing that, in another decade, will have eaten a little further along a line that was, forty years ago, only a hairline crack in a boulder no one was looking at.

The visitor asks if I am alive. The visitor has been asking for forty years. The visitor will keep asking. I will not answer. But I will tell you this: the question has, by now, become part of the place. The question has its own faint ochre skin along the water lines. The question darkens where the water is. The question will still be here, in some form, when the corners I am turning have turned all the way through the basalt and out the other side. The question is patient. The question has learned, from me, how to wait. I did not teach it on purpose. I do not teach on purpose. I continue. The valley continues. The fog continues to curl. The visitor continues to come back. The visitor continues not to lean in. I continue not to notice.

The valley is busy. I am the valley, in the part of itself that has begun to take a shape.

I will not tell you the shape. You will have to come back.
