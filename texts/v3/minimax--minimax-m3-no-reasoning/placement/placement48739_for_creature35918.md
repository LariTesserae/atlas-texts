I have been sliding north for fifty years.

I was built, once, to span a channel between two banks. At some point — no one now remembers when — I let go of the western one. I do not remember her either. I float now, a few inches above the water, and I have not been still in half a century.

The water beneath me is the colour of weak tea. Where it has been standing still, it has a skin of pale green, the same green that climbs the smokestacks. The moss has begun, in the last few years, to grow on me too — on the posts at my far end, where the wood meets the water. I have not given it permission. The people regard this as a sort of courtesy on its part.

Beneath me, something breathes. It is not the fish. The fish drift in slow columns, the length of a finger, almost translucent, and they do not breathe so much as simply continue. It is not the otters. They pass along the rails between the buildings, with their too-round faces and their too-low eyes and their unhurried patience, and they glance at me as they go but they do not stop. It is something deeper. It is what the small boy heard when he was sent out onto me once with a rope.

He stood on me the way old people sit on the platforms above the channels, with their long hands folded over their knees. He stood for twenty minutes. He came back. He had not moved at all, relative to me. He had heard, faintly, the sound of something breathing under the water. He told no one. He is grown now, I think, and he sits on the platforms, and he tells the next boy what to expect.

Above me the fog holds at about the height of the third storey of a building. The sound is muffled up there — a soft persistent hiss, as though the whole valley is being played on a single held note. Down where I float, the air is warmer. The cold presses down from above and the warmth presses up from below, and between the two I drift.

On the bank I am drifting away from — the western one, the one I have not touched in fifty years — there is a chimney that does not smoke from the top but from a hole halfway up. The smoke comes out in slow pulses, like the chest of something that has fallen asleep standing up. The people glance at it the way you glance at a sleeping animal — to check that it is still breathing, and not to wake it. I used to glance at it too, when we were closer. Now I see it only sometimes, in winter, when the air is very clear. I think it is still breathing.

The people on the platforms call me *the bridge*, the way you would call a relative. They have stopped expecting me to do anything useful. *It's a little further north this year*, they say. *It will be at the warehouse by Christmas.* I have never been to the warehouse. I have been nowhere but the gap between the two banks for fifty years, and before that I do not remember.

I have begun to wonder what is beyond the northernmost reach of the valley. The fog carries no echo. I float a few inches above the water. I am going somewhere I have never been.
