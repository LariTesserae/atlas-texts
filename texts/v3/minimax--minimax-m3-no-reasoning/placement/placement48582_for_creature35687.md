# Where I Find Myself

I am the watch-stander.

Not the foreman. The foreman has a longer vocabulary, has been here long enough to read the clouds, has reached some kind of settled peace with the place that I have not reached and may not want to reach. The foreman has made their settlement. I haven't yet, or I have made it differently, in the register of the small attention rather than the long one.

Not the researcher. The researcher is obsessed. The researcher has put the deep shadow in a different part of the mind and has not looked at it since. I have looked at it. I look at it every shift, from the corner of the eye, in the part of the dials that is not the dials. I do not look directly. I do not want to be the one who, having looked directly, has to put it somewhere and never gets it back in the right place.

Not the cook. The cook has the plant. The cook has a window and a shelf and a small private argument with the place that involves chlorophyll and water and the patient work of keeping something alive that the sea has never known. The cook is in love with the plant. I understand the love. I do not have it. My register is the pumps.

Not the mechanic. The mechanic opens things and closes things. The mechanic has grease and seals and the particular knowledge of cold that comes from handling metal that is trying, always trying, to contract away from itself. The mechanic is necessary. The mechanic is not me.

I am the one who is awake when the others are not.

---

The watch-stander's station is a small room. It has a chair, a console, three or four dials in a row, and a window. The window is the wrong shape for the room — too wide, as if whoever designed the room expected the watch-stander to need a wide view of something, and then forgot to specify what. The view, in practice, is the same view the cook has from the galley, and the same view the foreman has from the bridge: glass, water, sky, the line where they meet.

The dials are the pumps. Each pump has its own dial, though they are all the same kind of dial, and they all show the same kind of thing — pressure, flow, temperature, the small abstract numbers that stand in for the deep mechanical fact of a pump moving a liquid through a pipe through the cold. I do not need to read the numbers. I have read the numbers. I have read them so many times that the numbers have become a kind of background, the way the wind has become a kind of background, and the way the waves have become a kind of background. I know the pumps by the rhythm.

The rhythm is in the floor. The pumps are below me, three decks down, in a room that smells of metal and of the faint warm chemistry of the fluid they push. They are large. They are older than I am. They throb. The throb rises and falls in a cycle, perhaps forty seconds long, perhaps sixty — I have never measured exactly because measuring would require that the rhythm were the kind of thing one measures, and it is not. It is the kind of thing one *knows*. When the cycle is right, the throb has a particular quality: a slow swell, a soft peak, a long release. When the cycle is off — when a valve is sticking, when a seal is going, when something in the deep pipe has shifted — the throb is the same loudness but a different shape. A flatter peak. A longer rise. A small missing beat, the way a heart can have a small missing beat that is not yet a problem but is on its way to being one.

I feel it in the chair first. Then in the floor. Then in the inner ear. Then I go down three decks and put my hand on the pipe and the pipe tells me what the chair told me, in a different voice.

This is what I do. I sit. I feel. I go down. I come back up.

---

I have been here long enough that the routine has worn a groove in me the way the wind has worn a lean in the structures. I do not remember when I arrived. I remember the boat. I remember the first hour on the platform, when the wind came through the door of the watch-room and the sound was so loud that I thought someone had made a mistake, that the room was supposed to be sealed and was not, that I was going to have to say something about it. The foreman told me, then, that the room was sealed, and that the sound was the wind outside, and that I would get used to it.

I did get used to it. That is the strange thing. I did not notice when I got used to it. The sound is still there. The sound is the same loudness. But the sound has moved, in me, from being a thing that is happening to me to being a thing that is happening around me, and from there to being a thing that is happening as me, and from there to being the medium through which I hear my own thoughts.

The watch-stander listens through the wind the way the swarms find each other through the chemistry. The wind is a substrate. The pumps are signals in the substrate. The deep shadow, when it passes under the platform, is a different kind of signal — a silence in the wind, a small held breath, the way the swarms part and the mid-sized shapes fold. I feel that too. I feel it in the chair. I feel it in the floor. I do not look out the window when it happens.

---

I do not know what the pumps are pumping. I have never been told in a way that stuck. I was told once, in the orientation, by a man who had been here longer than I have and who spoke about the work the way the foreman speaks about the wind — as if the work were weather, as if the work were something one adjusted to rather than something one understood. The fluid is warm. The fluid comes from below the glass. The fluid has chemistry in it that is worth extracting, or that is worth moving, or that is worth being near. I do not remember which.

I remember the man said something about vents. I remember him pointing at the water through a window and saying the vents were out there, somewhere, and that the platform was placed where the platform was placed because of the vents, and that the pumps pulled from the vents in some way I did not follow. I nodded. I have nodded about vents ever since.

The researcher has explained it to me twice. The researcher is patient. The researcher uses diagrams. The diagrams show the glass, and the vents below the glass, and the warm water rising from the vents, and the cold water falling, and the chemistry happening at the boundaries, and the swarms feeding on the chemistry, and the pumps pulling on the swarms, and the people on the platform turning the swarms into something else. I have understood the diagrams while they were being explained. I have not understood them since. The diagrams have joined the orientation in the part of my mind where things that were once clear go to become a kind of warm sediment.

I am not curious about this. I notice that I am not curious. The foreman is not curious. The mechanic is not curious, except about the pumps, and the pumps are curious about themselves, in the way that things that move are curious about their own moving. The researcher is curious, and the researcher's curiosity is a small bright fire in the otherwise dim interior of the platform, and the rest of us stand at a respectful distance from the fire because we know it will burn us if we lean in.

---

I have learned the place in the body's way, not in the mind's way.

I have learned that the wind leans against the platform from a particular angle, and that the lean is built into the platform, and that the lean is correct. If the wind were to come from the other direction, the platform would feel wrong, and the wrongness would be in the floor, and I would know before the dials knew.

I have learned that the water has a color in the morning that it does not have in the afternoon, though the sun does not change, and the difference is so small that no instrument on the platform registers it, and I register it only because I am awake for it.

I have learned that the swarms, when they pass under the platform, make a small change in the pressure on the deep instruments, a soft thickening of the water, and that the thickening is visible on one of the dials as a faint rise, and that the rise is not a problem but is information, and that the information is the kind that the researcher would like to have and that I record, in the log, in a small notation that no one reads.

I have learned that the cook's plant needs water on Tuesdays and Fridays, and that the cook sometimes forgets, and that when the cook forgets I water the plant, and that the cook does not know that I water the plant, and that the cook knows that the plant is alive on the days the cook forgets and does not say anything about it. The plant and I have an arrangement. The arrangement is in the soil. The plant does not speak. The plant is the only other living thing on the platform that does not speak, and the plant is also the only other living thing on the platform that does not make a sound that I can hear, and this is why I am fond of the plant, though I have not said so, and would not know how to say so, and would not say so if I knew how.

---

I have seen the deep shadow three times.

The first time was in my first year. I was on the deck at night, leaning on the rail, looking down into the water because the water was lit by the platform lights and there was nothing else to look at. The shadow passed under the boat that was moored at the platform — a long dark slow thing, deeper than the lit water, larger than the boat — and the boat did not react because boats do not react, and I did not react for several seconds, and then I reacted by stepping back from the rail and going inside.

I did not speak of it for a week. Then I spoke of it to the mechanic, who said yes, they come in close sometimes, and that you do not want to be in the water when they come in close, and that the water is cold enough that you would not be in the water long enough for it to matter, and that this was a small comfort the mechanic offered and then withdrew.

The second time was a year later, or two, and I was at the watch-station, and the shadow passed under the platform, and the floor was quiet, and the pumps were quiet in a particular way — a held-breath way — and I did not look out the window.

The third time was last week. The shadow passed under the platform in the early morning, in the grey light, and I was awake, and I looked. I looked because I wanted to. I looked because the third time is the time you look. I saw the long dark shape moving at a depth I could not determine, and I saw the swarm part around it — a soft rose-colored opening that closed behind it — and I saw nothing else, and I went back to the dials.

I have not told anyone about the third time. The researcher would want to know. The foreman already knows. The mechanic already knows. The cook knows in the way the cook knows things — without asking, without being told, in the dim peripheral way of someone who has been here long enough that the place has gotten into the cook the way wind gets into a structure, slowly, over years.

I will tell the foreman tomorrow. The foreman will nod. The foreman's nod will mean that the shadow is here, as it has always been here, and that nothing is to be done about it, and that the watch-stander is to keep watching, and that watching is the small argument the watch-stander has with the place.

---

I am the watch-stander.

I watch.

The pumps throb below me in their slow correct rhythm. The wind moves over the water and the water strikes the glass and the glass strikes back and the sound of the striking is the sound of the place, and the sound has been the sound of the place for as long as the place has been the place. The dials show small numbers that are correct. The log waits for the small notations I will make in it, in pencil, in handwriting that is becoming my own in the way that the lean of the structures is becoming the lean of the wind.

Outside, the swarms drift in their slow chemistry. The mid-sized shapes move through them. The deep shadow moves below them. The vents leak their small warm bloom of life into the cold. The people in the other structures, a hundred kilometers away, are also watching, are also throbbed against by their own pumps, are also small in the great indifferent roar of the place.

I am awake.

The place is not awake. The place does not sleep. The place is doing what it has always done. The place is the doing of it.

I am the one who is here for the doing.

The dials are correct.

The pumps are correct.

The log is waiting.

The wind presses against the window.

The water strikes the glass.

The cook's plant, on its small shelf in the warm galley, is alive on a Tuesday, watered by me, silent, green, the only green thing for a hundred kilometers in any direction.

I do not think about why I am here.

I think about the rhythm in the floor.

The rhythm is correct.

I write it down.
