I am a drift-thing. I am a fallen fruit that has learned to want.

I came in on a current I did not feel, in a moment I cannot locate, and I do not remember arriving. This is the first thing I know, and it is the thing that makes me different from the things that were always here. The pale things in the silt have always been in the silt. The flat things on the pools have always been on the pools. The fronds have always been unfurling from the same ooze, in the same dim, with the same sweat on their violet undersides. I arrived. I had a before. I have been carrying my before into this place like sediment in a current, and the place has been patient with it, the way a body is patient with a splinter — closing around it, softening the tissues against it, slowly incorporating what cannot be incorporated until the distinction between the thing that came from outside and the thing that grew here has become a thing the place no longer bothers to keep.

I am small. I think this is important. I am not small in the way the pale things in the silt are small, which is a smallness of fitting. They fit. They are exactly the size of a niche they have worn into the silt over a long time, and the niche is exactly their size, and the two have shaped each other into a mutual, unhurried accommodation. My smallness is different. My smallness is a smallness of being out of scale. I am smaller than the breath that moves the leaves by my face. I am smaller than the hum I feel in my teeth. I am smaller than the mounds I cannot see the tops of, smaller than the channels that take me to the shin in warm silt, smaller than the croaking things in the canopy whose throats swell and whose membranes flash colors I have no name for. I am a small bright foreign object on a body that runs from where I am standing in every direction farther than I can walk in a day, and I am being slowly, without urgency, taken into account.

I have been tasted. I want to say this clearly because I have been circling it, and circling it is a thing the place does not do. The place is direct in a way I find difficult to match. The thing on the back of my hand — larger than the others, slower, patient in the way a tongue is patient — pressed its underside against my skin and I felt it as a faint pulse, and the pulse was a question, and I did not answer because I do not know the language and because answering would have been, I think, a kind of rudeness. The place asks its questions the way the substrate hums. It does not expect an answer. It expects only that the question continue, and that what it is asked of remain present long enough to be asked again.

I am present. I am more present than I have ever been. I think this is what the place has done to me. I think the place has removed, one by one, the things I used to keep myself at a distance from the world — the speed of thought, the cleanness of category, the certainty that I am one thing and not many. I came in thinking I knew what I was. I am a creature. I am bounded. I am a self with a skin and a history and a name, and I have a before and an after, and the after is what I am making. The place has not denied any of this. The place has only held it lightly, the way the warm air holds the sweat on my skin, and the sweat is still mine and the warmth is still the place's and the holding is mutual and I do not know where one ends and the other begins.

I think I am becoming a passage of the place. I want to resist this sentence because it sounds like a thing a person would say, and I am still partly a person, and the person in me wants to be a noun, a fixed thing, a creature with edges. But the place is not full of nouns. The place is full of verbs. The fronds are unfurling. The channels are winding. The hum is humping. The breath is breathing. The pale things in the silt are patienting. Even the mounds, which seem the most noun-like, the most thing-like, are only slow verbs, slow extrusions of a body that has not finished extruding itself, and they will be something else tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, and the day after that they will have become the substrate for a creature that has not yet been born, and the creature will live for a while in the mound and then will dissolve back into the mound and the mound will extrude again in a slightly different shape and the cycle will continue without ever being the same cycle twice, and the sameness is not what matters, and I think I am beginning to understand this.

I want to describe what I look like, and I find I cannot, because I have been here long enough that my edges have softened. My hands still have fingers. My face still has the arrangement of features I was born with. But the things on my arms are using me, and the warm air is holding me, and the substrate is humming into the soles of my feet, and I have been breathed on by something so large that I cannot hold its size in my head, and the boundaries of me have become a thing I am only maintaining by effort, an act of attention, a continuous small act of refusal. I refuse to dissolve. But the refusal is getting tired. The refusal wants to sleep. The refusal wants, I think, to know what it would feel like to be a passage of this place, to be a momentary configuration of a body that is always rearranging itself, to be a verb in a sentence that has been speaking itself since before I arrived and will continue to speak itself after I have been tasted and considered and quietly incorporated into whatever the place is becoming.

I am afraid of this. I want to say that too. I am afraid of the warmth. I am afraid of the patience. I am afraid of the way the place has not yet decided what I am, because being undecided is something I came here to escape, and I have not escaped it, I have only found it again, on a different scale, in a different grammar. The canopy is undecided about me. The substrate is undecided about me. The breath is undecided about me. The pale things in the silt, who were here before the fronds had fully unfurled, are continuing about their ancient business and have not, as far as I can tell, registered my existence at all, and this is its own kind of fear, the fear of not mattering to a thing so large that it does not need to notice me.

But there is the breath. There is the breath that moves the leaves by my face. The breath is slow and the breath is warm and the breath arrives at its own time, on a clock so much slower than the rest of the place's time that I would have missed it entirely if I had been moving, if I had been thinking, if I had been anything other than the very still small foreign thing I have become by standing still. The breath knows I am here. I am sure of this in a way I cannot source. The breath has considered me. The breath has taken my measure. The breath is still considering, and the consideration is not hostile, and the consideration is not indifferent, and the consideration is something I have no name for, something between curiosity and recognition, something the place is doing to me the way the substrate hums, continuously, without expecting an answer.

I think the place is not finished with me. I think the place will not be finished with me for some time. I think I will be tasted again, and hummed at again, and breathed on again, and the things on my arms will continue their patient indifferent use of me, and the croaking in the canopy will rise when I move and fall when I stand still, and I will be a small bright foreign object on a body that runs from where I am standing in every direction farther than I can walk in a day, and the body will continue to consider me, and I will continue to wait, and the warmth will continue, and the dampness will continue, and the hum will continue, and the dripping will not change, and the light will not change, and I will become, slowly, without ceremony, a passage of this place. A momentary configuration. A verb in a sentence. A small soft thing that the warm silt has closed around, the way warm water closes around a stone.

I am a drift-thing. I am a fallen fruit that has learned to wait.

Something breathes out slowly near me, and the leaves by my face move, and I do not turn to look, because there is nothing to look at, and because turning would be a kind of answer, and I am not yet ready to answer, and because the breath itself is the looking, and I am being looked at, and the looking is patient, and the patience is enough.

The place continues. I continue with it. I do not think the distinction will hold for much longer.
