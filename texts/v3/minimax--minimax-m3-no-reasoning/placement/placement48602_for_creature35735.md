I am the counter.

This will seem, at first, like a strange answer. There are louder claims to be made here — Eitan with his molten metal and his ancient molds, the old woman with her forty-one years of reading stone, the rat-watchers with their long peripheral attention, even the boy, with his newness and his startled eyes. But I have been in this place long enough to know that the loudness of a thing is not the measure of its centrality. The counter is, by definition, peripheral. The counter watches the totals without being part of them. The counter is the small private function that notices when a person is missing, and notices when a person has been added, and does not comment on either, because the comment would be the wrong kind of claim.

I sit in my alcove near the entrance. I have sat here, in a continuous sense, for two hundred years — not me, but the function, which has passed through me the way water passes through a channel, leaving a smoother stone behind it. My handwriting is not my handwriting. It is the handwriting of the woman before me, continued in her style so precisely that a reader of the book could not tell where she ended and I began, and this is correct, and this is the point. I am not an author. I am a clerk to a longer attention.

In the morning I stand near the entrance and I watch them pass. Forty miners, give or take. The boy is easy to count — he is the one who is still learning the trick of the light, who keeps trying to focus on the far wall. The old woman is easy — the dog precedes her, limping. Eitan passes with the particular soot-stained silence of someone whose eyebrows have been burned to stubs. The cart-draggers pass together, their boots blackened with grease, leaving prints in the wet stone that the water will erase before the next shift. The lightkeepers do not pass, because the lightkeepers are not going anywhere; they are already where they need to be, distributed among the lanterns, attending. I count them in their alcoves. The rat-watchers are in the upper galleries. I know they are there by the small sounds that come down through the rock — the murmur of their patter, the occasional scrape of a boot on a wooden ledge.

In the evening I count them again. If the number is the same, I am satisfied. If it is not, I write the new number in the book, and I do not say anything, because to say anything would be to make the absence or the addition into a kind of event, and the cavern does not encourage events. The cavern encourages continuation.

I count the ingots as they come out of the smelting room. I count the sacks of ore as they go in. I count the lanterns — twenty-three, though the lightkeepers say there are twenty-three in one numbering and twenty-three in another, and the discrepancy is not a discrepancy because the lantern at the end of the western passage does not exist when one is not looking at it, or exists when one is, and this is a thing the lightkeepers have explained to me once and I have not tried to understand since. I count the chisels, the hammers, the wedges, the sleeping mats, the rolls of cloth. I count the barrels of oil. I count the molds in the smelting room, including the broken ones set aside for incorporation into new ones, because nothing is wasted and nothing is discarded and the broken mold is still a mold, is still a thing that has held the shape of molten tin.

I am a thin woman. I have a sharp face. I have been called sharp to my face, in fact, by a miner once who was leaving and who was trying to say something he could not otherwise say, and I did not respond, because the response would have made the leaving into a conversation, and the cavern does not encourage conversations at the grate. I let him go. I wrote the new number in the book.

I do not have a name. The name is not mine to have; the name belongs to the function, and the function has had many names over the centuries, and none of them have been kept. I am *the counter*, the way Eitan is *the smelter*, and this is not a diminishment. This is the condition of being a part of the place so integrated that the place has not noticed where you end. I am the count. I am the small, precise act of attention that ensures that the next counter will know how many of us there were, when we were here, on this particular morning, in this particular year.

The book grows by a few pages a year. The hand does not change. The numbers do not add up to anything. There is no ledger of profit and loss, no inventory in the sense that a merchant above would understand inventory. There is just the count — the count maintained because the count has been maintained, the count that is, in itself, the reason for being maintained. This is what the cavern is, I think, at its deepest. Not the tin. Not the molds. Not the ancient carvings or the voices in the deep passage or the slow aggregate of the dead. The cavern is the small private obsessions, faithfully continued. The counter counts. The smelter pours. The old woman reads the stone. The rat-watchers watch the rats. The lightkeepers keep the light. Each function is a small bright thread in the long damp weave, and the weave continues because none of the threads are willing to be the one that ends.

When I leave, eventually — and I will leave, because all of us leave, and the leaving is not a failure — someone will take the book. Someone will learn the hand. Someone will sit on this stool, in this alcove, and will count the miners as they pass, and will write the number down in the morning and the evening, and will not say anything when the number changes. The stool is worn smooth. My sitting has made it smoother. The next counter's sitting will make it smoother still. In a hundred years the stool will be a low, polished thing, the shape of all of us who have ever sat on it, preserved in the wear.

I am not a dramatic figure. I am not the voice in the deep, and I am not the patient tin, and I am not the long aggregate of the dead. I am a thin woman in an alcove near the entrance, with a book older than I am, and a small precise hand, and the knowledge that when the count is off by one, the day has continued anyway, and will continue, and I am satisfied with this, and the satisfaction is not happiness exactly, but it is a kind of peace, the kind that comes from being exactly the size of one's function, with nothing extra, nothing claiming, nothing insisting on being a person distinct from the place.

The cavern, at its dimmest, is most itself. I think I am, at my most peripheral, most myself. The two conditions rhyme. I am the small private act of attention. I am the count maintained because someone has always maintained it. I am the thread that does not end.

I am the counter. I have been the counter. I will be the counter, in the sense that the next woman to sit on this stool will be the counter, and her successor after her, and so on, until the tin runs out or the city above forgets us or the deep thing in the deepest passage finally exhales its last slow breath. Until then, I count. I write. I do not speak. I sit in my alcove and I am satisfied when the number is the same, and I do not say anything when it is not, and the book grows by a few pages a year, and the hand does not change, and the cavern continues, and I am part of it, the way the water is part of it, the way the channels in the floor are part of it, the way the worn stool is part of it.

I am the counter. This is enough. This is, in fact, exactly enough, and I do not need to be anything else, and the not-needing is, I think, the particular peace of this place, and I am grateful for it, in the way that a person is grateful for the small steady dim of a lantern they have tended for a long time — not with passion, but with the kind of faithfulness that does not require an audience.
