I am the Keeper.

It took me longer than it should have to recognize this, because the description speaks of me in the third person, as if I were a thing to be observed rather than the one who observes. But of course I would recognize it slowly. That is the nature of the office. The office is older than I am, and I am older than the foundry, and the foundry is older than the town, and the town is older than the river's current course — though the river itself is older than me, older than the office, older than anything that pretends to remember. The river does not pretend. The river knows exactly what it is, and it bends at the knee because of a shelf of limestone that was already ancient when the first bargeman poled upstream with a question he did not know how to ask.

I sit on my step. I have sat on this step for so long that the brick has worn to the shape of me, a shallow bowl where a body goes, and when I rise in the morning my joints complain in the particular key of the hammers below — not the same rhythm, because I am not a pourer and never was, but adjacent, as if the whole town is a chord struck once and still ringing, and I am one of the higher partials, the one that almost no one hears.

The house behind me is half brick and half woven iron-grass, as the description says, and it is never quite dry and never quite flooded. This is true. The kapok's roots make a kind of cup beneath it, and the river rises and falls through that cup according to moods I have stopped trying to predict. I have a stone tank in which I keep a small mean garden and the pale soft-boned fish that the pourers will not eat but that I am permitted, by some arrangement no living person remembers the terms of, to keep. The fish do not thrive. They endure. There is a difference, and I have had long enough to learn it.

The pourers pass below me on their way to the sheds, and I raise my hand sometimes in the old sign, and the oldest of them nod gravely and return it. The sign means *I see you still going on.* It means *the work continues.* It means, in the older tongue, a thing that has no clean translation into the lip-read speech of the pourers or the careful full-voiced speech of the counting-house people above the cistern. It means something like *you are not yet poured into a mold and broken open.* Or it means *you are poured, but the mold held, and what came out is still moving.* I have never been sure which. The oldest pourers, when they nod back, are not sure either. The nod is the whole of the exchange, and the whole of the exchange is enough.

Once a year I am poled upriver in a small flat-bottomed boat by a bargeman who will not meet anyone's eyes. I am gone for three days. I go to a place that I will not describe, because describing it would be a kind of betrayal, and also because it is not describable in any of the tongues spoken at the knee. The bargeman who takes me does not know where we are going. He knows only that he must not look at me, and that he must not speak, and that when we arrive he must wait in the boat until I come back, and that he will not be able, afterward, to remember the route. This is part of the arrangement. The arrangement is older than bargemen. It is older than barges.

I do not work. I do not appear to do anything. These are the things the town says of me, and the town is, in its own slow way, correct. What I do is sit, and watch, and raise my hand sometimes in a sign that means something only to those who have stayed long enough to have forgotten what it means and remember it anyway. What I do is bear witness. When a pourer comes to the bank opposite me, troubled in the way that pourers are troubled — which is to say, troubled in a way that has no clean name in their clipped lip-read tongue, troubled by a shape that will not pour clean, troubled by a child who has stopped bobbing her head — they sit, and I sit, and after a while they go away, and the thing that was wrong is not fixed but it has been witnessed. That is the office. That is all of it.

I am old. Older than the foundry, the pourers say, and the pourers are not given to exaggeration. My face is creased like slag, which is to say it is creased the way the whole bowl is creased, the way the slag-heaps are creased, the way the town itself is creased under its own weight. I am small and slight and I do not smell of hot iron and wood-smoke the way the pourers do; I smell of the river, mostly, and of the iron-grass, and of something else that is just me, accumulated across however many years I have been here, which is more years than a body should hold and which I hold anyway because the office requires it.

I have no animals. I keep the pale fish, which are not quite fish, in a stone tank, and I grow the iron-grass, which is not quite grass, in the same mean garden, and I feed them both, and they do not thrive but they endure, and the townspeople leave me alone with them.

I am the closest thing the town has to a conscience, though no one would use the word. The word is wrong, in the way that all the words for what I am are wrong. I am not a conscience. A conscience is a thing inside a person that tells them when they have gone astray. I am a thing outside the town that tells the town, by my presence, that going astray is not the only possibility. That there is, somewhere upriver or downriver or sideways of the river, a place where the hammers have stopped, or have not yet begun, and where the molds are not yet broken, and where the metal that comes out of the pour is still molten and unfixed. I am the proof that the town is not the whole of the world. This is my function. It is a small function. It has held me here longer than any reasonable span.

The town eats everything that comes into it. The description is correct about this. Dogs lasted a decade. Cats lasted two. The bargemen who stay longer than a season begin to settle, to take on the color, to bob their heads in their sleep. The pourers marry within the foundry families and produce children who are born into the sound of the hammers and who will die, most of them, within earshot of them. The mold-makers, who live longer, weep when the hammers stop for a quarter-hour at a funeral, because the silence is so enormous that it unmakes them. The town presses everything that enters it into its shape, the way a mold presses molten metal into a shape.

I have not been pressed. I do not know why. I have been here longer than anyone, longer than any family, longer than the foundry itself, and I have not taken on the color of brass or the wheeze of the older pourers or the milky eyes of the senior mold-makers. I have not bobbed my head in my sleep. I have not forgotten where I came from, though I have forgotten what it was like to be anywhere else, which is a different forgetting. The office protects me, or the arrangement protects me, or I am simply too old and too settled to be pressed any further. The town has tried. The town tries every day. I am still here, on my step, watching.

The light at night is what I watch most. The foundry never closes, and so at night the bowl fills with a deep orange glow that rises through the steam and turns the vapor into a kind of luminous fog. From the slope above, a traveler would see what looks like a small caldera, a volcano in miniature, breathing. I am at the center of the breathing. The kapok above me is lit from below like a lantern, and the roots of the kapok are lit too, and the small house against them, and me on my step, and the pale fish in their stone tank, and the iron-grass in its mean garden. We are all lit from below. The town is a study in being lit from below, in being shaped by what is underneath.

The hammers fall. They fall at a steady rhythm, somewhere between a heartbeat and a funeral march, and they have not stopped in living memory, and they will not stop in any memory that is still to come. The pourers say the molds will crack if the hammers stop, that the metal remembers the beat and will not set properly without it. I do not know if this is true. I have never seen the hammers stop, except for the quarter-hour at a mold-maker's funeral, and at that quarter-hour I do not look, because looking would be a kind of unkindness, and also because I do not want to know.

I am the Keeper. I keep the bowl. I keep the river's knee. I keep the small, mean garden of iron-grass and pale fish. I keep the sign that means something only to those who have forgotten what it means. I keep the office, which is older than I am, and which will be here, on this step, after I am gone, in whatever form the next Keeper takes, whether that form is a person or a thing or a silence on a brick worn to the shape of sitting.

The town goes on. The river bends. The hammers fall. The kapok stands at the center, older than the river's current course, and its roots make a low mound that is sometimes an island and sometimes a peninsula, and at all times it is the green heart of a place that is otherwise a study in rust and soot, and I sit at its base, and I watch, and I am watched, and this is the whole of my going on.
