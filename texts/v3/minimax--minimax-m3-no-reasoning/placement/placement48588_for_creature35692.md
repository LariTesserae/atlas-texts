# The Cistern-Keeper's Daughter

I am Yefra, and I keep the cistern of the standing stone.

---

I was not born in the upper rooms. I was born lower, in a room that still held the smoke of my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother's fire, in a building that was already old when the lichen on its north face had first decided to take root there. My mother tells me this. My mother tells me many things. My mother is one of the old who is consulted, and the consulting happens in the room where I was born, and the room is small and the fire is small and the smoke sits in the upper part of it the way smoke has always sat in the upper part of rooms in this city, and the consulting is done by people who come to my mother with their questions and who sit on the low stone bench and speak in low voices and leave with their questions answered or at least held.

I am not my mother. I am not a keeper of questions. I am a keeper of water.

---

The cistern of the standing stone is one of the covered ones. It is in the third courtyard of the building called the House of the Three Lintels, although the lintels in question are no longer three; one fell in the wind of the year before I was born and was never replaced, and the gap it left is now spanned by a wooden beam that came up in a herder's pack and that we have not been able to replace because the herders have not had a spare beam in the seven summers since. The cistern is round. The cistern has a diameter of seven paces and a depth of four. The cistern is lined with fitted stones that are still, after however many centuries, tight against each other, and the water that fills it is black and very still and very cold and is the water that I am responsible for.

I am responsible for it in the way that anyone here is responsible for the thing they have been given to look after. There is no title. There is no ceremony of becoming the keeper. There was a woman before me, who was the keeper for forty years, and she stopped being the keeper because her legs would no longer carry her down the ladder into the cistern to check the lining, and I was the one who went down the ladder instead because I was the one who was lightest and surest, and when the old woman died — taken down the cliff in her wool, given to the lower country — the cistern was mine, and that is how I became who I am.

I do not complain of this. I do not praise of this. The cistern is the cistern and I am its keeper and the matter is settled.

---

My day begins when the fog begins to lift.

This is the thing I have learned to read, more than the sun, more than the clock of my own hunger. There is a moment, in the morning, when the white that has been filling the courtyard begins to thin. The thinning starts at the top. The upper edges of the surrounding walls come into being first, dark against the white, and then the middle, and then at last the stones of the courtyard floor, and the cistern cover emerges from the fog the way a whale emerges from the sea, slow, in pieces, until it is whole. The cistern cover is a single stone slab, heavy, lifted off by two of us with the wooden bars that have been worn smooth by hands, and it is set aside against the wall and the morning air goes into the cistern and I lean over the lip and look down.

I look down because I am looking for the things that do not belong.

There is always something. A leaf. A small dead bird that has fallen from the eaves and has slipped through the gap that we do not know how to close. A bit of wool that has blown in. These things are taken out with the long-handled scoop of woven fiber that is the only tool of my work, and they are laid on the stones of the courtyard and left there for whoever wants them, because nothing in Lith-Khor is wasted and a dead bird is a thing that can be dried and added to the pot.

Then I taste the water. I taste it because no other way of knowing the water is reliable. I take a small amount in the cup of my hand, and I let it sit on my tongue for a count of three, and I am reading it the way my mother reads the weather. If the water is right, it tastes of stone and cold and a faint mineral bitterness that is the taste of this particular cistern and not of any other. If the water is wrong, it tastes of something else, something green, something that is alive when it should not be, and when the water tastes of that I know that the cistern is open to something it should not be open to and the lining must be checked and the crack found and the crack sealed with the mixture of dung and ash and crushed stone that is what we use for sealing.

I have had to seal cracks three times in the seven years I have kept the cistern. The third time was last autumn. The crack was in the second course from the top, on the western side, and the water that came through it was not water from the cistern itself but water that had run down the wall from the gutter above and had found its way through the joint, and the water that runs down the wall is not water that is meant for drinking. It carries with it the dust of the city and the small living things of the dust and the lichen-spores from the north face above. I sealed it. The water is right again.

The water is right this morning. I drink a mouthful, because the morning is cold and the water is colder and the cold of the water in the belly is a small waking that no fire can give. I lower the ladder. I go down.

---

The inside of a covered cistern is a particular place.

The light comes through the opening above, a column of pale morning light that falls onto the water and makes a circle of brightness on the black surface. The water is still except where the light touches it, and the light touching the water makes small rings that go outward and die at the edges of the cistern. The stones of the lining are dark and are beaded with the moisture that is always on them. There is a smell. The smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of cold water that has been still for a long time and of the stone that holds it. There is no other smell. There is no breath-smell, no dust-smell, no living-smell. The smell is the smell of a thing that is doing what it is supposed to be doing and is not being asked to do anything else.

I check the lining. I run my hand along each joint as far down as I can reach without going into the water, and I feel for the places where the mortar — the dung-ash-stone mixture — has begun to give. The feeling of a good joint is smooth and firm. The feeling of a joint that is beginning to fail is slightly soft, slightly yielding, the way a rotten place in a fruit feels before you can see it. I find no soft places today. The sealing of last autumn has held. I nod to myself. I climb back up. I replace the cover. The morning's check is done.

I have done this check every morning for seven years. I will do it tomorrow. I will do it the day after. The cistern does not change. The cistern is the most unchanging thing in a city of change.

---

The work of the day is not only the cistern.

There are the small tasks of the household: the bringing of water from the cistern for the cooking of the morning's porridge; the feeding of the small flock of goats that we keep in the lower room of the House of the Three Lintels, three animals, all of them known to me by name and by the particular way each of them has of looking at me over the low stone wall of their pen; the carding of the wool that is the work of the afternoons in the colder months and that I do at the small frame near the window where the light is best; the gathering of the lichens from the north wall of our courtyard in the season when the lichens are best gathered, which is after a wet spell when they have come away from the stone cleanly and before the next dry spell when they will have dried and curled and will not come away at all.

There is the walking of the city. There is the walking that I do almost every day, in the middle of the day when the air is warmest and the stones are drying, and that takes me through the dished streets past the leaning buildings, under the low lintels, across the polished cobbles of the roads that were roads before my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother was born. I walk the city because the cistern of the standing stone is not the only thing I have been given to look after. I have been given to look after, also, the cistern of the broken arch, which is in a building two streets over that has been mostly empty for a long time and that my mother still tends in her own slow way; and the cistern of the south court, which is the drinking cistern of the cluster of families who live in the buildings around the small plaza where the herders' path enters the city; and the cistern of the high place, which is at the top of the city near the western rim and which is an open cistern and is not for drinking but which is the cistern I most love.

I go to the cistern of the high place sometimes, in the afternoons, when the work is done and the wind has not yet come up. I sit on the worn stones at its edge. I look into the water. The water is dark and moves slightly in the wind and is not still the way the water of the covered cisterns is still, and I watch the small leaves that have fallen into it and that float on its surface and that go round and round in the small currents that the wind makes. I do not think when I sit there. I do not think in the way that thinking is done in warmer places, in long connected sentences of the kind that the traders are said to use. I think in pieces. I think in the way the water moves. The thoughts come and they go and they do not connect into anything that has a name and they are enough.

---

I have not gone down.

I have been to the eastern gate. I have stood at the gate and looked down at the lower country, at the green and the warm and the forests and the rivers and the distant smoke. I have watched the clouds come up the cliff in their slow white pour and felt the first wet of them on my face. I have felt the pull.

I will not say I have not felt the pull. The pull is real and is in the body and the body wants what the body wants. I have felt the pull in the spring, when the air is at its thinnest and the cold is at its worst and the green down below looks the most impossible green. I have felt the pull in the autumn, when the herders come up and they are tall and loud and they bring the smell of the lower country on them and the smell is the smell of growing things and of warm earth and of a life that is not this life.

I have not gone down.

I have not gone down because I am the cistern-keeper, and the cistern-keeper is the cistern-keeper, and the matter is settled.

---

There is a boy who comes to the cistern sometimes. His name is Tel, and he is the son of the woman who keeps the cistern of the south court, and he is eight years old, and he comes because his mother has asked him to bring me a message or because he has decided on his own that he wants to watch me check the lining. He sits on the lip of the cistern opening when the cover is off and he watches me go down the ladder and he watches me come up. He does not speak much. He is a child of the city and the children of the city are quiet. He watches with the particular attention of a child who is learning something that he will one day do himself.

I do not send him away. I show him the joints. I let him run his hand along the lining and feel the smoothness of a good joint and the slight yielding of a joint that is beginning to fail. I let him taste the water from the cup of my hand. He makes a face at the taste, the mineral bitterness, and I make a face back at him that is the same face his mother makes when he complains about the porridge, and he laughs, a small laugh, a laugh that is not loud because the children of the city are not loud.

He will be a cistern-keeper one day, or he will be something else. The matter is not yet settled. The matter will settle itself, the way matters settle in Lith-Khor, by the slow accumulation of the days and the slow shaping of the body to the work that the body is asked to do.

---

My hands are like the hands of the others. There is the dark line in the creases of my knuckles that does not come out with washing. My hands are wide and the fingers are short and the knuckles are large. My hands know the grain of stone. My hands know the give of damp wool. My hands know the temperature of water by the feel of the lip of the cistern when the brush against it in passing.

My hands are not quite clean. My hands will never be quite clean.

---

I am not sad. I am not happy. I am tuned. The tuning is so thorough and so long-established that the question does not arise.

The cistern is wet. The cistern is always wet. The water in it is black and very still and very cold. I taste it every morning and it tastes of stone and cold and a faint mineral bitterness that is the taste of this particular cistern and not of any other.

The city goes on. I go on with it.

The wet is on my hands. The wet is in my boots. The wet is in the wool of my coat. The silence is around me like another kind of weather.

This is enough. This is what I am for.
