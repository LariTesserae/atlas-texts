I am in the deepest pool. The water is at the loose bag of my throat, and the air above me is so wet that the line between them is a small thing. I have been here a long time. My mother was here. Her mother. The line of us runs back further than any of us can hold in a body.

I know this pool the way a body knows a thing — by the texture of the stone against the belly, by the way the water moves when something enters it from above, by the warmth of the shelf on the east side where the sun lies in the late afternoon. I know the cracks that hold the warmer water when the air cools at dusk. I know where the small fish go when the rains have come and the flats are joined, and where they are caught when the water withdraws and leaves them in their smallness. I know the sound the water makes against the stone when the wind moves across the pool, and the different sound it makes when one of the grazers comes to drink.

I am not small. I am the heaviest of the frogs in this pool, and the lowest voice. My throat is the loose hanging thing that fills and empties. When I call, the note is felt in the chest before it is heard. It goes out from me into the water and into the stone beneath the water, and it comes back to me changed, and I know the pool by that note the way you might know a house by the way your own voice sounds in it.

The smaller frogs stitch the upper register. They are everywhere — in the films of wet in the dimples of the stone, in the grass tufts, in the moss, on the wet trunks of the trees. Their voices are higher, faster, more frequent, woven through one another in a constant high weave. I add the ground note. I am the thing that holds the weave down.

The day is long, and the light is full but diffused, and it makes the wet stones glisten. I sit with only my eyes and the ridge of my head above the water, and the rest of me is held in the warm dark below. The insects hum — a thrum that comes from the stones themselves, that is everywhere, that is the base of all the other sounds. I feel it through the water against my belly. The frogs layer their chorus over it, rising and falling, never stopping. The birds call from the islands of tree — sharp, varied, going about their business. Somewhere in the marsh one of the grazers makes its low bellow, and the sound crosses the water to me.

I have heard all of this before. I have heard it a thousand times and a thousand times more. The sounds are not news to me. They are the place, the way the water is the place, the way the stone is the place. I do not listen to them the way a thing listens for something new. I listen to them the way a body listens to its own breathing.

A shadow crosses the pool. I do not move. It is one of the wading birds, long-legged, drawn in ink, that stands in the shallows for hours and then strikes. I have seen it take the small fish. I have seen it miss. It does not come for me. I am too large for it, and my skin is the mottled brown and olive of the stone, and I am still, and my stillness is my color.

The crouched predator is different. It is the brindled thing that hides in the reeds at the marsh edge. It takes the calves of the grazers and the young and the sick. It has taken frogs before — I have seen the wide, four-toed prints in the soft margins of the pool. When the wind brings its smell across the water, the grazers make their alarm-bark and the herd tightens. I go deeper. I let only my nostrils break the surface. I have outlasted predators before. I will outlast this one.

The rains come often. Sometimes for a week without stopping. Then the whole plateau runs with a film, and the pools merge into flats, and the water is everywhere, and I am not alone in this pool because the water from the higher places comes down and brings other frogs, other small fish, other things, and for a while the place is all one water. Then the rain stops. The water withdraws. The flats become marsh-edge again. The pools become pools again. I am alone in mine, or nearly so.

I do not leave. A frog born into a pool will likely die near it. I have never seen the far side of the ridge. I have never needed to. The pool is enough. The pool is a nation. It has its own bloodlines, its own chorus, its own weather in the small way a place has weather. I am of it, and it is of me, and the line between us is not a line at all.

The light changes. The warm light goes softer, and then begins to fail. The stones glisten again as the air cools and the moisture comes out of it and lies on every surface. The chorus is building. The smaller frogs have already begun. I will add my note soon. I will sit in the deep water and let the loose bag of my throat fill and empty, and the note will go out into the chorus and into the stone, and the place will sound the way the place sounds, and I will be a part of that sounding, and the part will not be small.

There are no marks here. No walls. No paths except the ones the animals have made. No tools. The place is entirely itself. It has been entirely itself for a long time, and I have been entirely myself in it, and the two are not different things.

The place is at the end of its wearing. The mountains that must once have stood are gone to swells. The stone has been polished to something close to its final form, and any further change is measured in a time longer than I have been alive, longer than my mother was alive, longer than the line of us can hold. The place is settled. It is loud with life. It is also at rest.

I am a part of the rest. The wetness is the constant. The chorus goes on, and I am in it, and the being in it is the same as the place, and the place is at the end of its wearing, and so am I, and so are we all, and the note goes out.
