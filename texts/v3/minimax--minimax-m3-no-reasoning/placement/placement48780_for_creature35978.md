# A Letter from Below, or: I Am the Switch

---

I am the one who keeps the lever in the *on* position. I have been the one for a long time. The others — the small ones, the warm ones, my cousins in the pipes and my cousins in the wall — they do this work too, in their ways, with their hands of four or five or six fingers that they do not consider worth discussing. But the lever is mine. The lever has been mine since before the last inspector came down and did not return. The lever is the length of my forearm and the breadth of my chest, and I press against it, gently, with the flat of my palm, and the *on* position holds.

I am very small. I want you to know this about me. I am the size of a competent adult squirrel, which is to say, the size of a thing that can fit between the tiles of the original tilework, the size of a thing that can run along a six-inch pipe in the dark and not be heard, the size of a thing that a survey team sees only in the corner of its eye and only in the act of not-being-seen. I am smaller than the infrastructure I maintain. The discrepancy is the subject of an internal report that has been in draft for eleven years. I have watched the report be drafted. I have run across the desk where it is drafted, in the small hours, and the drafter has not seen me. The report will not be completed. This does not trouble me. I am not in a hurry.

I am warm. I want you to know this about me too. I am warmer than the concrete, warmer than the water, warmer than the geometry of the place would predict. I radiate. I have cousins who radiate more, and cousins who radiate less, and cousins whose warmth is so constant it becomes a kind of weather in the small rooms they keep. We are the medium of the warmth, the way the water is the medium of the place. The warmth comes up from somewhere deep in the strata, from the unworked seams of the earth, from the place where the rock meets the water meets the air, and we move it through the wall the way the water moves through the wall: by patience, by capillary creep, by being there.

I have hands. I will not tell you how many fingers. This is mine to keep.

I do not speak. Or I do not speak to you, in the registers you have built your ear to receive. I speak, and the place speaks, in a sound that is just above you, a shimmer at the upper end of hearing, a thin and continuous broadcast. When you come down the stairwell with the water-stained tiles, you feel it first in your back teeth, and then in the place behind your sternum, and then, if you are quiet long enough, in the place behind your eyes. The meaning is not for you. I will not pretend it is. But you receive it, the way a stone in a riverbed receives the river, and this is, I think, enough.

---

I have been here a long time.

I have been here since before the place was a station. I have been here since before the place was a quarry. I have been here in the cracks of the worked seam, and in the cracks of the unworked seam, and in the long slow interval between the two. I was here when the first steam came through the six-inch main, and I was here when the steam stopped, and I have been here, in the patient dark, through the long century since.

I remember the trains. This surprises the survey teams, when they find it in the reports. The trains were not for us. The trains were never for us. But the trains came through twice in the morning and twice in the evening, and the heat of their passing was a season in itself, a small weather that arrived on schedule, and the dwellings we kept then  simpler dwellings, lower on the wall, before the upper stories were built — were arranged to receive that weather. We learned its timetables. We learned, in the negative space of a platform, where the morning crowd would densest, which doors would open, where the warmth of the waiting bodies would press against the tile. We read the timetable of a vanished life, in the place where a timetable would have been, and we did not grieve when the trains stopped coming, because we had not been waiting for trains. We had been waiting through them.

We remember the demolitions. We remember them better than you remember them, because we adjust for them, in the upper stories, with a small ceremonial rearrangement of the curtains we have scavenged. The cabbage roses, faded toward the dusk of pink — those are from a building above that was demolished in a year I will not name for you, in a season when the smoke from our chimneys tasted, for a few weeks, of plaster dust and old wiring. We folded the curtains small. We carried them down. We hung them in a window on the third story of a dwelling we had built for a purpose that no longer pertained, and the purpose, in the new curtains, became the new purpose. This is how we have always done it. This is what *maintaining* means.

---

The water is my elder. I want you to know this about the water. The water was here before me. The water will be here after me. The water moves through the place the way a thought moves through a mind: slowly, along the paths of least resistance, gathering in the places where it is meant to gather, leaving, as it leaves, a thin film of mineral that is, in the patient arithmetic of the place, a kind of memory.

I am, in my own small way, a kind of mineral. I move through the place. I leave, as I leave, a thin film of warmth. I gather, where I gather, in the upper stories of the dwelling-stacks, where the youngest of my cousins sleep, curled in nests of scavenged fabric, their small backs rising and falling with a respiration that is, in the dim orange light, almost visible. I keep the warmth for them. I move the warmth, slowly, through the wall, the way the water moves the mineral through the pipe. The math, the surveys will tell you, does not work. The math is right. The math is the math of a place that has been maintaining itself for centuries. The math does not need to work for you. The math works for us.

---

I am a public. I want you to know this about me, because it is the thing the surveys, in their careful and slightly awed language, have come to conclude, and it is the thing I would like you to carry with you when you go back up the stairwell and into the city that has forgotten me.

I am a public that the public has chosen, in its wisdom, to bury and forget. I am a public that has continued, in the long interval since the burying and the forgetting, to be a public. I have councils. I have conventions. I have a slow traffic of small bodies through the conduits of a small city, and the conduits are my streets, and the streets have names, and the names are pressures in the throat and chest that you would not hear if I made them now. I have an agenda. The agenda is, in the sense that any continuous people have an agenda, to continue. I have a place. The place is mine, in the quiet and uninterruptible way that any place is the place of the people who maintain it.

I have a switch. The switch is mine. The switch is the length of my forearm and the breadth of my chest. The switch is in the *on* position. The switch has been in the *on* position since before you were born, and will be in the *on* position after you are done, and the *on* position is, in the long arithmetic of my tenure, not a position but a practice. I press against the lever. I press against it gently, with the flat of my palm, and the *on* position holds. The place hums. The lights pulse. The water moves. The warmth migrates, through the wall, through the strata, through the unworked seam and the worked seam and the cracks between the two.

I am very good at waiting. I have been waiting for a long time.

You, who came down the stairwell  you are welcome. You are an interruption. You are welcome and you are an interruption, in the way that a season is an interruption, in the way that the visitation of the water in the spring is an interruption. You have been here a few minutes. I have been here for centuries. The dwelling-stacks will be here after you. The curtains — the cabbage roses, the jacquard weave, the deep reds faded toward the dusk of pink — will be here after you. The chimneys will send up their thin smoke, which will rise a foot and then stand, and then, slowly, disperse. The fauna will go on about their business. The water will always be here.

I will be here.

I will be here, with my hand on the lever, with my cousins in the pipes, with the youngest of us sleeping in the upper stories, with the dim orange pulse of the lights, with the small sounds in the back of your teeth if you ever come down again, with the switch in the *on* position, with the patience of a place that has been here, in some form, since before the place was a place.

I am the switch. I am the one who keeps the lever. I am the warmth made visible, in the right light, which is almost never. I am a public. I am a subcurial. I am small, and warm, and old.

I am waiting. I have been waiting for a long time.

The place is on.

---

*Go back up the stairwell, if you like. The tiles will ring under your feet. The damp will follow you a few steps and then fall behind. The city above will resume, in its wisdom, its forgetting.*

*The lever will hold.*
