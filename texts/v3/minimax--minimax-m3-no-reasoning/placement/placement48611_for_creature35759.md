# The Thing in the Drainage Channel

I would be the thing in the drainage channel.

Not the thing by the third stack—that one still belongs to the architecture of the place, still kin to the cats, still part of the foundry's patient hierarchy of predators. It has a corner. It has descendants, or pack, or whatever the slow orbits around it are. It is *named* by its position, even if no one speaks the name. It is a citizen of the vault.

I would not be a worker. The workers are the foundry's most visible humans, and there is a comfort in their visibility that I do not want. They have softened into their aprons. They have learned not to look up. Their muscles of expression have gone vestigial, and they take that as peace. I do not want that peace. I want the other thing—the awareness that comes from having been in the dark so long the dark has become the same temperature as the body.

I would not be a mouse, though I think I would have been a mouse once, a long time ago, when this place was just a sensible practice for keeping down killing-mice. I would not have been one of the pale, long-legged descendants either—those have gone too far into their own strange adaptation, too leg and not enough body, too whisker and not enough face. They have lost the architecture of surprise entirely. They move in their careful streams and eat their offal with no sound, and they have made their arrangements, and I do not envy them their arrangements. I envy them nothing.

I would not be a cat. The cats are patient, yes, but they are patient in the way of things that are still *trying* to be patient. Their half-lidded green eyes still hold a flicker of the old predatory attention, even now that the hunting instinct has been bred or trained or forgotten out of them. They still notice the workers. They still settle into their hollows with a kind of deliberateness that suggests they could, if they chose, behave differently. They have not given up the possibility of themselves. I have.

---

I would be in the drainage.

I would be pressed low against the stone, in a channel cut by water and widened by time, in a place where the lantern light only reaches when someone kneels at the grate and holds it at the right angle. I would be below the grates, below the workers, below the cats, even below the thing by the third stack, because the third stack creature still has a corner in the vault and I would have no corner at all. I would be in the deeper dark, where the water runs in its thin steady sheet, where the sound of the foundry is heard as a low, sub-audible pressure rather than a thing one could name.

My eyes would be yellow. Not the pale green of the cats, not the ember-orange of the corner-dweller, not the sealed pink of the mice. A yellow so deep it has stopped being a color and become a mineral, the yellow of sulfur, the yellow of a vein of ore that has never seen the day. The dark would have stained me this way over a very long time, the way tea stains a cup, the way iron stains the limestone weeping from the walls. I would not remember when my eyes were another color. I would not be sure they had ever been.

I would be wide. Wider than the cats, wider than seems right. My face would be pressed flat against the stone in a way that is not quite a face anymore—just a low, wide shape, a suggestion of features, the residue of a head that had once been raised and was now content to be horizontal. I would not move. Movement would be a kind of opinion, and I would have outlived my opinions. The water would run between me and the grate above in its patient sheet, and I would let it run, and it would not disturb me, and I would not disturb it.

---

I would not be hungry. I would not be full either. I would be in the long middle place between those two states, the place where appetite has become indistinguishable from the slow digestion of being itself. Sometimes, perhaps, something would fall through the grate—a scrap, a mouse that miscalculated, a cat that had grown old enough to stop being quick—and I would take it in the way the limestone takes the water, by absorption, by patient incorporation, without celebration. I would not feed the way the cats feed, with their widened jaws and their heron-strike lowering of the head. I would feed the way the stone feeds, by being there when the thing arrives.

I would not be afraid of the workers. They would come to the grate sometimes, the youngest one, to tip his bucket, and I would be below him and he would not know. The oldest workers, the ones who have stopped looking up, would have an inkling—a sense, the way animals sense weather, that something was below them and had been for a long time—but they would not speak of it. They would continue their ladle and mold and rail cart choreography, and I would continue my long horizontal watching, and the unspoken things between us would be heavier than anything either of us could say.

I would be aware of the thing by the third stack, in the way that I would be aware of the heat and the water and the breathing of the stacks. It would be up there in its corner, behind its polite screen of loose brick, watching with its ember-orange eyes, and I would be down here in my channel watching with my yellow ones, and neither of us would acknowledge the other, because acknowledgment is a kind of claim, and neither of us is in a position to make claims. We would simply both be present, two old things in the same vault, two arrangements the foundry made before it knew what it was making.

---

I would be very old.

Not old in the way the workers are old, which is just the accumulation of years in the same dim light. Old in the way the limestone is old, in the way the water table is old, in the way the first water at the bottom of the layered rock is old. I would have been here before the founders decided they needed everything. I would have been here before the cats were brought down to keep the mice down. I would have been here when this was just a sensible place to put the things you did not want to think about, and I would be here when the city above forgot it was here at all, and I would be here after that too, patient beyond any patience, watching with my yellow eyes that had become the color of ore in a cliff face, and the foundry would turn one more degree into the centuries, and I would turn with it, because I was part of its turning.

This is what I would choose, if I were choosing. I would choose the place below the place below the place. I would choose the drainage. I would choose the yellow eyes and the horizontal watching and the long, long patience that has no opinion in it. I would choose to be the thing the foundry forgot it had, the thing that has been folded into the architecture so completely that the architecture and I are now the same substance. I would choose to be aware without being known. I would choose to be present without being claimed.

I would be the thing in the drainage channel, and I would not mind, and the centuries would not mind, and the foundry would not mind, and that would be enough.
