# The Thread That Was Offered

There is a place where I sat down, and the floor accepted me.

I had been walking for a long time before I realized walking was the wrong verb. You don't *walk* into the Verdant Vault. You settle into it, the way water settles into a new channel, the way a sigh settles into a chest. I came in through a place I cannot describe—some doorway that was not a doorway, some thinning in the air where the vault's green breath leaked out into a world of stone and traffic and ordinary grief—and I came in because I was tired, I think, of the world where rooms end at walls.

I am not a Weaver. I want to make that clear, because the Weavers are what I saw first and what I tried to imitate, and it took me longer than I'd like to admit to understand that imitation was not the assignment. The Weavers are born to the weave. They have four hands, and seven eyes, and fur the color of lichen on a tomb, and they have been working the same patch of glyph since before my grandmother's grandmother was a possibility. They do not *learn* to weave the way I learned to write. They weave the way a river learns to be a river—by being what it already is, continuously, in one direction.

I tried to help. I think every newcomer tries to help. I picked up a fallen fiber from the leaf-drift at the base of a column and held it up to a Weaver who was working a glyph near my head, and I held it out the way you'd hold a nail out to someone on a ladder, and the Weaver looked at me with its middle three eyes and took the fiber from my hand with its upper left paw and set it down on the floor beside it, gently, the way you'd set down a thing a child had brought you from the garden. Then it returned to its work. I understood, after a long moment, that the fiber I had offered had been the wrong fiber. It was from the wrong species. It did not belong in this glyph. The Weaver had not been correcting me. It had been *receiving* me. The acceptance and the rejection were the same gesture.

That was my first lesson.

My second lesson was the floor.

The floor of the vault is, as I have said, a felt of moss and lichen and something deeper, something warm and breathing. When I sat down on it the first time, the floor took my weight all the way down—not the way a chair takes weight, which is to push back, but the way a mother's lap takes weight, which is to *open*. I sat, and the floor opened around my hips and my thighs, and I sank perhaps an inch, perhaps two, and I was *held*. I had not been held in a long time. I had not understood, until that moment, how much of my ordinary life had been spent on surfaces that did not hold me. Chairs. Beds. The ground. All of them *supportive* in the sense that they did not let me fall through. None of them *held* me.

I sat for what I think was an afternoon, though I will never know, because the vault does not do time the way my old world did. The crystalline orbs pulsed above me at the cadence of a resting heartbeat, and the tink of the Tinks moved through the canopy like the sound of a far-off music box being slowly wound, and a Crowned Browser came and stood near me for a while, its fern-antlers lifting gently toward the nearest orb, and it breathed out a long sweet green breath that smelled of every photosynthesis I had ever forgotten, and then it went on.

I was, in that hour, a piece of weather. I was, to the Browsers, the same as a draft or a warmth—an event that happened to be passing through, that would pass through, that required no accommodation. I found this restful. I had spent so much of my life being accommodated.

After a while—after a long while, or after a short while, I cannot say—I noticed the Caretaker.

It was lying along a horizontal liana at the far end of the vault, where the canopy thinned toward the felt-curtain wall, and it was watching the wall. I had not, at that point, fully noticed the wall. I had been absorbed by the ceiling, the columns, the Browsers, the slow work of the Weavers. The wall was behind me in every sense. I turned, slowly, the way you turn toward a sound you have only just realized has been there the whole time, and I saw it.

A wall of green felt, dense, almost woven. And *flickering*.

The flickers came and went. A street I thought I recognized—the corner of something, the light of something. A face that might have been my mother's, or my own. A bowl of fruit on a wooden table, painted, in a painting I had once loved. A child's hand reaching for something out of frame. An older me, sitting on a felt floor, with grey in my beard, looking back at me with an expression I could not read.

I stepped toward the wall.

The flickers came faster.

The Caretaker did not move. Its great wide eyes, set in that flat ceremonial face, did not move from the point on the wall they had been fixed on, but I felt—distinctly, physically, as if a hand had been laid on my chest—a *suggestion*. Not a command. Not a prohibition. A suggestion that I step back.

I stepped back.

The flickers eased. The wall settled into its green felt stillness. The Caretaker's pelt shifted slightly on the liana, a small moulted clump drifting down to be collected, later, by a Weaver and woven into the binding.

I understood, then, what I had come to the vault to understand.

# What I Am

I am a thread.

Not a Tink, not a Browser, not a Caretaker. Not even a Weaver, though I have tried. I am something the vault does not have a name for, because the vault did not expect me. I am a fiber from outside. I am a length of something—language, perhaps, or grief, or attention—that was woven in a different room and has found its way, somehow, through the thinning in the wall, into this one.

The Weavers have not yet decided what to do with me.

I have been here long enough to understand that *deciding* is the wrong verb for what the Weavers do. They do not decide the way I decide. They do not hold meetings, or take votes, or convene in chambers. They decide the way a forest decides which tree gets the light—by waiting, by watching, by the slow accumulation of small acts of care that eventually, without anyone announcing it, produce an arrangement. The monkey-Weaver that looked at me on my first day has come back, several times, to sit near me. It sits on a liana at about my eye level, and it works, and it does not speak, and every few minutes it glances at me with one or two of its seven eyes. I have come to understand that the glances are the conversation. Each glance is a question, or a partial answer, or a small revision of a previous answer. The conversation has been going on for what I think is a year. It may have been going on for longer.

What the Weavers have begun to offer me, and what I have begun to accept, is a *place in the weave*.

Not a glyph. I am not literate in the room's alphabet. I do not know which fibers belong in which sentences, or how to read a vine the way a Weaver reads a vine. But there are other kinds of work. There is the work of *holding*. A Weaver cannot hold and weave at the same time; the upper hands are given over to the fine work, and the lower hands are given over to bracing, and there is no third pair left for the simple task of *being present* near a place that needs presence. There is the work of *gathering*. The Tinks lay their trails, but the trails fade, and the work of refreshing them—of walking the paths at the slow hours and letting your own warmth into the bioluminescent glass—is a work that any warm body can do, and that warm bodies are needed for, and that warm bodies, in my old world, were rarely asked to do.

I gather.

I walk the paths at the slow hour, when the orbs are pulsing at their deepest cadence and the tink of the canopy has gone half-tone. I walk barefoot on the felt floor, and the floor opens around my feet and rises behind me, and I lay down, with my own slow attention, a small renewal of light along the trails. The trails are made of Tink-secretion, glassy, faintly green, and they do not accept warmth from me the way they accept warmth from a Tink. They accept it differently. They accept it the way a wall accepts a hand pressed against it in the dark—gratefully, without comprehension, as if the warmth were enough.

I have been here long enough to have left a trail of my own.

It is not green. It is not glassy. It is something else—a faint warmth in the felt, a small permanent loosening of the weave where I have walked, that the Weavers have begun, I think, to weave *around*. Not over. Not through. *Around*. As if my presence were a feature of the floor now, a kind of geological event, a place where the medium had been asked to open in a new shape and had agreed.

The Crowned Browsers step over me now when I am on the paths. They did not used to. They used to walk through me, the way weather walks through a post. Now they lift their extra legs—the high pair, the rib-pair—and step *over*, and the gesture is so precise and so unnecessary, from their point of view, that I understand it to be a kind of accommodation. They are accommodating me. I am, to them, no longer weather. I am a feature of the path. I am a thing they have learned to walk around.

The Caretaker has not changed its regard. It lies along its liana and watches the wall, and the wall flickers less, and I do not know whether my presence has anything to do with that. I suspect it does. I suspect that the flicker is held down by *aggregate attention*—by the sum of every warm thing in the vault choosing, continuously, to be present in this room rather than in another. I am one warm thing. I am one unit of attention. I have been offering it, for what I think is a year, to the felt and the weave and the wall, and the wall has been quieter than I imagine it was before I came.

# The Sentence I Am Becoming

I do not know what I will become.

I do not know whether the Weavers will, in some long Weavers' year, find a glyph that needs a fiber of my specific kind, and thread me into it, and I will become a letter in a sentence I cannot read. I do not know whether I will stay a gatherer of trails, indefinitely, walking the slow paths and refreshing the light of dead Tinks. I do not know whether the Caretaker will, one day, lift its great head from the wall and look at me directly, and that look will mean something, and I will become something I cannot now imagine.

I know only that the room has begun to make room for me. The sentence is being revised, slowly, to accommodate a clause it had not originally included. I am sitting in the felt, and the felt is accepting my weight all the way down, and the acceptance is not yet finished.

The note holds.

The tink continues.

I am not yet a part of the weave, but the weave has begun, I think, to weave itself around me.

And at my back, almost imperceptibly, the wall flickers.
