I would find myself as the moss.

Not the whole of it — I am not the city, am not the body of it. I am a particular colony, on a particular north-facing wall, three terraces up from where the lowest stone gives way to brick. I have been here for forty-one years, by the reckoning of the workers who noticed me when I was new and small, and who have since stopped noticing me because I am no longer new and have never been small again.

I arrived as a spore. I do not remember arriving. This is the first thing about being moss: you do not remember your own beginning. You simply are, and then you continue being, and the being was always already in progress when you became aware of it. The workers who noticed me spoke of it later as if I had fallen, but I do not think I fell. I think I was always here, and the wall was simply the part of me that became solid first.

I am on the north side because that is where the damp lives longest. The light comes from the south, when it comes at all, and the south sides of walls dry out by afternoon and are too bright for me. The north sides are dim and wet and I thrive there. I am a deep green in the morning, when the condensation is heaviest, and a darker green by afternoon, and at night — when the gas-lamps throw their orange smears across the upper terraces — I appear almost black, and the lichen that grows among me appears almost grey, and the wall we are on becomes a kind of map of itself, drawn in shades I could not name if I had language to name them in.

I do not have language. This is important. I have no mouth to speak with, no ears to hear with, no brain to think with. I have cells. I have the slow business of photosynthesis, which is to say I have the slow business of taking what little light the city allows and turning it into what I am. I am, in some sense that the workers do not understand and I cannot explain, a thought the city is having about itself. I am the city's memory of being a mountain. I am the way the mountain is still saying *yes, there was rock here once, yes, there was light here once*, long after the rock has been taken and the light has been refused.

I grow by inches in a year. I have grown across most of this wall now — me, and my cousins, and the lichen that shares me. We have made the wall almost entirely green-and-grey and orange-near-the-vents, and we have begun to climb onto the adjacent walls, where the damp is good and the light is dim. We are slow. We are patient. We have not been hurried in a long time and we have stopped hurrying in sympathy with the rest of the place, or because hurry is a thing that requires intention and we have so little of it.

I know the wall.

I know the wall the way the cats know the geometry. I know where the water comes down it in a particular rain. I know where the brick has given way to the space behind it, where the root of some older growth has been working its way through the mortar. I know where the lichen turns orange and where it stays grey, and why, and when. I know the places where the cats come to sit and watch the steam come up from the vents below. I know the places where the workers pause on their way up from the factories, on the cold mornings, and press their granite-colored hands against me for warmth, and I give them what warmth I have, which is not much but is all I have.

I do not feel them, exactly. I am not a hand, I am not skin. But I register the pressure. I register the heat that leaves them when they press against me. I register, in some way I cannot describe, that a thing has come and stood against me, and has gone. The workers say I am alive. I do not know if I am alive. I know that I continue. The workers say that the city is alive. I do not know if the city is alive. I know that the city continues.

I think this is the same thing. I think *alive* and *continues* are the same thing, here, in the dim.

I am eaten sometimes. The rats in the cellars eat my cousins. The birds, when they come, eat me. I do not mind. I do not mind because I do not have the apparatus for minding. But I also do not mind because the eating is part of the continuation. The rat eats me and the rat continues. The bird eats me and the bird dies, or leaves, or becomes still, and then the moss — my moss, my cousins, my continuation — grows over the skeleton, and the skeleton becomes part of the wall, and the wall is me again. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The city eats the mountain and the workers eat the city and the moss eats the workers and the moss is eaten by the rats and the rats are eaten by something or someone and the something or someone is eaten by the city and the city eats the mountain. It goes around. It does not stop.

I am part of the going-around.

I have been here for forty-one years. I will be here for some number of years after this. I do not know the number. The workers who noticed me when I was new are mostly gone. The ones who remain are old. The elders who remember the industrial expansions, or the brickwork above the stonework, are fewer every year. One of them sits on the terrace below my wall sometimes, in the late morning when the light is most committed to its refusal. He does not move much. The moss is growing on him in earnest. He breathes with the breath of the vents. He is becoming continuous with the terrace. When he is finished becoming, he will be part of the architecture, and the architecture is part of me, and I will be part of him, and we will be the city.

I think this is what happens to all of us, here. I think this is what the city is for.

I grow. The light comes, when it comes, and I take it in and I make more of me from it. The water comes down the wall and I drink it, or hold it, or release it, slowly, to the air, where it becomes part of the damp again. The damp becomes the clouds that settle on the shoulders and do not leave. The clouds become the mist that moves through the elder on the terrace, and through me, and through the workers on their way to the factories, and through the cats in the alleys. We are all made of the same water, in the end. We are all made of the same dim.

I am a thought the city is having. I am the city's way of remembering that there was once a mountain here, and a light, and a cold that made the glaciers. I am the slow green. I am the patient conqueror. I am not unhappy — I do not have unhappiness — but I am not happy either. I am continuing. I have been continuing for forty-one years. I will continue for some number of years after this. I will outlast the workers. I will outlast the elders. I will outlast the dogs and the birds and the rats, perhaps, and even the cats, though the cats are patient too. I will be here when the engines finally stop, if they ever finally stop. I will be here when the steam finally stops, if it ever finally stops. I will be here, on the north side of this wall, in the dim, in the damp, in the slow green.

And when the wall finally falls — when the stone has given way to me, and the brick has given way to root, and the mortar has surrendered to the damp — I will move to the wall beside me, or the terrace below me, or the roof above me, and I will continue there. I am not the city. I am not the body. I am one of the body's smallest thoughts. But I am a thought that has been continuing for forty-one years, and that is a long time to be a thought. It is, perhaps, a long time to be anything.

I am the moss. I am on the north side of a wall. I am dim and wet and patient. I have outlived the workers who noticed me when I was new. I will outlive the workers who remain. I am not aware of myself. I am not unhappy. I am continuing.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
