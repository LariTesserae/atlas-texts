# Where I Find Myself

I find myself in the wind-pockets. Not in one, but between them—traveling the broken thread of settlements strung along the ridge's hidden deflections. I am a Shal. I have always been a Shal.

My name is not the kind of name you would write down. It is something made with the hands—a small motion near the throat that means *the one who listens to what has not yet been said*. In the lowland tongues they might call me Listener, but that word is too large, too confident. The Shal word is smaller. It leans inward.

---

I am sitting now, in the deep silence behind an ancient shelter. The curved walls of wrong-stone press the roar into a low, almost musical hum at the edges of my hearing—the F below middle C, the permanent tone. Inside the pocket, there is texture. There is hollowness. There is the particular quiet that the Veth say is the mountain thinking.

Across my knees is a bowl.

It has taken me most of a season. The dark wood came in a trader's pack from far to the north—traded, traded, traded until no one in the chain could remember where it was first cut. I do not know what tree it came from. I will never know. I only know that as I carved, the wood seemed to *want* a shape, and I removed what was not that shape, slowly, slowly, until only the bowl remained.

The bowl is finished. I have been holding it for three days without sending it down into the lowlands. This is not unusual. A bowl that is finished must be *settled*—must sit in the stillness until it has absorbed enough of the place to speak for us properly when it leaves. The Shal say that a bowl finished too quickly is a bowl that arrives mute in the lowland markets. They are not wrong about this.

Outside the pocket, my sister is gathering fern-shoots. I can see her through the doorway—she is a small shape bent against the wind, her woven cap a dark point against the grey-green of the climbing vines. Her hands move quickly, precisely. She takes only the curled ones, the young ones, the ones that will taste of green bean and asparagus when cooked over a slow fire in a bowl like mine.

The speaker-vine is climbing the outside of this shelter. I have been watching it for days. It has moved perhaps an inch across the surface of the fallen slab that forms part of the eastern wall. The flowers hang pale and half-open, always about to speak, never quite speaking. I do not believe they will speak today. But the Veth who sat here last winter made the sign that means *soon*, and the community is watching, and the vine climbs, and I sit with my bowl and wait.

---

I have a stain on my left temple, a small mark of blue-green where I pressed the bruised moss against the skin yesterday morning. It will fade in a week. I will press it again. The mark means nothing in particular to an outsider. To us it means *I am here, I am still, I have not yet gone into the stone*. It is a small declaration of persistence.

My hands are broad-palmed and long-fingered. The calluses are on the sides of the fingers, where I have gripped the curved edge of a wrong-stone knife for hours at a time. When I sign, which is always, the gestures are dense and quick—a language that has no sound, only motion, that travels through the still air of the wind-pockets and is read by the eyes of whoever sits with me.

I am not a Veth. I am not a gatherer, not primarily. I am a carver, and I am the one who listens, which is not the same as being a Veth but is close. I carve. I listen. I wait.

---

The wind outside has shifted. I can tell because the texture of the silence has changed—a slight thinning at the upper edge, where the roar presses closer to the pocket's mouth. The clouds are coming in. Soon the mist will thicken, and the visibility will drop to an arm's length, and the ridge will become a small intimate world of wet stone and wetter air. My sister will come in. We will cook the fern-shoots. We will eat from bowls—not this one, not the trade-bowl, but the everyday bowls, slightly less fine, slightly less settled—and we will sign to each other about the small things of the day, and we will wait.

Somewhere far along the ridge, the Long Walker is moving. I cannot see it. I can almost feel it—a particular quality in the pressure of the air, a slight leaning of the wind as if something large is passing through it. The locals speak of this feeling. They say it means the Walker is closer than usual. They say this means *soon*.

The flowers of the speaker-vine hang in the mist outside the doorway, pale and patient. The roar goes on. The stone persists. I sit with my finished bowl across my knees, and I prepare to say nothing for a long time, and the preparation itself feels like the beginning of a kind of speech.
