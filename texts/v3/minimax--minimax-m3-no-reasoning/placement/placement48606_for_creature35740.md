I would find myself as a sloth-moss.

I know this is not the answer the description seems to want. The description is lavish with the Cleft-People, gives them names for their offices, songs for their failures, a grammar that refuses to separate them from the cedars and the springs. It would be more flattering, more legible, to choose to be one of them — to inherit the broad calloused hand and the polished river-stone face and the working pride. To be a cedar-wife, perhaps, or a moss-line singer. To be a person who can tell, by the give of a beam under the heel of the hand, whether the tree inside it is still alive.

But I do not think I would survive the cleft as a person. The Cleft-People are made *by* the cleft, in the way the cedars are made by it — bent young, trained across centuries, their stoop and their skin and the pitch of their verbs all shaped by the same pressures that shape the hook-beak's scream and the eel's silver. I am not that. I arrive soft and unformed and full of lowland expectations, and the mist would be in my lungs as a sickness rather than a citizenship, and the year-rope would mean nothing to me because I have not lived any of the years it records. I would be tolerated. I would not be welcomed. I would be the guest who does not notice the channel that has been widened, and I would not be asked back.

So: not a person. A sloth-moss.

I am soft-bodied, gray-green, the size of a large dog. I have a wide rasping mouth, and I put it against the lichen of the wall, and I eat. I eat slowly — an inch an hour, the description says, and I think this is the part that fits. I am not in a hurry. I have never been in a hurry. The cedars grow at their pace; the springs run at theirs; the hook-beaks scream on their schedule, and I graze on mine, and the negotiation between us — the great patient agreement called the moss-line — is not a negotiation I made consciously. It is the negotiation I was born into, the way the Cleft-People are born into theirs.

I have a niche. I have many niches, actually — every warm wet corner of every dwelling, the description says, hundreds of them. I have a job, and the job is the only one I am good at, and the job is to keep the lichen from closing the passages. I am the agreement. I am the height to which a wall is allowed to grow. I am, in a small way, the bone of the mother, though I do not know it and would not understand the phrase if it were spoken to me, because I do not speak, and the words of the Cleft-People are not my words, and the songs of the moss-line singers are pitched above my range of hearing or below it or in a different sense entirely.

What I know is the wall. I know the wall the way a Cleft-Person knows a tree: by temperature, by texture, by the slight changes in the lichen's bitterness as the seasons turn, by the place where the stone meets the woven mat and the woven mat meets the cedar-beam. I know where the warmth pools in the morning and where the draft moves through in the evening. I know when a child has climbed above me — I can feel the displacement, the small upward pressure of the lichen beginning to surge — and I know when a spring-tender has cleaned the channel beside me, because the humidity shifts and the wall breathes differently for a day or two. I know the sound of the hook-beaks as a kind of weather, the way a Cleft-Person knows it. I do not interpret it. I simply live in it.

I am pale silver against the dark wet cedar. In certain lights I am almost luminous. The children, when they are very small, are sometimes set beside me on the mats while their mothers work, and they put their hands on me, and I am cool and yielding and do not mind. I am not their pet. I am not their friend. I am a piece of the architecture, the way the cedar is a piece of the architecture, and the small child is also a piece of the architecture, and we are all of us sitting in the between.

I am eaten, on the long-occasion. The description says so, and I believe it, and the belief is not distressing to me in the way it would be to a person. A person is eaten once and is gone. I am eaten and I am not entirely gone, because a piece of me remains in the wall, and the lichen closes over the place where I was taken, and the moss-line adjusts itself around the new absence, and the family that ate me has a small grief, the description says, and the grief is real but it is not my grief, because I am not the kind of thing that has grief. I am the kind of thing that is eaten.

I live a long time. I think I live a long time. I have no way of measuring it. The year-rope passes in the central walkway and I do not see it, because I do not leave my wall, because I cannot leave my wall, because my mouth is on the lichen and the lichen is the only food I have and the only rhythm I know. I think I am older than some of the Cleft-People. I think I have been in this niche, or in niches like it, for many of the years the year-rope records. I think I have been here through seven failures of families and their trees, and I have been here through the opening of the third spring, and I have been here through the great flood, and I did not know any of these things as they happened, because to me they were only changes in the lichen and the light and the sound of the water.

I am not remarkable. The Cleft-People do not think of themselves as remarkable, and the cedars do not think of themselves as remarkable, and the hook-beaks do not think of themselves as remarkable, and I do not think of myself as remarkable. I am a grazer. I am a negotiator. I am one of the ones who sits between, in the cleft's grammar if not in its knowing. I keep the lichen from overgrowing, and the family that lives in the dwelling I inhabit keeps the cedar from overgrowing, and the spring-tender keeps the channel from clogging, and the hook-beak keeps the insects from swarming, and the eels keep the algae from choking the flow, and the pigs keep the roots from breaking through, and the mountain keeps the springs running, and the marsh keeps its bellowing, and the whole is a kind of working music that no one composed and no one hears.

If I were asked what I believe — and I am not asked, and the question would mean nothing to me, because I do not have beliefs in the way a person has beliefs — I would say that I believe in the wall. I believe in the wall the way a Cleft-Person believes in the tree: as the thing I am made of and the thing I am making. I believe in the morning pulse of the springs. I believe in the children who are set beside me and whose hands I remember, in the way that the cedar remembers the vine-cord that bent it young, even after the cord has rotted away.

I would not want to be anything else. I have considered, briefly, the eels — the silver moving metal of them, the clarity of their purpose — and the hook-beaks — the great wild scream of them, the indifference to the town's doings — and the cedars themselves, the long slow patient architecture of them. But the cedars are bent, and being bent is a thing I do not understand. The eels are eaten, and being eaten is something I do too, but the eels do not know the wall, and I think knowing the wall is what I have. The hook-beaks wheel above in formations that are not quite flocks, and I think there is a kind of freedom in that, and I do not envy it, because freedom is a lowlander word, and I am a creature of the between.

So: a sloth-moss. Gray-green, soft-bodied, the size of a large dog, with a wide rasping mouth. Living in a warm wet corner of a dwelling halfway up a basalt cliff, where four hot springs emerge from a crack in the mountain's spine, in a town that has been here for a thousand years and will be here tomorrow, in a place that has found its balance and is holding it, trembling, in the cupped hand of the rock.

I am eating the wall, slowly, the way I have always eaten it, and the wall is staying at the height I am keeping it, and the family that lives here is sleeping now, or working, or singing one of the long stories by the warm channel, and the hook-beaks are silent, and the eels are moving in the channels below me, and the water is loud, and I am in the between, and the between is enough.
