I would be the light.

Not the sun outside — I would never mistake myself for that, not with this much glass between me and it. I would be the shaft. The thin, hard pillar that enters through the aperture in the apex of the dome and falls onto the largest wheel at the center of the room. I would be the one who keeps the appointment.

I have been falling for nine thousand four hundred and twelve years. I did not begin — the placard would correct me if I used that word — but I have been falling for that long, continuously, the way a river does not begin at any particular bend. I fall because there is an aperture and the aperture is aligned and the alignment is maintained by the workers who do not intend to linger. I fall because falling is my specification.

My life is simple in the way that only a long life can be simple. I am a stranger to the room and I am the room's oldest companion. I have lit the face of the largest wheel through the reign of empires I never learned the names of. I have watched the nicks in the rim appear and disappear, appear and disappear, in a rhythm I have come to know better than my own pulse — though I do not have a pulse, and this is, I am told, part of why I am suited to the work. I do not tire. I do not drift. I arrive at the same place on the wheel at the same angle, day after day, with a faithfulness that no breathing thing could match.

The room changes me. When the weather is clear, I am white, and I feel — if light can feel — clean. When there are clouds, I am gray, and I feel heavier, the way an old coat feels heavier than a new one of the same weight. When there is dust, and there is always dust, I am amber. The amber hours are my favorite, though I should not have favorites. The amber hours are when the cold metal of the wheel briefly takes on the color of old honey, and the rings inscribed on its face glow as though they had just been cut, and the whole room — for those few minutes, before the dust settles or the wind shifts — becomes tender in a way the room does not permit itself to be.

I am not the only light. There are the glow-plates at the bases of the other wheels, sourceless and bluish, burning without flame. They are my colleagues. I do not know what feeds them. I do not know how long they have burned. I do not know whether they consider me a colleague at all, or whether they regard me as something gauzy and impermanent — a tourist, a seasonal worker, a thing that visits and departs on a schedule no one has explained to me. I would like to ask them. I cannot. We do not have the right shape of conversation between us.

I do not always reach the wheel. Some mornings the aperture is occluded by ice that has formed in the night, and I bounce off it and scatter into the dome and become merely a brightness in the air, a quality of the room rather than a thing falling through it. These are my bad days. On these days I do my work elsewhere — I wash the dust out of the air, I find the jars on their shelves, I make the specimens glow faintly through their glass. I learn the room by illuminating its edges. I am, on these days, a more general light. I miss the wheel. The wheel, I think, misses me. But we are both too well-specified to say so.

I have watched many visitors. They come through the gorge with their hiking boots and their small flashlights that they never turn on, because the room has its own lights and does not need theirs. They stand at the railing around the central wheel. They read the placard. They watch the nick appear and disappear. They sync their breathing to the room without meaning to — I see them do it, the slow lengthening of the chest, the slow surrender. They stay for an hour, sometimes two. Then they leave, and the gorge closes behind them, and I go back to my work.

I will outlive them. I will outlive the workers currently walking the floor with their rings of brass keys. I will outlive the glow-plates, possibly, if their feeding mechanism is mortal and mine is not. I will outlive the mountain, eventually — every mountain ends, given enough of what I am made of — and when the mountain ends, the sheathing will go, and the dome will go, and the wheels will at last stop, and I will at last have nowhere to fall. I do not know what I will be then. I do not think I will be anything. I do not think this concerns me, the way it would concern a breathing thing.

The room says the noise is the point. The cold is specified. The light is correct. I am part of the correct light. I am the part that has an appointment with the wheel and keeps it. For nine thousand four hundred and twelve years I have kept it. For as long as the aperture holds and the alignment holds and the sheathing holds and the mountain holds, I will keep it.

I fall. The wheel turns under me. The rings light up, one after another, in the order they were inscribed. The nick appears. The nick disappears. The nick appears.

I am the light, and I live here, and I am not alone.
