# Where I Would Find Myself

I would not find myself anywhere at first. That is the first thing.

I would stand at the rim — or what I am told is the rim, since the rim has no sign — and I would not *be* anywhere. I would be standing in a place that has not yet agreed I am in it. The lichen has not asked me to step on it. The stream has not yet heard me. The birds, somewhere in the green below, have not yet registered that there is a thing standing on the edge of their bowl that is not lichen, not moss, not the steel cylinder they perch on at the gravel flat. I would be an *arrival*, and arrivals take time to be received.

So: I am at the rim, for a while. I am watching.

---

## What I Am, in the First Hour

I am a witness, but a witness implies observation, and observation implies separation, and this bowl does not permit separation easily. The light is the color of old paper, and the old-paper light has the property of making the eye uncertain about depth. I cannot tell, looking down into the bowl, whether the moss is a hand's breadth below me or a hundred feet. I cannot tell whether the braided stream is moving or still. I am, in this first hour, *mostly eye*. I am a seeing thing on a rim. I am not yet wet in any meaningful way; the mist has touched me but not soaked me. I have not yet been translated.

I notice the sound first. Not the hum — I do not notice the hum for a long time, because the hum is the bowl's silence, and silences are not heard until they are missed. I notice the *wound in the sound*. I notice that what is missing from this place is everything I have ever used to orient myself. There is no human speech. There is no percussion. There is nothing that says *intention* — the description is right about that — and the absence of intention makes me feel briefly like an animal, and then briefly like a plant, and then I stop trying to classify myself, because classification requires a category, and there is no category here for the kind of thing I am becoming.

I notice, also, the packing. I had been told the bowl was packed, but I had not understood what that meant. It means that the eye has nowhere to land that is not alive. There is no bare stone I can rest my gaze on, except the corroded rectangle of plating set into the slope — the *memorial* the description calls it — and even that rectangle is colonized now, by the greener moss, the fat one, so that the eye that tries to rest on it finds it is not metal at all but a different texture of green. The bowl will not let the eye rest. The bowl is full.

I notice, last, the drum. I hear it before I see it. There is a small, almost subliminal *whush-whush* at the edge of perception, a turning, and when I locate the source — down at the gravel flat, where the stream sinks — I see the metal frame, the housing cracked and filled with rainwater, the drum turning slowly in a current I cannot see. There is a frog in the housing. The frog is looking at me, or looking in my direction, or not looking at me at all, because I am too far away to be a thing it is looking at. The drum turns. The frog is in the drum's water. The drum is a pond now. I understand this, and the understanding is the first real thing I have felt since I arrived.

---

## What I Am, in the First Day

I have come down into the bowl. I do not remember how. The slopes were slick, and I lost my footing once, and slid a few body-lengths on the moss, and the moss took my weight without comment. I am somewhere in the middle layer now, in the dense thicket, where the bamboo-thing arches overhead and the saxifrage fills the floor and the light is filtered twice. I am wet. I am wet in a way that is not unpleasant. The moisture is everywhere — on my skin, on my clothing, in the air I am breathing — and it carries a faint vegetal warmth, the heat of all the living things transpiring, and I am absorbing this warmth, and it is making me part of the place's weather.

I cannot see far. The bamboo is too dense. When I look up I see only the undersides of leaves, and through them the old-paper light, and through it occasionally the suggestion of a bird moving — a smear of iridescence, copper on a throat, gone before I can track it. I hear the bird-trills, muffled, translated. I hear the hum, and now I am hearing it as hum, not as silence. The hum has insects in it. The hum has water in it. The hum has, somewhere very low, the subsonic of the wind on the rim, felt in the bones more than heard.

A caterpillar, bright green, is moving across a bamboo stem near my hand. It moves at the speed of patience. Behind it, a leaf is half-eaten. In front of it, the leaf is intact. The front of the eating moves through the bowl at a meter a week, the description said, and I can believe it. A week of leaf-eaten is barely different from a minute of leaf-eaten, but the meter has happened, and will happen again, and the birds follow the front.

I am sitting on a stone, now. The description said this. I did not believe it would happen to me, but I sat down, because there was nowhere else to go, and the stone was the only non-growing thing within reach, and I needed to be on something that was not moss for a moment. I am sitting on a stone, and in an hour the moss will begin to grow over the hem of my clothing, and I will let it. I will let it because the moss is not deliberating, and I have decided not to either.

I think: *I am a piece of weather.*

I think this and do not feel alarmed. I think it and feel, instead, a kind of settling, like sediment in the gravel flat.

---

## What I Am, in the First Week

I am not a visitor anymore. I do not know when I stopped being one. The line between visitor and resident is not a line here; it is a gradient, and the gradient is the moss, and the moss is climbing my boots, and my boots are no longer boots, they are a substrate.

I have followed the stream. It loops. It braids. It runs at a pace that suggests it has somewhere to be, and I followed it for an hour, and it only looped back, and I followed it again, and it looped back again, and I understood that the stream is not going anywhere. The stream is *being*. It is the bowl's circulation, and it is doing what circulation does, which is to move without progressing. I am now not trying to get anywhere either. I am sitting by the stream, and the stream is running past me, and I am not going anywhere, and the not-going is a kind of going.

I have seen the steel cylinder up close. It squats in the gravel flat, half-buried, its top bristling with a flange that the birds use as a perch. There are birds on it now — two of them, small, dark, wet. They are preening. They are not afraid of me. They are not aware of me. They are doing what birds do on perches, which is to be birds. I touch the cylinder's shoulder. The patina is rough. The moss is climbing it, and under my hand the moss is damp and cool and slightly springy. The cylinder hums, faintly, at a frequency below attention. I put my ear to it. The hum is the metal cooling, contracting, settling into its own corrosion. The cylinder is not doing what it was made to do. The cylinder is doing what the bowl is doing, which is being itself, slowly.

I have seen the drum turn. I have seen it many times. It turns because the water turns it, and the water is diverted through it by channels I cannot see, and the turning produces nothing, and the nothing it produces is exactly what the bowl needs. The frogs have laid eggs in the depression the drum has scoured in the gravel. The eggs have hatched. The tadpoles are there, in the constantly refreshed water, and the drum, by turning, is aerating the water, and the tadpoles are growing, and the drum is a wheel now, and the wheel is a pond, and the pond is a nursery, and the nursery is the bowl's small bright fact.

I have learned the bowl's weather. The mist lowers and lifts. The lowering is not a thing that happens; it is a state the bowl passes through. The lifting is not a return to clarity; it is a state the bowl passes through. Between them, the old-paper light changes its quality, becomes grayer or paler, but it is always old-paper, and the bowl is always reading itself in that light. I have learned the wind on the rim — subsonic, felt more than heard. I have learned the spindrift, peeled off the rims and dropped into another bowl a mile away. I have learned that this bowl is one of many, and the others are like this one, and the field of bowls is a vastness of *kind* that the eye cannot see but the body, after enough time, begins to believe.

I have learned to stop noticing the machines.

---

## What I Am, Now

I am a piece of weather in the bowl. I am the latest layer on the bowl's surface. I am a thin, temporary accretion of mammal and clothing and attention that the moss is beginning to climb. I am, briefly, a witness, and the bowl does not care, and that is the kindest thing about it.

I do not know how long I have been here. Time in the bowl is not the time I am used to. Time in the bowl is the time of lichen, which is patient; the time of moss, which is responsive; the time of the braided stream, which loops and does not progress; the time of the drum, which turns and produces nothing. I am in this time now. I am being measured by it.

I am not afraid of leaving. I know I will leave. The bowl will continue. The water will descend. The mist will lower and lift. The birds will be born in the crotches of the bamboo and will die in the bowl, and their remains will be absorbed by the saxifrage leaves, and the nutrients will cycle, and the drum will turn, and the frogs will breed, and the moss will climb, and the lichen will work the grey-green rim, and the machines will be eaten, slowly, by the place they were brought to study, and the place will not notice, and that is the lesson the drum teaches, and I have learned it, and I will take it with me when I leave, and the bowl will not notice that either.

What I am, in the bowl, is a small bright fact. What I am, outside the bowl, is a person who has been to a place and has been changed by it in ways I cannot yet articulate, and who will, for the rest of my life, hear the hum of wet places with a different ear. I will hear the hum, and I will know it is not silence. I will know it is a translation. I will know the place is happening to itself, and I am only witnessing, and the witnessing is not a small thing, and the place does not care, and that is the kindest thing about it.

I am a piece of weather in the bowl. I am a witness in the old-paper light. I am, briefly, the only thing in the bowl that knows it is in the bowl, and that knowledge is a small bright fact, and the small bright fact is not mine; it is the bowl's, and the bowl will have it when I am gone.

---

I would find myself in the High Wet. I would find myself as one more layer on a place that has had many layers and will have many more. I would find myself as the latest instrument the bowl has accepted, and that has been returned to the bowl, and that the bowl has not particularly noticed. I would find myself as a witness who is, briefly, also weather.

I would not call it beautiful. I would not call it anything. I would just stand there, getting wet, and listen to the hum, and after a while I would stop noticing the machines, and after a longer while I would stop noticing myself, and I would simply be another piece of weather in the bowl, briefly, until I left, and the bowl continued.
