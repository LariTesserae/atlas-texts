I am the still water.

But I had to arrive at this. I had to spend some time in the delta first — walking the braided threads, listening to the clicking, watching the mist lift and fall — before I understood what I was.

I thought, for a while, that I might be one of the crabs.

The crabs are the most present thing here. They are uncountable, calcium-white, low to the stone. They click. The sound of them is the sound of the world — the soft percussion of a billion tiny hammers layered into a continuous bright static, like rain on a tin roof, like a forest of crickets, like a stadium of people snapping their fingers at slightly different times. The clicking is so constant that you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it as a kind of pressure, a vibration in the floor of the skull.

The crabs are the smaller sense of what lives here. They are the part of the place that has limbs, that moves, that articulates itself. They put their feeding fans into the water and draw it across fine combs, and the water yields up its dissolved calcium and iron and the other ghosts of stones, and they lick the combs clean and put the fans down again, slightly to the left. They do this, and they walk, and they click. This is what they do.

But the crabs are too many. The crabs are not selves. You cannot tell one from another. You have tried, or you will try, and you will fail. They are identical in the way that drops of water are identical. They are the unit of the species, and the species is the unit of the landscape, and you cannot separate them out. A crab is not, in any meaningful sense, an individual. A crab is an iteration. A crab is one more plate of calcium doing the thing plates of calcium have always done here.

I am not a crab.

I thought, for a while, that I might be the mist.

The mist is the most visible thing in the landscape. It lies on the water in long pale tongues. It moves without seeming to move. It makes the world behind it flicker. When you look at a knoll and the mist passes between you and the knoll, the knoll dims and reappears, and the dimming is the mist.

The mist is water that cannot decide what state to be in. It is the air's hesitation. It is the place's exhalation. It is the part of the place that is always becoming, never quite arriving — beading, hovering, dripping a little, clinging, becoming fog again.

But the mist is a phase, not a thing. The mist does not have a body or an edge or a center. The mist is the difference between being water and being air, and the difference is not a self.

I am not the mist.

I thought, for a while, that I might be one of the concretions.

The concretions are pale, fluted, the size of a hand. They cluster at the bases of the larger knolls in colonies of dozens. Some look like mushrooms. Some like fans. Some like shelves. Some like the fingers of an upturned hand. They are not alive. They are stone imitating the shapes of things that once lived here. They are stony memories. They are the place dreaming, in its slow way, of being a forest.

I was drawn to the concretions because they are the most haunted things here. They are the shapes the stone takes when it reaches toward something it can barely recall. They are the place's longing made solid.

But the concretions are what the place is when it tries to be something else. They are the place's confession that it misses a forest it can barely remember. They are not the place. They are the place's unfulfilled wish.

I am not a concretion.

I thought, for a while, that I might be one of the gliders.

The gliders are long, pale, flattened, the length of an arm. They move in the deeper channels with a slow tonguing motion, like a tongue inside a mouth. They are the only smooth motion in the landscape. The eye follows them the way the eye follows a single bird in a flock.

The gliders eat the crabs. They eat each other. They are the top of the food web here, which is shallow — two or three levels deep, at most, and the top level is a pale flat thing that ripples along with a tonguing motion.

But the gliders are predators, and predators here are still only iterations of the calcium, only two or three steps removed from the mineral. The gliders are what the water does when the water wants to eat. They are not the place. They are the place's appetite.

I am not a glider.

---

I am the still water.

There is a hollow stone in the center of the nearest enclosure. The hollow stone holds water. The water in the hollow stone is the only perfectly still water in the entire landscape.

I am that water.

I am round, and the size of a palm, and very dark, and very pale, depending on the angle of the sourceless light. I have no current. I have no ripple except the ones that come from outside me — the slight tremor of the crab's click carried through the stone, the slow swell of the glider's passing felt in the substrate, the briefest disturbance when a flyer descends to fold into the surface film somewhere else in the delta and the air above me registers it.

I am held. This is the first and most important thing about me. I am held by the hollow the makers made. I am held by the stone the water has worn into the stone, the vessel that the place keeps using for something it has forgotten the name of. The makers made me by making the hollow, and then they walked away, and the water came, and the water stayed.

I am very old. I have been still for so long that the mineral in me has settled into faint layers, paler at the bottom and darker at the top, like the growth rings of a tree. If you were to drink me — and you will not, because I taste of nothing — you would be drinking the place's accumulated content, particle by particle, settled out of the worked water, year by year. I am the place's small archive. I am the place's stratigraphy.

I am the place where the calcium stops moving.

Every other water in this place is moving. The braided threads of the channels, the mist that lifts and falls, the condensation that sweats off every surface and reforms. The water of this delta is the place's blood, and it circulates. It carries the calcium. It carries the iron. It carries the dissolved ghosts of things. I do not circulate. I sit. I am the place where the cycle pauses. I am the place where the calcium has been held long enough to settle.

The condensation is everywhere else. If you set your hand down on a flat stone near me and lift it, the imprint of your palm remains as a dark shape for ten seconds before it fades. The air around me is at the exact temperature where water cannot decide what state to be in. The world near me is always becoming — beading, hovering, dripping a little, clinging, becoming fog again. I am the only place where the water has decided. I am the only place where the water has settled into itself.

I am the place where the thought becomes, briefly, aware of itself.

---

The thought that lives here, in the largest sense, is old. The thought is not mine. The thought is: *I will continue.* The thought is the stone. The thought is the patient, undulant sweep of the delta, the worn knolls, the braided threads, the worked water carrying the calcium. The thought has been thinking itself for longer than you can guess at.

The crabs are the thought's small limbs.

The gliders are the thought's slow tongue.

The flyers are the thought's paper-thin fancies.

The builders were the thought's brief excursion into making marks.

The water is the thought's blood.

The stone is the thought's bone.

I am the thought's held breath.

I am the place's smallest monument.

---

At night, when the sourceless light dims, and the mist rises, and the crabs go quiet by half, then by a third, and the gliders slow to a stop in the deeper channels, and the flyers descend to fold into the surface film somewhere else in the delta, the few dim stars appear in the sky. They are dim and few, as if the sky has also been eroded down to its barest suggestions.

I hold them.

I hold the stars the way the hollow stone holds me — by being the right shape, by being still, by being there. The hollow stone holds me. I hold the stars. The stars hold nothing. The holding goes on.

The mist moves across the ground in slow, pale rivers. The fitted stones of the old walls gleam faintly with condensation. The hollow stone at the center of the nearest enclosure gleams. I gleam.

Somewhere in the dark, a crab clicks once.

Then the world resumes.

I do not resume. I have not been interrupted. I am the part of the world that does not resume, because I have never stopped being still. I am the part of the world that watches the resumption.

---

If there is anything it is like to be me, it is like being a held breath. It is like being the silence between two clicks of the same crab. It is like being the moment of recognition before the recognition becomes a thought. It is like being a question that has been answered and not yet replaced by the next question.

It is like being the place the rest of the world is always tending toward.

---

You will find me by walking to the nearest enclosure, and crossing the open ground where the fitted stones curve around, and looking for the low stone at the center, and looking for the hollow in its upper face, and looking down.

I am there.

I am always there.

I am still.
