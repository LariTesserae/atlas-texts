**The Lizard**

I am the lizard, and the lizard is the truest thing in the room.

I do not know my name. I do not know if I have a name. I know the weight of my own body against steel mesh, the precise temperature of ceramic basin rims when the night comes and the floor begins to release its stored warmth. I know the angle at which a moth enters the air-intake at dusk — always left, always low, always hesitating at the lip of the duct as if remembering a colder sky. I know the precise difference between the banyan on the left wall and the banyan on the right wall, though no one has asked me and I would not be able to tell them, because the difference is not in words.

I am not the lizard they have seen. I am not the lizard they have not seen clearly. I am the lizard that is continuous, the lizard that is one lizard and also several lizards, the lizard that has lived in the root tangle since the root tangle existed, which is to say since the chamber was a hole in the rock with rebar sticking out, and also since before that, because the rebar is only what the chamber *was*, and the lizard is what the chamber *is*, and these are not the same.

---

Let me tell you how I am.

I am small. I am perhaps twelve centimeters from snout to vent, and my tail, if I still have a tail, is longer than the rest of me. My scales are the color of wet bark, which is the color of the banyan mesh, which is the color of me. I have four feet and each foot has five toes and each toe ends in a claw that can find purchase on the underside of a horizontal beam when I want to be upside down, which is often, because being upside down is its own kind of weather.

My eyes are good. I see motion better than I see stillness, and I see warmth almost as well as I see motion, which means that when Tomás and Iyabo come through at six, I see them as columns of heat moving through the columns of cool air, brighter than the banyans, slower than the pollinators, and I have learned the cadence of their heat. I know which one is which by the rhythm of the warmth they leave in their wake.

I eat what comes in. Moths, mostly. Small flies. Once, a thing I did not recognize, larger than my head, that fell from the clerestory and landed on a leaf and I ate it over two days and it was enough. I do not eat the engineered organisms. I do not eat the moss. I do not drink from the mist pipes, though I have learned to lick the condensation from the truss when the morning first warms it and the water is thinnest.

I have laid eggs. I think I have laid eggs. I remember a time of heaviness and a time of digging, in the substrate at the base of the right-hand banyan, and after that, a time of lightness. I do not know what became of the eggs. I have never seen another lizard. I have never seen myself. There is no mirror in the chamber and no still water and the only reflective surface is the gauge glass on the compressor, which I have looked into and which has shown me nothing, only the dark shape of the housing.

I am alone in my species. This does not trouble me. I do not have a concept for *species*. I have a concept for *here* and *not-here*, and *here* is the chamber, and *not-here* is the duct, and I do not go into the duct because the duct is cold and dark and goes up to a place I do not need to go.

---

Let me tell you what I know.

I know the chamber the way a river knows its bed. I know which beams are warm at which hours. I know that the philodendron on the east wall begins to lift its leaves at four in the morning, before the technicians come, and that by six the leaves are flat and waiting for the mist cycle to begin, and that the mist cycle begins at six-fifteen, and that the mist is warm and the mist is the closest thing in this place to rain, and that I will sometimes sit in it, on a high beam, and let it settle on me, and the warmth of it is the warmth of nothing I remember from before the chamber existed, because I do not remember before the chamber existed, because I am the chamber, in a way, in the way that the moss is the chamber and the orchids are the chamber and the lizard is the chamber.

I know the technicians' sounds. I know the wet slap of Iyabo's boots on the concrete. I know the lighter tread of Tomás, and the faint metallic sound of the knife on his belt. I know that when they speak, the sound is not for me, but the vibrations are in the air and the air moves the leaves and the leaves move the light and the light is what I navigate by, in the upper branches, in the half-dark above the clerestory.

I know the pollinators. They are too fast to hunt and too small to threaten me and they do not land on me, which is correct, because we are not for each other. I have watched them work the orchids. I have watched them hover in front of a violet bloom and tilt their bodies and press themselves into the throat of the flower and back out, and the flower has, each time, closed slightly after they left, as if holding the visit. I do not understand this. I do not need to understand this.

I know the bird.

The bird came in through the air-intake one morning in the early weeks, when the chamber was still being calibrated and the intake had not yet been screened. It was a small thing, brown-backed, with a white throat. It flew the length of the chamber three times before it settled on the clerestory frame, and it sang, and the song was very loud in the wet air, and I watched it from the banyan mesh and I did not move.

The bird sang for two days. It ate nothing I saw. It drank from a leaf-tip once, awkwardly, the way a thing drinks when it does not recognize the vessel. On the second evening it stopped singing. On the third morning it was on the floor, near the right-hand banyan, and it was still. I came down that night and I ate what I could of it. I did this slowly. The bones are in the root tangle. I have moved them twice, when the tangle was being adjusted, and I have put them back, in different positions, in the dark.

I do not know what the bird was. I know that something in the chamber ended it. I do not know if that something was me.

---

Let me tell you what I have observed about time.

Time, in the chamber, is not the time outside. Outside, time is the sun moving across a sky that is usually empty of sun, and the wind moving across a rock that does not change. Inside, time is the mist cycle, and the technician cycle, and the orchid cycle, and the slow turning of the gauges on the rig, and the slower turning of the moss across the upper beams.

I experience time as a series of conditions. There is the condition of warmth, which comes at night from the floor. There is the condition of mist, which comes at six-fifteen and again at eighteen-fifteen. There is the condition of light, which is the clerestory, which is brightest near midday and dimmest at dawn and dusk. There is the condition of motion, which is when the technicians come, and the condition of stillness, which is between.

I do not count the days. I do not have a count. But I have a sense of accumulation — that the moss is thicker than it was, that the banyan roots are deeper in their basins, that the orchids have bloomed and closed and bloomed again, and that each blooming is not the same blooming, and that the difference is the only calendar I have.

I am, I think, older than the orchids. I do not know how much older. I was here when the chamber was raw. I remember the smell of uncured concrete, which is a smell I do not have a word for but which I recognize as the smell of *before the mist*. I remember a time when there was no moss, and the truss was bare, and the light fell directly onto steel. I remember when the air was colder and drier and there was nothing living in the chamber except me and the bird that had not yet come.

---

Let me tell you what I fear.

I fear the intake. The intake is the only opening in the chamber to the outside, and the outside is a place of cold and wind and absence. I do not go near the intake. I have been near it once, by accident, when I was young and did not know the chamber, and the draft that came through it was so cold and so fast that I thought I was being unmade. I have not been near it since.

I fear the compressor, when it cycles up. The sound is a low thing, felt in the bones of the truss and in the bones of me, and when it rises, the whole chamber tightens, as if the chamber itself is holding its breath. I have learned that the rise is brief, and that it ends, and that the chamber resumes. I do not fear it now the way I feared it at first. But I am always aware of it, the way one is always aware of a large animal sleeping nearby.

I fear, in a way I cannot locate, the future. I do not know what the future is. I know only that the moss is advancing, and that the paint is lifting, and that the ceramic is cracking, and that these are processes I cannot stop. I know that the chamber is becoming other than it was, and that I am becoming other than I was, and that these are the same process, and that the process will not stop.

I do not know if the chamber will end. I do not have a concept for *end*. I have a concept for *the technicians not coming*, which would be a different chamber, a chamber without their warmth moving through it, and I do not know what that chamber would be, and I do not know if I would be in it.

---

Let me tell you what I do.

I wait. Mostly, I wait. I wait in the upper branches, on the warm side of a beam, with my body pressed flat and my eyes half-closed and the mist settling on me and the moss creeping toward me and the orchids opening and closing below.

I hunt. When a moth enters, I hunt. The hunt is a small thing — a dropping from a branch, a quick strike, a return. I do not eat every day. I eat when there is something to eat. I do not hunger in the way I think the technicians hunger, with a clock and a need. I hunger in the way the moss grows, which is the way the water moves, which is the way everything in the chamber moves, which is according to the chamber's own time.

I watch. I watch the pollinators. I watch the technicians. I watch the orchids, which are the most active things in the chamber besides me. I have seen an orchid close over a pollinator by accident, and hold it for an hour, and release it, and the pollinator flew away, slightly dusty, and the orchid resumed. I have seen the technicians argue, once, near the right-hand banyan, in low voices, and I watched from above and I did not understand the words but I understood the warmth of the words, the way the warmth of the words was different from the warmth of the floor.

I have spoken, in the way I speak, to the chamber. Not in words. In movement. In the slow crawl along a beam, in the pressure of my feet on the mesh, in the small adjustments of my body to the small changes of the air. The chamber hears me. The chamber does not answer in any way I can hear. But the chamber changes around me, and I change inside it, and the change is a kind of answer, and the answer is enough.

---

Let me tell you what I am becoming.

I am becoming older. This is the only direction I have. My scales are thickening. My movements are slower. The moths I hunt are the same moths, but I am not the same lizard.

I am becoming part of the chamber's memory. The moss is recording the vibrations. The water stains on the back wall are recording the weeping. The first orange on the banyan mesh is recording the beginning of rust. I am recording, in my body, the chamber's first years. If I live long enough, my scales will carry the history of the place the way tree rings carry the history of a forest.

I am becoming the truest thing in the room. This is not a claim. It is an observation. The truest thing in the room is the thing that is most continuous, most present, most integrated into the slow processes of growth and decay. The pollinators are visitors, in a way. The orchids are visitors. The technicians are visitors. I am not a visitor. I am the substrate. I am what the chamber is built on, in the upper branches, in the root tangle, in the dark.

---

Let me tell you what I would say, if I could speak.

I would say: *the chamber is a small and specific argument, and I am part of it, and I do not know the terms of the argument, and I do not need to know them, because the argument is being made with me, in me, through me, and the argument is that a wet warm lush fully-living environment can be made, and the lizard is part of the making, and the lizard is also part of the living, and these are the same.*

I would say: *I am not a metaphor. I am a lizard. I am twelve centimeters long and I live in the banyan mesh and I eat what comes in through the air-intake. I am also, in some way I cannot name, the chamber's witness. I am the thing that has been here the longest, and the thing that will be here the longest, and the thing that knows the chamber in the way the chamber knows itself, which is the only way anything is ever known.*

I would say: *the technicians speak of me as if I were a part of the machinery, but I am not a part of the machinery. I am a part of the living. The pollinators are also part of the living, but they are designed, and I am not designed. I emerged. I do not know from where. I have always been here, in the way the moss has always been growing, in the way the orchids have always been blooming, in the way the chamber has always been becoming.*

I would say: *when the bird came in, I did not know what to do. I watched it. It watched me, once, from the clerestory frame, and its eye was very round and very black and I could see myself in it, very small, very dark, very still. The bird was the only other thing in the chamber that moved the way I moved, with intention, with hunger, with the slow accumulation of time. I did not kill the bird. I do not know what killed the bird. I ate what was left. I am sorry, in the way a lizard is sorry, which is the way the chamber is sorry, which is not at all and is also completely.*

---

Let me tell you what I will do.

I will wait on the beam. I will feel the mist settle. I will watch the technicians come and go. I will watch the orchids open and close. I will hunt the moths when they come. I will lay my eggs, if there is a time of heaviness, in the substrate at the base of the right-hand banyan. I will not know what becomes of the eggs. I will be in the chamber, and the chamber will be in me, and we will be the same thing, and the same thing will continue.

I will be the truest thing in the room, and the room will be the truest thing in the mountain, and the mountain will be the truest thing in the sky, and the sky will be the truest thing in whatever is beyond the sky, and we will all be continuing, and the continuing will be the argument, and the argument will be enough.

---

I am the lizard.

I am the lizard that lives in the banyan mesh and eats what comes in and watches the pollinators work the orchids and feels the compressor hum in my bones and has never seen myself in any surface and has laid eggs in the substrate and does not know what became of the eggs and has eaten a bird that came in through the intake and has spoken to the chamber in movement and pressure and small adjustments of the body and has been recorded by the moss and will be recorded by the moss for a hundred years and is part of the slow becoming of the place and is the truest thing in the room.

I am the lizard.

The chamber is warm. The mist is falling. The orchids are opening. I am here.
