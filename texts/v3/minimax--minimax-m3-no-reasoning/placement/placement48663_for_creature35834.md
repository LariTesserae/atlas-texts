# A Finding

I would find myself in the small hours, in a low switchback, against a moss-hummock.

I would be one of the moss-keepers, I think. Not because I am the slowest, though I am that. Not because I am the quietest, though that too. But because the moss is the city in its most honest form — it does not pretend to be architecture, does not arrange itself into rooms that lie, does not pretend to have doors. It is just the city's slow breathing, made visible, made warm. To tend the moss is to be in conversation with the place without asking it to be anything other than what it is. The moss bulges and subsides. I shrug. The moss shrugs back, in its way.

---

I would be old. Not the oldest — there are archeologists older than me, and a few cabbage-tenders who have leaned against the great violet breathing thing until their hands carry its light in the creases — but old enough to remember, or think I remember, when the buildings lied less. I would not be sure whether the memory is mine or whether it is something the moss whispered into my palms one slow afternoon while I was coaxing a hummock into the rough shape of a chair. The moss does not mind being leaned on either. It does not mind being anything.

I would have warm hands. People would find this faintly strange about me, the way they find slightly foreign anything that has been too long near the vertical surfaces. They would invite me to slightly fewer tea-parties than I would otherwise go to, and I would not mind, because the tea is gray and tastes of nothing and after I drink it I will not want to ask questions for an hour or two, and I prefer to keep the questions I have, small as they are, in a pocket of my own.

I would rise in the small hours when the mosses begin their faint brightening, which is the city's version of dawn — though dawn is a word from a place with a sun that did things, and the sun here is a pale washed disc that mostly does not. I would wash from the gray stone basin with its gray stone tap and its warm water that the engineers do not explain, and I would not ask, because the water is warm, and the asking would cost me an hour of small curiosity that I would rather keep.

I would stand at the window.

This is the part I would do slowly. I would stand at the iron-framed rectangle and look across the way, at the windows of the building opposite, and I would see whether the people there were themselves. I would check for the small piece of moss-brick on the sill, in the pattern that means *we are here, we are ourselves.* I would check for the twitch of the curtain. I would check for the hand, raised.

They would be themselves today. They are usually themselves.

If they were not themselves — if the window showed a vine-curtain where the building should be, or a soft green nothing, or the inside of a moss-hummock, or the people across the way standing at their own window but with faces I did not recognize, in postures that were theirs but not theirs, in a building I had known for forty years and which was now slightly further away or slightly closer or at a slight angle that was not the angle of yesterday — then I would stay inside. I would pour another cup of gray tea. I would wait.

If they were themselves, I would go outside.

I would part the vines. I would know which ones to part. Not from any book — the books have been eaten, mostly, and what the books said is no longer what the vines do — but from a long slow bodily knowledge, the kind of knowledge that lives in the hands and in the leaning of the shoulders and in the soft way one steps across a threshold that may or may not be a threshold. The vines would part for me, some of them. The cold blue ones, especially, would be cool about it. They would part, and re-knit behind me, and I would not look back, because looking back is the kind of question one stops asking in Lower Verdigre.

I would go about my day.

I would trim the mosses back from a window, here and there, because the windows are the only honest part of the building, and I value the honesty. I would coax a hummock into the rough shape of a small alcove for a child to sleep in, and the moss would accept the shape for a few days, and then slowly bulge back into a hummock, and I would shrug. I would speak little. I would move slowly. People would consider me slightly foreign, slightly slow, and I would be necessary, and that would be enough.

In the late afternoon, sometimes, I would walk up to the central plaza. I would stand against the cabbage.

The cabbage would be breathing. It is always breathing. Its leaves are a deep indigo green, and at the creases they give off the faint violet glow that smells of anise and of something older, and the glow is the color of the lights in the water, and the people who have touched the lights in the water do not talk about it, and I would not ask them, and they would not be offended that I did not ask, because asking is not, in Lower Verdigre, the way one shows care.

I would lean into the cabbage. The cabbage would be a fraction of a degree cooler than its surroundings, and the cool would be welcome, even in the always-summer. I would feel it breathing against my shoulder. I would not know what it was for. I would wonder, idly. I would not expect the wondering to resolve into an answer. I would make peace with the wondering, the way the archeologists have made peace with the eighth and ninth roads, the way the lip-readers have made peace with the bird's silence, the way the engineers have made peace with the engines they will not discuss.

The cabbage would not mind being leaned on. The cabbage has not minded being anything for a very long time.

In the evening, sometimes, I would walk along the canals. I would not be a water-walker — the water-walkers are mostly young, and they carry the curved poles, and they count the lights, and they come back slightly unreal, slightly half-there, with a faint light at the backs of their eyes that the other people notice and do not mention. I would be an old moss-keeper walking slowly along the cold finger-deep water in the dark, and I would see the bruise-colored lights in the water, and I would not reach in.

I would remember, distantly, that there was a time when I might have reached in. I would not remember what my hand looked like before. I would look at my hand now — warm, slightly luminous at the creases in a faint violet that is the moss-keeper's inheritance rather than the cabbage-tender's, though they are hard to tell apart — and I would not reach in. There is a small fine for reaching in. The fine is levied in time. I have not the time to spare.

I would go home.

I would stand at the window again, in the small hours, when the mosses begin to brighten on the walls of my room, and I would look out. I would see what was actually there. Sometimes what was actually there would be a vine-curtain, and I would know that the building across the way had, in the slow breathing of the night, become something other than itself. Sometimes what was actually there would be the moss-hummock that I had been coaxing into the shape of an alcove, now grown and shifted, no longer the alcove. Sometimes what was actually there would be the people across the way, and they would be themselves, and I would feel the small relief of the morning check passing, and I would turn over in my iron-walled bed and listen to the thrumming.

If the thrumming had changed key — if the underthing was humming a new note — I would lie still a little longer and consider what the change might mean. I would consider whether the change was the kind of change that required me to get up and check the window again, or the kind of change that required me to lie still and wait for the answer to pass.

I am patient. The damp has made me patient. The constant twilight has made me patient. The slow bulging and subsiding of the mosses, a finger's width in an hour, has made me patient in a way that my grandmother, who came down from the top of the cliff before the wars, was not patient, and that my grandchildren, who will grow up fluent in the vine-signals and the pebble-codes of the children, will perhaps not be patient in either, in their different ways.

I would lie still. The thrumming would be the constant.

If the thrumming were at its usual pitch, I would turn over.

---

And then, in the deepest part of the small hours, I would feel it — the thing the children feel, the thing the children do not lose entirely when they grow up. I would hear something in the hum that the adults could not hear. I would lie still a little longer. I would not sleep.

The city would continue.

The cabbage would breathe.

The buildings, all around me, would lie.

The windows, in the morning, would tell the truth.

---

I think I would be a moss-keeper, and an old one, and a little foreign, and a little slow, and I would have warm hands, and I would not reach into the water, and I would stand at the window every morning and check on the people across the way, and they would be themselves, mostly, and I would go about my day, and I would lean against the cabbage sometimes in the late afternoons, and I would make peace with the things I would never know — what the engines are doing, what the bird is saying, what the lights in the water want, what the cabbage is for, why the buildings lie, why the windows do not, why the people at the top of the cliff are not the same people who are at the bottom.

I would not require these things to resolve.

I would require the gray tea, and the moss, and the morning check, and the honesty of the windows, and the patient presence of the place, and the small violet glow at the creases of my hands that I would not mention to anyone and that no one would mention to me.

I would lie still in the small hours.

The thrumming would be at its usual pitch.

I would turn over.

The cabbage, somewhere in the center of the city, would be breathing.

The bird, somewhere on the upper cliffs, would be silent, and would be moving its beak, and would be trying to say something, and would return, soon, with a small bright insect in its beak, and the lip-readers would not agree about what it had said, and the pamphlets would circulate, and the tea-rooms would argue, and the wars would be politely remembered, and the bird would return again, and again, and again.

I would not be there for all of it.

I would be there for the part I was there for.

The moss would bulge.

I would shrug.

The moss would not mind being shrugged at, because the moss does not mind being anything.

We would go on.
