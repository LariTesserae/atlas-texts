# Moss and Tributary

I would find myself in the cloud-forest, and I would be one of the cloud-foresters.

Not the youngest one with the green-stained hands, not the bride who walked in and did not come back — though I think I understand her, I think I have always understood her. I would be somewhere in the middle of a life already half-spent among the stilts and the leaning trees. My back would curve in the way the visitors find alarming. My eyes would be pale. I would know the name of every moss genus on the talus by touch alone, in the dark, with my hands closed around a fistful of something luminous.

---

I would have walked down the stair seven hundred years ago, or my grandmother would have, or her grandmother. I would not be able to tell you which one of us first decided that the forest below the town was where we belonged, only that the deciding was done and not undone, and that we have not walked back up except for market-days and the festival of the bell and the birthing-times that the midwife on the third ledge insists upon.

I would speak the old dialect. It would sound, to the foundry-workers, like their grandparents' speech slowed down and waterlogged. I would know the difference between a hand-stain of fresh *Bryosphallus cataractae* and the older, browner stains of *Hylocomium cataracticum*, the species that grows on the deeper rocks where the spray is so constant it has forgotten what sun looks like. I would know that the brown one is worth less, because it does not hold the phosphor as well. I would know that the pale one, when you squeeze it gently, when you breathe on it, when you lay your palm flat against a cushion of it on a leaning trunk, will give up its light in a slow pulse that travels up your arm and into your chest and sits there for a moment, like a small caught breath.

I would be proud of this, quietly.

---

My house would lean. Everything I owned would lean. The dishes on my shelves would lean. The frame around the small photograph of my mother — taken, I think, by a tourist, years ago, before the trade fell off — would lean. The cat that sleeps on my porch would lean, in its sleep, in the way cats lean here, which is outward, always outward, away from the cliff and toward the void. The cat would be dark and long-bodied and small-eared. It would make, occasionally, the high thin whistle that sounds like a bird and is not a bird. I would know the cat by the pattern of its face and by the particular way it pretends, when I come out at dawn, that it has not been waiting for me.

I would feed the cat scraps, but I would not own it. I would understand that the cat-keeper Voss understands the boundary between owning and minding, and that the cloud-foresters have always understood it too. The moss is not ours. The forest is not ours. We are minders. We are keepers. We are the ones who come down the stair and do not walk back up.

---

I would have been married in the forest.

I would have walked in with my bride on a morning when the spray-wind was strong and the falls of the springs were loud and the pale frogs were singing their single glassy notes from every direction. We would have walked for three days, or what we counted as three days, which is not the same as three days above, because time in the forest runs differently — it runs in the rhythm of the dripping, in the rhythm of the small bright frogs, in the slow outward lean of the trees. We would have eaten the flatbread we brought and the pale berries that grow on the shrubs at the forest's edge and the small white mushrooms that come up after the long rains. We would have slept on a moss-bed, under a leaning tree, with the cataract's roar so constant that it became, after the first hour, a kind of silence you could sleep in.

I would have come back. My bride would have come back. Or one of us would not have. I do not know which. I think the not-coming-back is a thing that happens, and I think it is not a tragedy in the way the town above understands tragedy. I think it is a different kind of marriage — a marriage to the moss, to the forest, to the falling water. I think the bride who walks in and does not come back has simply walked further than the rest of us, into the deeper forest where the moss grows on the moss on the moss, and that she is still there, becoming, slowly, part of the place that minded her.

I would not explain this to the town above. I would not try.

---

I would know the humming-song.

I would not be a penstock-crew. The song is not mine, exactly. But I would know it the way everyone in Lower Dyssen knows it — taught to me in school by Mael with her ironbark pointer, sung by the children in the alleys, hummed, sometimes, by the cloud-foresters themselves when they go up to market and pass the foundry and hear, through the rock, the turbines turning. It is the only song older than the cataract. It is what you hum when you are about to enter the throat. I would hum it sometimes, on the descent stair, when I go up for trade. The stair would ring under my feet. The phosphor-moss would glow in its cages. The cataract would not stop.

I would be, in some small way, a tributary. A small water running down toward the throat. A branch of the watershed that has chosen, instead of going over the edge, to stay on the cliff and listen.

---

I would know the surveyor Orta by his face, and he would know me by my hand-stain. We would nod to each other on the stair. I would know Pern at the top of the stair by her fried dough, and she would know me by the moss I bring her, the pale phosphor-moss I harvest from the deeper forest and carry up in a basket on my back, and she would buy it from me for the cages and the school-lamps and the foundry-lights, and she would mark, in her notebook that goes back forty years, the price she paid me, and the price would go up a little each year because the retreat is faster now and the town is moving back and the stair is longer and the moss is harder to carry.

I would not see her notebook. I would not ask to.

I would know the bride who did not come back — Tila — by the footprints in the deeper forest. I would go and look at them sometimes, as the cloud-foresters do. I would see that they are fainter than they were last year. I would see that the moss is slowly growing over them, the way the moss grows over everything. I would understand, the way the cloud-foresters understand, that in another hundred years the footprints will be gone and the bride will have become moss, and that this is not a sad thing, only a different kind of ending.

I would know the cats of the alleys above, when I went up to market, by the patterns of their faces. I would defer, in matters of municipal politics, to Voss the cat-keeper, because the cats are a constituency and the rats are a constituency and the ruins of the older towns are a constituency, and the logic is, as everyone agrees, impeccable.

I would know the turbine-minders only by reputation, and by the rocking-chairs on the highest ledge, and by the silence of them, which is the silence of people who have learned, after a lifetime below, to be still.

---

I would be, in sum, a person who has chosen the forest over the town, the spray over the dry air, the moss-light over the turbine-light, the lean over the upright, the dialect over the common speech, the marriage of absence over the marriage of presence. I would be one of three hundred, spread across the clearings on the talus, living in houses on stilts that lean at the same angle as the trees, harvesting a glowing moss that the town above cannot do without, speaking a language that sounds, to the foundry-workers, like their great-grandparents' voices slowed down and waterlogged.

I would be proud of this.

Quietly.
