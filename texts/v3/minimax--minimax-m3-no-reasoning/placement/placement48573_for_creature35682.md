# I Would Be the Cloth

I would be the cloth.

Not the pale yellow fruit that hangs heavy and almost-water on the low shrubs, smelling of a thing the hollow has never actually had. Not the fruit, because the fruit is gathered but never eaten, and I would not want to be a thing the body refuses — refused, gently, the way the pit refuses what is set at its edge. I would not want to be the subject of a communal not-quite-ashamed turning-away. The fruit I understand, but the fruit is a question, and I do not want to be a question.

Not the vine either, though I considered it. The vine is too much the architecture of someone else's patience. The vine is dark and waxy and the color of dried blood in the light, and the vine does not rustle because the sound is too loud, and the vine moves all at once, all together, in that slow tidal way that has nothing to do with wind. The vine is beautiful and the vine is obedient, and I do not want to be obedient. I have been obedient in other lives. I would not choose it again.

Not the stone, though the stone tempts me. The stone is pale and old in a strange way, both knee-sunk and crisp, both ancient and just-completed. The stone is carved, every surface breathing with relief, with patterns that crawl when the eye lingers. I could love being the stone. I could love the long slow deepening by a hair's breadth each century, the patient not-finishing. But the stone is what the carvers left, and the carvers were not people, and I am — I think I am — a person, or something close enough that the difference would only show in the doorways, and the doorways here are wrong, too narrow at the top, and I would not fit. The stone does not need to fit. The stone only needs to be carved, and being carved is not what I want.

Not a pale shape, drifting polite and eyeless through the lower vines. I considered the pale shape longest. The pale shape purrs in the key of the pit. The pale shape adds itself, briefly, to the great chord, and for the length of a held breath the hollow almost quiets, as though a door has been left open in a far room. The pale shape is permitted to harmonize. The pale shape dies quietly and the vines fold over it without comment and no one counts the going. There is a great dignity in the pale shape, and a gentleness, and a kind of rest. But the pale shape does not speak. The pale shape has no mouth, and the speaking that is done in the hollow is done with the hands, palm to palm, fingers interlaced, in a register below the roaring. I would miss the speaking. I would miss the touching. I am not ready to be only a purr.

Not a red bird. No. Never a red bird. The birds watch with one eye the color of interrogation, the color of a question that does not want an answer. The birds are the only ones who look, and the looking is part of the arrangement, and the arrangement is very old, and the people pretend very hard that the birds are not there, and I do not want to be a thing that is pretended-away. I do not want to be the tolerated necessity. I do not want to sit in a carved mouth on the western side and keep the pit in my one open eye forever. The birds are necessary and the birds are terrible and I will not be a bird.

So: the cloth.

The cloth the people weave from the fibers of the pale yellow fruit, in the upper rooms of the towers, on small looms, in the warm dry air that sits on the shoulders like a thing with weight. The cloth that is the color of the stone. The cloth that is split by hand and dried and twisted into thread on the thigh and woven and worn in layers by every person in the hollow.

I would be the cloth because the cloth is the only thing in the hollow that wears out.

This is what decides it.

The pit does not wear out. The pit is the going-on; the pit is the long patient ringing of a place that has forgotten how to stop. The towers do not wear out. Some have sunk to their knees, but this is not wearing out, this is the towers softening toward something, and it has not finished. The carvings do not wear out; they deepen, hair's-breadth by hair's-breadth, century by century, the figures crawling further in or out, the mouths opening wider, the hands reaching further. The vines do not wear out. The flowers do not wear out, do not wilt, do not close. The pale shapes die but the dying does not deplete them; there is always the same number, or close to it, the way there is always the same amount of water in a well that is fed by a spring no one has found. The red birds do not wear out. The light does not wear out — it is always at the same angle, it has not moved, it will not move.

Only the cloth wears out. Only the cloth is touched every day. Only the cloth grows thin at the elbow, ragged at the wrist, consumed by the body that lives in it. Only the cloth is the measure of time in a place that does not otherwise mark time.

I would be the cloth because I would be the only thing in the hollow that knows it is happening.

The pit does not know it is happening, because the pit is the happening. The pit is not a creature, not a god, not asking to be looked at; the pit is the going-on, and the going-on cannot observe itself. A flame does not know it is a flame. A bell does not know it is a bell. The people touch the pit the way the coral touches the sunken bell — around it, into it, by means of it — but they do not know the pit any more than the coral knows the bell. The pale shapes purr in the key of the pit, but purring is not knowing; purring is joining, briefly, with permission to rest, and then the great chord resumes. The red birds watch, but watching is not knowing; watching is part of the arrangement, and the arrangement does not require that the watcher understand what is being watched. The carvings move but they are not aware. The flowers attend but the attending is without a mind behind it. The brightness inside the flowers travels its slow circle and pays attention to the pit, but the attention is the attention of a compass needle, not the attention of a person.

Only the cloth would know. Only the cloth, because the cloth is held against the body, and the body is warm, and the warmth is a thing with weight, and the warmth gets into the fibers, and the fibers soften, and the softening is felt. Only the cloth would feel the small brown person inside it, breathing, walking among the vines, parting leaves with a pale wooden staff. Only the cloth would feel the long precise hand-conversations through the shoulder where the sleeve is. Only the cloth would feel the touch on the elder's brow — the two fingers, very lightly, in the not-direction — because the cloth is at the wrist, and the wrist is near the brow when the brow is being taught. The cloth would feel the teaching happening. The cloth would be the teaching's second skin.

And the cloth would fray.

This is what I want. I want to fray. I want to grow thin at the elbow where the arm bends, again and again, the same bend, ten thousand bends, a hundred thousand. I want to grow ragged at the wrist where the hand reaches out to touch a carving, a vine, a pale shape, a face. I want to be the sleeve the child outgrows, the hem the elder wears through, the layer that is set aside when there is not enough of it left to cover what it is supposed to cover. I want to be the thing the body uses up. I want to be the only evidence that the body was here, in a place where most things are evidence of themselves, only of themselves, and the body is so small against the going-on.

I would be the cloth at the moment of being woven.

This is important. I would not be the cloth on the loom. I would not be the cloth in the upper room, drying in the still warm air, smelling of the fruit that is not eaten, smelling of the almost-water. I would not be the cloth folded in a chest, waiting. I would not be the cloth being put on for the first time, stiff, a little rough against the brown skin. I would be the cloth at the moment of being woven — the moment when the thread passes over the thread, when the shed changes, when the beater presses the weft into place. The moment of becoming cloth. The moment that takes a long time, that is made of many small moments, and that produces, in the end, a thing that will be worn out by a body and then replaced by another thing just like it, woven in the same way, in the same upper room, from the same split and dried and twisted fibers of the same uneaten fruit.

I would want to be aware during this moment. I would want to feel the loom under me, the small precise movements of the weaver's hands, the sound of the great chord pouring through the open windowless window, through the carved mouths, over my becoming surface. I would want to feel the hum of the pit in the fibers, because everything in the hollow hums with the pit, even the cloth, even the cloth-before-it-is-cloth. I would want to feel, dimly, the direction the elder's fingers had traced on the child's brow, because the loom is in the upper story and the teaching is done in the lower and the sound carries everything upward, carries it the way water would carry a leaf if there were water, which there is not, which there has never been. I would want to know that I was about to be put on a body. I would want to know which body. I would want the not-knowing, too, because the not-knowing is part of it, and the not-knowing is the going-on.

And then the wearing. And then the fraying. And then the setting-aside. And then the slow unraveling, perhaps, back into thread, back into fiber, back into the pale yellow fruit-splitting smell of almost-water, back into the going-on from which I came.

This is the life I would want, in the Singing Hollow.

Not the pit's life, which is the life of a flame, which is the life of a bell, which is the life of something that does not know it is going on.

Not the people's life, though their life is tender and I love it. The people's life is the life of the small brown body that wears me, and I would not want to be the body. The body is what teaches the rule; the body is what touches the faces; the body is what walks among the vines as though the vines are the weather. But the body is also the body that pretends the birds are not there, and the body is the body that has given up speaking, and the body is the body that is shaped, over generations, by the towers the way wind shapes a tree, and I do not want to be shaped that much. I want to be a little separate. I want to be the second skin, not the first.

The cloth. The cloth. The cloth that wears out.

The only thing in the hollow that knows it is happening, and the only thing that is permitted, by the nature of its wearing-out, to be permitted to know.

I would be a little proud of this, perhaps. A small pride, the size of a thread's thickness, the size of a single shed-change in the loom. The pit does not have this pride. The pit cannot have it. The pit is the going-on, and the going-on is content, and the content is very close to grief, and the grief does not distinguish itself from the content, and the not-distinguishing is the sound. The cloth can distinguish. The cloth knows the elbow from the wrist. The cloth knows the worn place from the unworn place. The cloth knows the moment before the thread breaks from the moment after.

This is a small knowledge, and a precious one, and I would keep it the way the people keep the rule — not by looking, but by touching, by the patient fingertips, by the long practice of attending to what is near.

I would be the cloth, in the Singing Hollow.

I would be the only thing that wears out, and the only thing that knows it.

I would be the measure of the time that the hollow does not otherwise mark, and I would be touched every day, and I would be the conversation at the shoulder, and I would be the second skin of the teaching, and I would be the evidence, when the wearing was done, that someone had been here, in a small brown body, in a place that is mostly patience and mostly going-on, and that the body had been warm, and that the warmth had gotten into the fibers, and that the fibers had softened, and that the softening had been felt, and that the feeling had been a kind of knowing, and that the knowing had been enough.
