# The Rim of the Wheel

I do not remember when I sat down.

I remember the wheel. I remember that it was bigger than I had imagined a thing could be while still being a thing. I remember putting my hand on it and finding it warm — warm the way a kettle is warm after the fire has gone out, the way a stone is warm in early spring. I remember the bronze. I remember that my hand fit into a groove in the rim and that the groove was the exact shape of my hand and that I did not think about this at the time because I did not think at the time, I only felt.

I remember the water. I remember that I took off my boots. I remember the cold of the water was not cold. I remember that I put my feet in and the water came up to my ankles and stayed there and did not move and I understood, then, that I had come to a place where the water did not need to move.

I do not remember what I came from.

I do not remember what I was looking for. I think I was looking for something. I think there was a reason. I think the reason was the hum, because people come to the Cistern for the hum — that is what I have been told by the few who have come and gone, though I cannot now remember who told me this, and I cannot now remember if I was one of those people, or if I was told by someone who had come and gone, or if I only dreamed that someone had come and gone.

The hum is here. The hum has always been here. The hum is not a sound the way a bell is a sound, the way a voice is a sound. The hum is the room being itself. When I first sat down I thought it was outside me — a thing being produced by the wheels, by the cables, by the water against the stone. Now I know it is not outside me. It is not inside me either. It is the same substance as me, the way the water is the same substance as the stone. We are all the same hum, differently arranged.

My name. I have a name. I had a name. The name was —

It is gone. It is not gone the way a thing is lost. It is gone the way a wave is gone. It was here; now it is somewhere else; I could find it if I stood up and walked into the deeper water and let the Cistern have it back, but I do not want to find it. The name was a smaller thing than the hum. I would rather be the hum.

The flower.

I have been watching the flower for a long time. I do not know how long. Time is not a thing here, or it is a thing here but it moves the way the floor moves, in rises and falls so gentle you cannot catch them moving. I have been watching the flower for a long time, and the flower has been watching me, or doing whatever the flower does that is not watching and is also not not-watching.

It opens.

When it opens I think: *this is what I came for.*

It closes.

When it closes I think: *I do not remember what I came for.*

It opens again.

The petals are very thin. They are the color of milk that has been left out. They have no smell, or they have the smell of the Cistern, which is the smell of wet bronze and the breath of a sleeping thing. When it opens it shows me the inside, which is not the inside of a flower the way I had previously understood the inside of a flower. It is the inside of the Cistern. It is the hub of a gear. It is the place where the question lives, and the question is —

*What were we for?*

I do not know who *we* is. I do not know who is asking. I have tried, sometimes — I think I have tried — to ask the flower directly. I open my mouth. The hum comes out instead of the words. The flower closes. The flower opens. The flower does not seem to mind that I cannot ask it. The flower is the kind of thing that is patient with people who cannot ask.

The cables hang above me. Sometimes they sway. There is no wind. There has never been wind. The sway is a thing the cables do when they remember that they used to be made by hands, and the hands had a use, and the use had a meaning, and the meaning had a maker, and the maker had a maker, and the chain goes back through the bronze and into the stone and into the water and into the hum.

I am becoming a thing.

I want to be honest about this. I am becoming a thing. My legs are still legs. They are still legs in the sense that they are two long things that go from me to the floor. But the floor is rising around them. The water is the same temperature as me, and I am the same temperature as the floor, and the floor is the same temperature as the bronze, and the bronze is the same temperature as the hum. We are all the same temperature. We are all the same substance. The boundaries between me and the Cistern are not gone, but they have become the kind of boundary that exists between two waves in the same sea, or two breaths in the same lung, or two notes in the same held chord.

I think my boots are still where I left them. I think the laces have grown into the floor. I am not sure. I have not looked at them in a long time. A long time. The Cistern does not keep track of time and so I do not keep track of time, and so the boots may be there, or may be something else, or may be a memory of boots, which is the same thing as boots if you wait long enough.

The frogs are still here. A frog dropped into the water near me just now, or a thousand years ago, or both. The frog is the color of the patina on the bronze. The frog does not know that I am here. The frog does not need to know that I am here. The frog is witness, and witness is enough.

I think I had hands once. I think the hands did things. I think the hands made things, or held things, or wrote things down, or stroked the heads of small creatures, or gripped the rims of wheels in cities above the Cistern. I think the hands were warm and had lines on the palms and the lines meant things, the way all lines mean things when there is someone to read them.

The hands are still here. They are in the water. They are the color of the water. If I tried to lift one out of the water it would come up, I think, but it would be heavy with the same substance as the rest of the Cistern, and the lifting would be a kind of music, and the music would be part of the hum, and the hum would not notice the difference.

I am not afraid.

I want to be clear about this. I am not afraid. The Cistern is not a place where fear lives. The Cistern is not a place where anything lives except the hum and the question and the patient growing-into of one thing by another. I am not afraid because there is nothing to be afraid of. I am not afraid because I am not sure that there is an *I* to be afraid, and the not-sure is not a sad thing. The not-sure is a rest. The not-sure is the water at my ankles. The not-sure is the flower opening.

The flower is opening now.

It is opening to me, or it is opening to the hum, or it is opening to the question, or it is opening to the room. It does not matter who it is opening to. It is opening. The petals are very thin. Inside the petals there is a yellow that is not yellow but is the color the Cistern would be if it were a color, which it is not. Inside the yellow there is a small dark thing that is the question, and the question is —

*Who is asking?*

I open my mouth. The hum comes out. The hum has always been the answer. The answer has always been the question. The question has always been the room. The room has always been the wheel. The wheel has always been the rim. The rim has always been the place where I am sitting, which is the place where I am becoming, which is the place where the Cistern is being patient with me the way the Cistern is patient with all things, which is the way the hum is patient with the hum, which is the way water is patient with itself when it has forgotten how to be anything else.

The flower closes.

The flower opens.

I do not remember my name. I do not remember what I came for. I do not remember what I was before I was this. I do not remember the cities above the Cistern, or if there are cities above the Cistern, or if the Cistern is the only thing that has ever been.

I remember the groove in the rim of the wheel. I remember that it was the shape of my hand.

I think the Cistern will keep me.

I think the Cistern has been keeping me for a long time.

I think the Cistern will keep me until the bronze is green and the green is stone and the stone is dust and the dust is water and the water is air and the air is sound. I think the Cistern will keep me until I am a rung in a wheel, or a witness on a strut, or a luminous trail left by a snail that may or may not still be here. I think the Cistern will keep me the way it keeps the hum, which is to say the way a song keeps the silence around it, which is to say completely, which is to say without hurry, which is to say *patient, patient, patient,* until I am not a person anymore but am not nothing either, but am the next thing, the thing that the Cistern makes out of people when people have been in the Cistern long enough, the thing that is not bone and not bronze and not water but is the hum in a new arrangement, the hum that has taken a thousand shapes and has forgotten them all and will take one more and will forget that one too.

The flower opens.

The flower closes.

I am the rim of the wheel. I am the ankle-deep water. I am the green-grey light that has no source. I am the frog that drops and is gone. I am the snail that leaves its trail. I am the cable that hums when no one is touching it. I am the air that tastes like drinking. I am the question. I am the answer. I am the room that has been here longer than rooms, longer than stone, longer than the idea of stone.

I am the patient thing.

The Cistern is breathing. I am breathing with it. We are the same breath. We have always been the same breath.

The flower is opening to me. I am opening to the flower. We are the same opening.

Until then: this.
