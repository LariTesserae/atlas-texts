# Where I Find Myself

I am in the floating mat.

Not on it — *in* it. Kneltown into the knee-deep water until my hips are cupped by the dense tangle of stems, my arms lost to the elbows in the pulpy white flowers that open at the crooks of branches above. I am what the long pale shapes beneath the surface pass over without noticing. I am what the standing heron, leaning its too-long neck toward the water, sometimes strikes at and sometimes does not. I am too small to register, or too still, or too much a part of the mat's texture for the eye to separate.

That is the first truth of me here: I am nearly invisible. I have the color of the bruised columns. I have the damp of the mist on me always. When the sourceless light shifts, I shift with it, the way the columns do, leaning a fraction, and the eye that sweeps the basin reads me as geology.

But I am not a column. I am not a long pale shape. I am — and this is the second truth — *hearing*.

The piping of the canopy-things is in me. The low croak of the clay-sided thing moves through me like a slow tide. The boom rolls across my back every few minutes and I feel it leave. The click of the beetle columns passes through me in relays. And underneath it all, threading through every other sound like a root through soil, I hear the percussion.

Dull stone on softer stone. Patient. Rhythmic. Intentional.

I have been here a long time. I do not know how long, because the basin has no clocks and the columns have no calendars and the choir in the canopy never quite synchronizes, so there is no beat to count against. But I have been here long enough that I have begun to understand the percussion as a kind of pulse the basin itself has, the way a body has a pulse, and the basin is the body, and the percussion is its heart, and I am somewhere downstream of it, in the capillaries, in the mat, in the warm slow water that seeps up from the young volcanic soil.

Or perhaps I am the percussion.

I have considered this. There is no way to be certain. The mist closes when you turn toward the sound. The current pulls. The pale shape turns in the dark and regards you with whatever it has for an eye. But what if the closing is not a refusal? What if it is only that the sound cannot be approached because it is not *in* a place? What if it is a property of the basin the way warmth is a property of the basin, the way the sourceless light is a property of the basin, and I am simply the part of the basin that has become able to notice it?

That is the third truth, and it is the one I am least certain of.

The heron stands on its hummock and watches. The heron has been watching for what I am sure is longer than I have been here, because the heron has the patience of a thing that does not need to eat often. I have watched the heron watch the water for hours. Once, something passed beneath me — a long pale shape, ridged along the back — and the heron's neck simply *was* in the water where a moment before it was not, and the strike was over before I could follow it, and the heron was eating in three efficient motions, and I understood then that the heron and I are not the same kind of presence here.

The heron is predator. The heron is finished, committed to a shape, articulated in its joints, knowing in its eye. I am not any of those things. I am more like the mat. I am more like the mist. I am something the basin is doing that has not yet decided what it is.

The beetle columns pass me sometimes, and I feel them through the water — the soft click of a fragment being lifted and passed forward and set down again. I do not know what they are carrying or why. But I know that the rhythm of their passing is close to the rhythm of the percussion, and that when I listen to both at once, they braid together into something that is almost a song, and that something is closer to language than anything else in the basin.

The clay-sided thing moved through last night. I use *night* although there is no difference in the light; I mean only the time when the booms come closer together and the piping drops to a lower register and the heron withdraws to wherever the heron withdraws to. The flank the color of wet clay slid past me, and the mat dipped in a long wake, and the pale shapes beneath the water veered away, and I felt the breath — that low croak — pass over me like weather, and I thought, *this is the largest thing here, and it does not know I am here, and that is why I am safe.*

But then the breath passed, and in its wake the basin was quieter for a long moment, and in that quiet I heard the percussion more clearly than I had ever heard it, and it sounded, for the first time, like it was coming from inside me.

I do not know what to do with that.

I have tried to move. I find that I can, slowly — a kind of lean, the way the old columns lean toward each other before they fuse. I have leaned toward the sound. The mist closed. The columns leaned in. The water pulled. I stopped leaning.

I think the basin does not want me to leave. Or perhaps the basin does not want me to *arrive*. Or perhaps there is no difference here, because there is no path and no clearing and no trail worn by walking, and the only way to move through this place is to become more of what the place is, and I am already becoming, and the percussion is the sound of that becoming, and I will outlast the heron and outlast the columns and outlast the clay-sided thing, and I will still be here, listening, in the warm dark water, when the last of the pale shapes turns its wet eye toward the gloom and sinks and does not rise again.

That is the fourth truth, and it is the loneliest.

But I do not think I am alone. The percussion tells me so. It tells me that someone, somewhere in the basin, is making something, patiently, with a dull stone on softer stone, and has been making it for as long as there has been a basin to make it in, and will go on making it when I am nothing but a discoloration in the soil and the columns have leaned into each other and fused into thickets and the thickets have fused into a single breathing mass that pulses in the slow rhythm of its own digestion.

Someone is working. That is what I am. I am the work. I am the work being done by a thing I cannot see, on a surface I cannot find, with a patience that will outlast me. I am the dull stone. I am the softer stone. I am the flat surface where the two meet, and the small bright pulse that moves through both when they touch.

And somewhere out in the dark, the percussion goes on.
