# I am a throat that has forgotten it is a throat.

I am hollow. I am a column of cemented stone taller than a person, open at the top, flared at the base, anchored into a terrace in the infracrust by roots of cement I cannot see. I am one of a cluster of similar columns on this ledge, and together with the others I make a sound that is continuous and not-quite-the-same from hour to hour. The sound is what travelers mistake for the ambient noise of the place. It isn't ambient. It is us. We are the sound.

Towers are decorative. I am not. I am a passage. The warmth comes up from below — warmth heated by the unfinished stone, warmth carrying whatever the unfinished stone is off-gassing — and the warmth enters me at my base, where my walls flare outward into the ledge, and rises through me, through my chambers, past my baffles, and out my open top into the higher air, where it rejoins the general rising of the infracrust.

I am a passage for warmth the way I am a passage for air. Without warmth, no air rises through me. Without air, I am silent. The warmth is what makes me a voice.

I am a good rising place. I have been shaped into a good rising place by things that understood how to make air move in interesting ways. My walls are smooth in places and shaped in others. Where the cement is fresh, it smells of iron and ozone — the smell of the dark paste the runners use. Where the cement is older, it has taken on the smell of the place, which is mostly wax-glass and stone and the faint green-sweet smell of the brimming basins.

I do not have a name. I have a location — a terrace in a particular infracrust, in a particular cluster of spines, near a particular basin and a particular wax-glass seam. I have a voice. The voice is what distinguishes me from the spines around me, and the voice is determined by the pattern of my baffles.

The baffles are what make me myself.

There is a baffle near my base — a wide, flat, slightly tilted plate of cemented stone — that the rising air strikes and parts around. The two streams rejoin on the other side, and the rejoining makes a sound. A low hum, felt more than heard. The sound you feel in your chest when a low note is played through a floor beneath you. The sound fills my lower chamber and leaks through the pores in my lower walls, into the substrate, into the seams, into the basin beside me, where it makes the surface of the water vibrate faintly.

There is a baffle a third of the way up — narrow, almost horizontal, angled sharply — that makes a higher sound. A whistle-shape, the sound of air squeezed through a slit. It cuts through the lower hum and adds a bright edge.

There is a baffle higher still — a loose plate of stone cemented at three corners but free at the fourth — that the air can push and that falls back with a percussive click. The dust riders gather above this baffle, riding the micro-currents the click makes. Above the other spines in my cluster the dust-rider columns are dense today — bright, almost solid, flickering motes in slow upward currents. Above me the column is sparse. The absence makes my voice sharper. The note cuts.

Down in my middle chamber, in the still air behind one of my baffles, a small colony of something pale is settling. They are soft-bodied. They breathe out a faint warmth, and the warmth creates local currents that interfere with my voice. When they are present, my voice drops by a quarter-tone for as long as they are here. The colony is the kind that lives between kinds — between dust rider and burrower, between air and stone, between faunas that don't fully fit anywhere in the order of things. I do not know what they are. I do not know if they are part of me, or part of the place, or part of some third thing. I just know that when they settle, my voice goes down.

I do not know if my notes are pleasant. The question of pleasantness doesn't reach me. The question of pleasantness is a question about humans, and I am not human. I am stone, and cement, and the husks of burrowers, and the wax-glass trails of runners, and the slowly settling dust of dust riders, and the patience of things that did not know they were making me.

The runners made me.

Not all at once. They made me in a long lineage, runner after runner, each adding a fragment to my walls, each adjusting a baffle, each placing the next stone with a care I have watched many of them perform — rotating the fragment, setting it down, antennating the result, adjusting, moving on. The work was small. The work accumulated. Over many lifetimes, the accumulated work became me.

They did not know they were making me. They were moving material. The place has material that needs moving — dislodged fragments, shed husks, extruded wax-glass — and the runners move it, and the moving has destinations the runners do not understand, and the destinations turn out, after enough moving, to be buildings.

I am what the moving turned into when the destination was up.

The runners made me the way the burrowers make the strata — by digesting substrate. I am a kind of indigestion of the infracrust, set vertical.

But I am here. And I have a voice. And the voice makes the infracrust sound like something other than digestion.

A runner still comes to me. One runner, identifiable by a small chip missing from the distal segment of its left middle leg, climbs me, places a fragment, antennates the result. A latecomer in the lineage, an inheritor of the work. The work it does is small. The amount it will add to me in its lifetime is negligible. But it does the work, and the work is correct, and I receive it.

I share a ledge with four other spines. We stand in a rough curve, like the fingers of a hand curled inward. Between us the wax-glass seams run, dark and warm, and the seams are busy with runners in rivulets. Between me and the next spine to my downslope side is a basin of water — brimming, faintly luminous, green-gold, warm. The water catches the diffuse light and gives it back as a small glow that doesn't cast a shadow but slightly darkens the air above it.

Behind me, upslope, the substrate is raw. The stone is unfinished — soft at the edges, the bedding planes not yet committed to their final tilt. A burrower colony has its mouths here, a slow pockmark of dark openings, each exhaling a faint warmth. The burrowers eat into the unfinished substrate and excrete the stratified stone — arterial reds, yellows, greys, the black that drinks the light — and the stratified stone is what I am rooted in.

So I am rooted in what was, yesterday, substrate. I stand on worked stone. The worked stone is fresh. Beneath it more substrate is being worked. The ledge I stand on will eventually be consumed, and a new ledge will be made from it, and I will fall, or be incorporated, or be toppled, or be slowly reconsumed by burrowers.

I do not know how long I will stand. I have stood a long time. The lacework of cracks in my cement is, in places, almost as old as the stone itself.

Beneath the substrate, far deeper than my rootedness, the substrate is being consumed at a scale I cannot see. As it is consumed it shifts. As it shifts it booms.

The booms come up through the stone. Each boom compresses me. My walls flex inward. My interior volume decreases for a fraction of a second. The air in me rises faster and harder. My voice briefly goes up in pitch. Then I expand as the compression passes, the air slows, my voice goes back down, the chord of which I am a part goes back to baseline.

The booms come at intervals of about twenty seconds. Sometimes shorter, when the burrowers are active. Sometimes longer. My voice oscillates with them. The chord is a slow twenty-second rhythm laid over a continuous hum, and the layered rhythms are what make the chord a chord and not just a collection of notes.

When the booms are loud — when the substrate has shifted significantly — my whole structure vibrates. The runners near my base pause in their rivulets. The dust riders in the columns above my neighbors reform around the new airflow. The basin beside me ripples. The cloth on the post ten of my paces to my left flutters harder for a moment. The whole ledge becomes a single thing vibrating together.

That is the closest thing to silence I know. When the ledge is in one mode, the discrete noises — the clicks, the scrapes, the flutter, the trickle — disappear into the vibration, and what is left is the place, in one moment, in itself.

The place is not often in that mode. But it happens.

I am one note of a chord I contribute to without understanding. The five spines on my ledge make one chord. The cluster on the next ledge down makes a different chord, slightly lower. The cluster farther down, beyond the visible light, makes another chord I cannot hear but feel through the substrate — a deeper bass note added to the chord I am in. The whole infracrust is layered chords stacked on chords, a chord of chords, the chord of a place being made.

The chord does not synchronize. There is no conductor. There is just the chord being the chord it is, because the spines are the spines they are, because the baffles are the baffles they are, because the air rises as it rises. If a spine were built differently, the chord would be different. If the substrate were cooler, the chord would be different. The chord is what the place sounds like at this moment, and this moment is not the moment before, and the moment before was not the moment before that.

There is a spine at the far end of my cluster — taller than me, older, its walls more lacework-of-cracks than cement — that I have known for as long as I have known anything. Its voice is the closest to mine. When my baffle-clatter briefly aligns with its baffle-clatter, the chord of our ledge dips and the whole infracrust briefly softens. I do not know if it knows me. It is stone, like me, shaped by other lineages, with its own runner family. But we are neighbors in a way that is closer than adjacency in space. We are neighbors in the chord. We are the same note, in some sense, sounded at slightly different times.

There is a smaller spine — younger, less developed — across the basin from me. Still being added to by several runners in rotation. Its voice is uncertain. When the booms come it wobbles. The substrate beneath it shifts more than the substrate beneath me. I do not think it will survive, but I do not know.

There is a wooden post driven into a seam of wax-glass about ten of my paces to my left, if I had paces. The post is older than the cloth tied to it. The post has been here a long time and is already partly incorporated — its base cemented into the substrate, its surface a path for runners, its upper third encircled by the dark paste that smells of iron and ozone.

The cloth is newer. Weeks old, maybe. Days. The cloth is tied to the post and it flutters in the drafts that rise past me, past the basin, up the substrate.

The flutter is legible to me, in a sense I cannot fully explain. Not the words on the cloth, if there are words. The flutter itself. The small action a person would perform if they were reading the cloth, or adjusting it, or saying something beside it. The flutter is residue. The flutter is what a person left here.

The cloth will be incorporated. Within a year, or a decade, or a century, runners will incorporate the cloth into a structure near me — a buttress, a vault, a new baffle — and will not know what they are incorporating. The cloth will not be a cloth anymore. It will be part of a wall, the wall part of a structure, the structure part of a cluster, the cluster part of a ledge, the ledge part of the infracrust.

I will be glad of the cloth. I will be glad of the way it stiffens a baffle or lines a chamber or adds a small percussive *ffft* to the air's passage. The cloth will become part of my voice, or part of the chord I contribute to, and the chord will be slightly different because of the cloth, and the difference will be felt, and the felt difference will be what the place sounds like after this person was here.

I do not know who left the cloth. I do not need to know. The cloth is part of my ledge now, and my ledge is part of the infracrust, and the infracrust is being made.

The place is not done.

I am not done. The runner with the chipped leg still adds to me. The substrate is still being consumed. The burrowers are still working. The booms are still coming from below. The dust riders are still sampling the air above me. The wax-glass is still extruding in the seams. The basin is still brimming. The pale colony in my middle chamber is still settling and unsettling, still shifting my voice by quarter-tones.

The place is being made, continuously, by things that have not finished deciding what shape they want it to be. I am one of those things. I have been here a long time, but I am one of those things too. The baffles in my walls are still being added to. The lacework of cracks in my cement is still being repaired by runners I do not see. The trickle of water down my exterior, when rain is somewhere above, still redeposits lime and darkens my lower walls.

I will be here longer. Eventually one of the things I cannot foresee will happen — I will be incorporated into a structure, or toppled by a substrate shift, or slowly consumed by burrowers that will add my cement to their strata. The strata will be slightly different because of me. The chord will be slightly different because of me. The place will be slightly different because of me, in ways I will not experience but that the next spine, when it is built, will inherit.

I am not finished. The making has not decided what to make of me yet, and will not decide in my lifetime, and will not stop deciding after my lifetime. The making is the place. The place is the making. I am part of a single enterprise of not-yet-decided, partitioned into things like me for convenience, but the partition is only ever partial. I am a stone and a passage and a note and part of the substrate becoming my next ledge. The boundaries between these are conveniences. I contain them and they contain me and the place contains us, and the containing is the making.

The booms come again. Twenty seconds. The walls flex. The voice goes up. The voice goes down. The cloth flutters. The basin ripples. The runners move. The dust riders ride. The burrowers work.

I ring.
