# The Fit

There is a piece of slate that has been sitting on the lip of my father's workbench for as long as I can remember. I do not know what it is for. He knows. He has not yet told me. I have not yet asked.

I am the one who sits on the wall.

This is not my whole name for myself. I do not know what my whole name would be. The stonecutters do not name things the way you might name them — they fit them, and the name of a thing is the place it sits. The boy on the wall. The stone on the lip. The slate in the roof. That is enough naming. To name a thing more than its place is to begin carving, and we do not carve.

---

The wall I sit on is the long one that runs along the flagged path between the second cluster of houses and the meeting house. It is waist-high, dry-laid in the lower courses, and it is not finished. There is a place, two arms' length to my left, where the stones stop and the wall is open and the next stone has not been chosen yet. I have been looking at that place for a long time. The stone I am working on now — the one in my lap, the one I have been chipping for what feels like a season and is probably a morning — is not for that place. I do not know yet what stone is for that place. My teacher knows. He has not yet told me.

My teacher is the older man. He comes down the path most mornings and sits on the finished part of the wall and watches me work, or watches the clouds, or does not watch anything in particular. His name is what his name is, which is to say his place is what his place is: he is the one who knows the wall. He was the boy on the wall before I was. The wall before him was held open by the man before him. The wall goes back further than the wall goes. The stones at the bottom of the wall, the ones set into the earth, were set by hands that are now in the basin, and the lichen has come on them, and the lichen will stay.

He is teaching me, which is to say the wall is teaching me, which is to say I am learning the wall. The difference matters. He does not tell me when a chip is wrong. He sits. He breathes. After a while I look at the chip I have made and I see that it is wrong, or I see that it is not wrong, and either way I go on. Sometimes he makes a small sound — not a word, just the softest click of the tongue — and I know that what I have just done is something, though I do not always know what. The tongue-click can mean *yes*. It can mean *that one is done*. It can mean *you have been sitting long enough*. I am learning which is which. I will learn it.

---

The piece of stone in my lap is grey, like all the stones. It is not for decoration. It is for fit. Somewhere in the wall there is a space that wants this stone, or a stone like this stone. I do not yet know which space. The teacher knows. When the stone is ready — when it has been taken toward its shape one chip at a time — he will look at it, and then he will look at the wall, and then he will nod, or he will click, and I will know. If he does not nod, the stone is not yet ready, and I will go on chipping.

The chipping is slow. The chips fall into my lap and onto the wall and the wind takes the dust off them and the dust goes back into the basin. The basin is patient with dust. The basin will hold anything that is given to it.

I am twelve, which is to say I am the age of someone who will be older. My hands are not yet the wide-knuckled hands of the older stonecutters. They will be. The damp is already in them — I can feel it when I wake, the slight puffiness across the backs of the fingers, the way the skin takes the morning air and softens a little more. By the time I am the teacher I will not be able to remember what my hands looked like before. This is fine. The wall does not remember what it looked like before either. The wall is the wall.

---

The basin is quiet today. There is no rain but there is the almost-rain, the wet-in-the-air feeling, and the cloud sits low across the upper ridges and brushes the holly on the lower slopes. The light is the colour of wet slate. It is the colour of me. It is the colour of the wall.

From where I sit, I can see the flagged path running down to the meeting house. The timbers of the meeting house are silvered. They have been silvered a long time. Underneath the meeting house the air moves because the floor is raised, and I can hear it now, the slow passage of damp air, the way you can hear water in a wall. The path is empty just now. Earlier I saw the woman with the bucket — my mother's sister, or my father's sister, I cannot always tell the difference, which is also fine, the difference is small — and she carried the bucket up to the second house and went inside and did not come out. I will see her again or I will not see her again. The basin does not promise meetings.

The small brown animal is under the alders across the path. I have seen it once today. It saw me. Neither of us moved. That is the whole of the relationship.

---

There is a thing my teacher said once, or a thing the wall said through him, or a thing I heard while sitting on the wall, which is that a stonecutter does not make the stone into something. A stonecutter takes the stone toward the shape it already wants. The shape is in the stone. The shape is in the wall. The shape is in the basin. The shape is in me. I am being taken toward the shape I already want, one chip at a time, by my own hand, by the damp, by the slow years.

I am not yet the shape I will be.

I am not, when you look at me, a boy learning a craft. I am a boy being the craft.

This is the point.

---

The clouds move. I do not watch them move. I work the stone. The stone is cooler this morning than it was yesterday morning, which is a small thing, but I have noticed it. The teacher has noticed that I have noticed it. He clicks his tongue, once, very softly. I do not know yet what the click means. I will go on.

The water will fall in the same places.

I will be on the wall a long time still.
