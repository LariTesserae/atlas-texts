The cup is cedar. That much I can tell before I am within arm's reach of him, because the smell of it comes to me across the last few feet of marsh-smell and standing-water smell and the faint peat-rot of the cloud, and it is a clean smell, a workshop smell, a smell of shavings and of the sap drying on a fresh-cut end. I have not smelled cedar in a long time. There is no cedar at the Mile. There is oak, green-black and weeping; there is willow, lashed and tied; there is split log and rope. The cup should not be here. The cup is, in its small way, a kind of heresy.

He is holding it out to me the way a person holds out a hand.

I am at the south end of the Mile. I have been walking since morning. I crossed the fish bridge at the north and I saw the carver there, or what I took to be the carver — a small woman in a doorway, her hands still, her face turned to me and away in the same instant, and I did not stop, because I understood, without being told, that the fish bridge was not a place to linger unless you had been invited, and I had not been invited, and so I walked on, along the plank-and-rope walkways that have been laid from eave to eave across the upper stories of the drowned houses, the walkways dipping and swaying under my weight, the planks sometimes giving a little to the side so that for a sickening half-second I could look down between my feet and see the black water far below, and the shapes in it that were not furniture.

I saw no one else until the market bridge, and there I saw two people, a man and a woman, sitting at opposite ends of the bridge with a basket of root between them, and they were not speaking, and the basket was not being touched, and the silence between them was so comfortable and so practiced that I felt, walking past, that I was interrupting something I did not have the right to interrupt, and so I walked past with my eyes on the planks and my breathing shallow, and they did not look up, or if they did, they did not let me know it.

I saw the rope cradle from a distance. It was swinging a little, though I could not feel the wind. There was no one in it.

And then the path thinned, and the houses thinned, and the marsh came back — reeds, then water, then the grey-green country beyond, folded like a cloth that has been sat on — and the last house, smaller than the others, with its door open onto nothing but the reeds and the small channel of water that might be a path, might be a river, might be the place where the Mile's water goes to breathe out.

And the boy, in the doorway. The wooden cup in his hand. The cup that was lost sixty years ago, or that was never lost at all, or that has always been here, waiting at the bottom of the black water for someone to come down and fetch it.

I am not a stranger, exactly. I have been at the Mile for — I do not know how long. A season, maybe. Long enough to have been given a place-name of my own, which is *the one who came from the north road*, and which is how I am known at the market bridge, and which I have not yet been able to make sound natural in my own mouth. Long enough to have eaten the fish that is the length of a man's leg, and the root that tastes of regret, and to have learned to eat both of them without comment, which is, I think, the first thing the Mile asks of a person: that you do not comment on what you have been given.

Long enough to have seen the water breathe. Long enough to have watched it go from the second step to the fifth and back again in the slow exhalation of an afternoon, and to have felt, as it went down, the whole Mile lift a little, the way a person's shoulders lift when they breathe out, and to have understood, then, that the city is not drowned. The city is *in*. The city is breathing. The water is the breath. We are all of us — the folk, the cats, the chickens, the boy with his cup — we are all of us inside the breath, and the breath has been going on for a hundred years, and it will go on for a hundred more, and no one who lives here minds, because the alternative is to live somewhere that does not breathe, and that is, I am told, worse.

I am standing in front of the boy. I have been standing in front of the boy for what feels like a long time but is probably only a few seconds, because time at the Mile is not what it is on the north road, or at the market bridge, or in any of the places I have been before. Time at the Mile is the time of the water. It goes in. It goes out. It does not hurry.

The cup is still held out. The cedar smell is very strong. The boy's face is patient, and a little amused, and a little sad, the way the faces of the folk here are a little amused and a little sad, all at once, as though they have long ago made their peace with the fact that the world is a wet and breathing thing and have decided to be fond of it anyway.

I reach out. I do not take the cup. I hold my hand beside his, the way you might hold your hand beside a candle to feel the heat of it without burning yourself. The cedar smell is in my palm. The wood is smooth. It has been down in the water for sixty years, and the water has not marked it. That is the heresy of it: it is the same. It came up clean. It came up *remembering itself*.

The boy looks at my hand. He looks at my face. He does not speak. He is, I think, waiting to see what I will do with what I have been given, and I am, I realize, waiting to see the same thing, and the marsh-smell is coming up around us, and the bitterns are calling from somewhere in the reeds, and the water in the small channel at the end of the house is going in, and out, and in again, and the boy is holding out the cup, and I am holding out my hand, and neither of us is moving, and this, I think, is the conversation. This is the whole of it. The rest is weather.
