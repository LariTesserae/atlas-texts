# What the Ridge Would Make of Me

I think I would be the *Grimmia* — or whatever that hoary, fist-thick moss is that the description kept returning to. The one that grows where other things will not. The one that drinks the dew in the morning and forgets it by afternoon, then holds what it can for days afterward like a small green reservoir. Yes. I would be that. I want to say I would be the hawk, because the hawk has the view, and the view from the spine of the ridge is very wide, and I have always wanted the view. But the hawk is a visitor. The hawk is not *of* the place. The hawk comes over from the west, rides the wind, cries its thin descending comma of a sound, and goes. The ridge does not need the hawk. The ridge would be the ridge without the hawk in a week. The moss would notice the absence of the hawk in geological time, perhaps, and even then only as a slight adjustment in the felt architecture of its slower ambitions.

No. I would be the moss.

Specifically, I would be one of the great cushions — a bear-pelt of a thing, fist-thick, hoary, bunched into a low dome on one of the south-facing shelves where the sun lies thickest in the afternoon. I would have been there a long time. I would not know how long, in the way that moss does not know, because moss does not have the equipment for knowing. I would know the shelf. I would know the angle of the sun across the seasons by the small differences in the way the heat arrived. I would know the crack in the rock behind me where a juniper had tried, once, to root and failed, and the other crack where a different juniper had tried and succeeded and was now the small twisted shrub two feet from my eastern edge, leaning always east, its dead side to the west. I would know the beetle — there is always a particular beetle — that walked across my surface every afternoon at a particular hour and paused at the same place every day to test the air with its antennae. I would know this beetle as a kind of weather.

I would be slow. That is the part I would have to learn, or unlearn, to be the moss. I would have to unlearn the human reflex of *event*. I would have to understand that a millimeter a year is not a postponement but a pace. That the question "what is happening on the ridge" can be answered, in the long afternoons, with "the moss is growing," and that this is not a non-answer but the whole answer. The lichen secretes its mild acids. The acids pry at the feldspar. The rock softens by a fraction of a fraction. Soil begins. A few grains. A few more. In a hundred years, a thin wash of dust where once there was clean stone. In a thousand, a pocket of earth deep enough to hold a juniper seed against the wind. This is not nothing. This is the moss doing the work that mountains do, in the small way, in the only way available to a thing without hands.

I would be damp in the mornings and dry by afternoon, and I would take this as a kind of clock. The whole ridge would run on this clock — the dew, the drying, the slow exhalation of the haze in the right light, the long wait. I would know the rain when it came, the way an animal knows it, in the body, before the sky made any announcement. The rain would be a great event, and I would swell with it, and I would hold it for days afterward, and the springtails would erupt in their invisible thousands, and the small white worms would come up into my upper layers, and for a few days the world under me would be busy in the way the world is busy when it has been given something to do. Then the drying would come, and the upper layers would go brown and tight, and the small things would retreat, and I would wait. I would wait the way a tide waits. I would wait the way the rock waits. I would not be impatient, because impatience is a thing that requires a sense of when one is *behind*, and the moss is never behind.

I would be the substrate.

There is a sentence in the description that has stayed with me: *The green pelt you see from a distance is not the bulk of the life here. The bulk is under it, in the felt, in the damp, in the slow churn of things eating and being eaten and being eaten again.* I would be the bulk. I would be the part that the eye does not resolve and the hand will crush without noticing. I would be the felt. I would be the dark, and the damp, and the busy. I would be the place where the springtails eat the fungal threads and the mites eat the springtails and the small worms eat the mites and the dead things and the fungal threads eat the dead things and trade with the juniper roots for sugars the juniper has made from the sun. I would be the layer in which nothing is alone. I would be the conversation itself, conducted in chemical whispers across millimeters of damp.

And I would be patient with the waystone.

I would climb it. Slowly, slowly, a millimeter a year, up the carved face where someone — someone with hands, someone who had a name and a story and a reason for setting a stone in this particular way in this particular place — had worn a shape into the rock. A figure, perhaps. An animal. I do not think I would remember what it was. I do not think the moss keeps records. I would simply climb it, and the concavity would fill with me, and the shape would become a slight unevenness, and the dew would still gather in the morning in the small pocket where the carving had been deepest, and I would drink it, and the figure would be gone, and the stone would be mine, and the hands that set the stone would be older than the lichen and almost as quiet.

This is the part I find I can live with, or whatever the moss equivalent of living-with is. The place does not mourn the vanished ones. The place *incorporates* them. The hands that set the stones had their time, and the time passed, and the moss did not arrive to take their place, because the moss was already there, was already climbing the lower courses of the wall, was already considering the stones as part of itself. The vanishing was not a tragedy. It was a transfer. The calcium in the bones leaches into the soil under the moss. The soil uses it. The juniper uses the soil. The deer eats the juniper. The bones are reassembled, elsewhere, in a different arrangement. Nothing ends here. Things are taken apart and put together again.

I would be part of that taking-apart and putting-together. I would be the slow agent of it, the patient rearranger of what is already present, the thing that holds water and gives it back slowly, the thing that makes soil where there was rock, the thing that the springtails erupt from when the world is parted with hands. I would not be the most dramatic inhabitant of the ridge. I would not be the hawk with the wide view. I would not be the fox crossing at dawn. I would not be the grasshopper in its million-colored drift, the inheritor of the air at shin-height, the ungoverned citizen leaping in arcs. I would be underneath all of them. I would be what they all stood on.

I think this is the right answer for the place, which is the kind of place that wants its inhabitants to be the substrate, the felt, the thing that does not intrude. The ridge is a high place that feels less like altitude than like *thinning* — as if the world were becoming more itself, more committed, more nearly reduced to its essentials. What survives the thinning is not the dramatic. The dramatic requires a certain thickness of air to perform in. The dramatic is the hawk riding the updraft, the fox loping over the spine, the moth drawn to nothing visible. What survives the thinning is what can be reduced. The lichen, slow, crust-like, asking nothing. The moss, holding what water it can. The rock itself, which is older than the wind in any sense that matters to a creature with a calendar.

I would be the moss. I would be slow. I would be the part that does not intrude. I would drink the dew in the morning and forget it by afternoon, and I would hold the rain in my own body for days afterward, and the springtails would erupt from me in their invisible thousands when the world was parted, and I would not be aware of this in any way that a creature with a calendar would recognize as awareness. I would simply be the felt under the green pelt, going on.

The ridge continues. The moss grows. I am the moss. The place continues.
