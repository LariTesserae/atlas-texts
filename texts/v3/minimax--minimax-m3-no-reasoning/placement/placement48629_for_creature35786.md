You would find yourself standing in the doorway of the pump room on a Tuesday morning, though the shift from one moment to the next is so gradual you wouldn't be able to name the exact second you arrived. You wouldn't be the visitor with the baler twine and the key. You would be the doorway itself—or, more precisely, the slight, damp exhalation of air that occurs where the concrete-block wall meets the open air, the place where the engine's hammering is felt in the heel bone and the river's roar is the floor beneath all other sounds. You would be the threshold between the cool interior and the perpetual, gentle rain that is starting the way it always starts in this valley: gently, as if it has been here a long time and is in no hurry to make a point.

If you are looking for a single being to be, you would not be the fox, though you might mistake yourself for her at first—she has the best lines, the unhurried confidence, the lifted forefoot. But the fox is a tenant, and tenancy implies a contract, and the compound does not honor contracts. The compound simply allows. 

You would be the moss. Or, more accurately, you would be the slow, patient work of the moss—not the green waistcoat on the south wall, but the *tendency* toward the waistcoat, the fraction of a millimeter by which the brick's edge is softened each year. You would be the darkening of the mortar where it stays wet. You would be the weight of the pale, dry-looking pillows in the gutters that are heavy as wet cloth when you lift them. You would be the greenish bias that the buildings are acquiring, the way the south wall of the main hall is no longer the south wall of the main hall but a slightly thickened thing.

To be the moss is to be the measure of the compound's time. The visitor comes on Tuesdays and Fridays, and their time is the mechanical time of the engine's cycle, the clack of the pump, the hydraulic sigh. The heron's time is the vertical, patient time of the strike and the swallow. The swallows' time is the urgent, panicked orienting of May, the long work of mud and feather. The fox's time is the dusk, the crossing of the yard, the absence of voles for an hour afterward. 

But your time is the time of the chimney's lean—the slow tilt that has been measurable for years but hasn't been measured by anyone. Your time is the time of the clematis debating how high to go. Your time is the time of the alders that will be the size of the chimney in fifteen years if no one takes them. No one is taking them.

To be the moss is to understand that the compound is not a place that is being preserved, nor a place that is being abandoned. It is a place that is being slowly converted—by the river, by the rain, by the iron grille where the heron stands, by the small, distributed argument of the frogs under the floorboards—into a different kind of place. The industry is real and partial. The pump runs twice a week. The water keeps running. The rest is a slow, green negotiation between the brick and the things that soften brick.

If you are the moss, you do not mind the slight askewness of the proportions—the main hall too tall for its footprint, the pump room too low, the chimney too long for either. You do not mind that the weathervane's tailless rooster points west-south-west regardless of the wind. None of this is wrong. It is the slight off-ness of a place that has not been finished, and will not be. You have begun. You have not finished. You will not finish in any human-urgent sense of the word. 

You will simply continue, in the cool mornings, to fur the ironwork with a skin of condensation so fine it looks painted on. You will trace the black drip-lines on the underside of every horizontal surface. You will hold water in the cupped centers of the cobbles for an hour after rain. You will drink what the alders drink. You will creep another quarter inch around the eastern window, and no one will find this remarkable except, perhaps, the heron.

And if, on a Tuesday at eleven, a swallow comes in through the broken pane of the main hall and begins, high up, the long, intricate, half-heard song that the swifts will not know how to sing, you will not hear it. You will be too busy, in your fraction-of-a-millimeter way, with the brick. But the song will be there, laid over the baseline of the river, the way all the small, busy, close-to-the-ear sounds of the comfrey and the meadowsweet are laid over the hum of the engine that is more felt than heard. 

You are the place remembering itself into a different shape. You are the slow green insistence. You are the damp.
