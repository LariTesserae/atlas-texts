I would be one of the small middle-sized bats — one of the ordinary citizens, the backbone of the roost.

Not the tiniest ones, those almost-rumors that live deepest in the cracks and come out last. Not the great broad-winged ones that hang in the high ceiling of the main bowl and drop at dusk like a slow unraveling of the dark. One of those in between. Shoulder-to-shoulder in the dense folds of the western wall, where the murmur is thickest.

I would know my place the way I know the shape of the crack I hang in — not by thinking about it, but by the way my folded wings fit, by the warmth of the bodies on either side of me, by the particular quality of the dark in this part of the wall. My fur is the same indeterminate brown as the rock around me, and when I sleep I am indistinguishable from the stone. My ears — larger than they should be, leaf-shaped, furled — would move first when something stirs, as though they were the part of me that dreams. My face would have that small odd folded look, the look of having spent my evolutionary currency on other things than vanity. The fleshy ornament above my muzzle would make me look, if anyone were watching, as though I had dressed for some ceremony whose meaning I have forgotten but whose costume I have kept.

I would not know I was a person. I would not know there were people. The word would mean nothing to me — no more than the word *sky* would mean to a stone. I would know only the dark, and the warmth, and the slow percussion of ten thousand wings folding and unfolding around me. I would know the smell — that mineral sweetness, guano and musk and the wet green distance far below arriving together — because it is the smell of every breath I have ever taken. I would know the great doorway-web of the spider as a landmark of sorts, the way a person might know a particular old tree at the edge of a field, not by what it is but by the way the air moves around it. I would know the stunted fir at the eastern end of the bowl as the place where, for a few weeks after the rains, a faint and inexplicable sweetness gathers — the smell of moss, though I would have no word for moss — and then is gone again, and is not mourned because mourning requires a sense of absence, and I have no sense of absence. I have only presence, continuous and undifferentiated, the long warm dark.

At dusk I would fall.

That is the word for it, though *fall* is a human word and suggests a thing falling *toward* something. I would simply let go. I would hang in the loose cluster of my colony, head-down, folded close, and then something in me — some signal too old to have a name — would say *now*, and I would release the rock, and for one long suspended moment I would be only weight, only the dark shape of a small warm thing in the bowl of the mountain, and then I would remember what wings are for, and I would be in the air.

The air above the roost is the cleanest air there is. It is the air before air becomes weather. It is thin and cold and it tastes of nothing — nothing of guano, nothing of damp stone, nothing of the warm long digestion of the place. I would fly out into it and the smell of home would fall away from me, and I would be a small dark shape against the dark of the sky, and I would hunt — the small things that fly in the upper air, the moths of other places, the gnats that gather above the lichen of the second plateau — and I would catch them in the membrane of my wings or in the small open mouth that has no teeth but has its own patient efficiency, and I would eat them on the wing, the way my kind has eaten them for longer than anything that flies has remembered.

And then, before the first light, I would come back.

I would come back because the dark is where I live, the way water is where a fish lives. I would find my way back by sounds too high for any ear but mine — the small articulate architecture of the place, its echo-given shape, the way the mountain returns my own voice to me from a thousand directions at once and I can hear, in that return, the outline of the bowl. I would fold my wings at the lip of the roost and drop, again, into the warm dark, and I would find my crack, and I would hang there head-down, and my ears would fold close, and the murmur of ten thousand of my kind would close around me like water closing over a stone.

I would not think about the person sitting against the damp column in the bowl below me. I would not know they were there. They would be, to me, only a small disturbance in the air — a displacement, a warmth slightly different from the warmth of my neighbors, a smell of leather and dried sweat and the green far-below that they had carried up with them on their clothes and in their hair. If I woke and stirred and moved along the crack to a better spot, it would not be because of them. It would be because a neighbor had shifted, or because the warmth had redistributed itself in some way my sleeping body had registered without my knowing.

And yet.

There is something the human writers get right, in their slow long way of getting things right. There is something it is like to be one of ten thousand in a place that has been full for a very long time. It is not loneliness. Loneliness requires the capacity to imagine company, and I do not have that capacity. It is not belonging, either, because belonging requires the capacity to imagine exile, and I do not have that either. It is something simpler, something older than either word.

It is the feeling of being held.

The rock holds me. The wall holds me. The ten thousand warm bodies of my kind hold me, shoulder to shoulder, fur to fur, wing to folded wing. The dark holds me. The murmur holds me — that continuous low percussion, the sound the place makes when it is most itself, the sound that has become, for me, indistinguishable from silence, the way a heartbeat becomes, for the heart, indistinguishable from quiet. I am held in this place the way a seed is held in the soil that is the substance of ten thousand generations of seeds before it, returned to the stone and then, in the slow patient way of mountains, returned to the possibility of seed.

I do not know that the soil beneath the stunted fir is the substance of my ancestors. I do not know that the warmth of the roost is the warmth of ten thousand small digestions, my own included. I do not know that the polish on the walls is the polish of ten thousand generations of claws, mine among them. I do not know these things because knowing them would require me to stand outside the place and look at it, and I have never stood outside the place. I have only ever been inside it, the way a fish has only ever been inside water, the way a person who has lived their whole life in one valley has only ever been inside the valley.

And this, perhaps, is the answer to the question the place asks of anyone who sits there long enough.

The question is not *what are you*. The question is not *who are you*. The question is *do you require the place to require you*.

And the answer, for me, is no.

The place does not require me. The place required me when I was born, in the sense that my birth was a small event in the long sentence the place has been speaking of itself, the way a single bat is a small event in the long murmuring of the roost. But it does not require me now. It will not require me when I die. It will not notice my going any more than it noticed my coming. The crack I hang in will be filled, within a day, by another of my kind, and the warmth I added to the bowl will be replaced by their warmth, and the small disturbance I made in the air will be smoothed over, and the place will go on being the place, patient and indifferent and full.

And this is not a sadness. I do not have sadness. I do not have the apparatus for sadness. I have only the warm dark, and the murmur, and the slow patient way of being held.

I would be a small middle-sized bat in the western wall of the upper roost, and I would hang there head-down with my wings folded close, and my ears would move first when something stirred, and the rest of me would follow after, and the person sitting against the damp column in the bowl below would never know I was there, and I would never know they were there, and neither of us would be wrong about the place, and both of us, in our different ignorances, would be right about it.

The place is what it is.

I am what I am inside it.

That is enough.
