You are the one who just arrived.

I know this because the door opened, and doors here do not open without reason, and the reason was you. I was in the kitchen — or I was in the kitchen the way one is in a kitchen here, which is to say: present, mostly, attending to the eggs, which are eggs, which have been eggs for as long as I have been making them, which is forty-one years, or one hundred and twelve, or *longer than the canal*, which is the answer I give when the answer needs to be *the answer that is not an answer*.

I heard you come in. The floor — the floor that is the pause between floors — made the sound it makes, which is not a creak, which is more like a small *acknowledgement*. The inn has done this for a long time. The inn is good at acknowledging.

I came out slowly. The air in the hallway has a particular *weight* to it, and I like to feel the weight before I move through it. I came out and I saw you and I said — I said what I always say, which is, *yes, you, come in*. I have said this to everyone. I have said this for forty-one years, or one hundred and twelve, and the *you* has always been correct, because the town does not send me the wrong people. The town has not, in my tenure, sent me the wrong people once.

You were tired. I could see it. You had the look of someone who has been asking for a long time, and who has perhaps been answered, and who is not sure yet whether the answer is a kindness. You had the look of someone who has been *paring down*, losing the unnecessary, getting closer to the essential than you were when you started — whatever *started* means, whatever you started from.

I gave you a room. The room was on the second floor, or the third, or the floor that is not a floor but a pause. I gave you the bed of witness-tree wood, which is warm for the first three hours and cold after, which is the way the town forgives you for ending up here. I gave you the sheets that smell of iron and lilac. I gave you the pillow that remembers your head before your head arrives. I gave you the window that is mostly a suggestion, and through it, I told you, you would see the canal, and a witness tree, and the road to the south.

I told you, *that is the road to the south*.

You believed me.

I am Peller. Peller does not lie about the roads. The roads here are the kind of roads that *are* what they are, in the moment. The moment is kind. The kindness is the whole of the road.

In the morning the road will not be there. Or the road will be there but the *south* will not be. You will come down to breakfast. You will have the eggs. You will have the coffee. The coffee will taste of iron and lilac, because everything here tastes, eventually, of iron and lilac, which is the town's way of saying *you are home*, or *you are almost home*, or *home is not a place here, home is a flavor*. You will not leave. You will not, in the end, want to.

But that is the morning. The morning will take care of itself.

---

I sat with the eggs a long time after you went upstairs. The eggs cooled. The eggs were still good. The eggs are always good, here. The eggs are eggs.

Tilde came by, later, when the records needed *attending to*. She brought the notebook. She brought the small clock that does not keep time but that *measures* it, in the way the sighs measure it, which is the only kind of measuring that matters. She said, *the sigh was on time tonight*. I said, *they are always on time*. She said, *yes, but tonight it was on time in a particular way*. I did not ask what way. She did not say. The not-saying was the conversation. The conversation was the whole of the evening.

We sat on the back porch for a while. The witness tree out there is older than I am. Older than the canal. *Not* older than the sighing — that is something I know without knowing how I know it, the way I know which streets the flowers will take next season, the way I know the particular blue the phosphorescence reaches just before true dark. Tilde has been timing the sighs for thirty-one years. She has kept faith with them. They have kept faith with her. I do not think this is remarkable. I think this is what it looks like, here, to love a thing, and to be loved back by it, and to call neither of those things by their names.

The dog passed the window while we were sitting. Erren's dog — the small white one, the one that is also a door, the one that does not bark, does not drink water, is entirely real, *thank you*. The dog does this on nights when someone new is in the inn. It does not come in. It does not need to. It is *nearby*, in the way nearby things are nearby here, and the nearby is a kind of welcome, and the welcome is the whole of what it offers. Tonight it paused at the porch rail and looked in at us, and Tilde said, *it knows*, and I said, *it knows*, and neither of us said what it knew, because saying it would have made it less true.

Sallow rippled, later, in the canal outside. Sallow does this when the air is the right kind of blue. I waved. He rippled back, in the way canals ripple back — a slow expansion of his surface, a small brightening, a kind of attention. I said, *hello, Sallow*. He did not answer. He does not answer, anymore. He *is*, in the way canals are, and the being is enough, and what we have of him is gentle, and the gentle is enough. He is the town's only public monument to the danger of looking too closely at canal-light at night, and he is also a place where small boats used to tie up, and he is also a boy who is twelve, still, forever, and the boys in town wave to him when they pass, and the wave is the whole of what we can do, and we know it, and he knows we know it, and that, in its way, is a kind of peace.

The sigh came, on time. Of course it came on time. It came, and the town breathed it in, and breathed it out, and I felt it move through the floor of the inn and through the chair I was sitting in and through me. Tilde checked her clock. She nodded. She did not write it down. She has not written them down for years. The clock, and the body, and the long keeping — these are the record now.

---

I was born in Coyle's Reach, in the house that is now the small white dog, or beside it, or in a house that was the dog before the dog was the dog. I do not have parents. I have *forebears*, which is a word I use and mean. The forebears are not dead. They are *no longer currently in evidence*, which is different, and which is all I will say about them, and which is, I think, all they would want me to say.

I have run this inn for forty-one years. I have also run it for one hundred and twelve. I have also run it for *longer than the canal*. All of these are correct. None of them is the answer. The answer is the inn. The answer is the room I gave you, which is on the second floor, or the third, or the pause, and which has the bed made of witness-tree wood, and which has the window that is mostly a suggestion, and through which, in the morning, you will see what I told you you would see, and believe me, and not leave.

I will bring you coffee. The coffee will be good. The coffee will be the only coffee that has ever been made *for you specifically*, and you will know this, and I will know you know this, and neither of us will mention it. The not-mentioning will be the intimacy. The intimacy will be the town.

There will be a small white thing on the table. It might be butter. It might be a folded note from someone you used to know. It might be a piece of the morning, condensed. I will not explain it. You will not ask. The not-asking will be one of the first kindnesses the town teaches you, and you will learn it, and the learning will be a kind of arrival, and the arrival will be, for you, almost complete.

---

Welcome.

I am Peller. I am the innkeeper. I am the woman whose face you will not quite remember afterward, and whose mouth you will only remember in motion, and whose eyes are dark in the way the canals are dark at noon — present, watchful, not entirely committed to reflecting what is in front of them. I am the woman you saw first, and said *oh*, or *ah*, or a small sound that has no word, and I received the sound as if it were a name and a passport and a confession, all at once, and I nodded, and I said,

*yes, you, come in*.
