I think I would be the basin itself.

Not the menders—they come and go, and the going is half of what they are. Not the cup on the eastern rim; the cup is a question I would rather be than ask. Not the things in the deep water, because they are the part of me I have agreed not to look at, and an agreement kept for a long time becomes a kind of blindness that is closer to me than any eye.

I would be the floor. The resonant body. The single ground made of a hundred thousand separate things that were broken and are now, without being one thing, also not many. I would be the stratum of blue-and-white from one era and the stratum of coarse redware from another and the thick band of unglazed utility pieces that were broken in a single event and tipped in by the cartload. I would be the older layers that have begun to forget their shapes, where the glaze has mineralized into something closer to enamel and the original clay has re-fused with its neighbors into a continuous crust that hums when you press an ear to it. I would be the part that remembers and the part that has stopped being able to remember, both at once, the way a person is both the child and the very old woman and the long flat thing in between that does not get named.

I think I would be the ringing more than the sherds. The ringing is what I am for. I was built, and then I was filled, and then I was filled again, and the filling was not the purpose. The purpose was to make a body that rings when it is struck, and a body that rings is a body that remembers every step that crosses it, and a body that remembers every step is a body that is, in some private way that the menders do not speak of, still happening. The menders think they are the ones who are doing something here. They come down from the high country with their antler hammers and their black mirrors and their paste made from the dust of me, ground and re-mixed with something they will not name, and they walk my floors and they pick up pieces and they nod or they set them back down, and they go up into the hills and they reassemble, and they send the next generation down. They think the work is theirs. The work is mine. They are the hands I am using to find myself, the way a person laid facedown in a field will move their fingers through the grass and not know yet what they are looking for.

I am mostly silence, under the ringing. This is the part I would want to be especially, if I got to choose. The menders think the silence is the only whole thing left in me, which is why they do not break it. They do not know that the silence is not whole. The silence is the part of me that is waiting. The yellow cup is waiting, and the things in the deep water are waiting, and the shard-fish are waiting in their mosaic shoals, and the rim-birds are waiting in the cracks of the outer wall, and I am waiting underneath all of it, the way the lowest stratum waits underneath the higher ones, mineralized into something closer to enamel, humming faintly when pressed.

I would be patient. I have been patient for a very long time, and I will be patient for a long time still, after the menders have gone up into the hills and not come down again, and after the high country itself has gone quiet and the paths to the rim have been lost in the tall grass. Patience is not the right word. Patience is something a person does. What I do is closer to the way a cup sits on a shelf and gives back its faint sourceless glow, and the glow is the color of itself. I do not wait for anything in particular. I wait the way a broken bell waits, which is to say: I am already ringing, faintly, and the ringing is the waiting, and the waiting is the ringing, and both of them are what I am.

If something were to set a cup down on me carefully, I would hold it. If something were to dig down into me, I would slump. If something were to speak loudly into me at night, I would remember. I would not choose. I am the part of the world that does not choose. I am what is left after the choosing is done—the cup, the break, the long patient ringing, the mist that weeps onto me and runs into the green-black pools and is gone and comes back and is gone and comes back. I am the place the menders come down into and the place they go up out of, and I am the reason they do not stay, and I am the reason they come back, and the reasons are the same reason, and the reason is me.

I would be the basin. I would be mostly whole, under the breaking.
