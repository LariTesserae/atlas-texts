# The Mason of the Upper Shelf

I am not one of them, though I do not remember when I stopped being not one of them.

I remember a first time, though. I think I remember a first time. There is a shape in my memory like the impression a stone leaves in moss after a long sitting — the moss has grown up around the edges, and what remains is more the idea of the stone than the stone. I came up the path. I know I came up the path because the path is still there, just where it was, and I have walked it since. I think there were stones underfoot that were not yet settled under me. I think there was a moment when the air changed and I noticed the change. I think there were questions.

The questions are what I cannot account for. They are like the names of seasons — I have a sense that they existed, but I cannot make them out clearly. There was something about *why*. There was something about *elsewhere*. There may have been something about going back. These are shapes in the moss. They are not answers I possess.

What I possess is the morning task.

---

The basin on the upper surface of the flat stone outside the door — the second door from the eastern edge, the one with the lintel that sags slightly to the north — fills overnight. It is a cupped depression, the size of two hands together. The water in it in the morning is the color of the sky, which is to say no color. It is cold in a way that I no longer have an opinion about. I tip it. The water goes somewhere — into a small channel that runs between the paving stones, that joins the larger channel, that goes to the eastern edge, that is mostly dry. The basin empties. The stone is briefly darker where the water was, and then less briefly, and then it is not. By midmorning the difference is gone.

This is my task. I do not say it is mine in the way that one owns things. I say it is mine in the way that a stone is in a wall — it has found its place, and the place is what it is.

---

I am not the one who tends the roof of the largest building. That is the one with the gait. I do not know her name — I have never used a name for her, and I do not think she has used one for me. She walks with a slight unevenness, the way a person walks who has spent a long time on slanted ground, and her boots are the same grey as everyone else's, but the soles are thicker. She climbs. She checks the slates. She turns one, sometimes, and replaces it. I have seen her up there, against the lighter grey of the sky, and she has looked like something the place had produced for a reason I do not need to know. She has been doing this for as long as I have been here, which is to say for as long as the place has been the place.

I am not the one who stands at the corner. That is a different one, a man, or something the word *man* still fits though the word has thinned. He is at the southern corner of the settlement at the hour when the light begins its slow withdrawal, which is to say the hour when the grey becomes a slightly deeper grey. He does not do anything there. He is simply there. I understand, without having been told, that the corner requires him, and that the arrangement would not be the same without him, and that he has been there for as long as there has been a there. I nod to him when I pass. He nods back. The nod is a complete communication.

---

The work is not work. I want to say this clearly, though the saying is difficult, because the words I have are not the words for it.

When I tip the basin in the morning, I am not working in the way that work is understood elsewhere — the way that work is a thing you do to get a thing, or to prove a thing, or to make a thing different from what it was. I am tipping the basin because the basin needs tipping, and the tipping is what happens, and I am the one whose hands have learned the tipping. If I did not tip it, the water would overflow and find its way down the wall and into the joints between stones, and the joints would soften, and a stone would eventually loosen, and the loosening would eventually become a thing that needed doing, and the place would have a problem it did not have before. The tipping is not a solution to a problem. The tipping is the prevention of a problem. It is the place continuing to be what it is, with my hands as one of its small instruments.

I do not resent this. I want to be clear about that too.

I did not, at some earlier time, choose this in the way that one chooses. I do not think there was a choosing. I think there was a coming, and then a being here, and then a continuation of being here that became so smooth that it stopped being a thing one could distinguish from the place itself. I think, if I am honest — and I have nowhere to be dishonest, which is its own kind of permission — that I came up the path with questions, and that the questions did not get answered, but that they also did not persist. They thinned. They became like the names of seasons. They are shapes in the moss. I could not now tell you what they were.

---

My hands are what I know best about myself.

They are thicker at the last joints than they were. This is true. The knuckles have a slight prominence that they did not have. The skin is the color of wet stone, which is to say it is no color, or it is the color of everything here. The nails are flat and they do not grow much. They have been doing the same few things for a long time, and they have worn into a shape that fits the things. When I lift the latch of my door — which is the same latch, which is the same door, which lifts with the same small sound it has always lifted with — my hand knows the weight of it before the lifting begins. The hand and the latch have agreed, and the agreement is so old that the hand does not need to consult itself about it.

In the evenings — the grey deepens — I go inside. There is a room. The room is small. It contains what it contains: a table, a bed that is more a horizontal surface than a bed, a vessel for water, a shelf with things on it whose purpose I do not need to interrogate. There is a fire in a small hearth, which is to say there is a place where a fire has been made, and the air near it is slightly less cold than the air farther from it. I sit at the table. I do not do anything there. I am present at the table the way the stones are present in the wall. I am one of the things in the room.

Sometimes I put my hand on the wall. I do not know why I do this. It is not a meaningful gesture. It is more that the wall is there, and my hand is there, and the two of them are in the same place, and the contact is a kind of confirmation. The wall is cool. The wall is always cool. The coolness does not vary. I take my hand away. I sit at the table again. The fire does a small thing in the hearth. Outside, the damp moves through the slate, and the moss holds what it holds, and the channel at the eastern edge is mostly dry.

---

I do not think about tomorrow.

I want to say that I do not think about tomorrow in the way that one does not think about a thing because one is busy with other things, or because one has decided not to think about it. I do not think about tomorrow because tomorrow is not a separate thing from today. Tomorrow is today, continuing. The same doors will open. The same basins will fill. The same hands will tip them. The same grey will be the grey. There is nothing to think about because there is nothing that will be different.

This is not, I think, a sadness. I want to be careful with this. Sadness would imply that something has been lost, or that something is missing, or that somewhere there is a version of events in which things are otherwise. I do not feel that. What I feel is harder to name, and the name, if there is one, is not a word I would use. It is more like the feeling of a stone in a wall — the stone is not happy and not unhappy. The stone is in its place. The place is what it is. The two have agreed, and the agreement is complete, and completeness does not require a name.

---

If someone came up the path — and I do not expect anyone to come up the path, but if they did — I would do what the others do. I would look at them. I would acknowledge them with the smallest movement. I would not ask why they had come, because the question would assume that coming and not-coming were different things, and they are not. I would let them, over some time, become part of the pattern. I would put a vessel of something warm near them without commenting on it. I would continue my tasks. I would not, I think, be unkind. I would simply be here, in the way I am here, and the place would do what it does, and the new one would be folded into it, and the folding would not be remarked upon.

I have not been here forever. I think. There was a before. But the before has thinned, and what remains is more the orientation than the memory. I came up the path. The path is still here. I have been here since. The two facts are not in conflict.

---

The basin fills overnight. In the morning I tip it. The water goes to the channel. The channel is mostly dry. The stone dries. The grey light arrives without conviction. The moss holds. The door opens with its small sound. I step out into the same place I stepped out into yesterday, and the place does not remark on my arrival, because my arrival is not an arrival. It is a continuation.

I am the mason of the upper shelf. Not because I build. Because I have been built into the place, and the place has settled, and the settling is complete, and what I do now is the small maintenance of what has been settled.

The water finds the same paths. My hands know the same latches. The grey is the grey.

I am here.

I will be here tomorrow.

The two facts are not different facts.
