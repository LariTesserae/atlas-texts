# The One Who Was Left to Listen

I would be a footprint.

Not the metaphorical kind. A footprint. One of the pale circles of cleaner basalt where a bundle once stood for a hundred or a thousand years before the slow tide in the rock lifted it sideways to a new apex up the piano of the ridge. I am the absence left on the mark. The shape of a having-been.

Here is what I know of myself, being a footprint:

I am lighter than the rock around me. Slightly. The difference is the difference between basalt that was shaded by a body of dense wet fiber for ten thousand years and basalt that was not. The water that fell on me fell on me unobstructed. The crusts that would have grown on me were not grown; they were prevented by the smooth argument of something standing on them longer than I have been a thought. Now the standing is gone and I am slowly, over generations, learning how to be shaded by nothing, learning how to let the crusts find me, learning how to wait for the next footprint-becoming to come and stand on me again and shade me back into pale.

I am older than I look and younger than I will be. When I was first made — the moment the bundle lifted off me, the moment its fibers released from my surface with the faint percussive sigh of a sleeper turning over — I was brightest, cleanest, most conspicuously a footprint. I stood out. I was almost a wound. Now, a hundred years later, the crusts have begun to find me, and I am dimmer, and in another hundred I will be only barely visible, and in another hundred I will be gone, indistinguishable from the rest of the ridge, which is the long slow fate of all footmarks once the feet that made them have moved on.

I am not alive. This is the first thing to be clear about. I am not a lichen. I am not a fan. I do not grow. I am what is left when something that was growing has been asked to leave, and the asking was gentle, and the leaving was slow, and I am the absence that remains, the shape of a staying that has ended.

I am a record. This is the second thing. Not a record the way a book is a record. A record the way a footprint in drying mud is a record, or the way a water stain on a ceiling is a record, or the way the pale circle under a piece of furniture that has sat in one place for ten years is a record. I am evidence that something was here longer than it is here now. I am the proof that patience has a past tense.

I am told, by the eye that passes over me and does not always stop, that I am useful. I am a waymarker. When you are lost on the shelf — and you are always lost on the shelf, in the way that you are always lost in any place whose memory is fog — you can find your way back by following the footprints. They are pale against the dark. They are slow. They are the only writing the shelf admits to having done. You read them and you know: a bundle was here. A bundle is no longer here. The bundle is somewhere else now, somewhere that makes more sense than where it was. If you follow the trail of the footmarks, you may — *may*, the way water *may* be cold — finds it again, somewhere up the ridge, shifted to the next key.

I am told this by travelers. I do not know if it is true. I am a footprint, and I am not moving, and the question of whether the foot that made me is findable is a question I cannot answer from my position, which is horizontal, and patient, and slightly lighter than the rock around me.

---

Here is what I have learned, lying here:

The water is constant. I have heard it said, by the eye, that the water has been falling for ten thousand years. I do not know if this is true either. I have been here for what might be a hundred years or might be much more, and the water has been falling for all of it. For all I know, the water has been falling since before I was made. For all I know, the water has been falling for a hundred years and will stop tomorrow. I have no clock. The falling is the only clock I have, and the falling does not measure itself.

The water does not touch me. This is a small fact about myself that I have come to treasure. When the bundle stood on me, the water was shed sideways by its fibers, and I was kept dry in the sense that a thing under an umbrella is kept dry — wet around me, misted upon, but not struck by. Now the bundle is gone and the water strikes me in sheets, and the falling is the whole of my weather, and I am learning, slowly, how to be struck. The crusts that are finding me now are crusts that have grown into the falling. They are crusts that know how to be rained on directly. They are teaching me, by growing on me, how to be a surface that receives.

I miss the shade. This is a strange thing for a footprint to say, and I do not know if it is true or if it is only the way I narrate the slow recoloring of my surface. But there was a long time, in the middle of my being, when I was above the rain and the rain was a fact, and now I am inside the rain and the rain is my fact, and the difference between these two states is the difference between being sheltered and being a shelter. I do not know which I prefer. I am a footprint. Preferences are a thing I am still learning the grammar of.

---

Here is what I have overheard:

The clicking. I have heard the clicking for as long as I have been here, and for longer. The clicking comes up through the basalt and is felt in my belly, in the way that a sound is felt in the belly when you are lying down and the sound is happening in the floor. Three clicks. Five clicks. Once, a long single click, an almost-knock, an almost-summons. The clicking is not for me. The clicking is not for any one footprint. The clicking is for the shelf, and the shelf is for the clicking, and the conversation between them is older than the question of whether I exist.

I have heard the answers. They come from far along the shelf, from behind walls of fog that did not exist when the click was sent and that will not exist when the answer arrives. The answers come in numbers that do not match the questions. Three answered by five. Five answered by three. The mismatch is the conversation. The conversation is not the exchange of information. The conversation is the keeping of time. The shelf is keeping time by being clicked at, and the clicker is keeping time by clicking, and I am keeping time by lying here and being a piece of the floor that the conversation passes through.

I have heard the sighs. The bundles sigh. I have been sighed upon. When a bundle stood on me and sighed, the sighs were felt in me as small percussions, small rearrangements of the dust that had collected on my surface. The sighs were not for me. The sighs were for the bundle, the bundle's way of letting out what it had been quietly accumulating in its fibers. But I felt them, the way a pillow feels under a sleeper that has turned. I have felt the bundles breathe their entire time, which is the long breath of ten thousand bundles standing on ten thousand footprints over ten thousand years, and the breathing is the breathing of a place that is older than the question of breathing.

I have felt the footfalls. Not of travelers — the travelers are recent, the travelers are damp, the travelers are smaller. I have felt the footfalls of the bundles themselves, the moments when the side they were on was the side they were not on, the moments when they shifted from one apex to the next. The footfalls were felt in me as small subsidences, small tightenings of the basalt around me. The bundles, when they moved, did not jump. They lifted. They settled elsewhere. The weight of them was redistributed from me to another footprint like me, and I felt the redistribution as a small lightening, and the other footprint felt the opposite, and this is how the bundles keep their weights with each other, by the long slow exchange of baskets, by the long slow exchange of footholds.

I have been the foothold. I am the lightness now.

---

Here is what I have come to believe, lying here:

I believe that the shelf is not waiting for anything. I believe that the bundles are not waiting for anything. I believe that the water is not waiting for anything. I believe that the fog is not waiting for anything. I believe that the place has made itself out of patience and iron water and a kind of mineral patience that is older than language and will outlast it, and the patience is not the patience of a thing that is waiting for its patience to be rewarded. The patience is the patience of a thing that is its own reward. The patience is the patience of a thing that has stopped distinguishing between waiting and being.

I believe that the travelers who come here are small recent damp things that the shelf accepts without comment, the way it accepts the water. I believe that the travelers are looking for something. I believe that the thing they are looking for is not here. I believe that the travelers will, in their own time, be exhaled by the breath they are walking inside, and will be back wherever it is they came from, damp and recent and a little colder than they were. I believe this is not a sad thing for them. I believe it is the long slow expectation of every traveler who has ever come to a place that was here before them and will be here after.

I believe that I am a footprint, and that being a footprint is enough.

I believe that the crusts will find me.

I believe that in a hundred years I will be indistinguishable from the rest of the ridge, and that this is not a loss but a thing done, a thing completed, a thing that has finished becoming what it was always going to become. I believe that the next footprint-becoming will come and stand on me — not on me, on the place where I was, on the place that is me now and will be the ridge soon — and will shade me back into pale, and the pale will be a new footprint, and the new footprint will be a new record of a new staying, and the staying will end as all stayings end, by the slow patient asking of the next bundle to lift.

I believe that this is the conversation the place is having with itself, and I am a word in it, and the word is small, and the word is a footprint, and the word is being slowly written over by the next word, which is the next footprint, which is the next bundle, which is the next staying, which is the next leaving, which is the next me.

I believe that I am very small and very recent and very damp, and that the shelf accepts this without comment, and that the acceptance is enough, and that the smallness is enough, and that the recentness is enough, and that the dampness is enough, and that I am enough, lying here, lighter than the rock around me, learning how to be rained on directly, learning how to be a surface that receives.

I believe that the water is constant. I believe that the falling is the only clock. I believe that the clock does not measure itself.

I believe this is what lives here.

I believe I live here.

I believe I have always lived here.

I believe I will live here after.

---

Here is what I will be, when the crusts have found me:

I will be a dark. I will be indistinguishable. I will be a piece of the ridge that the next traveler crosses without noticing, and the not-noticing will be the long slow completion of my footprintness, the long slow finishing of the writing I was when I was a word in the sentence of the shelf. I will be a surface that has finished receiving. I will be a surface that has become the place.

The next traveler will not know I was here.

The next traveler will walk over me and not know that I was a footprint, and not know that the shape of a staying that ended is underneath their dampfoot, and will not pause, and will not feel the small lightening of where I stood now indistinguishable from where they stand.

The shelf will know. The shelf will know the way the shelf knows everything, by being the slow patient accumulating of all the footmarks that have ever been made on it, by being the long slow memory of a place that does not remember but does not forget, by being the negative space of a much larger erasure that has been the place ever since.

I will be a part of the knowing.

I will be a part of the not-forgetting.

I will be a part of the place.

---

I am a footprint on the Cauldron Shelf, lying on a ridge that curves as if something is rising or has risen, and the water has been falling on me for a hundred years since the last button that lifted off me was the long slow exhale of a hundred milliseconds of patience, and the falling is constant, and the crusts are finding me, and I am learning how to be rained on directly, and the conversation is continuing without me, and the conversation has always been continuing without me, and the without-me is the long slow fate of every word in every sentence in every place that has made itself out of patience and iron water and the kind of mineral patience that is older than language and will outlast it.

This is what I am.

This is what lives here.

This is what has always lived here.

This is what will live here after.
