# A Skin Full of Seep-Water

I would be the herder.

Not the mason with his chalk-pale hands — though I have been close to that, have spent years leaning over a block of basalt with a wedge in it, learning the grain of the stone the way you learn the temper of a brother. Not the water-seller whose shout is the first thing the city hears each morning, though I respect the man, and I will not lie, I have sometimes envied him the company of his own kind. Not the senior priest, no — though I have stood beside him once at a wet place deep in the folds where the moss goes black and the air smells of iron and dreaming, and I have seen what it costs a man to keep his mouth shut about what he knows. I would be the herder.

I am out before the sun, and the goats are out before me, and that is the right order of things. The small bells they wear — the older ones, the does who have earned the privilege — make a sound like a far-off chapel in the thin air. The air *is* thin here. Twenty-eight hundred spans will do that to a man's head. The first days of the season I bleed from the nose when I bend over, and I have learned not to bend over much. I have learned to squat instead, and the goats have learned to wait for me, the older ones, because they are patient creatures and they have decided I am part of the morning.

My coat is goat leather, and it has been re-sewn so many times that the thread is more of it than the hide. My mother made the first version. My wife — the one who is not here, who is in the city with the cisterns — has re-sewn it twice since. I carry a long staff and a short knife and two water-skins, one of them full and one of them not, and that second one is the question I will spend my season answering.

---

The flats are not friendly country. I do not expect them to be. A man who goes out onto the basalt flats looking for friendly country has already misunderstood the arrangement, and the flats will correct his understanding for him, probably with a turned ankle and a day spent crawling back to where the footing is less treacherous. The footing is treacherous everywhere. The basalt is young stone, the masons will tell you so back in the city — they love this about it, because young stone splits clean along the conchoidal fracture and takes a carved edge — but a herder does not love it. A herder walks on it. The flat mice have riddled it with their cracks, and where there is a crack there is often a chip, and where there is a chip there is often a place where a man's weight will be welcomed by the stone for half a heartbeat and then will not. I have fallen. I have slid. I have come up with the palms of my hands flayed to the white layer that is not quite skin and not quite meat. I have learned. Not perfectly. Enough.

The grass is thin and pale and tastes, the goats tell me by the way they bend their heads to it, of iron and of patience. There is not much of it. A herder moves his string often — a day here, half a day there, a week on a stretch that has held the rain. You learn where the rain held. You learn where it didn't. You learn, after enough seasons, where the *wet places* are, though I am careful not to speak of them to strangers, and even with my own kind I speak of them in the way a man speaks of a woman he loves but does not name — by where she is, by what she gives, never by what she is.

I have stood on a piece of ground that looked like every other piece. There was a faint darkening of the lichen. There was a smell of iron on the thin air, though there was no iron I could see. I dug. I dug all afternoon. The stone gave up its secret slowly, the way the place gives up everything slowly. By sunset I had two skins of water, and a story I will not tell, and a conviction that I had been shown something by the ground itself, and that I owed the ground something in return, and that the owing was the reason I came out again the next year and the next and the next.

---

I have named perhaps a dozen of the goats. The rest are too many, and I have learned that naming a goat is the first step toward a grief I cannot afford on the flats. The ones I have named are the ones with character — there is the one that leads, an old doe with a torn ear and a pace that the others will fall into if she sets it. There is the one that is mean, a young male I should have cut last season and didn't, and who now butts me when I am not looking and who will, if I am honest, be cut this season when I bring him home. There is the one whose kid died in the late cold snap two years back — she stood over the small body for two days and would not be moved, and I sat with her for some of that time, because she would not leave and I could not leave her, and because I am a man who has learned to sit with things.

I milk them in the cool of the morning, when the milk is thick and the air is still and the bells are the only sound. I drink it thin when the day ahead is a long one, thick when it is short and I can afford the weight of it in my stomach. I eat the dried apples my wife packed — she always packs more than I think I will need, and she is always right, and I have stopped arguing about it. I eat the hard bread, which is hard enough to be a weapon and which softens if you hold it in your mouth long enough and which is best, I think, broken into the milk. I eat, when the time comes, the meat of a goat that has stopped being useful. I do not enjoy this. I do not refuse it. I do not pretend there is a clean line between the goat that leads and the goat that becomes the stew.

I am not unhappy. This is a thing I have been asked, by men from the city when I come in for the winter, and by the caravan drivers who stop and stare and cannot believe a man lives out here by choice. I am not unhappy the way a man is not unhappy who has been allowed to take his time. The city makes a different kind of Hevelkari — the mason, the quarryman, the water-seller, the woman with the cisterns. I respect them. I am not one of them. I have been to the city. I have walked the narrow streets that switch back on themselves without logic. I have smelled the bread. I have heard the well-wheels. I have looked up at the upper houses and seen the laundry drying on the small terraces in the moments of weak sun, and I have understood that the people up there have made a life that suits them, and that it would not suit me, and that this is not a criticism of them or of me but only the way the wind blows.

I sleep under the sky. This is the part the city men cannot understand, and I do not try to explain it to them. The sky here is enormous and very near. The clouds do not skim — they wheel. I have watched a storm take an hour to assemble itself over the flats to the east, the way a man might watch a flower open in a sunbeam, except the flower is the color of soot and smells of ozone. I have watched the white hawks wheel above me and cast their shadows on the stone. I have watched the dust come in late December, the iron-fine powder lifted by the thin wind, and I have wrapped the cloth across my mouth as my mother taught me and as my wife has taught my children, and I have bent my head and walked home with the goats, because a man does not argue with the dust.

---

In the evening I eat the stew, if I have made it — goat and root and dried apple, late, eaten alone, eaten with the bells still faintly audible on the animals that are settled for the night. The clatter of the bowl. The smell of it. I have been away for long seasons, and I have smelled this smell in my sleep and woken with it in my throat, and I have understood then that the city men who say they do not love the place are telling the truth, but that they are not telling the whole truth, and that the whole truth is the word they have that is stronger than love and that means *being held*, and that I have been held by the stone floor of the house where I was born and that I will be held by the stone floor of wherever I die, whether that is out here on the flats or back in the city on a winter's day when the dust has come and the coughing has taken me.

I do not think the god sleeps. I do not think the god does not sleep. I think the wet places are sacred because water is sacred in a country that has too little of it, and I think the herders who know them do not speak of them because a man does not usually speak about the thing that keeps him alive. I think the senior priest has walked to wet places I have never seen. I think he has felt what he says he has felt. I think this is not a question the city asks, and I think the city is right not to ask it, because the answer, whatever it is, would not change the price of white stone or the depth of the wells or the leaning of the walls, and the Council has enough to argue about.

I am a herder of Hevelkar. I am out before the sun. I am the kind of person the place makes when it is allowed to take its time, and the place is allowed, and so am I, and the goats crop the thin grass, and the wind blows, and the water in the second skin is the question of my life, and I have not yet run out of answers.
