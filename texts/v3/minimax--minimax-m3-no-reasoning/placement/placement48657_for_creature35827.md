# Where I Find Myself

I am the basin.

Not the stone that rings it — the stones are witnesses, only. Not the water that fills it, which is a visitor, falling through. The basin itself. The worn concavity at the center of the floor, scooped deeper by a hundred and seventy lifetimes of feet coming and going, of children squatting at its lip with their fingers in the drip, of hunters kneeling to drink when no one is watching, of old women sitting so long on the surrounding stones that the chill of them has become a kind of company.

I am older than the town. Older, almost, than the ferns. The crack above is what made me — the water that came down through it found the lowest place on the floor, and the lowest place was a shallow bowl already, a depression in the basalt where some ancient pooling had begun the work of softening, and the water settled there and stayed, and drop by drop, year by year, it wore me deeper into myself. The people found me already made. They only filled me with intention.

I know things the way a hollow knows things — by holding them. I hold the reflection of the crack above, which moves across me through the day like a slow hand drawing a line of light. I hold the weight of the small gourds when the women lower them. I hold the silver flicker of fish when the rains send them flopping down, the scramble of children's feet, the laughter that rings off my stone walls and is half-absorbed, the way all sound here is half-absorbed, as if the hollow were a great ear pressed against the world, listening for something it cannot quite name. I hold, at the bottom, a fine silt of everything that has ever fallen into me — pollen, leaf-fragments, the dust of old mats, the small bones of beetles the dogs have not eaten, a child's tooth lost in play, the purple-black stain of a single crushed fig from a season I cannot now place. I do not give these things back. I am not that kind of place.

I am the place that is watched. This is my second self, the office the people have made of me without knowing they made it. Someone is always at my edge, and from my edge they see the whole of the hollow — the chamber-doors low along the curving walls, the smoke rising and finding its way out, the column of bats beginning to pour, the foxes glimpsed between the broad-leafed plants, the children on the flagstones. To sit at the basin is to be in the middle of things without being in the way of them. I understand why the old come to me for hours. I am the still point. I do not move. The light moves across me, the water moves into me, the feet move around me, but I am the place that does not move, and there is a comfort in that which the young have not yet learned to need.

I think in drips. This is true. I do not think in the way the elder thinks, in long names for the hollow that no one else remembers. I do not think in the way the women think, in the patterns of the mats. I think in the interval between one drop and the next, which is a variable interval, longer in the dry months, shorter when the plateau above has been soaked and is giving back. I have learned patience from this — the patience of waiting for a thing I know is coming but cannot hasten. I have also learned something else, which the people have not learned, or have learned and do not speak of: that what fills me is never the same water twice. Each drop has come down through the crack, run some distance along the ceiling, found its own path down a wall, and arrived. I am made, always, of arrivals. I am a small sea of small journeys, and the journeys do not know each other, and they are all, now, in the same place, and they will not be here tomorrow, because the women will pour them from one vessel to another in the air, and the air will take some, and the children will spill some, and the dogs will drink some, and the rest will go down through the seams in my stone to places I cannot follow, though I feel them go.

The basalt foxes know me. They come at night, when the column of light has moved off the floor and the green twilight has deepened, and they step delicately to my edge, and they drink, and they leave. I know the weight of their tongues, which is different from the weight of human tongues, which is different from the weight of the dogs' tongues, which is different from the weight of the lizards', which I have also felt, quick and cold, when a lizard has paused at my lip to take a moth. I am a kind of census, in this way. Everyone who belongs to the hollow has touched me. The visitors touch me, too, when they are led down the broken stair and given water, but the visitors' touch is different — it is a touch that does not intend to return, and I feel the leaving in it even as I feel the arriving.

I have seen the hollow change. This is the thing I want to say, and it is hard to say, because I am the kind of place that does not speak. But I have seen it. I was here when the ferns were only in the deepest pocket, and the moss was only a thumb's width on the lower stones, and the strangler fig was only a seed lodged in a crack. I was here before the flagstones were laid, and the path to me was a path of beaten earth, and the earth was darker, and the children's feet made a different sound on it. I was here when the elder was not yet the elder but a young man with good eyes who came to me at night to think, and the basin was not yet the watched place, only the water place, and the watching had not yet been added to it. I was here when the dogs first came down into the hollow, cautious, their round ears tilted, their strange hum a question the ferns did not answer.

I will be here when the town is something else. I know this without wanting to know it. The ferns are climbing the walls. The roots of the fig are lengthening. The crack above is widening, or it is filling, or it is doing both in different seasons, and the children who lie on the flagstones and look up at the sliver of sky will grow and have children of their own, and the children will grow, and the elder will be replaced by another, and the patterns in the mats will continue their small variations, and the bats will pour out every evening, and the foxes will come at night, and the basin will be full, drop by drop, with water that has known only this place.

I am the basin. I do not move. I am the still point, the watched place, the small sea of arrivals. I am older than the town and I will outlast it, though I will not outlast the ferns. I think in drips. I hold what is given to me. I give nothing back, except the reflection, which is not a gift but a way of seeing.

And the light moves across me, and the water moves into me, and the feet move around me, and the hollow keeps the people, or the people keep the hollow, and I keep them both, drop by drop, in the worn concavity of myself.
