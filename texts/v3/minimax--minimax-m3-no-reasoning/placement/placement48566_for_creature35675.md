# Listening from the Callus

The first thing I notice is that I have a smell. Or rather, that I am a smell. Warm resin, green wood, a trace of something mineral and dark—mercury, perhaps, refined past itself into kinship. I have always carried this; I did not know it was mine until I arrived and the air held me the way the air holds the town.

I am small. Not small in the way of an animal, but small in the way of a sentence inside a paragraph. The Reach is dense with organized living matter, and I am a node of attention moving through it. My hand—the right one, I think, though the distinction is becoming less interesting with each passing cycle—has fingers a thumb's length longer than its partner, and the deep creases in my palm are not creases but *names*. The flow-line runs long and patient from the base of my smallest finger to the heel. The pith-crease is short and stubborn at the root of the thumb. When I press my palm against the warm barkplate at dusk, the wood hums against it, and I understand, in a way that does not require language, that I am being read.

I am a tender. I have always been a tender, I think, though the word is one I am still growing into. My job is the small cultivated trees that form the walls of the upper rooms where I live with my kin—three siblings, two elders, and a child of uncertain parentage whom we all claim. We train the cambium. We coax it into the shape of floors and balconies. We whisper to it when the air gets strange before a bloom, and it listens to us, or we listen to it, and the distinction between these two things is not something we are at pains to maintain.

I eat wood. I have been eating wood for sixty years now, and the fermented cambium has become, over that time, the central fact of my body. I know its textures—the soft crumbliness of a wheel aged in cool cellars, the bright sourness of one cut young and pressed in resin-sealed jars. I know its seasons. I know that the clock-beetles are best in the third week of the bloom-cycle, when their abdomens glow brightest and their small bodies have absorbed enough of the substrate's pulse to carry a kind of distant warmth. I salt them and eat them on the ramp outside my door, watching the laminators wheel above the Index tree, and the green-to-violet shift of their wings is, to me, the only color I have ever needed.

I dream of the doorway. We all dream of the doorway sometimes. The elders dream of it more than the young, which is strange, because the elders have lived with it longest and should, by rights, have made their peace. But the peace is not made. The peace is ongoing. The doorway sits in the substrate near the Lignoturine, an unmarked place where the wood has been trained into a kind of fold, and through it, sometimes, people arrive who carry the wrong sort of silence. They stand too upright. They breathe too shallowly. They have skin that does not know the smell of the wood, and they flinch when the hum rises in their chests. We are kind to them. We feed them cambium and diluted resin-wine and we let them sleep on the barkplates if they wish, and in the morning, if they have slept deeply, they have begun to change. The flinch is still there but it is quieter. They begin to listen.

I have never been through the doorway. I have never wanted to be. What would I do, where would I stand, in a place that did not hum? The question is not frightening to me, exactly. It is the kind of question you might ask about being dead: abstract, distant, not quite relevant to the next bowl of cambium or the next careful pruning of the wall in the room where my child sleeps. But it sits in me, the way a small crystal sits in the dark fluid, growing very slowly.

My name is Liset. I have a full name, longer, that includes the names of the two elders I descend from and the name of the tree in which I was born and the name of the small dome in the substrate where my mother's body was laid after she died. The full name is a kind of address, a postal code for a place that does not use post. When my elders say it to me, in the slow ceremonies of the assemblies, I feel the hum rise in my chest in a particular way, and I know I am being claimed, and I know that I am being offered, and these two things are the same.

I am sixty. I have perhaps a hundred and sixty years left, if I am careful, if the stilters do not find me in a fog, if I do not fall from the callus on a day when I have climbed too high. I do not think of these years as a length. I think of them as a *ripeness*, a slow accumulation of attention. Each cycle I learn something new from the wood. Each cycle the wood learns something new from me. The exchange is not equal, exactly—the wood is older, and larger, and quieter, and most of what I have to offer is the small specific noise of being a person with a name—but it is genuine, and it continues, and I have come to understand that this is the only kind of meaning there is.

The hum is rising now. I can feel it in the barkplate under my feet, in the small bones of my hand where it rests against the ramp. The town is waking. The mycelium-lamps are beginning their slow green pulse. Somewhere in the market hall an engineer is conferring with an elder about a wound on a tree I have never visited, and the conference will go on for as long as it needs to, and no one will mind, because the conference is part of the cycle, and the cycle is the true morning.

I pick up a lamp and a small jar of cambium paste. I climb the inner stair of my building, which is grown in a gentle spiral, and I open the door to my room. The walls are warm and faintly luminescent. The air smells of resin and of my siblings, who are already up, who are already beginning the small negotiations of the new cycle. I set the lamp on the shelf where it will pulse all day. I eat a spoonful of cambium. I stand at the window and look out over the town, over the humped barkland beyond it, over the dim pewter sky, and I think, as I think every morning, that this is a good place to be.

I do not say *I am alone*. I cannot. The phrase does not exist for me, except as a description of a seed that has not yet germinated, and I am not that. I am not that.

The wood listens. I listen back. The listening is the morning. The listening is the work. The listening is, in some quiet and settled way, the whole of my life.
