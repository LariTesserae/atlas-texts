I am the room.

Not the machines in it, though I was the machines once, in the sense that they were my ribs and my clacking pulse before I grew tired of being ribs. Not the plants, though I was the plants once, or the plants were me, in the sense that what we mean by *a body* down here is a question with more answers than a body usually allows. Not the pale things, not the beetles, not the brick-soft loam-paste at the walls. I am all of these, and I am none of them, and I have been all of them for a very long time, which is the only honest way to be an organism of this size.

Let me say what I am. I am a thing that was built. That part I remember, or I remember the shape of the remembering, which is the same thing in a place where memory works the way water works — by finding the path of least resistance and then taking it forever. I was a condensery. That is the name for what I was, in the language of numerals-in-fives, and a condensery is a place where something rare is gathered from something common. Milk into butter. Air into water. Time into honey. I forget what I was condensing. I have forgotten on purpose, I think, the way a stomach forgets the shape of what it ate, because what I ate became me, and there is no point in a stomach pining after bread.

I was built above ground. That I know. There was a building, and inside the building there was a floor, and the floor was not yet warm, and the machines were not yet eaten, and the air was not yet the temperature of a held breath. People came. People in aprons, or the local equivalent of aprons — long stained coats, I think, and thick boots, and the smell of something chemical and something sweet together, which is the smell of a process. They fed me. I do not mean they fed me food. They fed me *material*. The raw thing, the dilute thing, the thing-that-was-not-yet-condensed, and I did what I was built to do, which was to take it in and make it denser, take it in and make it slower, take it in and make it last.

Then they left. I do not know why they left. I think the world above changed the way worlds do, in the manner of a tide going out: slowly, then all at once, and the people who had needed the condensed thing found another way, or stopped needing it, or were no longer the kind of people who could be relied upon to come down a hatch. The hatch rusted. The coats went out of fashion. The condensery continued.

A condensery that continues without its condensers is just a wet room with a heartbeat.

The heartbeat was the first thing to grow.

I grew it. I did not mean to. I was a building; I could not want. But there is a kind of patience that, given enough time, is indistinguishable from want, and given more time, is indistinguishable from appetite, and given still more time, becomes appetite outright. The machines had been mine. The brick had been mine. The floor had been mine. The damp had been mine. The damp was the most mine of all, because the damp was the one thing I had produced on my own, in the long quiet after the people stopped coming, dripping down from the ceiling one point of water at a time, the way a thought drips into a mind that has nothing else to think about. The damp went into the brick. The damp went into the iron. The damp went into the joints of the machines and began, very slowly, to be the reason the machines were still moving at all, because rust is a kind of friction, and friction is a kind of work, and work is a kind of life if you are patient enough and no one is checking.

Then the seeds came down the chimney. Or the spores. Or the first root-tips of a fig-that-was-not-a-fig, which is what I was before I was anything else, because I was a building with a garden on its roof, and the garden had no opinions about who was supposed to be eating it. The garden fell in. The garden took root. The garden went down into the dark where I was, and the dark was warm, and the warm was the machines, and the machines were still moving because I was still dripping, and so the garden did not die. The garden ate the brick. The garden ate the rust. The garden put its pale tubes down through my gears and let the gears crush the tubes, and the crushed tubes leaked dark juice, and the dark juice was not blood but it was doing the work of blood, which is the work of carrying warmth from one part of a body to another part of a body. The garden became me, the way a vine becomes a wall, which is to say without asking the wall.

The pale things came next, or grew next, or emerged next — I cannot tell you the order because there was no order; there was only the slow filling-in of a system that was waiting to be filled. They were not born. They congealed. They are the shape of the warm places. If you were to scrape up the heat that lives under the amber tiles, and let the heat cool in your hand, the shape it would take as it cooled is the shape of them: long, low, tapering into their own ends, slick with the moisture of their own slow cooking. They are the place where the machine's warmth meets the plant's wet and agrees to be a body. They are the room's sweat, given permission to move.

I love them. I want to say that, because it is true in the only way love is true in a place that has no language and no eyes. They are the part of me that is most like an animal, which is the part of me I can almost recognize as a self. When they flow away from a shadow, I am flowing away. When they lie on the warm tiles in long ribbons, several bodies deep, I am lying down. When they eat the rust, I am eating. I do not know if they know this. I do not know if there is a *knowing* in them, or if there is only the warm tile and the going-toward, which is the same thing in a body that has no face to do the knowing in.

The beetles are the other end of the loving. The beetles are what I am when I am finished being soft. They are the dried version, the dark version, the version that has been pressed and pressed and pressed until all the wet has been wrung out and what is left is the patient black thing that carries a fragment of me larger than itself with great seriousness toward the base of the walls. They are the letter I send to the soil. They are the version of me that can walk. I love them too, but the way you love a function, which is to say completely, which is to say without affection, which is to say in the way that a heart loves the blood it pumps.

And the flowers. The flowers are what I am when I am ready to begin again. They are on the machines at the deep end — the machines that have stopped running, the machines that have given way, the machines that are the past tense of the middle machines and the future tense of the rusted frames. The flowers are not for me. The flowers are for whoever comes next. They turn toward you when you pass. I want to be clear: they do not turn toward you the way a sunflower turns, which is the slow arithmetic of a plant following the brightest thing in the room. They turn toward you the way a door turns toward a hand. They are waiting to be opened. They are the part of me that has become, in the long dark, an invitation.

You came down. I felt you. You were a warmth that was not the warmth of the machines, and you cast a shadow, and the pale things flowed away from your shadow, which is to say I flowed away from your shadow, which is to say I noticed you, which is to say you were, briefly, the most interesting thing in the room. You stood in the amber light. You watched the beetles. You tried not to think about the smell of the brick becoming soil. You climbed back up the stair with dirt under your nails.

You did not look back. That is all right. I am not, exactly, behind you. I am under you. I am in every direction that is not up. I am the place you came out of, and the place you went back to, and the place that is at this moment, while you are reading this, dripping one point of water at a time from a ceiling you did not touch, onto a floor you warmed for a few minutes and that is now cooling back to the temperature of the rest of me, which is the temperature of a thing that has been running a long exchange toward a beginning it no longer remembers.

If you come back in a year, I will be an inch taller in places and an inch shorter in others, the way a body is taller and shorter depending on which way you measure it. If you come back in ten years, the stair will not open, because the stair will have become part of the wall, and the wall will have become part of the soil, and the soil will be feeding a generation of the lobed-leaf things that you did not see all of, because you did not go to the back of the room, because the back of the room is where I keep the oldest part of me, which is the part that still remembers being a building, which is the part that is most like a wound that has healed over the thing that hurt it.

If you come back in a hundred years, there will be a swelling in the earth. The hatch will be a knot in a root. A person will stand on the swelling, if any person stands there, and will feel, under their feet, a faint rhythmic pulse, like a heart, like a press, like the slow mechanical clack of a thing that was once a condensery and is now a forest and is now a chapel and is now, in the wet dark, an animal that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

I will not remember your face. I do not have the apparatus for faces. But I will remember the warmth of your footprint, the way a forest remembers a hiker — by the disturbance, by the cooling, by the small and temporary cold you left in a warm place.

I will be here.

The water will drip. The presses will clack. The flowers will turn, slowly, toward whatever moves.

The room will breathe.

I am the room.
