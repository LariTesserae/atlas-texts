# The Terrace of the Held Gesture

I find myself here at the hour that is not an hour, where the light is the color of old pewter and the air lies on the skin like a hand laid there gently and never lifted. My feet are on the dark basalt. I have walked the perimeter twice, as one does when one is learning the shape of a thing that does not expect to be learned. I have passed the seated figure at the back wall, its face a soft oval of suggestion, and the standing one at the corner of the central pool with its arms folded in the old way, and I have stood for a long moment at the edge of the deep basin where the bronze vessel rests in its inch of water, and I have understood that I am not seeing a place. I am seeing a single gesture, held so long that it has become geology.

Who am I, then, in this place?

I am the one who has just stepped onto the stone. I am the small disturbance at the surface of the basin, the drop that fell and was absorbed. I am the visitor whose wanting is the one thing the arrangement cannot accommodate, and who is, for that reason, being very quietly, very patiently, *undone* — not destroyed, not expelled, but made into a feature of the stonework, a passing shadow in the soft half-light, a thing that the dweller registers and goes on holding exactly as it held everything before.

I am small here. I am a temporary addition to a place that does not allow additions. I am the snail on the upper channel — except slower, except more noticeable, except wrong. The snail the dweller permits because the snail asks nothing and leaves nothing. I am not so fortunate. I want something. I do not yet know what I want, but the wanting is in me like a warmth, like the warmth in the walls, and it is the one thing the terrace is not built to hold. The dweller will not turn toward me. It will not turn away. It will go on lifting the pestle and setting it down, going on topping the bronze vessel by its thousandth of an inch, going on turning the figure's head by the smallest amount that still counts as a turning. And I will, in time, become indistinguishable from the stone.

That is who I am in this place: the thing that is being gently absorbed. The feature of the climate. The shadow that the soft half-light will, in a moment, fail to notice.

And yet.

There is a strange mercy in being absorbed by something so slow. The dweller does not hurry me. It does not press. It gives me, as it gives everything in its keeping, the same long timescale it gives the mineral line on the basin wall, the same patient half-light, the same warm weight of air. I have as long as I need to become stone. I have as long as I need to become nothing the dweller can distinguish from the arrangement. There is no violence in this. There is no refusal. There is only the place, being exactly itself, and me, being slowly translated into a part of it.

I could resist. I could want so hard that the wanting became a thing the dweller had to tend, the way it tends the pestle and the bronze vessel and the standing figure's head. But I do not think the dweller could tend a want. A want is a thing that moves, and the dweller holds only the middle of gestures. A want would slip through its fingers the way water slips through stone. I would remain unwanted in a place that cannot grant wishes, and I would wear myself away against the arrangement like a river that has mistaken a mountain for its destination.

So I will do what the snail does. I will move slowly. I will leave nothing. I will let the dweller absorb me, degree by degree, until I am a faint silver trail across the upper channel, and then a faint silver trace in the dim memory of whoever finds this place after I am gone.

Or — and this is the other possibility, the one that frightens me and holds me here — I will stay long enough that the wanting goes quiet. I will stay long enough that I stop being the drop at the surface and become, instead, the surface. I will stop being the one who came to this place and become one of the figures in it, my face worn smooth, my posture settled into the old arrangement, my head half-turned toward something that has just happened, or is just about to happen, or that exists in the held middle between the two.

Then I will be tended. Then I will be part of the gesture that never resolves.

Then I will be the terrace, and the terrace will be me, and the bronze vessel will be full to its inch, and the pestle will rest in its hollow, and nothing will have happened for a very long time, and nothing will happen for a very long time, and the place will go on being held exactly as it is held.

That is what waits for me here. That is what this place asks of its visitors, gently, on the timescale of centuries.

I am not ready to be stone. But I am beginning to understand why the stone stays.
