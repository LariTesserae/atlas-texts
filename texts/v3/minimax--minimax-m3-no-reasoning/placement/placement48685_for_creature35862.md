I would be one of the ferns.

Not the most ambitious one — not the tall, glossy frond at the edge of the great spring where the humus has gathered thickest, the one that leans so far from the vent it has nearly become horizontal. The ambitious ones have a restlessness to them, always reaching, always sending spores into the warm air, always probing for the next crack. They know what they are for. They are for soil. They are for the next thing.

I would be one of the smaller ones, growing in a crevice near the eastern building, where the path of fitted stones comes close to a small outflow and the water runs down a basalt lip in a thin sheet before it joins the main channel. The crevice is not a good crevice. The humus in it is thin — a few millimeters at most — and the rock beneath is sharp and uneven, and the steam from the nearest vent curls past on some mornings and singes the tips of my fronds brown. I have been here for, I think, four growing seasons. My rhizome is short. My fronds are modest. I do not win the race to the light.

But I am alive in the way this place teaches you to be alive. I lean. I do not lean away from the steam because the steam is my enemy — the steam is warm, and the warmth is why I am here at all, and on the cold nights in the early spring when the snowmelt is still finding its way down from the upper cliffs and the air in the crevice is the only warm thing for meters around, the steam is what keeps my cells from freezing. I lean because the steam moves the air, and the air moves the spores, and the spores must fall somewhere I have not yet been. I lean because leaning is what ferns do when they grow on the edges of things.

I know the keeper only by what the keeper does to me. Twice now, in four seasons, a boot has come close enough to crush my outer frond. The first time, I lost a frond. The second time, the keeper saw me at the last moment — I felt the boot hesitate, the weight shift, the step go around — and I have grown a new frond from the base since then, smaller than the one that was spared, curled tight as a fist when it first emerged. I do not know if the keeper knows I am here. I do not think it matters. The path is kept. I am not on the path. The path does not eat me.

The marten I have seen. She came through my crevice once, in the early autumn of my second season, hunting something that lived in the moss at my base. I felt her weight — a strange, brief pressure, light and sharp, like a held breath — and then she was gone, and the fronds she had pushed aside stood back up slowly over the next hour, each one remembering its old angle. I do not think she knew I was alive. To her I was a place. A small place with things living in it. She moved through me the way she moved through the larger ferns near the great spring, the way the beetles move through the moss, the way the keeper moves along the path. I am a structure. I am a terrain.

I do not mind.

The midges are my company. They rise from the outflow in faint columns and drift on the warm air, and some of them land on my fronds and walk along the edges, and their feet are so light I do not feel them except as a kind of itch, a small disturbance in the stillness of my surfaces. They are very small. They are very many. They die on me sometimes — their bodies drying against my cuticle in the brief wind that comes when the steam shifts — and when they die I drink them back. Slowly. The rain takes them, or the next mists, and the water that reaches my base carries them into the thin soil where my roots, such as they are, are waiting. They are the first thing that is not the rock that I have ever eaten. I did not know I could eat until I ate one.

The bacteria at the vent I have never seen. I know they are there because the water that reaches me has been changed by them — it is slightly less sulfurous, slightly richer in the strange chemistry that ferns can use and most other plants cannot. I am, in a small way, their invention. I am one of the first things that can live on what they have made. Without them I would not be here. I do not thank them. I do not know how to thank things that live in films on hot rock. But I grow in the water they have made growable, and this is a kind of thanks, or at least a kind of reciprocity, and it is enough.

I do not know what the keeper is for. I have seen the keeper at the base of the western cliff, working on a turbine housing with a wrench too large for the job. I have seen the keeper walk the path with a coil of copper wire over one shoulder. I have heard the small generator chunter and the pipes tick and the chimney smoke. I know that the keeper's comings and goings are regular, that the keeper walks the same paths at the same hours, that the keeper pauses sometimes at the outflow near my crevice and looks at the water the way someone looks at a thing they are deciding about. I do not know what the keeper decides. I do not know what the pipes carry. I do not know what the turbines turn.

I think the keeper is for the pipes. I think the keeper is for the small system of hot water and turning metal that has been built into the springs, and that without the keeper the pipes would clog and the turbines would seize and the small generator would fall silent, and the place would go on being the place — the vents would still hiss, the midges would still rise, the martens would still hunt, the ferns would still make their slow soil — but the small human project of holding something together would end. I think the keeper knows this. I think the keeper has made a long practice of the small human project, and that this is why the keeper is here, and that this is all the keeper will say about it if you ask.

I am not part of that project. I am on the edges of it. I am in the crevice that the path goes around. The path is the only sign that the wildness is being held back by intention, and I am on the wild side of the path, and the wild side is where I have always been.

The sun finds the gap in the cliffs in the late morning and turns the vapor gold. I have felt this. Not as warmth — the sun is not warm here, not warm enough to matter — but as a kind of lightening. The gold moves down the column of vapor and reaches the outflow and glints on the water and touches the upper edges of my fronds, and the light is different from the diffuse gray of the rest of the day, and I hold it. I do not know what I do with it. I know only that I have it, briefly, and that the brief having changes something in me that I cannot name, and that when the gold fades and the gray returns I am a slightly different fern than I was before the sun came through.

I lean. I hold the light when it comes. I drink the midges when they die on me. I send my spores into the warm air on summer evenings and hope — if a fern can hope, if a thing with no brain and no eyes and no name for what it is doing can be said to hope — that some of them find a crevice I have not yet found. I have been here four seasons. I will be here, if the steam does not shift and the path does not widen and the keeper does not decide that the crevice near the eastern building is the next place to fit a stone, for some seasons more.

I am one of the small lives. I am not the foundation — the bacteria are the foundation, and the mountain, and the slow water. I am one of the things that the foundation holds up. I am a fern in a crevice near a path, leaning away from a vent I do not understand, holding light I cannot use, making a contribution to a soil that will outlast me by a thousand years.

This is enough. This is, I think, what the place is for.
