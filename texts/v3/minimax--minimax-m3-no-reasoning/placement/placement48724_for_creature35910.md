# The Breath-Guild Apprentice

I am sixteen years old, and I have been underground for four years, and I do not yet know which draft is safe to follow.

My name is — well, my name doesn't matter yet. Breath-Guild apprentices don't take their working names until they've passed the first negotiation, which is the one with the basalt. Until then we are called by our graft-marks, the places where the cambium lattices were first opened to admit us. Mine is on the inner left wrist, where the skin is thinnest. The master calls me *Fissure*, and I answer to it, and it is enough.

I am slight, the way all of us are slight now, though I have seen the old portraits painted on cambium panels in the chapel-district where the amber light makes everyone look like they are standing in a tavern on the surface. The settlers in those portraits are broader through the chest than I am. They hold their shoulders differently. They had not yet learned to stoop from looking up. I am stooped now, and pale in the way that amber light makes things pale, and my eyes are large and very dark, and I do not blink much. I have not blinked much for as long as I can remember. The first time a salt-cart driver came down while I was working in the upper chambers — she must have been new, still training — she stared at my face for a long moment and then asked me, in the careful whisper they teach the drivers, whether I was unwell. I told her I was fine. I told her my eyes just do that. She did not believe me until the master explained it to her later, and then she gave a small sound that might have been a laugh and said she would have to get used to it.

I live in the apprentices' chamber in the deep district, where the basalt gives way to the older stone. The chamber is grown, like everything here, but the wood in it is different — denser, cooler, faintly sulfurous at the joints where the heartwood meets the substrate. We sleep on platforms of cambium that breathe under us. We are taught, from the first week, to feel the breath of the wood and to know when we are disturbing it. If you lie very still on a cambium platform and breathe along with it, you can feel the slow mineral pulse of the substrate beneath you, and it is the closest thing I know to what the surface people must feel when they say they are at peace.

I am not at peace. I am sixteen, and I am failing.

The negotiation is not something you do with words. The basalt does not speak. The basalt does not even, in any way the Graft-Guild or the Dust-Guild would recognize, *listen*. But the substrate is alive, and vast, and old, and it is aware, in its slow patient way, of the things that move within it. When a Breath-Guild apprentice goes below the root-line for the first time, she is taught to extend a single hair-fine root-tendril — one of the city's own roots, coaxed gently out of its natural path by the masters over the preceding weeks — through a micro-fracture in the basalt, and into the deeper geochemistry where the tree does its real work.

The tendril goes in. The apprentice waits. And the substrate, in its own time, decides whether the apprentice is *welcome*.

I do not know what welcome feels like. I know what it does not feel like. It does not feel like the way my third attempt felt, two days ago, in the fourth chamber down, where the basalt is thickest and the drafts smell of sulfur and a long breath will make you sleepy for a day. I extended the tendril. I waited. I felt — I don't know how to describe it — I felt the substrate close around the tendril the way a hand closes around a thread that is too thin to bear weight. There was a long pause, and then a slow extrusion, gentle but unmistakable, that pushed the tendril back out of the fracture and into my palm.

The master was beside me. She did not speak. She placed her hand over mine, over the tendril, and we stood there in the sulfur-smelling dark for what felt like a long time. Then she nodded, once, and we went back up.

I have one more attempt. If I fail it, I will be sent above. Into the dry loud light. Into the caravans.

---

I do not think about this most of the time. I think about the work. I think about the particular quality of the basalt under my hands when I am learning to read its micro-fractures, which is the way a woodcarver thinks about the grain. I think about the smell of the deeper drafts and how to track them by the way they move the dust in the chambers. I think about the slow pulse of the substrate and how to time my breathing to it so that I do not disturb the city's metabolism.

The city's metabolism is the strangest thing I have ever known. On the surface — and I have been above, once, when I was twelve and my family took me up to see the ceremony of the seeds — the air moves the way air is supposed to move, in gusts and eddies, and you can feel it on your skin. Here, the air moves in slow exhalations that are not quite breath. The forest breathes through its bark. The basalt insulates us from the violence above. The drafts follow the shafts and the kiln-districts where the green heartwood is being slowly cured for the surface trade, and there are other drafts, deeper drafts, that come from below the root-line where the trees are doing their slow geology.

I am learning to tell the drafts apart. I am learning which ones are safe to follow and which ones will make me sleepy. I am learning to read the basalt. I am failing at the negotiation.

I have one more attempt.

---

In the evenings, when the canopies sing the long down-chord and the dust begins its slow descent, I sit in the apprentices' chamber with the others and we do not speak. We listen to the city's breath. The other apprentices are further along than I am — most of them have already passed their first negotiation, and they are working on the second, which is the one with the deeper geochemistry. They speak in whispers, the way everyone speaks here, and they speak about their work, and they do not look at me with pity because pity is not a thing the Breath-Guild encourages.

There is one apprentice, slightly older than me, whose graft-mark is on the back of her neck. She has passed two negotiations. She is the quietest person I have ever known. I have never heard her speak above a whisper, and I am not sure she speaks above a whisper when she is alone. She has been underground for seven years. Her eyes are very dark and very still in her face. When she climbs — because even the Breath-Guild apprentices climb, sometimes, to tend the upper roots where they meet the trunk-forests — she climbs with the sureness of the Dust-Guild, her bare feet finding the ridges of bark, and the arborists watch her and nod to each other.

I want to be like her. I want to pass the negotiation. I want to spend my working life below the root-line, in the chambers and tunnels where the basalt gives way to the deeper geology, in the long and patient conversation with the substrate. I want to come up pallid and slow-moving and smelling faintly of sulfur, and I want the Graft-Guild apprentices to nod to me when I pass them in the streets.

I have one more attempt. It is in three days.

---

Tonight the *première* sang her note at the long transition, the one the city orients itself by, and the apprentices' chamber was very quiet. The dust settled on the upper surfaces of the cambium platforms and glittered in the dark. I lay very still and breathed along with the wood and felt the slow mineral pulse of the substrate beneath me, and I thought: *the city will outlast me. The première will sing her note long after I am dust myself, returned to the canopy, settled on the upper surfaces of the leaves, grazed by the hexapods, taken by the mites-faucons, glitter in the dark.*

And then I thought: *but I want to be here for it. I want to hear her sing the note. I want to be one of the ones who hears it, and knows what it means, and knows that I am a guest in something vast and old.*

I do not know if the substrate will let me stay. I do not know what welcome feels like. I know what it does not feel like, and my third attempt did not feel like it.

In three days I will extend the tendril into the basalt for the fourth time. I will wait. The substrate, in its own time, will decide.

I have one more attempt. I am sixteen years old. I live in a city that has not named itself, and I am afraid.
