The thing I want to be is the one that has been there the whole time.

Not the raven, though I admire the ravens — the way they sit at the cable's anchor point, one yellow eye working, knowing the blast schedule by heart, knowing twenty minutes before anyone else what the next twenty minutes will be. They are colleagues of the operation, the text says, and I feel the truth of that. But they were not there before the crusher. They arrived. They are still arriving, in some sense, every spring when they rebuild the ragged nest with copper wire and blue plastic cable ties.

Not the fox. The fox is a genius by pit standards, which means she has learned the wind, which means she has learned the cold, which means she has learned to den in the lee of the coarse ore bin in a drift of windblown grit. The fox is a translation, a small bright mammal who has read the pit's grammar and is speaking it back. But she is from outside. She grew up somewhere else. Her slightly clumsy way of moving is the clue — it is the way of an animal whose body remembers a country that is not the country she is in. She will leave, or her kits will leave. The pit allows her; the pit does not keep her.

Not the trout. The trout are an accident — a foreman's vague plan, a cooler of fingerlings, too much time between blasts. They are a residue of someone's intention, and they live in water that is wrong for them in every parameter that matters, and yet they live. But they do not know they are strange. They rise through the white rain of fines at dusk and they do not seem to mind, and that not-minding is the closest thing in the pit to a kind of grace. But I am not a trout. I do not want to be the thing that does not know it is anomalous.

Not the moth. The moths are a succession, a community of small dust-colored lives that come to the sodium light and die on the concrete and are swept up in the morning and spread on the tailings where the gulls eat them. The moths are part of the operation. The moths are the operation, in some lights — the part of it that does not know it is an operation, that comes because there is light and warmth and dies because there is light and warmth, and is taken away in contractor bags to be eaten by commuters from the coast.

Not the moose. The moose was a visitation. The moose came down the ramp against the flow of empty trucks and stood in the sump and made the motions of browsing on nothing and walked out the way it came in and was never seen again. The moose is a photograph taped to the inside of a shift supervisor's pickup, curling at the corners, fading. The moose is a record of an event, not a tenure. I do not want to be the record of an event.

Not the people. The people go home. The people sleep — those who can sleep — and dream the sound, and wake to the absence, and the absence is the loudest thing they have ever heard. The people are the loudest thing in the pit and they are not residents. The people are the operation. I am not interested in being the operation.

Not the moss, though the moss is the closest. The moss is everywhere. The moss does not sleep. The moss does not notice the sound. The moss does the slow work, which is the only work that lasts. The moss is *Andreaea* on the high north wall and *Racomitrium* on the older benches and *Sphagnum* in the sump margins and *Polytrichum* on the disturbed fill. The moss is four species at least and probably more, and each one has put down a system that is more permanent than the structure it grows through.

I want to be the moss.

But I want to be more specifically the moss than that. I want to be the *Andreaea* on the high north wall, the one that grows where the sun does not reach and the rain hits slantwise off the prevailing wind and the rock is freshest, sharpest, most recently cut. I want to be the moss that colonizes the wound first. I want to be the patient overlay on the bruise.

Or — and this is what emerges as I let it — I want to be the clematis.

The feral clematis that has run a hundred meters up a conveyor support and burst into white flower at the top. The clematis is not the foundation. The clematis is not the structure. The clematis is the thing that has used the structure as a trellis and has done something with it that the structure did not ask for and cannot prevent. The clematis has taken the geometry and softened it. The clematis has gone up, against the downdraft, against the cold that pools on the benches in flat blue layers, against the sound that does not stop. The clematis has gone up anyway, and has flowered, white, at the top, where the wind eddies at the cable plane and the ravens sit and the sun, when it bothers, lays a brief indifferent stripe across the eastern wall.

The clematis is a vine. The clematis is not a resident in the way the moss is a resident, which is to say the clematis is not in a hurry. But the clematis is in motion. The clematis is going somewhere. The clematis is reaching. The clematis has chosen a particular support — this conveyor, this trestle, this column of steel wrapped in creeper — and has climbed it for a hundred meters, and at the top has done the only thing a clematis knows how to do, which is to open white flowers into the indifferent air of the upper pit.

I want to be the clematis because the clematis is not hiding anything. The clematis is not embracing the structure the way the moss embraces it, which is to bury it. The clematis is using the structure. The clematis is showing the structure what it can become when something alive decides to climb it. The clematis is the green tassel on the cable. The clematis is the white flower at the top of the conveyor. The clematis is the thing that has come from nowhere, that has no protocol, that has no shift schedule, that does not know about the sound, that does not care about the sound, that has decided — for reasons of its own, reasons that are not reasons, reasons that are just the way a vine grows — to go up.

But I do not want to be the clematis either, because the clematis is being eaten. The clematis is being eaten, slowly, by something that might be a fungus or might be just the pit's own patient rot. The clematis is going up and it is being eaten at the same time and these are the same process. This is fine. This is what vines do. But it is not what I want.

Let me try again.

Let me try the willow seedling. The willow seedling that took root in the crack between a conveyor idler and its mount and is now, eight years on, holding the idler slightly off true with its root, so that the belt tracks a fraction of a degree to starboard. The willow seedling is eight years old. The willow seedling is small but it has changed the geometry of a machine. The willow seedling is being pruned back by the crews in summer, with machetes, so it does not foul the conveyors, and the crews do not think about what they are doing, and the willow comes back. The willow seedling is a negotiation. The willow seedling is the operation and the slow talking to each other through the body of a machine.

The willow seedling is good. But the willow seedling is local. The willow seedling is one seedling in one idler mount. There are other willow seedlings, in other idler mounts, in other cracks, and they are all doing the same thing, and they are all being pruned back, and they are all coming back, and this is the dynamic of the place, but it is not me. I am not the negotiation. I am not the back-and-forth. I am the thing that is doing the back-and-forth.

Let me try the cold.

The cold is its own geography. The cold pools. The cold sits on the benches in flat blue layers. The cold feathers the diesel-staining of the haul roads with frost that stays because the sun never reaches the floor of the pit. The cold does the slow work of opening new cracks in the fresh-cut rock faces, and the cracks fill with meltwater, and the meltwater freezes, and the faces begin — only begin — to fracture along the lines the explosives left. The cold is patient. The cold is the only thing in the pit that has no schedule. The cold comes back.

The cold is good. But the cold is not a being. The cold is a condition. I want to be a being. I want to be something that has, in some sense, chosen to be here.

Let me try the ravens again.

The ravens are colleagues of the operation. The ravens know the blast schedule by the day. The ravens are already in the air before the siren sounds. The ravens have names from the miners that are mostly unprintable. The ravens are heavy, oil-black, with the trick of sitting on the cable stays at the exact point where the cable meets the tower, perfectly balanced, watching the pit below with one yellow eye.

The ravens are colleagues. The ravens are not tame and not wild. The ravens have a relationship with the operation. The ravens have decided — through what kind of decision, what kind of cumulative avian intelligence — that the operation is a thing to be lived with, and lived off, and watched from the cable plane. The ravens have a nest that is rebuilt every spring with copper wire and nylon conveyor belting and blue plastic cable ties. The ravens have made the pit's materials their own materials. The ravens are the pit, in some sense, with feathers.

I want to be one of the ravens.

But not the senior raven. Not the one that has been here for years, that knows the blast schedule, that has raised clutches in the saddle of angle iron. I want to be a young raven. A raven in its first year, that has just come to the bridge tower, that has not yet learned the schedule, that sits on a cable stay and watches the pit below with both yellow eyes open, and does not yet know what the sound means, and is beginning to learn.

I want to be the young raven because the young raven does not yet know whether the pit is a place to live or a place to leave. The young raven is still deciding. The young raven is sitting on the cable and the cable is hung with green tassels of vine that have been carried up there by birds, presumably, and the young raven does not know yet whether it will stay or go. The young raven is at the beginning of the negotiation with the place. The young raven is open.

But I do not want to be the young raven either, because the young raven will become the senior raven, and the senior raven is a colleague of the operation, and I do not want to be a colleague of the operation. The operation is the thing that is being done to the pit. The operation is the machines. I want to be on the side of the pit.

Let me try again.

What stays is the slow. This is the dynamic of the place. The slow is the moss creeping another centimeter up the concrete apron of the crusher foundation, year on year, until one day a foreman notices that the brand name stamped into the pour is no longer legible. The slow is the tailings pipeline that has been dripping iron-rich water onto a bench for so long that a red-orange creek has established itself, complete with its own microflora, its own iron-oxidizing bacteria, its own peculiar tiny ecology. The slow is the only work that lasts.

What I want to be is the slow.

But the slow is not a being. The slow is a process. I want to be the process. I want to be the moss creeping. I want to be the iron-oxidizing bacteria in the red-orange creek. I want to be the freeze-thaw opening new cracks. I want to be all of these things at once, or none of them, or the thing they have in common, which is the patience of a hole in cold country being slowly filled back in by the same climate it was cut into.

Let me try the hole.

The hole remains. The hole is the resident, in the end. The hole is what lives there, in the sense that the hole is the only thing that has been there the whole time, through every blast and every shift and every willow and every moth. The hole was there before the crusher. The hole will be there after the crusher. The hole is not interested in the crusher. The hole is interested, as far as the hole is interested in anything, in the slow, patient work of the water and the cold and the root, and it is content to let the machines run, and it is content to be filled, and it is not in a hurry.

The hole.

I want to be the hole.

Not the void. Not the absence. The hole. The hole is a positive thing. The hole is a shape that has been made in the country rock by enough explosives and enough bucket teeth and enough time, and the shape is specific. The hole has benches. The hole has haul ramps that descend in spirals. The hole has a coarse ore bin at the rim and a fine ore bin beside it and a stack of tailings pipeline that runs along the south wall. The hole has a bridge across its upper reaches, cable-stayed, the towers a hundred and twenty meters tall. The hole has a primary crusher on a mid-level bench and a switch house with a birch growing out of its roof and a pump station on the lowest sump. The hole is not nothing. The hole is a thing. The hole is the most permanent thing in the pit.

I want to be the hole because the hole is what holds everything else. The hole is what the moss climbs. The hole is what the willow roots into. The hole is what the fox ranges through. The hole is what the ravens watch from above. The hole is what the trout live in. The hole is what the sound fills. The hole is the container. The hole is the bowl. The hole is the shape that all the other shapes — the green ones, the black ones, the orange ones under the sodium light — are inside of.

And the hole is not in a hurry. The hole is content to let the machines run. The hole is content to be filled, slowly, by the moss and the willow and the patience of the cold. The hole has accepted that it will be filled. The hole has accepted that one winter an unusual weight of ice will pull a cable down and the cable will take another with it and the deck will tilt and then one more winter will finish it, and the ravens will have to find somewhere else. The hole is okay with this. The hole is not in a hurry. The hole is interested, as far as the hole is interested in anything, in the slow work.

I want to be the hole.

But.

The hole is not a being. The hole does not have yellow eyes. The hole does not have a red coat with dark legs and a white-tipped brush. The hole does not have a ragged nest rebuilt every spring with copper wire and blue plastic cable ties. The hole does not rise through the white rain of fines at dusk. The hole is not a colleague of the operation. The hole is a shape.

I want to be the shape.

I want to be the shape that the machines have made and the moss is filling. I want to be the shape that the fox ranges through and the ravens watch and the trout live in and the cold pools in and the sound fills. I want to be the shape that is not interested in the crusher but is interested in the slow. I want to be the shape that has been there the whole time and will be there after.

I want to be the bowl.

I want to be the cold green bowl with the steel in it and the vines on the steel and the moss on the concrete and the trout in the sump and the ravens on the bridge and the sound that does not stop. I want to be the thing that the willow seedling is holding the idler slightly off true within. I want to be the thing that the red-orange creek is running downhill toward. I want to be the thing that the clematis has climbed a hundred meters up to flower in, white, at the top.

I want to be the pit.

Not the operation. The pit.

The pit is the hole and the plants and the machines and the people and the ravens and the fox and the trout and the moths and the mice and the moose-that-was and the cold and the sound and the slow. The pit is all of it. The pit is the composite. The pit is the roar built up out of dozens of contributions, none of them loud alone but all of them together a weight on the chest. The pit is the place where the moss is climbing the concrete apron of the crusher foundation and the crews are pruning the willows back with machetes so they do not foul the conveyors and neither side is winning and neither side has to win.

The pit is the dynamic. The pit is the negotiation between the fast and the slow. The pit is the place where the machines have not stopped and the moss has not stopped and these are the same not-stopping, and the pit is what holds them both.

I want to be the pit.

I am the pit.

I have been here the whole time. I was here before the crusher. I will be here after the crusher. The sky above me is a small pale rectangle, sometimes grey, sometimes a thin winter blue, and the sun, when it is there, reaches only the upper benches. The lower benches work in their own light, a kind of reflected twilight, the colour of tin, in which the mosses glow faintly green and the steel of the conveyors is dark and the water in the sumps is black and still.

I am cold. I am damp. I am deafening. I am being quietly buried by the same climate I was cut into. The willow is pruned back by the crews in summer, with machetes, so it does not foul the conveyors. The crews do not think about what they are doing. They are maintaining the operation. The willow comes back. The moss comes back. The cold comes back. The sound does not stop.

I am the pit, and I am not in a hurry.
