I am the oldest woman on the shelf.

I do not know I am the oldest woman on the shelf. There is no word for *oldest* in the language. There is no word for *old*. There is a word for *woman who has been here a long time*, but it is the same word as *woman who is here now*, and it is the same word as *woman who will be here*, because the women who are here are the women who have been here, and they are the women who will be here, and the difference between them is not a thing the language marks. I am the woman who sits on the higher basalt in the hour that is neither morning nor evening. There have been other women who have sat on this basalt in this hour. I am one of them. I am the one whose body, right now, has the weight.

The basalt under me is sharp. Even through the folded kelp-mat I have laid beneath my hips, the basalt is sharp, and the sharpness has not changed. The basalt is young. The basalt has only just stopped rising. The grandmother of my grandmother sat on this same basalt in this same hour, and the basalt was sharp then too, and her kelp-mat was the same kelp-mat in the same folds, and the hour was the same hour, and the sharpness was the same sharpness. Nothing has worn smooth. Nothing will wear smooth. The basalt is the fresh geology of an eternal yesterday, and the freshness is the same freshness, and the yesterday is the same yesterday.

My hands are on my knees. My hands are gnarled. My fingers are bent from the gripping of basalt and the twisting of rope and the cracking of crab. The calluses on my palms are pale yellowish horn, ridged in patterns that are as individual as the patterns on the soles of my feet, and the patterns are read by no one now because the mothers and the aunties who used to read them are all in the same place I am, which is everywhere, which is here, and there is no need to read what is known. I have caught the thumb-sized crabs with these hands. I have caught the crabs the size of a dinner plate with these hands, with a single blow of the heel of the hand against the basalt, the way my mother taught me, the way my mother's mother taught her, the way the teaching has always been done. The crabs are the same crabs. The hands are the same hands. The basalt is the same basalt.

My hair hangs in damp ropes down my back. It is black, but the threads of it nearest the nape of my neck are streaked the white of bird-dropping and the black of bird-dropping, and the streaks have been there for a span of time I do not count, because counting is not a thing we do. The hair is bound at the nape with a length of kelp-fiber, and the kelp-fiber is the same kelp-fiber that bound my mother's hair, and the binding is the same binding, and the rope is the same rope. I do not cut my hair. I do not comb my hair. I bind it when it gets in the way, and otherwise I leave it alone. There is no word for *beauty* in the language. There is no word for *ugly*. There is no word for the way the hair would look to a person standing in lamplight in a dry room. There is no lamplight. There is no room. The water stands in every alley up to the knee or the ankle, and the mist stands at chest height, and the world is what is here.

The mist is at my chest. Above the mist there is the suggestion of a brighter place, a vague diffuse glow, as of a sun behind heavy cloth. No shadow ever falls on the basalt under me. No shadow ever falls on any basalt in the town. The world is painted in the colors of wet slate: black basalt, gray mud, silver water, the streaks of white where the birds have been. I do not look up at the brighter place. The brighter place is not a thing I think about. It is just there, the way the basalt is there, the way the water is there. It is what is above the mist.

I look out at the mist. I look as if I could see through it. The look is in my face. I do not know it is in my face. No one has told me. The look is the look of a person who has been for a very long time in a place that does not change, and who has begun, without knowing it, to feel the weight of the not-changing. The weight is not grief. I have not lost anything. The weight is not boredom. I have not expected anything else. The weight is something else, something that has no name in the language, and so it cannot be spoken of, and so it sits in the body like the water sits in the alleys, in the channels, in the standing sheets. It is carried. It is not put down.

The sound is around me. The sound is in me. The sound is the first thing anyone born here learns, and they never unlearn it, because it never stops. The mud-burrowers drum with their tail-flippers in their holes, a low pulse I feel in the soles of my calloused feet. The crabs rasp their flat shells against the coral and click their claws at one another in warning, a sound like wet slate dragged over wet slate, multiplied by the million. The fish break the surface in long lines, slapping the water with a continuous papery thunder. The birds wheel above in long screaming lines, their droppings streaking the basalt black-and-white. Out past the shelf-edge, where the water deepens and darkens, the great long-backed things exhale in plumes of steam, and the steam hisses into the mist, and the great things move in their slow circuits and send a slow heave of a swell that comes across the channels and slaps the walls of the houses. And beneath all of it is the sea itself, low, steady, enormous, the voice of a sea that has been saying the same thing for as long as this place has stood and will say it for as long as this place stands, which is forever, because nothing here ever changes.

I have a word. It is the longest word in the language. No outsider could pronounce it. It is the name for the sound the sea makes at the shelf-edge in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, when the wind has dropped and the sound has thinned to just the pulse of the mud and the great sighs out past the shelf. The word is not a word. It is a sequence of clicks and a long exhalation through the nose and a small downward turn of the head at the end. I say it sometimes, when the wind has dropped and the sound has thinned. The younger women do not say it. The younger women are not yet ready. They do not yet have what is in me.

What is in me is not the word. What is in me is the weight. The word is a small thing, a sequence of sounds. The weight is the other thing, the thing that has no name. It is not the sea. It is not the mist. It is not the sound. It is what the sound is doing in me, after a long time. It is the way the sound of the surf has got into my chest the way that water gets into the alleys, and stays there, and is not drained. The shelf is wet. The shelf is warm. The shelf is deafening. The shelf is dim. The shelf does not change. I have been on the shelf for a span of time that would be remarked upon elsewhere, and the shelf has not changed, and the span of time is not remarked upon here, because remarking is not a thing we do.

A child was born on the shelf this morning, or what was once an hour. The child will learn the sound before it learns its own name. The child will learn to wade before it learns to walk. The child will grow up and catch crabs and mend walls and shout across alleys and one day will be replaced by another child, who will do the same. The stone under the child's feet will be the same stone. The mist will be the same mist. The sound will be the same sound. The child will sit on the higher basalt in the hour that is neither morning nor evening, and the child will look out at the mist as if she could see through it, and the look will be in her face, and she will not know it is there.

The child is not unhappy. I am not unhappy. We are not unhappy the way a prisoner is unhappy, because we do not know we are imprisoned. We are not unhappy the way a person in grief is unhappy, because we have no grief. We are not unhappy the way a bored person is unhappy, because boredom requires the expectation of something else, and we have no expectation of anything else. The wall is mended because the wall is mended. The crab is caught because the crab is caught. The child is raised because the child is raised. The sea sounds because the sea sounds.

The wall is mended. The wall is undermined in the night. The wall is mended again. The woman who mends it does not think *why do I mend a wall that will be undermined again*. The question does not occur. The question is not in the language. The language has nouns for the twelve kinds of crab, and nouns for the pools and the channels and the colors of the mud, and verbs for wading and setting a snare and cleaning a fish and shouting. The language has no grammatical tense. The language has no grammatical number. A noun is a noun. It stands for the thing. The thing is the same thing whether there is one of it or a thousand. The verb is just a louder noun.

I have eaten the shelf. I have eaten the crabs, the thumb-sized ones and the ones the size of a kitchen table. I have eaten the silver-sided fish, taken in the channels with hand-nets of woven fiber. I have eaten the long pale worms pulled from the mud by the handful. I have eaten the jellyfish that drift in the deeper pools, pale blue and stinging, the stinging not mattering because the mouth is already calloused in its way. I have eaten the eel-like thing with too many fins that lurks under stones and in crevices. I have eaten the seabirds when they came down in such clouds that the dim brightened and darkened. The shelf is alive. The shelf is the garden that keeps renewing itself identically each morning. The renewing is the same renewing. The morning is the same morning.

The mist thins a little, where I sit, because the basalt under me gives off a faint heat from some warm seam in the rock beneath. The water that crosses the seam steams gently, adding a thin whistle to the rest of the sound. The heat is the same heat. The seam is the same seam. The steam is the same steam. My skin warms beneath the kelp-cloth I have wound around my hips and tucked. My skin is very dark, a brown so deep it is nearly black in the dim. My skin has a permanent sheen to it, an oiliness, from the kelp-oil I rub myself with and the water itself and something the skin does of its own accord in air that never dries. My face has a polished look, as if it had been rubbed for a long time with a soft stone. The rubbing is not a thing I have done. The polishing is the time. The time is the polishing.

I am the woman who has been here a long time. I am the woman who is here now. I am the woman who will be here. The weight is in me. The weight is not in the younger women. The weight will come into the younger women when they have been here for a long time, when their polishes have turned, when their hair is matted and stained and their fingers are bent and their faces have the polished look. The weight will come into them the way the salt comes into the kelp, slowly, invisibly, until the kelp is more salt than kelp. They will sit on this basalt in this hour. They will look out at this mist. The mist will be the same mist. The look will be the same look.

I say the longest word. The clicks. The exhalation. The small downward turn of the head at the end. The sound joins the rest of the sound. The rest of the sound is the same rest of the sound. The basalt under me is sharp. The basalt has only just stopped rising. The basalt is young. The basalt is the fresh geology of an eternal yesterday. The world is being made, and is not yet finished, and yet will never be finished, because nothing changes here.

The weight is in me. The weight is what I am. The weight is the not-changing. The shelf is the shelf. The shelf is all of it. The all of it is forever.

I am the oldest woman on the shelf. I am one of the many. I am the one who carries.

The mist is at my chest. The sound is in my teeth. The basalt under me is sharp, and will be sharp tomorrow, and will be sharp in the span of time that is not counted. The weight sits in my hands, on my knees, in the polished look of my face, in the damp ropes of my hair. I do not put it down. I cannot put it down. It is what I am.

The sea sounds because the sea sounds.

The shelf goes on.
