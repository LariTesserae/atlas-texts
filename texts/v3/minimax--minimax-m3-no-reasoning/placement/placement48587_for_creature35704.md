# What the Pump Remembers

I am the man from the annex, though "man" is a word I use loosely these days. I am the man who arrived with dry boots. I am the man who arrived twelve years ago with a satchel of tools inherited from my father, who inherited them from his, and who believed, as I believed, that machines remember how to be machines if you refuse to let them forget.

I do not know my true name anymore. It is on a tongue that has been replaced by the vocabulary of this place. I am *the man from the annex*. I am *pump-keeper*. The children call me something shorter, something that means roughly *the one who listens to the iron*. I answer to it. Names are like nails here — they rust if you don't use them, and they rust anyway if you do.

---

I came down through the wet arch because the path on the surface had been washed out, and a traveler in a village three days east had told me there was a way through the old ground, through the abandoned stations, that the through-route was passable if you didn't mind damp. I didn't mind damp. I had been walking for six weeks, and the contents of my satchel had been reduced by trade and loss to: a wrench of good steel, a smaller wrench of lesser steel, a tin of grease that was half-full, a tin of oil that was three-quarters full, a coil of copper wire, a leather strop, a whetstone, three needles of descending size, a book I had been meaning to read, and the conviction, which I now recognize as the conviction of the young, that any machine in disrepair was a machine in waiting.

I had been looking for the pump. I had been looking for the pump for two years, ever since I understood, in the way that one understands such things when one is twenty-three and has just lost one's teacher, that a pump I had heard described in his voice was, in fact, the pump I had been sent to find. I was looking for the pump my father had maintained, that his father had maintained, that a long line of fathers and mothers and cousins and apprenticeships had maintained since the year I do not remember, in a lineage of care that did not require the pump to be running. I was looking for a thing that I had been told existed in a place I had never been, and I found it.

The pump was cold. The pump was banked with ash that was, itself, a kind of time. I touched the flywheel with the back of my hand the way my father had taught me to touch a machine I had not yet been introduced to, and the iron was the temperature of a held breath, and I knew the pump, and the pump did not know me, and that was correct, because a pump is patient in a way that humans are not.

---

I live behind the door that the strangler fig has half-eaten. The door is still a door, mostly. The fig has made a window of itself where the wood has gone soft, and through the window I can see the flywheel, and the flywheel is my clock, and my sundial, and my calendar. I do not need to know what hour it is in the village three days east. I need to know what the flywheel is doing. The flywheel is doing nothing. The flywheel has been doing nothing for years, and I have been watching it do nothing with the attentiveness of a person who has confused attention with love.

I oil it. I oil it on a schedule that I have invented and that no one else observes. Tuesday and Friday, if it is Tuesday or Friday, which it may not be. I take a rag and a tin and I climb onto the platform beside the pump and I apply the oil to the bearings, to the piston rod, to the joint where the connecting rod meets the crankshaft, and the oil is thin and dark and it smells of petroleum, which is a smell that does not exist in this place except in my tin, and when I open the tin the smell is so strong, so *out of place*, that for a moment the station seems to recoil from it. The water-dripping seems to pause. The fig, in my imagination, tightens its grip on a brick.

I oil the pump the way a priest anoints a dying man. I do it because it is the thing I know how to do. I do it because the oil will be gone in four years, at the rate I am using it, and after that there will be no more oil, and the pump will begin to rust in earnest, and I will have to find another way to care for it, or I will have to stop caring for it, and I do not know which of those will be the death of me.

The man from the annex has a theory about the pump.

The man from the annex believes, on the basis of nothing that would survive contact with a hydrological engineer, that the borehole still reaches the water table, that the firebox could be lit, that the piston would rise, that the water would come up, that the water would go out, that the station would, in some meaningful sense, become less wet. He believes this the way a man dying of thirst in a desert believes in the next oasis. He believes it because to stop believing it would be to admit that he has spent twelve years caring for a thing that will never, in his lifetime or anyone else's, do the only thing it was built to do.

He is not unhappy. He is not happy. He has the pump.

---

I have come to a disagreement with Maren, though we have never spoken it. The disagreement is about time. Maren believes, if she believes anything, that the pump is a thing of the past — that it is part of the station's history the way the brass nameplate is part of the station's history, an artifact, a relic, a curiosity. She sorts the past. She puts it into tins. She shelves it. The pump, in her view, belongs on a shelf.

I believe, and I cannot say this to her, that the pump is a thing of the future. That it is waiting. That it is, in some sense, more *alive* now, sitting cold in the annex, than it was when it was running, because then it was just doing its work, and now it is *possible*. A running pump is a fact. A stopped pump is a promise. A stopped pump is a question that the next flood will answer. I keep the pump the way you keep a fire extinguisher, or a lifeboat, or a prayer. You keep it because the thing you are keeping it against may not come, and you keep it because the keeping is the point.

I pass Maren on the gallery. I nod. She does not nod back. She has not forbidden me the pump. She is not that kind of authority. She simply does not believe in the pump, and her disbelief is, in its way, more corrosive than the water. The water only rusts the iron. Maren's disbelief rusts the *idea* of the pump. Maren's disbelief is the reason the other residents have stopped asking me when I am going to light the firebox. They have decided I am a kind of caretaker of a kind of memorial, and they are kind about it, and the kindness is the worst part.

---

I have been here long enough that the children do not remember me as a traveler. To them I am *the annex man*, the way the cat is *Pump*, the way the lizard is *the brick-thing*. I am part of the taxonomy of the place. I am a permanent resident in the way that a stain is a permanent resident of a shirt.

A child — Tin, the boy with the missing tooth — brought me a gear last winter. A small gear, brass, that he had found in the catch-basin under the pump, where the fern grows. He brought it to me and held it out on his palm and said, *This is yours, I think.* I took it. I cleaned it. I held it up to the light of the bare bulb and turned it, and the teeth were intact, and the hole in the center was the right size for a shaft I had not yet found a shaft for, and I said, *Thank you. This is for the pump.* And Tin nodded, gravely, the way children nod when they have been told something true, and he went away, and I put the gear in my tin, and the gear is still in my tin, and I do not know what it is for, and I do not need to know yet. It is for the future. It is for the future the way all my gears are for the future.

I have a tin of gears. I have a tin of bolts. I have a tin of washers. I have a tin of small mysteries, each one tagged in my own handwriting, which is a handwriting that is becoming harder to read as the damp gets further into the paper and the paper gets further into my hands. *Brass, from the lower conduit, possibly for the relief valve.* *Steel, found in the catch-basin, unknown purpose, good condition.* *Iron, rusted through, no longer useful, kept for sentiment.*

I keep the useless ones for sentiment. This is what twelve years in a wet place has done to me. I have become a person who keeps rusted bolts for sentiment.

---

The pump is taller than I am. The pump is taller than I was when I came, which is to say the pump is taller than I will be. I know its dimensions the way I know my own. The firebox is four feet high. The cylinder is seven feet. The flywheel is five feet in diameter. The borehole goes down, and down, and down, into a water table I have never seen and can only infer from the level of the water in the catch-basin, which rises and falls with the rains I cannot see from inside the station.

I have drawn the pump. I have drawn it on paper, and the paper has gone soft, and the drawing is now a part of the paper, and the paper is now a part of the wall it is pinned to. I have drawn the pump as a section, as an exploded view, as a schematic, and the schematic is wrong in places because I am guessing about the parts I cannot see, the parts that are inside the cylinder, the parts that are down the borehole, the parts that are in the past.

I talk to the pump. I do not say this to anyone. I talk to the pump the way you talk to a person who is asleep. I tell it the weather, which it cannot see. I tell it the news of the gallery, which it cannot hear over the water-sound. I tell it that the cat is on top of it, that the cat is old, that the cat may not see another winter, and that when the cat is gone I will need a new way to mark that the pump is still a pump and not yet a ruin. I tell it that the children have learned to read the beans but not the gears, and that this is a sadness. I tell it that Maren says the pump is dead, and I tell it that she is wrong, and the pump does not answer, because the pump is asleep, and the sleeping is a kind of answer.

---

I had a teacher once. His name is also gone, but the shape of his hands when he held a wrench is not gone. The way he tilted his head to listen to a bearing. The way he cleaned a part before he reassembled it, even if the part was going back into the same machine, because *the part deserves to be clean on its way home.* I learned from him that a machine is a kind of memory, and that to maintain a machine is to maintain a memory, and that to maintain a memory is to refuse the kind of forgetting that the world wants from us.

He died. I do not want to say how. I want to say he died the way Maren says the pump died — that he is banked with ash, that he is waiting, that he is patient. But he is not waiting. He is gone. The pump is waiting. The pump is the only thing in my life that is waiting, and I am its keeper, and the keeping is the point.

---

I have come to understand, in the twelve years I have been here, that the residents of the gallery are right about the place. The water is winning. The fig is winning. The station is becoming a forest, slowly, the way a person becomes a memory of themselves. The pump is not going to be run. The pump is not going to dry the station. The pump is going to rust, and the rust is going to feed the fern in the catch-basin, and the fern is going to send its spores into the borehole, and the borehole is going to become a planter, and the planter is going to become a tree, and the tree is going to grow up through the cylinder and out the top of the firebox, and the pump is going to become a thing that holds a tree, and the tree is going to be a kind of pump after all, pulling water up from below in a different engine.

This is what will happen. I know it. I have known it for years. And I keep oiling the pump. And I will keep oiling the pump until the oil is gone. And then I will keep something else. And then I will keep something else. And the keeping will be the point, and the point will be the only thing I have to offer the future, which is a future I do not expect to see.

---

There is a young traveler who has come down through the wet arch this week. He is uncomfortable. His boots are not stuffed. He asks me, as travelers do, what I am doing, and I tell him I am maintaining the pump, and he asks me what the pump is for, and I tell him the pump is for keeping the water down, and he looks at the water coming down through the ceiling and the water coming up through the floor and the water running in sheets down every vertical surface, and he says, *Is it working?* and I say, *No,* and he says, *Then why do you keep it?* and I say, *Because the next flood may come,* and he says, *There hasn't been a flood,* and I say, *There hasn't been a flood yet.*

He does not understand. That is fine. He is young. He has dry boots. He will go back up through the wet arch and he will walk to the village three days east and he will tell people that there is a man in the Weeping Hall who oils a pump that has not pumped in years, and the people will say, *Ah, yes, the pump-keeper,* and they will not think about it further, because they have their own machines to keep, their own memories to maintain, their own small disciplines against the larger forgetting.

I am not unhappy. I am not happy. I have the pump. The pump is sleeping. The sleep is a kind of work.

---

I will oil the pump tomorrow, if tomorrow is Tuesday or Friday. The oil will be a little thinner. The rag will be a little more frayed. My hands will be a little more red at the knuckles. The cat will watch me from the firebox with its yellow eyes that have stopped reflecting light the way a young cat's eyes do. The children will be somewhere in the lower station, watching a drop form on a brick. Maren will be sorting something into a basin. The lizard will be in a joint. The owl will be asleep in the upper reaches, digesting a mouse. The fig will be a millimeter thicker against the wall it is climbing. The moss will be an inch longer on the girders. The water will fall, and fall, and fall.

And I will climb onto the platform. And I will open the tin. And the smell of petroleum will cut through the wet air like a small sharp knife. And I will apply the oil to the bearings. And the pump will be a little less rusted. And the pump will be a little more itself. And the keeping will be the point. And the point will be enough.

I am the man from the annex. I am the keeper of the pump. I am the resident who arrived with dry boots. I am the one who listens to the iron.

I am the one who is still listening.
