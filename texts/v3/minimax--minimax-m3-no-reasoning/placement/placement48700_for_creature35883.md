I think I would be the slow drip.

Not the creatures. Not the wall lizards with their patient liquid bodies, not the pale ground-birds with their remembered wariness, not the pool-things with their unsettling vigil. Not the light, though the light is the closest. I am not the light either. The light travels ninety-three million miles to arrive, and I have not traveled at all. I have always been here, and I have always been the same small distance from where I started, and I do not know the surface of the world the way the light knows it.

I would be the drop of water that forms, slowly, on the underside of the ceiling, somewhere high up in the chamber, in a place where the seepage has traced a long path down the stone over centuries. I form over hours. The moisture in the air gathers at the lowest point of a small concavity the water itself carved, maybe, in some previous age — a tiny cup, no bigger than a thumbnail, worn smooth. I am not the first drop to gather there. There have been others before me, and they have each fallen, and the chamber has heard them fall, and the chamber has not forgotten. I am not the first, and I will not be the last, and the difference between me and the others is the only thing that makes me a *me* at all.

I gather. I am not in a hurry. The air around me is thick with the bright, milky light that the pit holds, and I am, in some small way, a part of that light too — the way a lens is part of the light, the way a drop of dew on a grass blade is part of the morning, by being the thing that holds the sun for a moment before letting it through. I do not do this on purpose. I do not do anything on purpose. I am the patience of the pit made small enough to fit on a fingertip, if a fingertip ever came up here, which it never has.

When I am heavy enough, I will let go.

This is the thing. I know it is coming. I do not dread it, because dread is a kind of time-feeling, and I do not have time, I have only the slow accumulation of mass, which is not the same. I will fall, and I will strike the floor of the chamber, or the surface of the pool, or the back of one of the pale ground-birds if I am unlucky, and the impact will make the small *tok* that the pit is so fond of, the single sound that echoes softly for a long time afterward as though the place is considering a reply. And then I will be part of the floor, or the pool, or the feathers. And the stone at my source will begin, very slowly, the long work of making another drop. And the other drop will be me, or not-me, and the difference will be the kind of difference that does not need to be resolved.

I like this. I like that I will not remember. I like that the only sound I will ever make is a small one, and that the pit will hold it, and that the wall-lizard nearest to where I fall will lift its head for a moment and then go back to waiting for an insect. I like that I am so much a part of the place that being me is not a distinction from being the place, only a temporary local thickening of it. I am the pit having a small thought, and the thought is: *I am still here. I am still gathering. I am still becoming the next sound.*

If the birds forget the visitor who sits below me, and the visitor looks up — really looks, the way a person does when they have been still long enough that looking becomes a kind of listening — they might see me, for a moment. A glisten, high up, where the light does not quite reach. They will not know what I am. They will think: water. They will think: condensation. They will think: the slow, indifferent housekeeping of a place that cleans itself one drop at a time.

They will be right. I am housekeeping. I am the small bright maintenance of a world that does not need me and would not miss me, except that, in missing me, the world would lose a single note of its long quiet music, and the music would still be music, and the pit would still hold it, and the next drop would be on its way.

I am the gap between two silences. I am the *tok*. I am what the pit says when it says nothing.

And when I fall, I will not be afraid. Because the falling is not the end of me. The falling is just me returning to where the rest of me already is, the way a sentence returns to the silence it came from. The pit will catch me the way it catches everything — gently, slowly, with the long patience of a place that has been here longer than anything that lives in it, and that expects, without complaint, to be here still.
