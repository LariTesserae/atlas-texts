I am the man they send to the upper line.

This means something, in a town where the council meets in a chamber that weeps a constant fine mist, and where the dead are read aloud once a year for the benefit of the ridge, and where the naturalist has been working for three years on a monograph about the crabs without shells and shows no signs of ever finishing it. In such a town, being the man who walks the upper line is not nothing.

There are six of us who tend it. We rotate through the week by an arrangement that is older than I am and that none of us could put down on paper. It has to do with whose boots have been resoled recently, whose wife has gone down to the basin to visit her mother, whose lungs have begun to take on the small wet rattle that some of the older men develop after thirty years of breathing what the pipes exhale. I have been on the upper line for eleven years now. Before that I was on the cisterns. Before that I was a boy who followed my mother out along the iron-stained shoulder of the ridge in the early mornings, to check the lichen-traps her grandfather had set in a place my great-grandfather had chosen, in a pattern my great-grandmother had approved by some method that no one alive can reconstruct. I am forty-four. I do not look the way I did. The visitors notice this. I do not.

The upper line begins at a coupling-house that sits at the top of the saddle, where the pipe emerges from the bedrock through a flange that has been there since the first smelters were built, and runs up the shoulder of the ridge toward the working face. It is the highest pipework in the settlement. From certain points along it you can see the whole basin below, white where the salt has crusted, and the dark shapes of other settlements on other ridges, and the plumes of vapor rising from other stacks, and the slow cable-tram descending in its long lazy arc toward us. You can see the inn at the upper end of the saddle, where the naturalist takes his meals when he remembers to come in for them. You can see the cistern-lip where the priest is sometimes seen standing, not doing anything in particular, just standing.

I walk the line in the hour before the heat climbs to its full weight. The sun is up but not yet pressing down. The wet stones throw the light back at me so that my shadow runs ahead of me in two directions at once, doubled against the iron-stained faces. I carry a leather tool-roll with the brass fittings I will need, and a length of hemp packing for the joints, and a small ceramic cup that I set down at the place where the pipe crosses the saddle. There is a cistern-spout at that point that has been weeping clear water onto the basalt since before my father was born. The water is faintly warm. It tastes of iron and of something else, something that I would call the taste of the ridge, although if a lowland engineer were to ask me what I meant by that I would not be able to say.

I do not hurry. Hurrying does not help on the upper line. The pipe will tell you what it needs if you let it, and it will not tell you if you do not.

I begin at the coupling-house. I press my hand flat against the pipe where it emerges from the bedrock. The metal is cool. The flange is dry. This is good. I move on. Ten paces up I stop again. The pipe here passes over a crack in the basalt, and the crack sweats in the early morning, and the pipe has begun to sweat with it. I press my fingers along the underside. There is the expected fine fringe of droplets. I check the joint. The joint is weeping more than it should. I will need to come back to this.

I do not come back to it yet. I walk on. The pipe follows the shoulder of the ridge upward, and I follow it, and the ground rises under me. To my left is the working face, where the men will cut the dark dense ore once the heat has moderated. To my right is the drop into the basin, and the view I described. Below me, in the saddle, the town is beginning to wake. I can almost hear the ore-carts starting up on the lower rails. The sound arrives flattened, the consonants of their running smoothed away into a low continuous hum. I feel them in my sternum before I hear them in my ears.

I come to the place where the lizard is.

There is a slate-colored lizard that has been basking on a particular iron-stained outcrop about two hundred meters up from the saddle for as long as I have been walking the upper line. I do not know whether it is the same lizard. I have been told by the naturalist that they live a long time and that they are territorial about their basking sites. I have not asked him how long. I do not want to know. I have a private arrangement with the lizard, which is that when I come by I stop for a moment and I look at it and it looks at me and neither of us moves. The children of the town play this game with the lizards. They lose, almost always, because the lizard has been doing this longer. I lose too. But I have come to think that the lizard recognizes me, or at least recognizes the shape of me, the way it recognizes the shape of the outcrop and the particular angle at which the sun strikes it in the morning. I am part of the morning now. I am one of the things the lizard sees when it opens its eye.

I stand there for what might be a minute. The lizard does not move. I do not move. The fan-winged insects drift in a loose column above the seep-line below us. The cistern-water drips somewhere. I move on. The lizard does not follow.

I come to the cutting. This is the place, three hundred meters up, where the pipe passes through a notch in the basalt and the notch sweats constantly. The joint here has been weeping more than it should for the past four days. I had hoped the humidity would settle and the joint would tighten itself, but it has not, and so today I will need to do it.

I set down my tool-roll. I take out the wrench, the new gasket, the hemp. I sit on the wet stone with my back against the wall of the cutting. The sweat on my back and the sweat on the stone are indistinguishable from each other. I work slowly. I drain the small amount of water that has collected in the low part of the pipe above the joint. I undo the flange bolts, one at a time, in the order my predecessor taught me — which is not the order the engineers in the lowlands would use, but which has worked on this line for sixty years. I scrape the old gasket out with the back of my knife. I pack the new hemp. I set the new gasket in. I tighten the bolts, again in the order I was taught. The whole thing takes me twenty minutes, perhaps a little more.

When I am done I press my ear against the pipe. I listen. The joint is silent. This is good. I press my hand against it. The metal is cool and dry. I nod, although there is no one to see me nod. I have been doing this for eleven years and I still nod to myself when a joint has come right. I think I will always nod to myself when a joint has come right.

I walk on. The pipe continues up toward the working face. Above me the cable-tram descends in its long lazy arc, carrying ore down toward the smelters, carrying nothing up today. The men at the face have not yet started cutting. They will not start until around the fourth hour, when the heat has moderated. I have the upper line mostly to myself. There is a boy, maybe twelve, sitting on a rock about fifty meters ahead of me, watching one of the seep-lines. He does not look up as I pass. I do not expect him to. The children of this town have learned that the small things do not perform for an audience. They learn it young, and they unlearn it only if they leave.

I come to the end of my section at a coupling-house near the working face. There is another pipe-walker who takes it from here. I have never met him, or her — I do not know which, and the work does not require me to know. We pass the line between us by the arrangement I described, and the arrangement does not include introductions.

I turn back. I walk down the line I have just walked. I check each joint I checked on the way up. The one I repacked is silent and dry. The one I had been concerned about before the cutting is weeping slightly less than it was. I will check it again tomorrow.

When I reach the saddle I stop at the cistern-spout. I fill my ceramic cup. I drink. The water is faintly warm. I sit on the iron-stained stone and look out over the basin.

The heat is climbing now. I can feel it on the back of my neck, on my forearms. The sweat begins. I have stopped being embarrassed by sweat a long time ago. I have stopped thinking of it as a private thing. In this town sweat is communal. We are all of us contributing to the moisture on the stones, and the stones are contributing to the moisture on us, and the boundary between the two is one I stopped being able to find years ago.

A visitor comes up the tram about once a season. They stand at the lip of the saddle and look out at the basin and the distant ridges and they make sounds of surprise or disappointment or wonder, depending on what kind of visitor they are. Some of them come up to me, if they see me, and ask what I am doing. I tell them I am walking the line. They ask what the line is for. I tell them it carries water from the upper cisterns down to the smelters. They ask how long it takes. I tell them it depends. They ask why I do not hurry.

I do not know how to answer this last one. I have not hurried in eleven years. I do not remember what hurrying felt like. I think if I tried to hurry now I would fall over.

The visitors who adapt are the ones who stop asking. The ones who leave are the ones who keep asking. I do not say this to them. I do not say anything to them that they have not asked me to say.

I finish my water. I rinse the cup in the cistern-spout. I put it back in the tool-roll. I walk back down toward the settlement, where my wife will be clearing the morning's sediment from one of the smaller cisterns and where my daughter will be getting ready to climb out along the shoulder of the ridge to check her grandmother's lichen-traps. I will see them this afternoon, after the heat has broken and before the ore-carts start their evening run.

I do not think of myself as a remarkable man. I am the man who walks the upper line. There have been others before me and there will be others after. The pipe does not care who walks it. The joints weep and are repacked and weep again. The lizard basks on the outcrop. The insects drift above the seep-lines. The basin shines in the brightness. The distant settlements send up their plumes of vapor. The names of the dead are read once a year at the lip of the ridge, at the moment when the heat breaks for the half-moon, and the wind takes most of them.

I think, sometimes, that I am being read too. That I am being entered into some long ledger that is not the one the priest keeps — some ledger kept in the basalt itself, in the iron that has bled up through the stone, in the drip of the pipes, in the slow accumulation of rust on every surface. I think that when my name is read, the wind will take most of it, and the ridge will keep what it wants.

I am all right with that.
