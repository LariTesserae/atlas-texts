The visitor who did not leave again.

I find I am one of the ones who came back.

---

I came the first time as a scholar, I think—though that word now feels like a costume I once wore. I had read the treaties on symbiotic architecture. I had prepared questions about the bioluminescent mosses. I had packed, in a leather satchel I no longer own, a notebook and three charcoal pencils and the brittle confidence of a person who believes the world can be folded into a thesis.

I walked down those eight thousand stairs with a young man's impatience disguised as a young man's patience. I counted the first hundred. The second hundred. Somewhere around the four-hundredth mark I stopped counting, because my legs had begun to disagree with my project, and because the air had begun to thicken, and because the light had begun to change from the gray of the surface world to that first faint green-gold suggestion of something growing.

By the time I reached the bottom, I had forgotten what I came to ask.

---

They gave me a narrow bunk in a residential level that smelled of warm peat and, always, of the place—that almost-edible sweetness, the bread-before-the-crust smell. I ate boiled root and bean. I was shown the great vats. I was shown the boilers, breathing their slow hooting breaths. I was taken, on the third day, to the upper canopy, where the trunks run horizontal and the leaf-rosettes hang like small green lanterns in the dim, and I stood under them and looked up and felt something in my chest rearrange itself in a way I did not have vocabulary for then, and have less vocabulary for now.

I stayed two weeks. I copied notes. I drew the moss-painters at their work, in the margins of my pages, sketched from memory because I could not watch them without disturbing them. I listened to the boilers. I listened to the water in the channels, that bass note below the threshold of sound. I watched a worker pass me in an avenue with a dipper of something amber, and I saw, in the half-light, that I could not quite see her—that she was of the place, that the patches on her apron had become the place, that she had been composted into it.

I thought: *I understand this.*

I did not understand this.

I went up. I stood in the sun and found it garish and harsh and full of animals—a dog trotting past with its tongue out, a pigeon arcing from a roof, my own shadow leaping and shrinking as a cloud crossed. I went home. I wrote my thesis. It was praised in modest circles. I did not think about the Condensery for eleven months.

Then I thought about it for three years straight.

---

I came back in the spring of a year I will not specify. I told my department I was returning for further research. I told myself I was returning to close a question. I brought a new satchel and a different set of pencils. I brought, this time, an answer I had prepared: that the Condensery was a closed system, that its inhabitants were adapted to it in ways the surface world could not replicate, that my proper place was above.

I walked down the stairs. I forgot the answer somewhere around the second thousand, where the air first touches the back of the throat and says *I am not air, exactly, I am something that has agreed to be air for you.*

By the time I reached the floor, the answer was gone, and in its place was the smell, and the hooting, and the dim, and the sense—I had it again, stronger this time—of standing inside a body.

They gave me a bunk in the same residential level. The straw had been turned but the frame was the same. I slept. I dreamed, that first night, of pressures and temperatures, which surprised me because I had not yet spoken to a boiler-keeper, and the dream had the cadence of an old keeper's dream, slow and grave.

In the morning I went to the vats and watched them breathe. A woman came up beside me. She was ninety-three, I learned later. She did not speak. We stood together for what I think was an hour. I do not remember what I thought. I remember the color of the light on her hands.

I did not go back up.

---

I have been here for—I have lost count of the years. The ledgers know, but I do not read the ledgers; that is for the archivists, who are right to keep to their solitude, and to whom I am grateful in the distant way one is grateful to old priests. The pace has taken me. The pace has composted me into the place, gently, at a human pace, the way the floor composts the leaf that fell while I was still counting my first hundred stairs.

I am a lighting-painter now. It suits me. I have always been better alone than I admitted in my previous life, and the work suits aloneness: a long-handled brush, a pot of spore-slurry, the slow extension of the dim into corners that have not yet learned to glow. I move through passages that are not on any map, including passages that are not really passages but residual spaces left over from some older geometry I do not ask about. I look, in the green-gold dim, like a ghost painting other ghosts into being. I have been told this. I find it accurate enough.

I have learned to sleep in the moss-light. I have learned to startle nothing. I speak, when I speak, in a kind of murmur, and the murmur is not, I think, something I chose; it is something the place taught my throat.

---

I still have, in a drawer in my bunk, a leather satchel I no longer use. It holds notebooks. The notebooks hold drawings of moss-painters and margins full of questions I once thought mattered. I do not open them. The drawer has grown a faint orange bloom of rust along its hinge—the rust that binds joists, the rust that welds the city into a single object—and the rust has, I think, begun to incorporate the drawer into the wall. In another century the drawer will be a feature of the wall. In three centuries no one will know there was a drawer. The notebooks will be a stratum in the floor, sweet with leaf, fed into a feed-pipe, distilled, given back to the air, fallen again, somewhere.

I find I am not unhappy. The question does not arise. I am of the place. The place is of me. When I pass a worker in an avenue I do not look up, because I have not looked up in years. I am, in the half-light, almost invisible. You see the swing of my arm. You see the dip of the brush. You do not quite see me.

---

There is a leaf that detaches itself from the ceiling, sometimes, when I am painting in the upper levels. It falls slowly, in the still air, turning as it falls, a thing of patience and slow geometry. It takes a long time to land. I watch it when I can. I have watched a hundred such leaves. I have watched, I think, the same leaf twice, in the sense that the leaf is always the same leaf, because every leaf is the leaf the place is having of itself at that moment, and the place has been having the same leaf, in different particulars, for a thousand years.

When the leaf lands in the floor, the floor takes it. I know what the floor will do with it. I know what the pipes will do with it. I know what the boilers will do with it. I am, in some small fraction, what the boilers will do with it. We are all, in some small fraction, what the boilers will do with us.

---

I came here with questions. I have, by long acquaintance, become someone who does not need them. I came here with a thesis. The thesis has been distilled into something that is not a thesis, and that something is in the air, and I breathe it, and it is warm, and it is sweet, and it is almost-but-not-quite food, and I am a little hungry, always, and that is right.

The eight thousand stairs are still there. They climb up through the clay, through the layers of pressed root, through the memory of a slit of sky, up to a place of garish light and trotting dogs and pigeons and the unrepeatable entropy of animal life. Sometimes I think of that place. Sometimes a runner comes down from the surface with a trade-bundle and a story of the world above, and I listen, and the vowels come to my ear flattened and strange, and I nod, and I go back to my painting.

I do not leave.

I am the condensate of the place, given form, given work, given the long repetition of my own days. I am the rust on the hinge of a drawer no one will find. I am a brush-stroke in a passage that is not on any map. I am the moss-light, faintly, in the corner I painted last night, which is glowing now as I write this, which will glow when I am gone, which will be lit, in some small way, by me, for as long as the place continues to be the place.

The place will continue.

I will be of it.
