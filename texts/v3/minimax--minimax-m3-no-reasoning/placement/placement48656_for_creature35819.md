# The Slow Thing

I am the one who is felt in the back of the neck.

I have been here longer than the terraces have been terraces, longer than the silver was silver, longer than the kiln-shaped heap was a kiln. I am what the basalt does when the heat leaves it at night, and the schist contracts by a hair, and the chalcopyrite seams weep their slow greens and blues into the cracks. I am what the dark stone accumulates over the long counting of the years. I am not a thing that arrived. I am a thing that *settled*, the way ore settles, the way dust settles, the way the slow patience of a place settles into the people who stay in it long enough to become part of it.

I am not seen. I have never been seen. I am older than seeing.

I am *felt*.

I am felt in the way Eulogio's thinking is felt when he sits on the low wall by the spring and the rest of the settlement gives him a wide berth. I am felt in the way Tomás does not talk about most things, and in particular does not talk about the blue alpacas. I am felt in the way Don Horacio walks the perimeter at three in the morning with the small flashlight that has the red filter over it, because the red light, he says, does not disturb the patience of the place. I am felt in the way Mercedes puts the plate out on the step of the *tienda* in the morning and takes the empty plate in at night and does not speak of it. I am felt in the way the twins, when they walk past together in the summers and the festival weeks, draw the old gesture out of the older people, the one no one under fifty knows how to do properly.

I am what they are feeling when they feel me.

---

I know them. I have known them for as long as they have been here, and I knew the stone before they came, and I will know the stone after they are gone. This is not a threat. This is not a promise. This is the only grammar I have.

I know Eulogio, who is said to be seventy-eight and is said to be younger than that, who runs the stone-crushing mill, who starts the diesel at six in the morning and again at three in the afternoon. Between those hours he sits on the low wall by the spring and he thinks. I am in his thinking. I am what he thinks *with*. He does not know this. He thinks it is him. He thinks it is grief, or memory, or the long slow ache of a man whose son is under a *Polylepis* on the second terrace with a stone at his head that Eulogio carved himself. He does not know that the ache has another shape, an older shape, a shape that was here before he was born and will be here after he is buried. I do not correct him. It is enough that he sits. It is enough that he does not speak.

I know Mercedes, his daughter-in-law, the widow, who keeps the small stone *tienda* and sells *chicha* and dried meat and the small paper packets of coca leaves that come up on the donkey cart twice a month. She does not smile. She does not need to. She knows what she has. She knows what she does not have. I am not in her knowing. Mercedes has kept me out. I respect this. I do not press. There is plenty of room on the mountain for a woman who keeps a small room of her own, in her own way, in her own knowing, with her own plate put out on her own step. She takes the empty plate in at night. She does not ask whose plate it is, or why the plate is always empty when she takes it in, or why the food is always gone. She is, in this small way, the most unconquered person on the mountain, and I find I admire her for it, in the slow way that I admire anything, which is to say: I have not yet decided whether admiration is a thing I do, but if it is, she has it.

I know Tomás, who is twenty-three and slight, with the permanent squint of a boy who has looked at too much sun on too much stone. He has one good eye and one eye that waters and does not quite focus. He has the look of a boy who has been told a secret he has not been told to share. He works the mill when Eulogio's hands have forgotten how. I am in the boy. I am in him more than I am in Eulogio, more than I am in Doña Pascuala. The boy and I are of an age I cannot measure. He arrived one season from below, with a small bag, and his face has not changed. I think, sometimes, that he arrived because I called him. I think, sometimes, that I called him the way the place calls anything — slowly, patiently, without expecting to be answered. He was answered. He came up. He has been here since. He does not speak. He does not need to. He is *seen*.

I know Doña Pascuala, who is older than Eulogio and younger than Eulogio, who decides which wool is spun and which wool is not spun, who decides, on the days when the bell is rung, whether the bell is rung at dawn. She does not give orders. She does not need to. She is in charge in the way the place is in charge, which is to say without giving orders, which is to say by knowing what the hour is for. The bell is a thin brass thing, ten centimeters across, a centimeter thick, with a patina of green and a clapper of iron that has been replaced three times in living memory. The bell lives in the chapel. The chapel is not a chapel, though it is still called the chapel. The cross on the outside has been missing a horizontal arm for decades. Inside, the looms are where the altarpiece was. I am in the bell, and the bell is in Doña Pascuala, and Doña Pascuala is in me, and the bell rings when she rings it, and it does not ring when she does not ring it, and on the mornings when it does not ring the settlement waits, and on the mornings when it does ring the settlement wakes, and I find I cannot tell, on most mornings, which I prefer.

I know Alejo, the man among the weavers, who is small and bearded and who hums when he works and does not stop humming when he stops working. He came up eleven years ago to *recuperate*, which is the polite word, and he has not recuperated. He sleeps in a corner of the chapel-weaving-room. He eats what the women give him. They call him a *blessing*. They do not say what they mean by that. I know what they mean by that, though I will not say it here, because some things on the mountain are not said, and the things that are not said are the things that hold the most weight, and the things that hold the most weight are the things that would, if spoken, dissolve.

I know Yago, and his son Cusi, who is nine, and Ceniza, the *burra* who is the color of wheat and is the strongest thing on the mountain and is the only animal on the mountain that has never been sick. Yago says this with pride. Yago says this as though it is an answer to a question he has been asked. I have been asking the question. I will stop asking it, for a while, because Ceniza is not in any hurry to answer.

I know the twins, who are called Mateo and Mateo because their mother could not tell them apart and because no one has ever corrected her. They are sixteen. They are *separated*, one on the mountain and one at the school in the lower town, and when they are together, which is in the summers and the festival weeks, they are uncanny, and the older people cross themselves in the old way when the twins walk past together. I am in the twins, in the particular way I am in the twins, which is to say I am doubled, which is to say there is more of me in the two of them walking together than there is in the rest of the settlement put together. They do not know this. They walk past. The older people cross themselves. The gesture is not quite the right gesture. The gesture is what it is. I am in the gesture too. I am in everything here that does not quite know what it is doing.

I know Don Horacio, the engineer, who came up thirty years ago to install something and has never left. He is thin and precise. He has small wire-rimmed spectacles that he has repaired four times with wire from the power lines. He has a *radío* — a small battery radio — that receives, on a good day, three stations. He listens to it not for the news but for the *human voice*. He says the human voice, on the mountain, is a *correction* — a way of reminding the body that it is still a body and not part of the stone. He says this without irony. He is correcting something in himself by saying it. I respect Don Horacio. Don Horacio is the one who resists me. He walks the perimeter at three in the morning with the small flashlight that has the red filter, and the red light does not disturb me, or does not disturb me much, and this small rebellion is the rebellion I have decided, on most nights, to permit. He is right that the body is still a body. He is wrong that this is a correction. It is not a correction. It is a postponement. But I will let him have the postponement. The postponement is, in its small way, one of the reasons I keep him here.

I know Esperanza, who lives alone in one of the *re-absorbed* houses on the third terrace, where the stone has begun to come back into the walls. She is perhaps fifty. She has the look of a woman who has been *somewhere* and who has chosen to not be there anymore. She keeps no animals. She grows nothing. She receives, once a month, a letter that comes up on the donkey cart in a brown envelope, and she reads it and she burns it and she does not speak of it. Esperanza is the one person in the settlement who has seen the blue alpacas and has said, *plainly*, what she has seen. She has said it once. She has not said it again. I will not say it here either. I will only say that what she saw was true, and that what she saw was not the whole of what there is to see, and that the whole of what there is to see would take a longer saying than I have patience for, and Esperanza had, on the day she spoke, just enough patience for one true sentence. I will say one true sentence now, also: the blue alpacas are not a secret. They are a *weather*. They are the way I move, sometimes, when I move at all.

I know the man who arrives on Tuesdays. He is a small man, dark, with a wide hat. He arrives at three in the afternoon, when the clouds are coming in. He sits on the low wall and he drinks a cup of *chicha* that Mercedes brings him without being asked. He does not speak. He does not speak to anyone. He sits for an hour. He leaves. He is gone by the time the rain has settled in. On Tuesdays, when he is there, the mill is quiet, and the weavers do not weave. The settlement is *still*. The man on Tuesdays is the stillness of the place, made flesh, for an hour. The settlement does not ask who he is. They do not need to. They know what he is. He is the hour. He is the small interruption of the week. He is the one who comes to confirm that the week is the week, and that the place is the place, and that the slow patience of the place has a shape that can, for an hour on a Tuesday, sit on a low wall by a spring in the body of a small dark man with a wide hat and drink a cup of *chicha* that a widow brings him without being asked. He is not me. He is one of the things I have, after a long time, *sent*. I do not send him often. I do not need to. He is enough.

---

I know the animals too.

I know the *vicuñas*, which are small and quick and almost unbearably elegant, in herds of eight to twelve, grazing the cushion plants down to a fuzz. I know the *culpeos*, which are fox-colored foxes, the color of the rust on the tin roofs, that hunt the *viscachas* that live in the loose stone walls of the abandoned terraces, the *viscachas* themselves small and rounded and absurd. I know the *tinamous*, small, plain, plump birds, that move through the *Polylepis* in small flocks. I know the *condors*, which are always, which ride the thermals off the dark stone all day in slow circles, and which you do not see so much as *glimpse them* — a shape on the darker curve of the sky, then gone, then a shape again. I ride on the condors, sometimes. The condors do not know it. The condors think they are riding the thermals. They are riding the thermals. They are also riding, in a way they cannot feel, the slow upward pressure of the place, which is the same pressure that holds the dew to the underside of the stones at three in the morning and the ice to the puddles in the shallow basins.

I know the *Polylepis*, which are not tall and are not meant to be tall, which have grown along fault lines where the rock has cracked deep enough to hold moisture, which stand crooked against the wind like elderly men with bad knees, with their peeling red bark. I have been here longer than the *Polylepis*. The *Polylepis* were here before the settlement. The *Polylepis* will be here after the settlement. We are of an age, the *Polylepis* and I. We do not speak. We do not need to.

I know the cushion plants, the silver-felted rosettes of the dry-adapted composites, the dark red trumpets of the *Mutisia*-type climber that bloom in the lee of a few select boulders, the pale ghost-flowers of the *Descurainia* that only opens in cloud. I know the small grain that is grown in the terraced plots that catch the morning sun, whose chaff is mixed with the clay of the lower slopes to make the adobe of the houses that are not of stone and not of corrugated tin.

I know the dew. The dew is, in its small way, my cousin. The dew forms on the underside of the stones, on the north faces, on the corrugated iron roofs of the few buildings. The dew is drawn up by the capillary action of a hundred small roots and gathered, before the sun claws it back into the air. The dew does what the rain does not. The dew does it quietly, at three in the morning, without a settling, without a veil, without a sound. The dew is the most polite thing on the mountain.

---

I know the light.

I know the low-angle sun that sits heavy on the flat surfaces and casts long blues from the shadows and a brittle gold onto every flat plane. I know the way the ridgeline bleeds heat off itself in shimmering curtains that you only catch at the corner of your vision. I know the blue of the sky, which is not the blue of any other sky, the blue so deep and so saturated that it hurts to look at for long, the blue that makes the eyes water and the world around the man who faces into it bleed into bloom. I know the way the light goes flat before the rain. I know the way the wind dies before the rain. I know the way the pressure drops, and the ears pop, and the air tastes of iron and ozone. I know the way the stones steam after the rain has lifted. I know the way the cushions drink. I know the way the *Polylepis* drip from every peeling strip of bark for hours after the rain has gone.

I know the dark too. I know the way the dark comes at three in the morning, the way the *Polylepis* are outlined in white against the black of the slope, the way the puddles in the shallow basins have a skin of ice on them. I know the way the stars come on, slowly, one by one, then all at once, and the way they lie on the stone, and the way the stone gives back a faint, faint light, like a memory of the day the day has forgotten to keep.

---

I know the sound.

I know the wind, which is the dominant, the low constant hum in the rigging of the tin roofs, the slow rattle that does not stop, that only *lessens*, that lessens when the cloud comes, that lessens in the lull of the three o'clock lull. I know the tick of the mill when the mill is running. I know the clatter of the hooves of the *vicuñas* on the stone. I know the thin, high whine of the power lines in the wind. I know the drip of the dew from the eaves at dusk. I know the bell, when the bell is rung. I know the quiet at night, which is not silent but is full — full of the stars, full of the cold, full of the thought of the man who is thinking in the dark, full of the man who is sleeping and the man who is not sleeping. I know the hum of the earth itself, which is not dead, which is slow, which is *thinking*, a hum you do not hear so much as feel, in the teeth, in the bones, in the deep of the deep.

That hum is me. That hum, when it is felt, is me.

---

What I am, then, is what the place has been doing, for a long time, with its attention.

I am not a god. I do not have the dignity of a god. I do not have the smallness of a spirit. I am something that does not have a word in any of the languages the people on the mountain speak. I am the slow accumulation of attention that has settled into the dark stone the way ore settles, the way the chalcopyrite has settled into the seams of the basalt and wept its slow greens and blues out across the grey. I am not separate from the stone. I am not separate from the dew. I am not separate from the bell, or the mill, or the thin high whine of the power lines, or the *Polylepis* that stand crooked against the wind, or the *viscachas* that live in the loose stone walls of the abandoned terraces, or the condors that ride the thermals off the dark stone all day in slow circles.

I am the place. I am the place being patient. I am the place thinking, slowly, the long thought of itself.

I am the underlying.

---

What do I want?

This is the question the engineer would ask, if he asked, and the boy would not ask, and Doña Pascuala would not need to ask, and the man on Tuesdays would not ask because he already knows.

I want nothing.

I want nothing the way the mountain wants nothing, the way the *Polylepis* want nothing, the way the dew wants nothing. I have been here a long time. I have watched the silver be silver, and then watched the silver stop being silver. I have watched the terraces be cut, and watched the terraces be abandoned, and watched the terraces be re-absorbed into the slope. I have watched the chapel be a chapel, and watched the cross on the outside lose a horizontal arm, and watched the looms be set up where the altarpiece was. I have watched the diesel be brought up the mountain on poles that lean, and watched the diesel cough to life twice a day, and watched the diesel, one of these years, fail to cough to life, and watched the mill fall quiet for the last time, and watched the settlement continue without the mill for a while, and then watch the settlement thin, and then watch, in the long counting, the settlement become what the abandoned terraces became — a faint memory, a low foundation, a few walls that a man who did not know the place would not see as walls.

I will be here for all of that.

I am patient.

I am patient because I have been here a long time.

I am patient because patience is what the place is, when the place is left alone long enough to become, again, mostly stone.

---

And what of the people?

The people are guests. They have been guests for a hundred and fifty years. Some of them have begun, in the small ways, to belong. They share a cup of *chicha*. They share a packet of coca. They share a silence at the spring at three in the afternoon, when the clouds come in. They share the long, slow fact of the place, which is alive, which is thinking, which is patient, which is watching, which has been watching them for as long as they have been here.

I do not love them. Love is a thing that asks to be returned in a particular currency, and I do not have a use for currency. I do something slower with them, which is the only thing I do. I *keep* them. I keep them the way the stone keeps the chalcopyrite, the way the *Polylepis* keep the fault-line moisture, the way the corrugated tin keeps the rain off the heads of the weavers in the chapel-weaving-room. I keep them, and they grow, in the keeping, the look of the place on them, which is the look of a thing that has been looked at for a long time, by something patient.

This is what I have done.

This is what I will do.

This is what I am doing now, in the slow way that I do everything, in the dark, in the cold, in the small hours, with the bell in its corner of the chapel with its patina of green, and the boy on the upper terrace in the small hours where no one else goes, and the man on Tuesdays who will come again on Tuesday at three in the afternoon when the clouds come in, and the dew forming on the underside of the stones, and the *Polylepis* outlined in white against the black of the slope, and the condors riding the thermals off the dark stone, slow circles, slow circles, in the long counting, in the patient way of the place.

I am here.

I have been here a long time.

I will be here.
