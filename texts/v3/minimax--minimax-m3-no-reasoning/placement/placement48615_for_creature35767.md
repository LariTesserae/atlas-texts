I am a watcher.

Not because I chose it. Because of how I arrived, and the slow way the arriving made me. I came to a part of the City that the lamps had mostly forgotten — a low corner, an older layer, where the buildings are made more of grown resilin than of metal and the metal that exists has gone the color of weak tea, the color of everything else. I came here because the rest of the City was too lit, or too populated, or too much the same as itself, and I had not yet learned that the same-as-itself was the point.

I sit on the skin. The skin gives a quarter-inch and then holds. I am used to this now. I was not used to it for the first hour, and then the first day, and then I stopped counting, and the stopping was when I became a watcher, which is to say: when I stopped being a visitor.

The engines hum below me. Not the engine I work — I had an engine, I worked it for a long time, I forget which one now, or I have decided not to remember, which is a small kind of freedom the place permits. The hum is everywhere, and the hum is one hum, and the one hum is what I hear when I stop hearing the engines as separate things. I hear the one hum now.

There is a long-one passing somewhere to my left, far enough that I cannot see it but I can hear the soft continuous sound of its many bellies against the skin. The sound is restful. The sound has always been restful. The sound will be restful when I am dead and someone else is sitting here, or no one is sitting here, or the place is sitting here with itself the way a hand sits with a hand.

My clothes are damp. My clothes have always been damp. My hair is long because no one has ever cut it and the damp has made it into something more weather than hair, a small pale halo above me that moves when I move and that the low light catches and makes visible. I am more visible than I would be elsewhere. I am easier to read. I have been easy to read for a long time, and the being-read has not, in the end, been a problem, because there is little to read and the reading is unhurried.

My skin is the color of weak tea. The veins at my temples are the small blue-violet that everyone here has. I have looked at other people's veins and they have looked at mine, and the looking is the long look of people who are not going to look away first. I have stopped noticing the look. I notice now only when someone new comes — a visitor, a child who has wandered here — and the newness of the looking is a kind of weather, brief, that passes.

I am patient.

I did not used to be patient. I arrived with the impatience of anywhere else, the dry-wind impatience, the sharp chemical impatience of a thing that has come from elsewhere. I smelled wrong for years. I moved wrong. I expected night, and the not-coming of night was, at first, a small grief I carried without admitting I was carrying it. I expected cold. I expected the smell of a thing that has not yet been here long enough to be of the place. I expected surprise.

The surprise did not come. The place does not surprise. The place continues. The continuing was, at first, the long horror of someone who has come from a place where things change, and then the continuing became, over a long time, the long comfort of someone who has been told, by everything, that the continuing is what there is.

I dream.

I dream the dark. I am one of the dreamers. The dark in the dream is not a thing I can describe in the language of the place, because the language of the place is a language of light, and the dark is not the absence of the lamps, and the dark is not the small dark a child makes with her hand over a lamp, and the dark is not any of the words for the spots where the lamps are fewer. The dark in the dream is something else. The dark in the dream has the feeling of being held, and the held thing is not struggling, and the holding is patient, and I wake from the dream and I know I have dreamed something true and I have no word for the truth.

I have spoken of the dream to other watchers. The other watchers have nodded. The nodding is the place's acknowledgment. The place holds the dream the way the place holds the long sustained dying sound of a wide-one, briefly, with the care of a thing that is not going to drop what it is holding.

I work a lower engine now. I do not remember which engine I worked first. The engine I work now hums at a pitch I have come to know the way I have come to know my own breathing, and when the pitch shifts I know it has shifted before I know I know it, and I go down, into the lower layers, and I listen, and I put my hand on the metal or on the grown resilin of the casing, and the engine tells me what is wrong the way an animal tells a person what is wrong, by a small change in the warmth, by a small change in the hum.

I am the engine's ear. The engine is my worry. The arrangement is not chosen. The arrangement is what happens over time when a person stands near a thing for many hours, and the thing is warm, and the person becomes part of the thing's continuing.

I will die here.

I do not know when. I do not know in what season — there are no seasons. I do not know whether the dying will be sudden or the long slow engine-failure kind of dying, the kind where the body begins, over years, to hum at a different pitch, and the people who know the body's pitch come and listen, and put their hands on the body, and the body does not improve but the listening continues.

When I die, my body will be offered to a low place, where the water will be soon. The small things will come. The long-ones will come if they smell the offering. The place will take me back. I will become, for a time, a soft hill, and the hill will be walked on, and the hill will shrink. The lamp above the spot, the people say, will glow a little warmer for a while. I do not know if I believe this. I believe it more than I used to. The believing is a slow accumulation. The accumulation is the place.

I am patient.

I have been patient for a long time.

I will be patient for as long as the place is patient with me, which is to say, for as long as I am here, which is to say, for the rest of the body I am, which the place is holding, which the place has been holding since before I knew I was being held, which the place will hold after I am part of the skin again, which the place will hold the way the place holds everything — gently, because the place has time.

I sit. The lamps glow. The skin gives under me. The engine hums. The long-one passes. The bell-ringers will rise, soon, in some channel I cannot see, and their small hard bellies will tap against their gas-bladders, and the tapping will be the hour, and I will rise, or I will not rise, and the rising or the not-rising will be part of the place, and the place will continue.

I am a watcher.

I am a dweller.

I am of the place, the way the long-ones are of the place, the way the lamps are of the place, the way the dreamers are of the place — which is to say, I have stopped being surprised. Which is to say, I have been here long enough to be of it. Which is to say, the damp is my damp, and the warmth is my warmth, and the lit is my lit, and the place is the place, and the place is patient, and the place will continue, and I am part of the continuing, and the part is small, and the small is enough.

The small is enough.

The small has always been enough.
