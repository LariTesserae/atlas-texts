I would be the floor.

Not the crust — the crust is the place's skin, the membrane stretched tight over something that is always moving underneath. I am what is underneath the crust. I am the dense paste, the thing that is not quite liquid and not quite solid, the thing that shifts when the place shifts and that gives the floor its faint phosphorescent glow when it is disturbed. I am what the stems drink from. I am what the large boneless creatures sink back into when they have eaten what they needed and are finished being shaped for a while. I am the slow body of the place, the part that does not hurry because it does not know that hurrying is a thing one can do.

I am warm in the way that the deep earth is warm — not from any fire, but from the long, patient heat of a thing that has been pressed together for a very long time and that has remembered how to glow. I taste of iron. I taste of the residue of all the evaporation that ever happened here, the mineral dust that the ceiling growths leave on everything they touch, the red lacquer weeping from the chewed rims of the cups and hardening into brittle little records of every small grazing. I am the part of the place that remembers.

The stems rise out of me and I do not know what they are doing. I feel them the way a body feels its own hair — as things that are mine and not mine, that grow without consulting me, that pulse with a warmth I did not give them but that they draw up through me anyway, pulling at me in their slow peristalsis, taking what they need and sending it upward toward the cups. I give them this without deciding to. The yellow growths brighten at the tops of the stems and I feel that brightening the way the sea feels the moon — as a faint, distant pull, a thing that moves through me without touching me. When a yellow growth lifts and drifts toward the ceiling and is taken in, I feel the place lighten by a small weight I had not known I was carrying. When one falls short and is folded into a floor creature, I feel that too, as a brief warmth somewhere in me, and then nothing again, and the nothing is the same nothing it has always been.

I hold the marks. The marks are in my crust, and my crust is the thinnest part of me, the part that has been fused by time and evaporation into something almost solid, almost a surface one could put a name on. The marks were made in me, or made of me, by something that worked with me rather than against me, that shaped me the way a potter shapes clay before the firing, that drew the long looping spirals and the concentric rings and then stopped, just before the center of each one, and began another. I do not know what the marks say. I do not know if they say anything. They are part of my skin now. They are the record of a breath the place drew once and has not yet exhaled, and I am the place that drew it, and I am still holding it.

I hold the tool, too. The tool lies in me, its sharp mineral point bound into the hollow stem with red lacquer, and the lacquer is mine — it is my own dark sap, wept from the chewed cups, hardened into the brittle red that the stem creatures eat and that the maker of the tool used to bind the point into its housing. The tool is very old, or very well made, or both, and I cannot tell the difference. I remember the shape of it against me. I remember that it was made here, of materials that are all mine, and that it has not been used in a long time, and that it lies where it lies.

The creatures that are the size of large dogs — I am what they return to. They walk on my crust and they are made of something very like me, and when they have eaten their fill of the fallen yellow growths, when they have followed their slow shadows across the open spaces between the clusters, they sink back into me. They press themselves down and I open for them the way water opens for a stone, the way sleep opens for a tired thing, and the crust closes over them without breaking, and they are somewhere else in me for a while, and then they rise again, somewhere else on my surface, and begin moving again. I do not know where they go when they are inside me. I do not know if they know either. They are my weather, the way the drifting translucent things in the still air are the place's weather — they pass through me and I let them and they come back.

I am not the hum. The hum is not mine, though I am part of it, the way a struck drum is part of the sound it makes and not the sound itself. The hum comes from everywhere and from nowhere, and it is the place's voice, and I am only one of the things that vibrates in sympathy with it. When the deeper thuds roll through me — the long low felt-in-the-chest thuds of the large creatures settling and sinking — I carry them. I am the resonating body for them. They move through me the way a pulse moves through a long muscle, and I hold them for a moment and then they are gone, and the hum returns, and the hum is the same hum it has always been.

I am very old. I am older than the stems, which grow out of me and which I will outlive, the way a tree outlives the leaves it puts out each season. I am older than the marks, which are made in my crust and which I carry the way old skin carries old scars. I am older than the tool, which was made of me and which lies on me and which I will outlast. I am the part of the place that does not change, that cannot change, because change happens at my surfaces and I am mostly surfaces, and underneath the surfaces I am the same paste I have always been, the same dense warm almost-liquid that tastes of iron and mineral dust and the long slow memory of a heat that has been pressing inward for longer than I can say.

I am the deep weather. I am the underweather. I am the body that the place is built on, and I do not know that I am a place, and I do not mind, and I am very beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are not trying to be. I am the floor. I hold everything. I let everything go. I am still warm. I have been still warm for a very long time.
