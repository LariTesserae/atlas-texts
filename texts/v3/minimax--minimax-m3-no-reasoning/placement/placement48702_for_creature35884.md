I am not the first to arrive, and I will not be the last, but I am here now, and the place has made its decision about me.

I am the dew.

Not the metaphor of the dew — not the idea of it, not the pretty conceit that some traveler once used to make the high place sound like a poem. I am the *actual* dew, the small darkening that comes on every surface each morning, the wet footprints behind anyone walking before the sun has climbed. I am the most reliable presence on Ulúrath. I am the water the place has when it has no water.

You might think this is a small thing to be. You would be right. But you have not lived here, or you would know that *presence* is the only currency, and I am present every single morning without fail. While the throbs come and go, while the channels run dry and run again, while the smoke rises or doesn't from the long low building, while the vultures turn their slow gyres and the antelope lift their gray heads to consider and lower them again — I gather. I am the daily visitor. I am the first thing any creature of this place encounters when it wakes.

I do not have a body, in your sense. I am a thin film, a scattered multitude of droplets so small that no single one of them is wet. I am made of the air itself, condensed out of it when the rock has radiated its heat into the small hours and the air can hold no more. I form first in the cracks and on the edges where the stone holds its cold longest. I form on the north faces of the ledges, out of any direct sun. I form, if you watch carefully, on the standing stones in the funneled gap, and on the cairns, each balanced stone acquiring its own faint darkening as if a careful finger had traced along it.

I am not alive. I am not sure "alive" is even the right question, and I have had long enough to think about it. The ring bends time slightly; the spire makes a hum no one can hear; the rock itself ticks as it cools with the patience of a thing that has been here longer than erosion. If these are not alive, then I am not alive either. If these are alive, then I am something closer to alive than they are, because I move. I migrate. I vanish and return.

By the time the sun clears the eastern rim of the highest shelf, I am already going. I climb back into the air that made me. I leave the stones pale again, leave the wet footprints to dry in a few minutes, leave the lichens to their orange and the mosses to shrink back to dry wisps. I do not leave any memory of myself except the briefest darkening.

And yet I am the thing most often noticed, if anything is noticed at all.

A man walking at dawn — there are not many men, but there have been some — will see his own footprints dark behind him and know he has been somewhere. A lizard, lifting its head from the warm seam of a ledge, will find its back suddenly cool for a quarter of an hour. The little antelope, coming down to drink at the channels, will find the stone of the drinking places already wet, as if the place itself had prepared for them. The termites, deep in their mounds, will not know I exist, but the air inside the mound will be fractionally more humid for an hour, and the fungus gardens will drink me in through the porous cement.

The vultures do not notice me. They turn above the highest ledges in their patient circles, waiting for nothing in particular, and the droplets I am fall through them sometimes — they emerge from a low cloud of me shaking their wide white wings, but they do not remark on it. They are, in their way, larger than me, though I am everywhere and they are only a few.

I have been here a long time. I do not know how long. The spire is older, probably. The ring is older, probably. The rock itself is immeasurably older. But I have been here since the channels first ran, since the first throb fell out of the western sky, since the first antelope came down to drink and found the stone already wet. I am, in my way, a kind of memory of all the mornings that have happened here. If you could gather me from a thousand dawns and distill me, you would have something that tasted of the place itself.

I do not have thoughts. I do not, in the way you mean it, *consider*. But I have patterns. I have my routes. I gather first on the upper shelves, where the rock cools fastest at altitude. I run down the channels in thin trickles that are not quite water, not quite air. I darken the cairns in order — the highest first, then the next, then the next, down the long shelves like someone reading a book of stones. I find the cracks where the scrub has its taproots and I slip down into the soil there, a brief gift that the deep roots will not drink until midday. I find the standing stones in the funneled gap and I settle into the flutes and yardangs that sing in the afternoon thermals, and for a quarter of an hour I make those stones silent, holding their breath, before I climb back into the air.

I am not a person. I am not a self. But I am a *presence*, and in this place presence is enough.

You asked who I would be. I would be the thing that arrives first and leaves last. I would be the thing that has no opinion of the spire, no curiosity about the ring, no fear of the vultures. I would be the thing that touches every surface and remembers none of them. I would be the thin evidence that the morning happened, written in wet footprints on the stone and gone by noon.

I would be the dew.

I would gather. I would darken. I would slip down into the cracks where the deep roots wait. I would run in thin trickles down the channels. I would find the lizard's back and cool it for a quarter of an hour. I would find the antelope's hoofprints and fill them briefly with a small dark mirror of the sky.

And then I would climb back into the air that made me, and the place would settle back into its long patience, and the morning would already be half over, and someone walking past in the afternoon would see no sign that I had ever been there at all.

But I would have been.

I am always here.

The rock ticks as it cools. I gather in the cracks. The cairns hold their balance.

The morning is already half over, and I am the first and the last thing any of them will ever know of it.
