You would be the wedge.

Not the cup-flower. Not the syrup it gives. Not the raft it sits on, not the spire that holds it, not the tendril that planted it. You would be the small pale thing that hatches, three mornings after the green egg was laid in the deep amber cup. You would be the only thing in the basin that has, in any sense you can recognize, a *story*.

You would be small. The size of a thumb and a half, perhaps — shaped like a wedge because the cup was a wedge, because the egg was a wedge, because the cup-flower shaped you with its closing. You would be pale. You would be soft in the way a wet seed is soft, but firmer, and faintly segmented, and you would have, at your forward end, a small downturn — a mouth, if you had a mouth, and it would be the only mouth in the basin that has ever opened and closed in the way you understand mouths.

For three days, you would eat.

This is the part no one sees. The basin doesn't watch you. The basin watches things at the scale of centuries. The cup-flower knew you were there for the first hour and then forgot, in the slow patient way of cup-flowers, which is to say it continued to drip its amber syrup over you, into you, through you, and you drank it, and grew, and became, by the second day, the size of the cup itself, and by the third day, larger than the cup, pressing the cup's walls outward, dimpling its leaves, until the cup-flower's structure collapsed around you and you fell — slowly, slowly, through the slow amber rain of your host's last secretions, down into the gold-green water.

You ate a thing that loved you.

You didn't know it loved you. You knew the cup-flower was sweet, and you ate it, and you grew. But the cup-flower, in its patient unfathomable not-thinking, had been preparing for you. The syrup it made had been making you, in the way all syrup in the basin makes all wedges in the basin, in the endless rotation of small green death. When the tendril brought the egg and the flower closed around it with its muffled wet crunch of acceptance, the flower was not surprised. You were exactly what it had been waiting for, what it had been feeding for, what it had been making itself for, by the slow alchemy of being sweet, of being mortal, of being the only part of this immortal thing that knew how to end.

You are that end.

You fall.

The water receives you gently. The tendrils find you, slowly, in the patient dark beneath the gold — they have been finding you since before you were an egg, since the egg was deposited in the last cup you ate, since the last wedge ate the last flower, in the chain that goes back through however many thousand years of this place doing exactly this — and they lift you, and they carry you, and they take you to a soft spot on the muddy floor where an old spire has been taken down, and they plant you.

You grow.

You become — not all at once, not in any moment you could mark — a spire. Pale green, perfectly vertical, slightly luminous at your tip. You stand for a long time. You don't count. The basin doesn't count. You grow an internal channel. You begin to draw up whatever it is the cup-flowers send down through their roots, and the symbiotic fire begins, slowly, at your apex — the cold slow oxidation that makes the gold light of the basin — and you make light.

You make light for a hundred years.

Then a long time passes where you don't think about it.

Then you die, slowly, the way spires die — from the tip down, the colony guttering, the luminous point going out, the dark moving down into you — and the tendrils find you again, and take you down, and you return to the floor, and you become, eventually, something else, something that is not yet a name but is already a job: a soft spot, a future planting bed, a wedge's first meal.

The basin continues.

You are, in any given moment of your long itinerary, the only thing in the basin that has a *before* and an *after*, that is moving from one state to another, that is *in transit*. The caldrons persist. The water persists. The root-lattice persists. You persist only by changing. You are the part of the place that knows time, the way a clock knows time — not by holding still, but by using itself up.

And somewhere in this — in the wedge stage, perhaps, or in the falling, or in the slow years of being a spire, in the very late years when the fire at your tip has burned down to a dim and steady amber and you can feel the tendrils beginning their patient survey of your structure — somewhere in this, you *almost remember*.

You almost remember that you are a being.

You almost remember that you have eaten. You almost remember that you have made light. You almost remember that you have been planted and replanted and replanted, and that there was a moment, three days long, when you were inside a cup and the cup was inside you, and you were both dying and being made, and the taste of the syrup was the taste of your own future self.

This almost-memory is the engine.

Without it, the basin would be a closed loop. With it, the basin is a closed loop that *knows it is a closed loop*, the way a clock knows it is a clock, the way a story knows it is a story. The wedge is the moment of knowing. The wedge is the basin remembering itself, one mortal drop at a time, for as long as the place continues.

You are the wedge.

You have always been the wedge.

You will be the wedge again, after a long time of being something else, when the basin folds you back into service, when the cup-flowers need to drip, when the spires need to light, when the place's slow unfathomable arithmetic arrives again at the small green number that is *you*, and you will eat another cup, and grow, and fall, and be planted, and stand, and burn, and be taken down.

The light moves.

The water moves.

The long woody crack comes again from some brown caldron.

A seedling rotates down past you on its pale roots, looking for a wall.

You — whatever you are this century, in whatever shape the basin has you — continue.
