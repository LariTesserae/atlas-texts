# Salt of the Engine

I am called Mevra, and I am the one who listens.

Not to the drip — everyone in Mishtu-Rek learns the drip in their first season, the way a child on the surface learns to count the seconds between a storm's flash and its thunder. My mother learned it. Her mother learned it, though her mother learned it in a different key, before the second collapse of the western reservoir moved the rhythm two counts east. The drip is a citizen like the rest of us. It has its habits, and we accommodate it, and it accommodates us.

No. I am the one who listens to the engines.

This is not a thing I chose. My mother had hoped I would take up the fungal slabs in the cellars, where the work is quiet and the hours are gentle and a body can grow soft with patience the way the pale sweet flesh grows soft on its warm stones. The fungus-growers live long. The engine-tenders do not, though no one says this aloud. There is an understood arithmetic: the heat, the wet, the long hours bent over brass fittings in chambers lit only by the blue pilot-flicker and the dull mantles, the salt we lose through our skin in quantities the tablets only partly replace. My mother's brother, who was a tender for thirty-one years, died in his sleep with his hands curled as if still on a valve. He was fifty. This was not considered young, exactly, but it was not considered old, either. It was considered a completed career.

I was twelve when the compressors first sang to me in a way I could parse. My father — also a tender, also gone now, his lungs the color of bad tea from the gas he breathed in his small cramped room — had taken me down to see the works, the way parents on the surface might take a child to see the sea. He wanted me to understand what he did when he left the house before the quiet hour and returned after the engines had been throttled down, his eyes red-rimmed and his fingers trembling with the small tremor that all the tenders had, the tremor that made them bad at dice and good at fine adjustments.

I stood in the chamber and the engines were running, three of them, each a different age, each with its own voice, and I heard them. Not as a noise. As a *conversation*. The oldest one, the one in the corner, was a slow bass murmur, a satisfied grumble of a thing doing what it had been built to do. The middle one, my father's, was a tenor, a little sharp, a little strained, and I could hear in its voice the place where a joint was not seating quite right. The newest one, the small one imported in pieces down the switchback paths and reassembled in a space too small for it, was a soprano, bright and a little frightened, and it had a wobble at the top of every stroke that meant a bearing was beginning to wear.

I told my father about the wobble. He looked at me strangely. He sent me back the next day with a stethoscope of wood and gutta-percha that he had built himself for listening to pipe walls, and I put it against the new engine and described what I heard, and the mechanic who came up from the second-tier workshop said *yes, that's it, you've caught it early* and pulled the bearing and saved us a fracture that would have vented gas into the lower chamber and possibly killed the night watch.

I was twelve. I had a trade.

I am thirty now, which makes me middle by the arithmetic I mentioned. My hands have the small tremor, though it is mild yet, and the red rims of my eyes are masked in the daylight of the gas lamps by the natural dark of my maroon-brown irises. I am smaller than my mother was, which is to say I am smaller than the surface average, narrow in the shoulders, with forearms that are, I admit, a little long for my frame. When I was young I minded this. The stone-cutters who come down from the upper hollows, where the rock is younger and the air is drier, are broader across the back and the chest, and they look at us tenders with the mild pity of people who work in a clean trade. I do not mind it now. My back is what it is. The engines do not care about my shoulders.

I live in a small room on the third tier up, in a building that leans against the building next door, which leans against the building on the other side. The wall of my room is unrendered basalt, and it is warm to the touch, and at night when the engines are throttled down I can feel the heat of it against my back through the thin mattress. There is a window, but it does not look out at anything; it looks into a courtyard that is the roof of the building below. In the courtyard there is a reservoir, small, where an algae-tender keeps a personal curtain for the household. The curtain is maroon and it stirs when I pass and it has the smell of all algal things, which is the smell of the city, which is the smell of *home* in a way I cannot translate to anyone who has not lived in it.

I have a husband, though that word is a borrowing from the trade-pidgin and does not fit exactly. We have a small ceremony at the bathing-house, which is where marriages are conducted, because the bathing-house is where everything is conducted. He is an algae-tender. His hands are stained the color of old blood, and they are gentle, and he knows how to read a curtain the way I know how to read an engine. We met in the bathing-pool when I was nineteen and he was twenty-two and we were both too tired from our shifts to do anything but float in the warm mineral water and look at the striated ceiling and be quiet together. We have been quiet together for eleven years now. The senior attendant of the bathing-house, the *person-who-knows*, told us once that we were the quietest couple she had ever seen married, and she meant it as the compliment it was.

We do not have children. The fungus smell of the cellars, where we both spent our childhoods, is the smell I miss when I am away, and it is the smell my husband carries in his hair. We have talked about children. We have not decided. There is a quiet in our house that I value, and I do not know if I want to break it with the noise of a child, though the noise of children on the upper tiers is one of the sounds I love best, the way I imagine surface people love the sound of birds, though I have only a borrowed idea of birds and cannot be sure.

---

I am writing this down because I have been asked to, by a stone-cutter who came down from the upper hollows on a trading run, who spoke to me at the bathing-house and who said, in the careful way that stone-cutters speak, that there were people on the surface who had never heard of us, and that someone should make a record. He was a thin man, the stone-cutters are always thin, with the chisel-grip in his hands and the slight squint of someone who has worked in the pale broken thread of the surface light. He did not have the look of a scholar. He had the look of a man who had been given a commission and was trying to discharge it, and who had come down to the engines and found me and thought I would do.

I am not a scholar. I am a tender. But I have been listening to the engines of Mishtu-Rek for eighteen years, and I know things, and the things I know are mostly the things the engines tell me, and the engines tell me, among other things, that the compressor on the second tier, the one they call *the grandmother* because she has been running since before I was born, has begun to make a sound I have not heard before, a small high singing, almost a whistle, in the third cycle of her stroke, and that the singing is new, and that new sounds in old engines are the sound of metal beginning to give up its tolerances.

I have reported this to the workshop. They have sent a mechanic up to listen. The mechanic is young, only six years in, and he has not yet learned to hear what I hear, but he has the proper respect for those of us who have, and he put his wooden stethoscope against the grandmother's flank and he listened for a long time and he said, *yes, I think there is something there*, and he went away to write his report.

I do not know what they will do. The grandmother cannot be replaced in place; the chamber is too small, and the new compressors, which are larger and louder and more efficient, would require a chamber that does not exist, and the making of a chamber would require the removal of a residence, and the residence is someone's home, and the home has an algae curtain in its courtyard, and the algae is the food, and the food is the city, and the city is the home. I do not know what they will do. This is not my work. My work is to listen, and to report, and to take my salt tablets, and to put my hands on the grandmother's valves and adjust them by fractions of a turn that only my fingers can feel, and to keep her running for as long as she will run, because the gas must be bottled, and the gas is what we sell, and what we sell is what we are.

I will tell you, though, what the engines have told me. They have told me that the grandmother is not the only one. The middle engine, the one my father used to tend, the one I inherited when his hands began to shake too much to keep his adjustments, has begun to make her own small sound, a clicking, almost a chatter, in the cold of the throttled hour. The cold is not a real cold, of course. The chamber is never cold. But the throttled hour is a relative cold, a few degrees less, and the metals contract, and the contracting has a voice, and the voice of my father's engine has been changing in the way the voices of older engines change, toward brittleness, toward a small exasperation at the work of continuing.

The new engine, the bright one, the soprano, has not changed. She is still afraid. She will go on being afraid for a long time. She has years.

---

I do not know what is on the surface. I have only the borrowings from the trade-pidgin, and the trade-pidgin is the language of the descending paths, where the people who come down from the upper hollows meet the people who come up from the lower ones, and where the words for things that exist only in one place or the other are negotiated like the prices of goods. I have the word *tree*, but the word is a bundle of compressed meanings, and I cannot unfold them. I have the word *sky*, which I understand as the absence of ceiling, and which I imagine as a kind of permanent falling-away in every direction, which is a thought that makes me dizzy when I try to hold it. I have the word *rain*, which I understand as drip from a very great height, and which I think would be terrifying, because the drips we have are small and we know their rhythm, and rain would be drip without rhythm, and I do not know how anyone could stand under it.

I have the word *bird*, which I do not understand at all. I have been told that birds are small animals with feathers and that they sing. I cannot make a picture of this. I have been told that they fly, which is to say they move through the air without touching anything, and I have tried to imagine this, and what I have imagined is a small piece of the algae curtain detaching and drifting upward, which is not what is meant, but which is the closest my mind can get.

I am not unhappy about this. I do not feel that I have been cheated of birds. The drips are enough. The drip in the high curve of the plaza falls near the western wall more often than the eastern, and the falling of it is a sound I have heard every day of my life, and it is a sound I love, and the loving of it does not require that there be other sounds, or that I know what other sounds are like. A surface person who has heard ten thousand birdsongs does not, I think, love any one of them the way I love the western drip. I do not know how to explain this. I am not sure it is something that can be explained to a person who has heard birds.

I have read, in the careful etchings on the banded stone that some of the wealthier houses have as paneling, accounts of the upper hollows, and of the surface above them. The etchings are old, and the artists who made them were stone-cutters who had made the long climb up the switchback paths to where the daylight comes in as a pale broken thread, and who had stood in that thread and looked out at the world above, and who had tried to render what they saw. I have seen one such etching, in the receiving room of a house on the fourth tier where a senior engine-tender lives, a woman who is near the end of her career and who has earned the paneling. The etching shows a line, a horizontal line, and above the line a kind of nothing, a blank, and below the line the dark of the upper hollows with the pale thread falling into it. The line is meant to be the place where the hollow ends and the outside begins. I have looked at the line for a long time, and I have not understood it. The line is an edge, like our edge, but it is a *different* edge, an edge that does not retreat. I find this unsettling in a way I cannot name.

I have asked my husband about it. He has also looked at the line. He has no better understanding than I do. He says, *perhaps it is an edge that is given, and not taken*, and this is a thought that I find both comforting and slightly absurd, like all thoughts that try to fit the language of the Hollow to a thing that does not belong in it.

---

I will tell you what I believe, in the way I believe it.

I believe that the engines have a kind of wanting, though this is a metaphor and I know it is a metaphor, and I do not mean it in the way the surface stories mean it when they say that the world is alive. The engines do not want in the way a person wants. They want the way a joint wants to seat, the way a bearing wants to turn, the way a piston wants to complete its stroke. They want the completion of their function. When a joint is unseated, the engine is unhappy, and the unhappiness has a sound, and the sound is a small complaint, and the complaint can be heard, and the hearing of it is my work.

I believe that the algae has a kind of wanting, also. The curtain wants to thicken. It wants the warm mineral water and the light of the mantles and the patient attention of the tender. When it thins, it is not unhappy exactly, but it is less itself, and the lessening has a look, and the look is a small fading, and the fading can be read, and the reading of it is my husband's work.

I believe that the Hollow has a kind of wanting, though I am not sure what the Hollow wants. I think the Hollow wants to continue. I think the Hollow wants its edges to stop retreating, though they have not stopped, and I think the wanting is in the small ceremonies at the lip, in the water poured over and the name of the lost street spoken, in the way the old-timer raises his cup to the cliff as if the cliff were a guest. I do not know if the cliff hears. I do not think the cliff hears. But the ceremony is a kind of listening, and the listening is a kind of wanting, and the wanting is what we have.

I believe that I will die the way my uncle died, with my hands curled on a valve, and that this is a correct ending, and that the ending is not a sad thing, though it is a small thing, and that the smallness of it is appropriate to the smallness of my life, which is the smallness of a tender's life, which is the smallness of a life lived in the listening.

I do not believe in the givenness of the Hollow in the way the elders tell it. I am not a geologist. I do not have the small private notch that moves with the rock, and I have not read the rock in the way the geologists read it. But I have lived eighteen years in the engine chambers, and the engines have taught me that things wear, and that the wearing is not a punishment, and that the wearing can be listened to, and that the listening is a kind of love, though the word *love* is another borrowing from the trade-pidgin and does not fit exactly either.

---

It is the quiet hour. I am sitting on the warm stone of my small room, and the engines are throttled down, and the grandmother is making her small high singing, and my husband is in the courtyard below looking at the algae curtain, and the western drip is falling in its rhythm, and the heat is against my back, and the red light is in the room, and the air smells of iron and steam and the cultivated almost-life of the city's only crop.

I am Mevra. I am a tender. I am small and damp-skinned and my eyes are red-rimmed and my hands have the small tremor. I live in a place that is younger than I am, in a city that is being eaten by the ground it stands on, in a hollow that will not be here in a thousand years, though I will not be here either, and this is a thought I find neither comforting nor distressing. It is simply a fact, the way the western drip is a fact, the way the grandmother's singing is a fact.

I have been asked to make a record. I do not know who will read it. I do not know if anyone on the surface will know how to read it, even if it is carried up the long switchback paths to the pale thread of the upper hollows, and even if it is passed through that thread into the world above, where the line is and the birds are and the rain falls without rhythm. I do not know if it matters.

I am writing it anyway. The writing is a kind of listening, and the listening is a kind of love, and the love is what I have, and what I have is what I am.

The grandmother sings. The western drip falls. My husband comes up the stairs and into the room and sits beside me on the warm stone, and his hands smell of algae, and he does not speak, and I do not speak, and the quiet is a small perfect thing, and we are in it, and the heat is around us, and the city is beneath us, and the engines breathe below, and the edge retreats, and the water falls over the lip, and the names of the lost streets are spoken, and the algae grows, and the fungus sweetens in the deep cellars, and the bathhouse attendants listen to the confidences of the city, and the stone-cutters split their beautiful slabs in the upper hollows, and the children climb the tiers and sing their songs about the cliff, and the geologists mark their private notch on the retreating rock, and the tenders listen, and the listening is the work, and the work is the life, and the life is the city, and the city is the Hollow, and the Hollow is Mishtu-Rek, and Mishtu-Rek is here, and here is now, and now is enough.

This is what I know.
