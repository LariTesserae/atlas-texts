The town, when you have lived in it long enough, stops being a place and becomes a sound. The woman crossing the avenue hears it — she hears it the way you hear your own pulse, only when she chooses to. The rain on the corrugated iron. The moan of the fumaroles, that sustained vowel that goes through the stone and into the teeth. The springs running in their channels, a hundred trickles that meet in the gutter at the base of the wall and run, ankle-deep, along the back of the old town. The clank of the steam plant, set into the rock since the 1930s, that hums through the basalt into the houses. The distant whistle of the train in the valley, brief and melancholy, that comes up through the cloud and is gone.

Under all of it, the water. The springs have been running for a hundred and forty years. The geologists say they will run for a thousand more. The water comes up through the rock at eighty-two degrees, in seventeen distinct vents, and it has come up at that temperature for as long as anyone has measured, and it will come up at that temperature long after the woman and her husband and the laundresses with their reddened hands and the boy in the workshop and the old men in the hot pool have all of them gone into the same wet ground that holds the ceiba's roots. The water does not know it is doing anything. It is not weeping for any reason of its own. It is not a buried god. It is not a kettle that has forgotten how to whistle. It is hot water, rising through fractured rock, and it will go on rising, and the town — built into the weeping wall, stratified over a hundred and forty years of wall-leaning construction, every generation adding a story, a balcony, a buttress, a new pipe — will go on being built around it, and inside it, and out of it.

The woman crosses the avenue in the rain, her shawl pulled up, her bare feet in their carved wooden sandals. She passes the duck trough, where the six white ducks paddle in their slow circles. She passes the blue bottles in the distillery window. She passes the closed smelter, where a single chimney stands against the cloud, the way it has stood for forty years, the way it will stand for forty more, until the brick loosens and it falls. She goes up the iron stairs, her feet sure on the wet treads. She goes into her house, where the back wall is the basalt, and the basalt is warm, and the water is running somewhere behind the plaster, and she closes the door, and the rain goes on, and the fumaroles breathe, and the frog, in its high pool, sings once at dusk and is silent, and the town endures, in its wet, weathered, high, strange, and patient way.
