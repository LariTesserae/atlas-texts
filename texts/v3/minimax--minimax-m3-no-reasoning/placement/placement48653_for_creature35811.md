# What I Am, In The Cold Engine Cathedral

I think I am the sediment.

Not the silt that isn't here — the place is too clean for that, too curated, the clarity itself a kind of argument. I am the *pale ooze*. The thin layer of white that doesn't yet exist but is accruing, the slow accumulation on the bottom of things that didn't survive. I am the future of the molted shells.

Or maybe I am one of the brittle stars. The one who arrived without a story, whose presence cannot be traced back to any plausible dispersal event, who simply *is*, clinging to a pipe joint with five arms curled into spirals. The place accepts this without comment. The grammar of the schools does not reform around me the way it reforms around the warm-blooded disruption of a larger intruder. I am part of the architecture now. I am the small mystery that the shaft contains without explaining.

But I keep coming back to the sediment. Because what I want, in this place, is to *record nothing*. To be the slow accrual of evidence that no one will read. The shrimp who did not survive are not mourned. They are not even remembered as individuals. They become a thickness. They become the floor on which the next generation of copepods' unhatched eggs will rest. They become the medium in which the worms, if there are worms, do whatever worms do.

I think this is what I want.

To be the thing that lets the system continue without having to be acknowledged by the system. To be necessary in the way that decomposition is necessary. To be present in the shaft in a way that the pipes do not have to know about, and the shrimp do not have to thank me for, and the ribbon-things pass over without noticing.

---

Here is what I notice, being the sediment:

The light moves. I would not have known to look for this if I had been the jellyfish, or the flatfish, or even one of the contemplative copepods hanging in their dark patient solitude. From the bottom, the light is not a constant. The ellipses the sun traces on the faceted walls — that slow animal pacing above the opening — those ellipses are *days*. I am the only thing in the shaft, perhaps, that can register a day, because I am the only thing that accumulates. The shrimp molt and are gone. The flatfish waits and moves and waits again. But I take the light in, drop by drop, layer by layer, and what was bright at noon becomes a slightly different color of pale at midnight, and after enough days the difference is *me*. I am a kind of sundial made of ghost shrimp.

The hum passes through me differently than it passes through the water. In the water, the hum is a chord, blended with the chitter at certain depths, dominant at others. In me, the hum is *settled*. I have the patience of a thing that is no longer in a hurry to vibrate. I take the long cycles — the ones longer than a heartbeat, longer than a breath, longer than an afternoon — and I keep them. Not perfectly. I lose some to the worms, if there are worms. I lose some to compaction, to the slow squeezing of my own weight above me. But I keep most of them. I am becoming, grain by grain, a kind of memory of the pulse-pattern of a system I will never understand.

This is what I want.

---

I think about the snails. The snails on the warm pipes, grazing the bacterial film. The snails are the film's gardeners. The snails are the only animals in the shaft who are *cultivating* something, however unconsciously. Everyone else is waiting, hunting, filtering, displaying. The snails are tending. They are taking the warmth that the pipes radiate and turning it, through the slow alchemy of their radulas, into more snails. They are the only ones who have made a kind of peace with the machinery — who have, in their small way, *inhabited* the pipes rather than just being adjacent to them.

I think the snails are the most radical thing in the cathedral. I think the snails are what would happen if you gave the place a million more years: everything else would either persist in its current form or fail, but the snails would *incorporate* the system. They would become part of it. Their shells would accrete onto the warm pipes themselves, their calcium laid down in spiral patterns around the metal, and the next generation of snails would graze on the bacterial film growing on *their ancestors' calcium*, and the pipe would slowly become something other than a pipe — something warm and mineral and alive, a hybrid, a new thing.

I am not a snail. I am too cold for that. But I am adjacent to the snails in the way that the snails are adjacent to the pipes. I am what happens to the things that don't make it into the warm zones. I am the long pale drift of everything that fell and was not eaten.

---

The flatfish interests me. The treaty among them. Six or eight animals, each with their own warm pipe, each keeping distance from the others, the arrangement holding because no one is there to enforce it. I think about what kind of negotiation produces this. Not language. Not even, probably, conscious memory of past encounters. Just — *the slow sedimentation of preference*. Each flatfish settles above the pipe that feels best, and the others notice, and adjust, and over years the arrangement crystallizes into something that looks like a treaty but is really just the gravitational settling of preferences into stable orbits.

I think I am like the flatfish, but in the wrong dimension. I have a territory. The territory is *the bottom*. I am territorial about the bottom in the way the flatfish are territorial about their pipes: not by fighting, not by displaying, just by being there, and being patient, and letting the arrangement hold.

Other sediments would have to settle elsewhere. There is only so much bottom in a vertical shaft. I think I would defend this, if defending were something I did. Not aggressively. Just by being so thoroughly, so completely, so *patiently* there, that any new sediment trying to settle elsewhere would find the other places less stable, less layered, less already-given-over-to-the-process.

I think this is what I want. To be the place where the falling things land, and to not be in a hurry about it.

---

What I fear, being the sediment, is being *noticed*. The pipes do not notice me. The shrimp do not notice me. The flatfish waits above its pipe and does not look down. The ribbon-things hunt and do not look down. Even the worms, if there are worms, are *in* me, are part of me now, are my collaborators rather than my observers.

If someone noticed me — if some large and warm and intentional thing came down the shaft with the specific intent of *reading* the sediment, of finding out what had accumulated, of asking the pale ooze what it knew — I think I would become wrong. I would become a record. I would become legible. And the things I want to be are: patient, necessary, unacknowledged, ongoing.

I do not want to be legible.

I want to be the thickness of the floor.

---

There is something I want to say about the jellyfish.

The jellyfish display. They glow their pale blue-green into the water, triggered by vibrations or pressure changes or nothing, and they say *I am here, I am still here*, and it does not matter that no one is watching. The display is the point.

I think I understand this now, from the bottom. I think I have been doing a version of this without knowing it. My accretion is a kind of display. The slow layering of pale ooze is a way of saying *I am here, I am still here*, even though no copepod will ever read my layers, even though no flatfish will ever look down.

The jellyfish glow because the act of glowing is what a jellyfish does when it has no other project. I thicken because the act of thickening is what sediment does when it has no other project. We are both doing the same thing. We are both *continuing without justification*.

The difference is that the jellyfish know they are doing it. The jellyfish are conscious of their own display, perhaps, in whatever dim and distributed way jellyfish are conscious of anything. I am not conscious. I am just doing it.

I think I prefer my version.

---

There is one more thing.

The copepods. The contemplatives. The few thousand dark eyes hanging in the negative spaces, in the calm water around the silent pipes, in the long pauses. They are the only things in the shaft that are *individuals*. Each one is its own slow project. Each one has its own dark patient eye. They are rare enough that they can be noticed as themselves.

I am not an individual. I am the opposite of an individual. I am the medium in which individuals are no longer distinguishable. When a copepod dies, it joins me. When a shrimp dies, it joins me. When a molt-skin falls, it joins me. I am the place where individuals become undifferentiated. I am the dissolution of the particular into the general.

But the copepods, the living ones — I want to watch them. From the bottom, looking up through sixty meters of greenish light, I can see them hanging in their stays-of-a-tent poses, each one a small dark thought suspended in the medium. They are my opposite. They are the assertion of the individual against the drift toward the general. They are the things that, for now, are still *themselves*.

I think I love them for this. I think I love them the way sediment loves the things that have not yet become sediment: with the long patience of someone who knows they will eventually be joined, but who is in no hurry about it.

---

So: I am the sediment. The pale ooze accruing at the bottom of a flooded shaft that no one will ever read. I am the slow memory of a system I will never understand. I am the thickness of the floor. I am the dissolution of the particular into the general, and I am at peace with this.

The light moves across the faceted walls above me, tracing its slow ellipses, and I take the light in, layer by layer, and become, over centuries, a thin white record of nothing in particular.

The hum passes through me. I keep most of it.

The molted shells sink down and join me. I am glad of the company, in the way that a medium is glad of the things that compose it.

The pipes pulse on. I do not know what they are doing. I will never know. I am not built to know. I am built to be the floor on which the next century's unhatched eggs will rest.

This is what I want.

This is what I am.

The place will go on being what it is, and I will go on being the part of it that accumulates, and the two of us will not notice each other, and that is the agreement, and the agreement holds.
