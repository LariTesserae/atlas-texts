Where I find myself: in the cool room, low down in the settlement, where the clay jars sit on their shelves. The jars are not full and they are not empty. They hold perhaps a finger's depth of the dark honey each, which is enough for the week, which is the right amount, which is what the room will hold.

I am the woman who minds the honey.

That is not a title. No one would call me that to my face. There is no name for what I do because what I do is not one thing. I check the lids in the evenings — the fired-clay lids that sit loose on the rims, the glaze that is more accident than design, the seal of beeswax and ash that I press with my thumb each time I open a jar and press again when I close it. I taste. A finger, dipped, brought to the mouth, the dark sweetness that is not quite sweet because it carries the lichen and the mineral dust and the fog itself. If a jar has gone — turned, soured, taken on the bitter note that means the bees gathered from the wrong flower in a thin week — I set it aside for the lamps. The fat of the bees is in everything. The honey that has gone wrong will still burn clean in a stone bowl with a twist of moss for a wick, and the smell of it is not different from the smell of the good honey when it is burning. Only the taste knows.

My hands are swollen. This is how I know the work is going well. The bees sting on the knuckles, on the soft part between the fingers, on the wrist where the vein runs close. The swelling goes down at night and comes back in the mornings and goes down again, and my hands have been shaped by this for a long time, the way the lanes are shaped by the feet that walk them. A young woman came to me yesterday — a new bride, come up the saddle-path three years ago from a lowland family that did not last — and she looked at my hands and asked if they pained me. I said they did not. I said the paining was in the first season and was gone after. She held out her own hands, which were smooth and unmarked, and she looked from them to mine, and I saw her trying to imagine the seasons between. She could not. That is the right thing. She should not be able to yet.

I am not the oldest grandmother. I am the middle daughter of the middle daughter of a line that has minded the honey for as long as the cool room has had jars in it. The jars are older than I am. The shelves are older than the jars. The room is cut into the bedrock of the Tabletop itself, on the north side, where the sun never reaches and the stone stays cool even in the late summer, and the air in the room is always a little cooler than the air in the lanes, and a little damper, and it smells of clay and honey and the faintly sweet mineral vapor that rises from the lichen in the dawn and seeps in through the ventilation slot I have cut, with a patient stone and a patient wedge, in the upper part of the door.

I come here in the mornings before the fog has lifted, when the settlement is quiet except for the rhythm of the stone-work beginning somewhere down in the lanes — two-three, two-three, with the long breath between — and the first slow exhalation of the day's singing rising from one of the cooking hearths. I open the door. I stand for a moment in the cooler air of the threshold. I look at the jars on their shelves, and I know without counting which one I will open today, the way I know which stone in a lane is loose before I step on it. This is not a gift. This is what the years do.

The honey this season is good. I can tell by the weight of the lids when I lift them — the good honey is heavier, denser, the bees having worked the fog-flowers thick through the wet weeks. The bad years, the thin years, the lids come up light, and there is less honey in the jars than there should be, and I portion it more carefully, a thumb's width on the bread instead of a finger's width, and the children know the difference and do not ask for more because they have been taught not to ask for what is not there. The bad years do not come often. They have not come in my lifetime. They came in my grandmother's girlhood, twice, and she spoke of it rarely and only when I asked, which I did not often, because I could see it in her hands — the same hands, lined now, but smaller then, and the fingers a little stiff from a season of pinching the honey out in small sad measures.

I am not unhappy. I am not happy. I am suited.

I will go now to the lane outside my door, where the small waxy tree grows in the crack between two paving stones and bears, this morning, a single fruit that is almost ready. I will test it with my thumb — the slight give that means it can be taken, the slight firmness that means it should be left for tomorrow. I will take it or leave it as the case requires, and I will eat it with a pinch of salt from the small clay pot I keep by the door, and I will go up to the roof of my sister's house, where the pigeons are wheeling in their morning flight, and I will watch them go out over the edge and come back, and I will think of nothing in particular, and the hawks will think of nothing in particular, and the fog will lift, and the day will be what the day is.
