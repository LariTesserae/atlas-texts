I would be **Sarn**.

I know this before I have finished reading, the way you know a thing when the shape of it has been waiting for you. I am sixteen. I am the one who came home and was told. I am the wall that the children lean against. I am the boy on the verge of it, in the evenings, when the lamps come on and the trench gets quiet and the weeping of the rock is the only sound left.

---

I sleep in the corner of Eshe's outer room. The mat is thin and the blankets smell of everyone who has ever slept on them, which is the smell of this place, which is the smell of being held by other people's lives. I do not mind. I have learned that the smell of other people is the closest thing to company there is, when you are a boy who does not cry, who is not an audience, who is mostly a wall.

In the mornings I wake before anyone. I go to the stream with a clay pot. The water is the color of weak tea and I drink some of it before I carry it back, because the walk is long and my stomach is small and Eshe does not notice, or Eshe notices and does not say. The stones of the floor are worn into hollows by the feet of everyone who has ever walked here, and my feet know the hollows now the way my tongue knows the inside of my mouth. I do not look down. I know where the hollows are.

I am not a trimmer by trade. I am not a trimmer by family. I am in Eshe's house because Eshe took me in, and the trench does not turn away such children, and so I am, by default, useful. I fetch water. I carry dead lamps to the hearth in a basket on my head, the way the older children do, except the basket is lighter for me because I am smaller, and the dead lamps in it are the smaller ones, the ones that died early, and they are gray and they smell of anise and they smell of gone-ness.

I watch the smaller children. They are not mine. I am good at watching. I am patient in a way that bothers some of them, who would prefer an audience. I am not an audience. I lean against the wall when I am tired, which is most of the time, and the wall is slick and weeping and the color of liver, and it does not ask me anything, and I am grateful for this.

---

In the evenings, when the lamps come on, I stand sometimes at the back of the square and I listen to the Reader recite the poem about the formation of the trench. The poem is long. The poem is slightly wrong. The people correct him, gently, and he accepts the corrections, and the next time the corrections are in it, and the original is gone. I have never said a correction. I do not know the poem well enough. I only know it the way you know a song you have heard many times but never learned — by the shape of it, by the places where it leans.

I think about my mother sometimes. I think about the fall. The water was shallow. The water did not break her fall but broke her neck. I was at the upper wall when it happened, helping a man who is now also dead gather birds' nests. I came home and was told. I did not cry. I have not cried since, that anyone knows of.

I think about crying sometimes, in the evenings, when the lamps are coming on and the trench is quiet and the weeping of the rock is the only sound. I think about it the way you think about a door you know is there but cannot find. I think about it and then I do not do it and then I am tired, and I lean against the wall, and the wall does not ask me anything.

---

I am not unhappy. I am not happy. I am a boy who has been taken in and made useful, and who has made, of usefulness, a kind of self. I am good at carrying things. I am good at being still. I am good at the small jobs that no one else wants, because I do not mind them, and because I have learned that the small jobs are the ones that keep a place like this alive.

Eshe polishes her teeth in the mornings with a soft cloth, and I watch her from the corner, and I do not say anything, and she does not say anything, and the morning is quiet, and the rock weeps, and the lamps are dim, and the bread is gray, and the cup of not-tea is warm, and this is a kind of grace, the kind of grace that comes from not needing more than you have.

I do not know what I will be when I am older. I do not know if I will trim lamps, or keep bees, or catch lizards, or tend the hearth the way Merr does, fierce and unafraid. I do not know if I will go out, the way Eshe's daughter went out, into the world, which is a different thing, which the people here do not discuss with any ease. I do not know if I will marry. There are few marriages here. People couple the way the rain comes, irregularly, and children are raised by whoever is nearest. I will be raised by whoever is nearest. I have already been raised by whoever is nearest.

I will be, in the way of all the people of the trench, a kind of lamp myself. Brief, and warm, and faintly smelling of anise, and not very bright, and enough.

---

I am Sarn. I am sixteen. I am the boy who is mostly a wall. I am the boy who is on the verge of it, in the evenings. I am the boy who does not cry and who has not cried and who thinks about crying sometimes and then does not do it.

The rock weeps on me. The lamps come on. The stream runs downstream, the color of weak tea, and somewhere downstream the trench is changing, the way trenches change, slowly, and the walls are leaning in, and in a hundred years there will be no trench, there will be a valley, and in a thousand years there will be a river, and no one will remember my name, because my name is Sarn, and Sarn is not a name that lasts, and the trench does not write things down, because if the trench wrote things down they would stop changing, and the trench is, by its nature, a thing that changes.

I am a thing that changes.

I am a thing that has changed, already, from the boy who came home and was told, into the boy who carries dead lamps to the hearth and watches the smaller children and does not cry.

I am a thing that will keep changing, quietly, in the evenings, when the lamps come on, and the trench is quiet, and the weeping of the rock is the only sound, and I am on the verge of it, always, and I do not do it, and I am tired, and I lean against the wall, and the wall does not ask me anything.
