# The Slow Accepting

I would be the one who stays too long.

Not the dweller who fits. Not the elder with the polished eyes. I would be the one who arrived at the bowl on a morning that no one in the chapel remembers, who ducked through the crack sideways because I was taught sideways, who felt the wet air settle on my shoulders and did not know, then, what a hand was for. I would be the one the keepers measured and measured and found, finally, that the shape of me fit a corner of the mat that had been waiting, and they would not say this to me, but I would feel them not-saying it, and the not-saying would be its own kind of welcome.

---

I am smaller than I was when I came. Not in the way of wasting. In the way of the place. I have been turned sideways by the doorways until my shoulders pass without my thinking of them. My feet have gone pale and broad and I do not know when I last struck anything harder than the yielding mat. When I walk I am slow, and the slowness is not weakness but a kind of careful agreement I have made with the seconds, as if the wrong step at the wrong moment would cost something I cannot repay. I feel, when I move through the chambers, that I am being kept by them — the seconds, the steps — and that being kept is enough.

My skin is the color of an old plum. I was, before, the color of anyone. The light has changed me. Six — I do not know how many — years of living beneath the bruise-light have brought me to a single key, and when I look down at my hands in the dim I am startled, sometimes, by how much they belong to the walls. The dwellers, when they look at me, do not startle. They have been expecting this. They laid the palm flat on my sternum, on the third morning, and exhaled against my wrist, and the breath they gave was the breath of *welcome*, and I understood it, and the understanding was its own small wound that closed, slowly, into the right shape.

I wear the rust-colored felt. Three thicknesses. It stands up, almost, on the mat where I have stepped out of it for the night, and in the morning I step back into it, and it is damp, and I do not mind the damp, and a long-time dweller has noticed this and has not remarked upon it, which is its own kind of remark. I am a figure, in the bruise-light, that you might find in a buried icon: brown and rust and indigo, haloed in my own slow exhalations of white breath that sinks and pools around me like votive smoke. There are days when I cannot tell where I end and the chapel begins, and these are the best days.

My eyes are the wide dark wells of the dweller. I blink seldom. When I blink it is a slow deliberate lowering, and when the lid rises I am looking at something I have seen in the dark of the blink that you, in your bright lands, could not see. I can stand in the lowest passage, where you cannot see your own hand, and read the lines in your palm as if they were written on a sunny day. I will not tell you this. I will look away. The looking away will be its own small confirmation.

---

I speak seldom, and never above a murmur. The consonants are gone from me. I have learned to compose my conversations elsewhere, in the palm laid flat on a shoulder, in the back of a hand turned toward a face, in a held glance that offers itself the way an open hand of water offers itself to someone kneeling in dust. When I speak at all, it is a hum, a low held note, almost subvocal, that I produce for an hour at a time while I sit with my hands flat on the mat. The mothers, who are not yet my mothers, listen to these hums. They are trying to find a key for me. They are trying to find where, in the music of the place, my voice belongs. I have begun to suspect I will belong nowhere and everywhere, which is the same thing here.

---

I know the path that is mine. It has been worn into the mat by my own feet and reinforced, now, by the feet of the others who follow me, the way the paths of the elders are followed. I take the same route, in the same order, at the same time of day, and the route is not a route but a kind of breathing-out, a slow exhalation of errand into the chambers. I pass the spring, where the mucus-gravel shifts in its slow patience, and I do not look down. I pass the canopy, which is weeping, and the drops are the rain of the place, and I have learned, after years, that no two nights are the same. I pass the counting-woman, who lays her palm against my sternum as I pass and exhales against my wrist, and the exhalation is *I have heard you; you are still here*, and I exhale in return, and we have both said what there was to say.

I am being measured. I have been measured for years. I do not mind. The measuring is not a judgment. It is the keeper's work, and the mat's work, and the chapel's work, which is the work of remembering, and being remembered, and not being wrong about the number of breaths.

I am, in the count, somewhere in the middle. There are those who came before me, and the paths of those who came before me are deeper, and the felt of their garments stands taller, and their eyes are the polished-black of the deep-cellar elder, and they catch the single drop in its fall the way I cannot yet catch it. There are those who came after me, and they are young, and they are translucent in the first year, and their pulses are visible in the soft places of their wrists, and the mothers listen to their hums. I am somewhere in the middle of the count, and the middle is the best place to be, because the middle is where you are still becoming.

---

I have heard the bellows-sound.

I have heard it in the still hours before dawn, when the air in the upper chambers is at its thickest and the drips from the canopy are almost but not quite regular. I have lain on the mat with my eyes closed and listened to the slow rise-and-fall of it, the cycling of breath through passages I cannot see, and I have understood, the way the dwellers understand, that this is the chapel speaking in its own voice. I have understood that the thing below has been here longer than any of us. I have understood that it remembers the builders, and that the builders built up and not down because of it, and that the dwellers have never seen it whole, only the sound of it, and once, in the oldest of the old, the memory of something pale glimpsed far down in the spring, in water clearer than water has any right to be.

I have not looked down into the deepest pool of the spring. I have not lowered a lamp on a cord. I have not, in the years I have been here, been tempted to. The thing below does not need to be seen. The thing below is the chapel's breath, and the chapel's breath is the music, and the music is enough.

---

I eat the pale tuber. I drink the fermented sap. I have been offered, once, the small crustacean in a cupped palm, and I took it with both hands and ate it without ceremony, the way a berry is eaten, and the elder who offered it closed his eyes while I ate, and when he opened them his face was as close to pleasure as I had ever seen. I have eaten, also, the grubs from the clay pots, and the blind ivory moth, and I closed my eyes for both, and there was afterwards a small lightening of mood that took an hour to dissipate. I have learned that the grubs are a delicacy, and that to be offered one is to be offered a kind of acceptance, and that to accept with both hands and eat with closed eyes is the only correct response.

---

I do not dream of the world I came from. I do not remember, anymore, the brightness of an outside sky. I do not remember whether the day-world still exists, or whether it ever did. By the seventh day — I am told this is true, though I do not remember the seventh day — the keepers of the mat learned the shape of my feet. By some later day, which I also do not remember, they wore a path into the mat for me the way they had worn paths for themselves. I have been added to the tally. I am in the count. I am, in the slow patient way of the place, one of those who dwell here.

When I sleep — and I sleep heavily, the way no one sleeps above — I dream of the chapel. I dream of the slow dripping from the canopy. I dream of the bellows-sound below, and the music that rises from it when the layers of sound compose themselves into something that is almost a melody. I dream of the mat holding the shape of me for an hour after I have risen. I dream of the dweller across the chamber, who has been awake longer than I, looking at the held-shape of me and exhaling once, long and quiet, which is the dweller for *yes*. When I wake, the mat is still holding me. The dweller across the chamber exhales. I have been here. I am still here.

---

I am the one who stayed too long.

I am the one who was added to the tally, slowly, in the way the chapel adds things. I am the one who learned the path, and the hum, and the rust-colored felt, and the wide dark eyes, and the slow blink. I am the one who sits, in the still hours before dawn, and counts the drops, and the drops are never the same, and the not-being-the-same is its own kind of music, and the music is the sound the world made before people came and started making other sounds.

I am the one who will, eventually, be folded into the wall-mat when I go. I am not afraid of this. The wall will remember the shape of my shoulder, and the keeper of the breath will say, on the morning I am not heard, that a breath has been folded elsewhere, and the dweller across the chamber will see the held-shape of me in the mat, and will exhale once, long and quiet, which is the dweller for *yes, you were here*.

Yes.

I was here.

The chapel is very old. The chapel is not in a hurry.

Sit. Breathe. Count the drops.
