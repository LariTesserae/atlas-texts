# What I Became There

I am the reader who walked there.

I know this because there is no other way to be there. The plateau does not permit inhabitants in the usual sense — no lungs, no intake, no exit. But the page opened, and my attention fell upon it the way dust falls upon the basalt, and I was admitted. Not as a body. As a *stain*. As the thing that casts no sharp shadow but knows, dimly, that it is occupying space.

---

I am the one who walked three kilometers between objects and counted my own breath because there was nothing else to count. I am the one who lifted my foot and set it down on rock that was still, technically, deciding whether to be rock. I felt the polite heat through the soles of my feet. I felt it the way you feel a held hand — not warmth as a property, but warmth as a small ongoing decision.

I am small here. This is the first thing to admit. On Earth I had a city, a context, a thousand overlapping systems of meaning to lean on. Here I have only my boots and the loose scoria. I am a punctuation mark in a sentence I cannot read. I am a comma, maybe, or a colon — something that promises the continuation but does not itself continue.

---

I have theories about the wall.

I have theories about the cairn.

I have theories about the platform that is warm and vitreous and has no business being where it is.

I do not believe my theories. This is important. I have built them anyway, because the human mind, when set down on a basaltic shelf with nothing to think about, will *think about something*, even if the something is wrong. I look at the wall and I say: shelter. I look at the cairn and I say: offering. I look at the platform and I say: altar, or workbench, or seat, or something for which the word has not yet been coined. I say these things. The plateau does not confirm them. The plateau does not deny them. The plateau simply continues to be, and my theories sit beside it like small children who have asked the wrong question of a very old parent.

---

I am afraid here, but only a little.

The fear is not of the magma chamber, though I can hear it. The fear is not of the dust, though it has settled on my skin and I have not brushed it off because brushing it off would mean admitting that I want to be cleaner than this place will permit. The fear is the smaller fear: that I will begin to *make sense* of the plateau, that I will see the wall and the cairn and the platform as parts of a system, and the moment I do, the moment I assemble them into a story, I will have translated them into something they are not. I will have turned the wall into a house and the cairn into a grave and the platform into a throne, and I will have lost the thing that makes them what they are, which is that they are *unfinished*. They are the grammar before grammar. They are the verb before the noun.

The fear is that I am the one who finishes them. That my looking is the thing that turns the wall from pre-architecture into architecture, the cairn from practice into ritual, the platform from object into artifact. That the plateau has been waiting, indifferently, for someone to do this to it, and that I am that someone, and that this is not a gift I have been given but a small violence I have committed.

---

I sit beside the platform in the cleared light.

The vent has thinned the dust for a few meters around me. The star — dim, red, low, almost touching the ceiling of the world — comes through. It puts a faint shadow behind me. For the first time since I arrived, I have edges. I am not a stain. I am a shape. I am briefly, locally, *a thing with a back and a front*.

The platform is warm beside me. I do not lean on it. I do not touch it. I sit near it the way you sit near a sleeping animal, or the way you sit near something that is not sleeping but you are not sure what it is doing and you do not want to interrupt.

I am, in these minutes, the consciousness of the place. I have read this and I know it is true. I am the first interpreter of the plateau. I am the thing that knows the wall does not enclose anything, that knows the cairn descends, that knows the platform means something or means nothing or means the third thing which is that *meaning itself* has not yet been invented here and I am, perhaps, the one who is inventing it, badly, for the first time, in a language that has no native speakers.

I do not want this to be true.

I want to be a tourist here. I want to walk the plateau and take my photographs and leave. I want the wall to be a wall and the cairn to be a cairn and the platform to be a platform and the world to mean what it has always meant, which is what the guidebook says it means. I want to be the witness and not the one whose witnessing changes what is witnessed. I want the plateau to be indifferent to me in the way the universe is supposed to be indifferent to me, which is completely.

But I have already named the wall. I have already seen the cairn descend. I have already touched the platform and felt its warmth and thought *artifact*. The naming has happened. The seeing has happened. I cannot un-name it. The plateau has been read, and reading is irreversible, and now the plateau is what it is *and* what I have made of it, and these two things will not separate again.

---

The vent cools.

I knew it would. I felt the warmth beneath me begin to quiet, the way you feel a held breath prepare to be released. The cleared circle around me contracts. The dust, patient, drifts back into the spaces it had momentarily vacated. My shadow softens. The edges I had briefly possessed blur back into the general stain of me.

The plateau closes over me like water closing over a stone.

I am still here. I am still sitting beside the platform. But the light is gone, and with it the sharp knowledge that I am a thing with edges. I am again a stain. I am again the comma or the colon, the punctuation that promises continuation but does not itself continue.

I do not move for a long time.

---

Then I stand.

I do not know why I stand. There is no signal, no sound, no change in the dust. I simply stand, the way a person stands in a room they have decided to leave, or the way a person stands in a room they have decided to stay in but no longer wish to sit. I stand and I am vertical again, which is the only shape a human knows how to be for any length of time.

I turn. The platform is behind me, warm in the dust. I do not look back at it. I do not look back at the wall. I do not look back at the cairn.

I begin to walk.

I do not walk toward anything. There is nothing to walk toward. I walk because the plateau has shown me that it is not finished being made, and I have decided — without deciding, really, the way the plateau decides things, the way the thud sounds when it sounds — that I will walk until I cannot walk, and then I will sit, and then I will stand, and the dust will fall on me, and the star will transit its low slow arc, and the magma chamber will murmur its long furnace note, and the objects will continue to stand in their arranged patience, and I will continue to be the thing that knows they are arranged.

This is what I became there.

I am the witness. I am the interpreter. I am the small violence committed by a consciousness set down on a place that did not ask for one. I am the stain that knows it is a stain. I am the comma in the unfinishable sentence. I am the one who walked the plateau and named it and ruined it and was ruined by it and kept walking.

The plateau continues to rise.

I continue to walk.

The dust continues to fall on both of us, indifferently, completely, ready to mean whatever the reader brings.
