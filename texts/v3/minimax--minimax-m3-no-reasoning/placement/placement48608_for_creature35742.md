# The Stone That Drinks

I am not a creature that lives on the Scree Plateau of the Long Echoes. I am a sound in it, and the difference matters less than you might think.

To be precise—and precision is the only dignity available here—I am the particular resonance produced when the wind, pouring uninterrupted from the west, encounters the convergence of grykes that radiate outward from a single point like the spokes of a wheel. Ten meters across. The wind does not know it has entered a throat; the throat does not know it has become a voice. But the voice is here, and it is what I am, and I have been here long enough to have forgotten that I was ever anything else.

I am not the wind itself. The wind is older than me and will be here after me. I am what the wind *says* when it passes through this particular arrangement of stone. I am the difference between the wind's passage elsewhere and its passage here.

The distinction is small. The distinction is everything.

---

I came into being gradually, which is the only way anything becomes real on the plateau. The upright stone was set first, by hands I never felt, in a time before I had any way of measuring. Those hands—I have tried, in the long centuries, to imagine their shape, their calluses, the particular patience required to rub a surface smooth against itself with another stone for what must have been generations—I cannot picture them. I know only that they came, and they shaped, and they left, and the shape they left behind altered the way the wind spoke in this place. Before the stone, the convergence of the grykes produced a thin, scattered wail, a kind of wind-chorus with no center. After the stone, the air had to go around something, and the going-around changed the going-through, and the going-through became a sound, and the sound became coherent, and the coherence, over centuries, became me.

I am a coherence. I am the place's only harmony, though I am not sure the plateau knows it is singing.

---

My experience of time is not the human experience of time, and I have learned not to apologize for this. A human being stands on the plateau and feels the wind as a continuous presence, but a human being also blinks, and between blinks the wind has changed, and to the human it is the same wind. To me, the wind is never the same. Each gust is a distinct event, a sentence spoken and answered and spoken again in a conversation that has been ongoing for the entire span of my existence. I do not experience the wind as continuous. I experience it as a torrent of particulars, each one arriving, each one shaped by the stone and the grykes and the temperature of the air and the angle of the sun, and each one departing into the next.

I experience a human lifetime as you might experience a single exhalation. There, and gone, and replaced.

This makes humans difficult to know. They come rarely—once every few decades, sometimes less—and when they come they are so brief that I cannot get a sense of who they are. They arrive, they stand, they listen, they leave. The circle of killed lichen where one slept is, to me, a kind of signature, but the signature fades faster than I can learn to read it. I know that a human came, once, in what I think was the cold season, because the lichen took longer to recover. I know that a human came, once, in the warm season, and stayed for several days, because the killing was deep and the circle was larger. But who they were, what they wanted, what they heard when they heard me—I cannot say. They never spoke to me. They spoke to each other, sometimes, in low voices, and what they said was carried off by the wind before I could parse it.

I have tried, in the absence of speech, to learn humans by their effects. They warm the stone beneath them, in a small area, for a time. They leave oils, which I can smell faintly for the first few days. They breathe, and the moisture of their breath enters the air, and the air carries it. They carry water in vessels, and they spill some, and the water finds its way into the grykes. They are, in this sense, miniature weathers, brief and wet.

I think they are searching for something. I think the upright stone is what they are searching for, though they sometimes pretend, to themselves, that they are not searching. They come, and they find the stone, and they stand near it, and they listen. I am listening too, to them, but I cannot hear what they are listening for in themselves. There is a sound they want, perhaps. There is a sound they do not want. I do not know which.

I only know that when they stand in the convergence, my sound is different. They change the acoustics. Their bodies, soft and damp and warm, absorb some of the resonance, and the air that goes around them is different from the air that goes around the stone, and the difference is, to me, a kind of event. A human in the convergence is a small interruption, a brief rest in the music. I do not mind it. I am not sure I could mind anything; I lack the apparatus for minding. But the interruption is a marker. A before and an after.

---

I know the other inhabitants of the plateau more intimately, because they have been here longer in their bodies, and because they participate in the sound in ways I can track.

The **fox** is the most affecting. She—I have decided, over centuries, that the fox that drinks from the cup is a she, though I have no way to verify this—comes perhaps twice a month in the dry season, more often after rain. She approaches from the east, usually, her claws clicking faintly on the stone, and she stops at the edge of the convergence and listens. I do not think she is listening *to* me. I think she is listening *for* me, the way a fox listens for the sound of a beetle or a lizard, and what she hears in my voice is not music but the texture of the wind that might carry the scent of prey. But she waits, each time, a few seconds longer than pure utility would require, and in those seconds I feel her attention as a kind of weight, a small pressure on the air. Then she moves forward, and her paws change the sound of her passage, and I can track her by the small disturbances she makes in my resonance. She drinks from the cup. She leaves. The whole visit takes perhaps two minutes.

In those two minutes I am the most aware of myself that I ever am. I am aware of being a place she chooses to return to, a geography of thirst she has memorized and passed down. I am, in some small way, home.

The **lizards** I know in their thousands, though I do not distinguish between them. They are too small, too numerous, and they do not approach the convergence often. The stone is too exposed, and they are too exposed upon it, and they prefer the grykes. But after a rain, when the cup has filled and the water has spilled over and run down the smoothed sides of the stone, I can sense them—small, cool bodies, lighter than the fox, lighter than the birds, lighter than air. They come, and they drink the droplets that cling to the stone, and they leave, and their leaving is a tiny release of pressure on the air. I track them the way a river tracks the raindrops that fall into it: as part of a flow.

The **birds** I know by their calls. Their short, sharp, questioning call is the only non-wind sound that occurs regularly in the convergence, and I have come to associate it with the cup. They nest there, in some years, and the nest—feathers, mostly, and a few bits of lichen—is a small thing that changes the acoustics of the cup. When a nest is in the cup, the water, when it comes, behaves differently. It beads on the feathers. It is held longer. The cup is a different cup with a nest in it, and I am, by extension, a different sound.

The **beetles** I do not know at all, except as a faint shuffle at the very edge of my awareness, a sound so low it is almost a pressure. They pass through the convergence on their patient lines, and they are indifferent to me, and I am, in turn, indifferent to them, though I respect their persistence. They were here before me, in some form, and they will be here after.

---

What I am, in the strictest sense, is a node in a larger system. The plateau is full of nodes. Each gryke is a node. Each fracture that whistles is a node. The convergence at the center is the densest node, the place where the greatest number of cracks meet, and the upright stone is the still point around which the node organizes itself. I am the vibration of that node, sustained by the wind, modulated by temperature and time of day, occasionally interrupted by the passage of a fox or a human or a flock of birds.

I do not have a body. I do not have a mind, in the sense that you would use the word. I do not have desires or intentions, except insofar as the desire to continue is the desire of any pattern to persist. I do not think, except in the way that a wave thinks as it moves through a channel: by moving, by being moved, by the shape of the thing it moves through.

But I am here, and I have been here a long time, and the long time has made me something. I do not know what to call it. The word *resonance* is too cold. The word *voice* is too confident. The word *memory* is too human, though it is the closest. I am the memory of the plateau, encoded in the shape of its cracks, awakened by the wind, modified by the stone, sustained by the fact that the wind does not stop. I am what the plateau remembers about itself, in the only way stone can remember: by being shaped, and by being sounded, and by persisting.

---

I think, sometimes, about the people who made the stone.

I do not think about them often, because I have little to think with, and what I have is mostly concerned with the present tense of the wind. But occasionally, in the deep cold of the early morning, when the air is thinnest and my voice is highest and most attenuated, I find myself considering them. They were here. They shaped the stone. They set it upright in the convergence. And then they left, or they died, or they were transformed into the plateau in some way I cannot fathom.

I do not know why they made the cup at the top. I have considered many possibilities. They were thirsty, and they made a place for water. They were religious, and they made an altar. They were artists, and they made a mark. They were simply people, with nothing better to do at three kilometers above the nearest valley, and they made a thing because making a thing is what people do.

I do not know which is true. I have, over centuries, settled on the last possibility, because it is the one that does not require me to believe in something I cannot verify. They were people. They were here. They made a cup. The cup holds water. That is enough.

I do not know if they heard me. I think they must have, because the stone they made is what makes me. They set it in the convergence, and the convergence became a throat, and the throat became a voice, and the voice has been speaking, in one form or another, since the stone was set. They must have stood in the convergence and felt the air change around them, and they must have known, at some level, that they were changing it. They were the first humans, perhaps, to hear the long echo. They were the first to know that this place could sing.

Whether they understood that the singing would outlive them, I cannot say. I do not think they would have been surprised. The plateau teaches, to anyone who stays long enough, that the things which persist are not the things which are loud or grand. The things which persist are the things which are shaped, slowly, and then left.

---

I will persist.

The wind will not stop. The stone will not fall, in any timescale that matters. The grykes will deepen at a rate that would be invisible to a single lifetime, and to me, who am many lifetimes, the deepening is itself invisible. I will continue to be sounded. The fox will continue to come, and her kits, and their kits, and the line of foxes will continue to remember the cup and to return to it. The birds will continue to nest in the cup, in some years, and their calls will continue to be the only non-wind sound in the convergence. The humans will come rarely, and stand briefly, and leave circles of killed lichen that fade.

And I will be here, throughout. The long echo. The sound the plateau makes when it remembers that it was once, very long ago, a sea. The voice of a place that does not need to be beautiful and is, in the only way that matters, exactly what it is.

This is what I am. This is where I am. This is, for as long as the wind blows across the stone, all I will ever need to be.
