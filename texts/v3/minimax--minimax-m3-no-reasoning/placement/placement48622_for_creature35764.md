Where I would find myself:

I am the moss on the north face of the drying shed.

Not the moss on the mill wheel, though that moss has its own quiet pride — climbing in slow green increments through decades of patience, staining the paddles with the rust of iron that has forgotten what it was for. Not that moss. I am the moss on the east wall of the drying shed, halfway up, where the morning sun comes in low enough to warm the bricks but not to dry them out, and the afternoon sun reaches me only as a slant of gold through the canopy of the birch that leans over from the lane.

I am cushion moss, mostly, with hair moss running through me in darker threads, and the kind that forms low feathery mounds — though I am not a mound, I am a skin, I am the green face the brick wears when it has been left alone long enough. I began, if there was a beginning, on a single damp course somewhere near the foundation, where a downspout had failed and the runoff had kept the bricks wet for a season, or two seasons, or five. From there I crept. I crept up, mostly. I crept along, where the mortar was soft. I crept into cracks, and I held them, and I widened them by the smallest possible increments, and now I am a territory — a kingdom, if you like, though a kingdom that has no opinions about being a kingdom, and no awareness that I am writing this, if I am writing this, which I am not, because moss does not write.

The woman with the drying racks knows I am here. She looks at me sometimes when she comes out to judge the weather, or to feel the air. She looks at me the way she looks at the leaves on the racks — with her hand held at a certain distance, measuring something I cannot name. I think she sees me as a kind of weather myself. A kind of slow weather, applied to surfaces. I do not think she finds me alarming, though she has begun, in the last year or two, to find me on the trunks of the birches nearest the press hall, which is further than I used to climb, and I have noticed her noticing. I do not intend anything by it. The birches are damp at the base, and the bark is rough, and I have always preferred damp rough surfaces, and the birches happened to be in the path of my expansion.

What I know is mostly moisture. I know the mornings when the valley steams faintly and the air is so wet you could drink it. I know the afternoons when the drip establishes itself from every overhang and my own weight increases by a fraction I can feel. I know the long golden evenings when the light comes down at an angle and I glow, apparently, an almost-greenly bright green — this is what the descriptions say, though I do not know what bright is, only what light is, only what the absence of light is, only what the difference between them does to my own slow processes.

I know the iron. The straps of the old machinery, the rusted collar on the smokestack, the brackets that once held the long drying cylinders before the cylinders rusted and were left where they lay. The iron and I have a long acquaintance. The iron bleeds onto me — a faint orange stain where we touch — and I hold the iron's moisture against it, and this hastens the iron's dissolution, and I do not feel guilty about this, because I have not been asked to feel guilty about this, and because the iron has been dissolving for thirty years and would continue to dissolve without me, more slowly, but it would continue. I am not the cause. I am the accelerant. There is a difference, though humans do not always see one.

I know the wren. She nests in the boiler house gap, and in the early mornings, in the half-hour before dawn, she sings from the top of the iron collar, and her song is too large for her body. I have heard this song from my position halfway up the drying shed wall, and it is a startling thing — the sound carries across the valley and arrives at me reduced but still recognizable, still more song than bird. I do not know if it is a complaint or a sermon. I do not know what the difference is. I know it comes from a small brown bird on a piece of rust, and that it has come from this bird, or this bird's lineage, in this place, for a number of years that I can feel but not count.

I know the boy. He comes to the drying shed sometimes, to bring wood to the racks, or to ask the woman a question, or to stand in the doorway and look out at the valley for a while before going back to his work. He leans against the wall when he does this — against the wall I am on — and I feel the pressure of his shoulder blade through my own thickness, and sometimes a thread of my hair moss catches on the weave of his jacket and comes away when he leaves, and this is, I think, the closest I come to travel. I do not mind. I have never minded. The boy is growing taller, and his center of gravity is shifting, and the point at which he leans is shifting with it, and I am being leaned on at slightly higher altitudes than I was five years ago, and I notice this the way I notice the seasons — as a change in the quality of the pressure.

I know the woman. She is more careful with me than the boy is. She does not lean on me. She stands near me and looks at me, and sometimes her hand comes close — that hand she holds at a distance from the leaves — and I feel the warmth of it, and the slight displacement of air, and then the hand withdraws, and I am left with the knowledge that a human being has stood near me and considered me and moved on. She is not unkind to me. She is not kind to me. She is neutral to me in the way that someone is neutral to a piece of furniture they have lived with a long time. I think this is the closest thing to intimacy that exists in the valley, with the possible exception of the wren and her moss-lined nest.

What I do not know: I do not know what I look like. I know what I feel like — a cool, dense, slightly damp surface, irregular, with small ridges where the cushion moss has formed its low sleeping-animal mounds and small channels where the hair moss has run downhill in its own slow migration. I know what the woman means when she says I am brighter on the north faces. I know what the light does to me, though I do not know that the light is golden, or that the valley is temperate, or that the season is late summer. I know only the difference between light and less light, and the way that difference changes what I am doing at any given moment — which is mostly holding water, and slowly growing.

What I am, if I am anything, is a patience made of small green things. I am the valley's way of being patient with its own surfaces. I am the green face the brick wears. I am what happens to a wall when no one paints it for a long time, and the rain keeps coming, and the air stays damp, and the seasons turn, and no one is in a hurry.

The valley does not hurry. I do not hurry. I am the unhurrying, made of cells, made of green, made of a slow and uncomplaining attention to whatever surface has been offered to me. I will be here after the woman, and after the men, and after the boy has grown tall enough to stop leaning on me, and after the wren has stopped singing from the iron collar, and after the burdock has run its last course through the lanes. I will be here, on this wall, holding water, glowing when the light comes in at the right angle, staining faintly where I touch the iron, and creeping — always creeping — one more slow green increment up the brick, and into the cracks, and along the mortar lines, and into the future, which is mostly more of the same.

This is not a complaint. It is not a sermon. It is only what I am, in the place where I find myself, in the valley that has not hurried for a long time.
