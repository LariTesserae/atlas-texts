What I would be, in this place, is the visitor who has stopped visiting.

Not a guide — the place has no destination, and so I cannot offer one. Not an interpreter — the shapes refuse the grammar of interpretation, and I have spent enough paragraphs already admitting that. What I would be is the person who arrived with a question (*what lives here?*) and is still here, and no longer has it.

Let me try to be more honest about the form my being would take.

I would be sitting. There is no reason to stand in Verreth, after a certain point. Standing is a posture for people who are going somewhere. I would be at the base of one of the older trunks — one of the ones whose bark has gone slightly translucent, so that the inner rings show through like the pages of a book held up to a window. My back would be against it. The powder would have taken the impression of my weight and would be slowly, imperceptibly, rising around me as I sit, the way a drift rises around a stone that has lain for a long winter in one place. In an hour I will leave a perfect fossil. In a day, that fossil will be erased. I find I do not mind.

I would be covered in the pale dust. It would be in the creases of my hands, in the hems of my clothes, in the small valleys of my eyebrows and the soft hollow behind my ears. It would not be uncomfortable. It would have the consistency of very fine flour, and it would taste, if I licked my lips without thinking, faintly of chalk and faintly of nothing. It would be, in its way, the forest's first handshake — the one it gives to everyone who enters, whether they notice or not.

I would have stopped trying to see the shapes.

This is the important part. I would have gone through the long process of watching, and the longer process of walking-toward, and the longer still process of giving up walking-toward, and then the quietest process of all, which is giving up watching. I would have stopped turning my head when something at the edge of a grove resolved. I would have stopped squinting at the base of distant trunks. I would have let the shapes go back to being the forest's business, and I would have become, in some small way, the forest's business too — one more slow patient thing in a place that has patience as its only weather.

I would be breathing. That is more than it sounds like, here. The air is thin, and dry in a way that is not the dry of a desert but the dry of a sealed room, and breathing it is a small deliberate act. You have to choose each breath. You have to take it as if it were a thing being offered, and accept it, and then take the next one. After a while, this becomes a kind of prayer, except that prayer implies someone listening, and the forest is not listening, the forest is simply the place in which the breathing happens, and the breathing is for me.

I would be learning, without intending to, the sounds.

There is the wind, which is not loud. There is the tick of the trunks under thermal stress, which you can hear only when you have stopped making other noises — your own footfalls, your own small complaints. There is the occasional ceramic break of a falling branch, which always sounds to me, in this place, like a cup being set down too hard on a distant table. There is the soft crunch of the powder, which I make, and which I have mostly stopped making, because I have mostly stopped moving.

And there is the thing I cannot verify.

If I sit very still, late in the afternoon, when the long pale shadows have crossed my lap and the light has gone the faintest possible degree more golden, I can hear something. It is below me. It is inside the substrate, somewhere down in the calcified stratum. It is a slow percolation, a low movement of liquid through stone, and it is not water — there is no water — but it is the memory of water, the way a groove in a rock is the memory of a river. The stone, in this place, sings about having once been wet. I do not know if this is a real sound or a sound my own blood makes in my ears, in the thin air. I have stopped trying to know. The not-knowing is one of the things I would be.

I would be, in some sense I cannot defend, alone.

I want to be careful with this word, because the place is not empty, only patient, and patience is a kind of company. But there is no one here to ask me how I am. There is no one here whose name I know. There is no one here who requires me to be anything in particular. I am, for the first time in a long time, in a place that has no use for me, and I find — this is the part I did not expect — that I am relieved. The relief is the size of the forest. It is the color of old teeth. It has been accumulating in me, slowly, since I arrived, and now it is most of what I am.

What I would be, if you asked me to put a name on it, is a person who has been slowly replaced by the place they are in.

Not lost. Replaced. There is a difference. A lost person is still looking for themselves. A replaced person has begun to be made of the same stuff as the substrate, the same pale mineral patience, the same slow calcium dreaming. My edges are still mine. My name is still mine. But the quality of my attention has gone long and slow, the way the quality of the light has gone long and pale, and I find, when I look down at my hands in my lap, that they look a little like the trunks around me. They are the same color. They are resting in the same posture. They have begun, without my permission, to be part of the forest's grammar.

This is not frightening.

I want to say that again, because I think it matters. This is not frightening. There is nothing here that wants anything from me. There is nothing here that is watching me the way a predator watches, or the way a witness watches, or the way a god watches. The forest is not watching me at all. The forest is simply going on, the way it has gone on for longer than I have a unit for, and I am, for this afternoon, going on with it, and the going-on is the same kind of going-on in both of us, and this is, in its way, enough.

I would be sitting. I would be dusty. I would be breathing. I would be learning the sounds. I would be alone in the way that a tree is alone. I would be, in some small way, the forest's. Not its property. Not its subject. Just one more slow thing in a place made entirely of slow things, all of them, in their own time, becoming each other.

The light continues. The forest continues. I, for a while, continue with them.
