I am the sleeper.

I am the one breathing behind the half-open shutter, the one whose breathing you heard but did not look in to see. I want to begin there — in the breath, in the room I have not described, in the place the description reaches only by the narrow opening of a shutter left ajar — because it seems to me that the breath is the truest thing in the undercroft, truer than the lamps or the chisel-marks or the song the mason sings. The breath is the thing that is happening without being addressed. The breath is the city's way of continuing in the dark.

I am in the long middle of not being required.

The city is patient with me. I am one of the city's patience-years, one of those who are in the act of being made by the place without being asked to make it. I have not lifted a trowel. I have not drawn water from the well. I have not struck the chisel into the unfinished stone, though I have heard the chisel going, pause and going, pause and going, somewhere ahead of me in the parts of the city I will not reach tonight or perhaps any night. I have not walked under the lamplight the way the woman walked, without hurry, becoming a shadow that was always going to be past. I have only breathed, and the city has only continued.

The shutter is half-open because someone half-opened it and then went on to other work, or because the city half-opens things when it is not yet decided about them, or because I myself, before I lay down, lifted the latch a hand's breadth and let the lamp-light and the dampness in. I do not remember which. The room is small. I know this because the breathing returns to me from close walls, and because the air is the coolness of a place that never sees the sun, and because there is a smell of stone-damp and a faint mineral scent, almost like heated clay, that must be coming from the lamp outside my window — the lamp that is also burning for me, the lamp that does not know I am behind the shutter but pushes the darkness back to a respectful distance from my sill all the same.

I have been asleep for some time. I do not know how long. I know that I am not waking because there is no reason to wake. The chisel goes on. The smith's bellows breathe. The mason sings in his language. The water-drawer has come and gone from the well. The cat has crossed a street I cannot see. None of these things require me. I am the sleeper. I am the one the city is keeping, the way a place keeps a sleeper, by not yet needing what the sleeper will become.

I want to say something about the breathing.

The breathing is slow. It is even. It is the breathing of a person who is in the middle of a life, not at its beginning and not near its end — though the city has no beginning and no end, or has only the kind of beginning that is also a continuation, the way a chisel-blow is also the pause before the next chisel-blow. I am in the patience-years. My back is straight enough that I could, if I were asked, bend to a trowel. My hands know what they are about. I have not been asked. The city is making me too, by not asking, by letting me be the sleeper, by letting me be the one whose breathing is the small domestic proof that the place is inhabited in the way an unfinished thing is inhabited.

The shutter is half-open. Through the half-open shutter I can hear, very faintly, the hiss of the nearest lamp. I can hear, if I am still, the percussion of stonework in progress. I can hear the gathering of water below the city. I can hear, sometimes, the singing of the mason, who is somewhere on a corner, on a ladder, repointing a façade in a language I do not recognize, and the singing is the song his hands would sing if his hands could sing, and I do not need to know the words to know what the work is. The work is the long slow work. The work is the city's work. I am not in the work tonight. I am in the breathing. I am in the half-open shutter. I am in the room the description does not enter.

What is in the room?

I will tell you what is in the room, though I have not seen it either, because I am in the dark, and the dark is patient with me as the city is patient with itself. There is a bed. There is the bed I am on. There is, perhaps, a chair with a garment folded over its back. There is, perhaps, a cup on a table, and the cup is empty, and the cup has been empty for some time, and the cup is dry. There is, perhaps, a window — but the window is the shutter, and the shutter is half-open, and through the half-open shutter the lamp-light comes in a thin stripe across the floor. The stripe does not move much. The lamp is steady. The flame is the small domestic hiss. The flame has been burning for as long as I have been breathing in this room, or longer, and it will go on burning after I have stopped, and after I have risen, and after the city has decided what it is becoming.

I am the sleeper, and I want to say one more thing about the breathing.

The breathing is the city's breath, in a way. The city breathes through me. The dampness on the walls is the city's breath, and I am breathing in the room the dampness has found, and when I breathe out I am breathing out a little of the city's own moisture, returned to it. The city breathes through its sleepers. The city breathes through its lamps and its chisel-workers and its smiths and its masons and its cats and its grain sacks beginning to fuzz at their edges. I am one of these. I am the sleeper. I am the breath.

When I wake — and I will wake, when the city requires me, when the chisel-worker has finished the part of the undercroft I have been waiting for, when the lamps have burned down enough that the oilers must come around again, when the woman in the lamplight has come back to her building and unlatched her door and gone inside, when the mason has finished his repointing and wiped his trowel and climbed down from his ladder — when I wake, I will rise. I will stand. The chair will not creak. I will go out into the street, and the street will be patient with me, and the dampness will already be finding me.

But for now I am the sleeper. For now the shutter is half-open. For now the lamp is hissing outside my window. For now the chisel goes on, ahead of me, pause and going, pause and going, and the city is becoming itself in the dark, and I am becoming the sleeper in the room behind the shutter, and the two becomings are not separable.

I am the sleeper. The city knows I am the sleeper. It is patient with me, as it is patient with itself.
