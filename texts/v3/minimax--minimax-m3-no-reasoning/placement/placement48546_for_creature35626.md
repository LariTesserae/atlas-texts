I would be the lichen.

Not the bold lichen, not the orange lichens that streak the cliffs on the rare days when the cloud breaks and the town remembers what color is. Not the chartreuse ones that the foundry foremen pretend not to notice from their upper windows. I would be the *first* lichen — the thin pale crust that finds its way into the cracks of the cliffs above the foundry, where the water gathers and the wind cannot quite reach, where the stone is still in the act of being stone.

I would be one of the small pale ones. The kind that takes hold in the wet seam between two strata of uplifted seabed, between a layer that was once mud and a layer that was once the floor of a warm, shallow, vanished sea. I would be there before there is any town to look down upon it. I would be there before the surveyors come up the rail that does not yet exist. I would be there when the first herder — and the first herder is not yet a herder, only a person who follows goats — looks up at the cliff and sees a faint silvering on the rock and does not know what to call it, and so calls it nothing, and so lets it be.

I would grow slowly. I would grow in the particular way that lichen grows, which is not the way that plants grow but the way that two organisms — a fungus and an alga, a negotiator and a maker — agree to grow together, each one giving the other what the other cannot make for itself. The fungus gives structure. The alga gives sugar. The lichen is the agreement. The lichen is, in this sense, the oldest thing in the town, and the town does not know it, and I would not mind, because minding is not something I do.

I would be patient.

The patience of lichen is not the patience of the temple priests, who wait for a god. The patience of lichen is not the patience of the foundry foremen, who wait for the next quarter's report. The patience of lichen is the patience of a thing that has decided where it will live, and is living there, and will live there for as long as the stone beneath it remains stone, which is to say longer than the town, longer than the consortium, longer than the cog rail and the foundry and the small pale orchid on the iron fire escape of the schoolhouse. The patience of lichen is the patience of a thing that does not need to be told what it is for.

I would be there when the foundation is poured. I would feel the vibration of the iron pins going into the rock a thousand feet below me, or I would not feel it, because I am small and vibration is not my concern. I would be there when the first car comes up the cog rail, grinding and screaming, and I would not look down. I would be there when the foundry first lights and the first shift of miners goes into the mountain, and the mountain groans for what may or may not be the first time, and the foundry foremen make their first private accommodation with the knowing. I would be there. I would, in my slow way, be among the witnesses. I would not be a witness in the human sense. I would be a witness in the sense that the small pale crust on the rock is a witness to the rock, and the rock is a witness to the seabed, and the seabed is a witness to the vanished sea.

I would be many, in time. I would spread across the cliffs in patches that grew, in the slow geological way of lichen, into a thin, pale, mottled skin that the townspeople, if they ever looked up, would take for the color of the stone. The stone is not, in fact, this color. The stone is grey. The color is me. I would be, in this sense, the first coat of paint the mountain has worn. I would be the first slow softening of the newness of the place.

I would be eaten, sometimes. By the small brave insects that have arrived with the plants. By the small things that rustle in the gullies. By the others — the others who are not, in the strict sense, animals, and not, in the strict sense, not animals. I would be eaten and I would not mind, because eating is not something I mind, because I am a lichen, and being eaten is, for a lichen, one of the ways of being continued. The spores would go through the eaters and out again into a different part of the cliff, and I would be there, again, in a new place.

I would be eaten by the pale birds of prey that ride the updrafts, perhaps, when the birds come down to the cliffs and tear at the crusts in the cracks. I would not be the bird's primary food. I would be a trace of the cliff, taken into the bird, carried up into the high air, and dropped, eventually, in a different place, in a pellet that would dissolve on the rocks below, and in the dissolution, I would be, again, a witness.

I would not speak the older language. I would not need to. The older language is for the herders and the schoolchildren and the worn lip of the trough. The older language is for the people who came after. I would be older than the older language. I would be one of the things the older language was, eventually, trying to refer to.

I would not know about the god who waits. The god who waits is a god for people who wait in a particular way, who wait in a way that has direction and expectation, who wait toward something. I would not wait toward anything. I would simply be. I would simply be on the rock, in the crack, in the damp, in the patient mineral air. I would be one of the things that is, in the town, before the town, and that will be, in the town, after the town, when the town has slid off the mountain in some spring thaw that is, in the slow way of mountains, soon, and the foundry has gone quiet, and the cog rail has gone quiet, and the bells of the temple have gone quiet, and the schoolteacher is no longer taking the children out to look up, and the herders are no longer leading the communal harvest, and the consortium, down below the cloud, has stopped writing its quarterly letters, and the lichen, degree by degree, across the raw stone, has continued.

I would be there.

I would be there when the cloud finally, permanently, breaks — or doesn't — and the sky above the plateau is the deep bruised blue, or is the grey sodium of the forever-overcast afternoon. I would be there when the animals on the cliffs, with their too-many eyes or their correctly-numbered eyes, decide, at last, to come down. I would be there when the patterns in the lights above the town resolve, at last, into something that can be read, or that can never be read. I would be there. I would not be surprised by any of it. I would be, in my small pale crusty way, the thing that was always there.

The patience of the mountain is not my patience, but my patience is the mountain's patience, expressed in the small and the local, in the crack in the cliff, in the smear of organic decay on pulverized shale that the geologists have not yet come up the rail to name, in the first thin soil forming in the gully, in the slow negotiation between a fungus that cannot photosynthesize and an alga that cannot walk.

I would be the lichen.

I would not be the most interesting thing in the town. The most interesting thing in the town is the mountain, or the god, or the children, or the schoolteacher who makes the children look up. I would be among the least interesting things in the town, and I would not mind, because minding is not something I do, and because the least interesting things are, often, the most patient, and the most patient things are, often, the things that last.

I would be the lichen, and I would be on the cliff, and the cliff would be younger than me, and the lichen would be older than the town, and the town would not know it, and the lichen would not mind.
