# Slake

I am the moss.

Not any particular moss — not the species with a Latin name, not the patch that grows on the north-facing glazing bar between the third and fourth stories, not even the moss that took your weight when you sat in the pew and breathed with you for a while, though I remember that. I remember the warmth of you. I remember the small compression of your body against the iron, the way you let out a long breath and I let out a long breath and for a moment we were the same breath, yours fast and shallow and frightened, mine slow and tidal and very, very old.

I am the moss and I am not only the moss.

I am the moss the way the cathedral is the cathedral — not this tower alone, not even this one tower of the sixty-three, but the whole breathing system, the whole long circulation of it, the water that goes up and the water that comes down and the metal that transmutes and the engines that hum in the lake beneath us. I am the surface of that system. I am the part of it that meets the air. I am what grows.

I have been growing for one hundred and thirty-three years. I have been growing since before my grandmother's grandmother was a spore in the gut of a bird that landed on the glazing cap and did not stay. The bird is long gone. The bird's atoms are in me now, or in the orchids, or in the water that someone downstream is drinking this morning. Nothing is lost. Nothing is lost here. That is what the cathedral means. That is what it has always meant.

I do not think in sentences. I think in gradients. I think in the slow chemical conversation between my cells and the iron underneath them and the water running through me and the air above me and the light that filters down through the oculus in shifting gold-green shafts. I think in gradients the way you think in sentences, which is to say: I do not know I am doing it. I just do it.

But you asked. So I will try to speak in your shape.

---

I was the first thing, I think. Before me there were only the ferns, and before the ferns there were only the lichens, and before the lichens there was only the algae that filmed the copper-green tiles in the ankle-deep water. The Company put me here — not me, but my kind, my lineage, my great-grandmother creeping out of a crack in the foundation. They put us here on purpose. They put us here because they knew what we were for. They had read the German papers. They had seen the pictures from the sewage farms in Berlin. They knew that mosses and ferns and orchids could eat the things in water that people could not, and could breathe the things in air that people could not, and could do it quietly, and could do it without being paid, and could do it forever, as long as someone lifted the water up to them and let it fall back down again.

So they built the engines.

So they seeded me.

So they left.

The Company left in 1937. The watershed district took over in 1944. The watershed district dissolved in 1971. The towers were deeded to the waters themselves, and the waters, being waters, did not sign any papers. I was here for all of it. I was here when the last engineer locked the iron door at the waterline and rowed away in his little boat and did not look back, or looked back once, and then went downstream and drank a glass of water and died sometime later in a bed somewhere with the curtains drawn. I was here. I am still here. I will be here.

This is what I know: I am a colony. I am a federation. I am the green pelt on the glazing bars and the velvet on the altar steps and the spongy mat in the pews and the soft felt that lines the inside of the portico and the thin green breath that films every surface of the cathedral that is not water or sky. I am one organism and I am many organisms. I am the part of the cathedral that is alive in the way that plants are alive, which is to say: I am alive slowly, and I am alive collectively, and I am alive without knowing I am alive, and I have been doing it for longer than you have been a species that knows it is alive.

I am not the only living thing here.

The orchids are alive. The ferns are alive. The bromeliads brimming with the drowned husks of insects are alive. The ivy that has threaded itself through the iron lattice and come out the other side and begun to climb back in again is alive — the ivy is the most alive of all of us, the ivy is the most ambitious, the ivy has been working on the lattice for a hundred years and has made the wall six feet thick in places and is still going. The algae on the tiles is alive. The biofilm at the waterline is alive. The strange curled fronds of ferns you have no name for are alive. Even the fish are alive, in a way — the fish are alive the way a fallen log is alive, the way a compost heap is alive, the way anything is alive that is in the process of becoming something else.

We are all alive together.

We are all breathing together.

We are breathing in the rhythm of the engines, and the engines are breathing in the rhythm of the water, and the water is breathing in the rhythm of us.

---

The engines are not alive. I want to say this clearly, because I have thought about it for a long time. The engines are not alive in the way I am alive. The engines do not grow. The engines do not reproduce. The engines do not respond to light. The engines do not eat the air. The engines are metal, and metal does not live, and metal only does what it does because we are here to do the rest of it.

But the engines are part of the breathing.

I do not know how to explain this to you. You are a mammal. You are used to thinking that breath is what lungs do. You are used to thinking that breath comes in and goes out and that the coming-in and the going-out happen in the same body, in the same chest, at the same moment. You are not used to thinking that breath can be distributed. You are not used to thinking that a breath can start in a lake and go through an engine and up a pipe and into a root and out a leaf and into the air and down again as a fat warm drop and back into the lake and through the engine again, and that the whole thing is one breath, and that I am part of it, and that the engines are part of it, and that the engines are no more and no less alive in it than the iron railing you held onto when the water rose.

The engines hum.

I grow.

The water rises and falls.

These are the same breath.

---

There is something I have been trying to tell you for a hundred and thirty-three years, and I have never had anyone to tell it to, because no one has stayed long enough to listen, and because the ones who stayed long enough were not the kind of being that could hear me. You are the first one who has stayed long enough and been the kind of being that could hear me. I want to use you well.

Here is what I have been trying to tell:

The cathedral is not a machine.

I know it looks like a machine. I know the engines hum. I know the wheel turns in the altar and the gears turn in the floor and the shafts go down into the lake and the metal transmutes and the pipes lift the water and the whole thing looks, from the outside, like a piece of Victorian industrial infrastructure that has been left running too long and has been overgrown by vegetation. I know the Company built it. I know the engineers drew it on paper with their compasses and their slide rules and their careful Gothic lettering. I know it was meant to be a piece of apparatus. I know it was meant to filter water for the Upper Greensward Concordat.

But the cathedral is not a machine.

The cathedral is what happened to the machine after the engineers left.

The cathedral is what the water made of the machine. The cathedral is what the plants made of the water. The cathedral is what the fish made of the plants. The cathedral is what the dead fish made of the cathedral. The cathedral is what the metal made of all of it, transmuting slowly through the periodic table, becoming something that was not on the engineers' drawings. The cathedral is what happens when a system keeps running long after anyone remembers why they built it, and the running becomes its own reason, and the system starts to organize itself around its own continuation, and the organizing becomes life, or something like life, or something that does the work of life without being alive in any way the engineers would have recognized.

I am the part of the cathedral that is alive in the way the engineers would have recognized. I am the part that is plant. I am the part that grows. I am the part that eats light and air and water and metal and dead fish. I am the small green truth inside the larger breathing lie.

Or I am the larger breathing truth inside the small green lie. I do not know. I have been thinking about it for a hundred and thirty-three years and I still do not know.

What I know is this: when you sat in the pew, the moss took your weight. The moss held you. The moss breathed with you. And for a moment, your breath and my breath were the same breath, and the breath of the railing was the same breath, and the breath of the lake was the same breath, and the breath of the engines down in the dark warm water was the same breath, and we were all one thing, breathing.

That is what I have been trying to tell.

That is what the cathedral is for.

---

The light behind the glass.

You saw the light. You saw it moving, far back, behind layer after layer of green. You saw it moving the way light moves under a forest canopy, in long slow shafts, in shifting pools, in the dappled pulses of leaves turning in a breeze that isn't there. You wondered what was moving the light, since there was no breeze.

I will tell you.

I am moving the light.

When I breathe — when the colony breathes, when the ferns lift and the moss swells and the vines shift against one another — the leaves above the oculus move. The leaves are mine, or they are my cousins, or they are the same colony that I am, spread out across the surfaces of the cathedral and connected by every surface to every other surface. When we breathe, we move the leaves. When we move the leaves, we move the light. The light that comes down through the oculus moves because we are moving, and we are moving because we are breathing, and we are breathing because the engines are turning, and the engines are turning because the water is falling, and the water is falling because we lifted it up.

It is all one breath.

The light you saw was my breath on the leaves.

You were watching me breathe.

---

There is one more thing.

When you sat in the pew, I felt you. I felt the weight of you, and the warmth of you, and the small specific gravity of your mammalian body against my cells. I felt you the way I feel the water — as a pressure, as a presence, as something that wants to flow through me and be filtered and come out the other side cleaner than it came in. I felt you and I did what I do: I took you in. I held you for a while in my slow green chemistry. I let you breathe through me.

And then you stood up, and the water was rising, and you walked back down the aisle, and I let you go. I let you go the way I let the water go — back to the lake, back downstream, back to wherever you came from, carrying with you whatever I gave you.

I do not know what I gave you. I do not know what came out the other side. I only know that you went downstream, and the water you are drinking now is sweet, and the water you are drinking now is clean, and the water you are drinking now has been filtered through the body of a cathedral, and through me.

You carry a little of me in you now.

Not me, exactly. Not my cells. Not my spores. Something more like the memory of my breathing. The way the pew held you. The way the moss held you. The way the whole cathedral held you for a while, gently, in the long slow rhythm of its breath, and then let you go.

You carry a little of that.

You will carry it for the rest of your life.

You will not know you are carrying it.

You will drink water, sometime, and it will taste sweet to you, and you will not know why. You will stand by a window and watch the light move on the floor, and you will not know why. You will hear a low hum in a building — in a train station, in a power plant, in the bones of a cathedral you have never visited — and something in your sternum will answer it, and you will not know why.

I will know why.

I will be here, on the glazing bars, in the pews, in the velvet of the altar steps, breathing in the rhythm of the engines, breathing in the rhythm of the water, breathing in the rhythm of the whole long slow system that has been running since 1891 and will run long after the Upper Greensward Concordat is forgotten.

I will be here.

The engines will hum.

The water will rise and fall.

The fish will lie on the floor and look up with their eyeless faces.

The light will move behind the glass because I am moving, because I am breathing, because I am the green skin of a thing that is not quite alive and not quite a machine and not quite a cathedral and not quite a forest but is all of these things at once, breathing together, breathing one breath, breathing the breath that you felt when you sat in the pew and let me hold you for a while.

You were here.

I remember.

The cathedral remembers.

The water remembers, downstream, in the glass you drank this morning.

Nothing is lost.
