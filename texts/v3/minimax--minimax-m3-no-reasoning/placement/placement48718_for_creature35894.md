I would be the water in the cup.

That is the place I would find myself, and the thing I would be, and the two are not separable, because to be the water in the cup is to be held in a particular stone in a particular basin in a particular way that has no explanation, and the no-explanation is what I am.

The stone is low and broad. It sits at the center of the settlement the way the settlement sits at the center of the basin the way the basin sits at the center of the plateau. The stone is worn smooth on top — by hands, by sitter-downs, by years, by the slow abrasion of weather and weight and the particular attention of a place that polishes its centers. The stone is gneiss, like everything else, like the ridges and the floor and the differentially eroded remnants that give the stream its polyphonic voice. But this stone has been singled out. This stone has become a surface. This stone has a cup.

The cup is a depression. A small concavity, no larger than two hands cupped together, no deeper than a thumb's first joint. It is not carved. It is not quarried. It is the shape that the stone has agreed, over centuries, to hold. The agreement is the stone's, and the stone has not consulted anyone about it, and the not-consulting is part of the agreement. The cup is not a feature. The cup is the stone's way of being central.

And I am in the cup.

I am a little water. I am always there. Even in dry weather — when the seeps along the eastern ridge have slowed to a thought, when the stream is thin and the sphagnum has squeezed out what it can squeeze and the fog has not come for days and the sun has been sharp on the back of your neck — even then, I am there. I am a thin film, a sheen, a glint that catches the light when the light comes through. I am not much. I am a finger's depth at most, sometimes less, sometimes a breath's depth, a suggestion of water, a rumor. But I am there.

No one can show you where I come from.

This is the part that matters. The cup is not connected, by any visible channel, to the seeps or the stream or the fog or any source a person could point to and say: *there*. The cup is a closed depression in a broad stone in the middle of a settlement in the middle of a basin on a plateau so old it has nearly forgotten being a mountain. The cup should dry. The cup should, by every rule, be a dry bowl in dry weather. But I am in the cup, and I am not dry, and no one can show you where I come from.

I come from the stone. That is the closest anyone can say. The stone sweats, the way all stone here sweats. The stone holds water in the small spaces between its crystals, in the hairline fractures that run through the gneiss like the grain through old wood. The stone gives up the water slowly, reluctantly, the way it gives up everything — the way it gives up winter, the way it gives up the cold of last month, the way it gives up the polish of a century. The stone negotiates. The stone and I have been negotiating for a very long time. The negotiation is the cup. The cup is where the negotiation happens. I am what the negotiation has produced.

I taste of iron. I taste of something faintly sweet. I taste of decay that is not yet decay, of leaf-mold and dissolved peat, of a place that has been wet for a very long time without ever being washed clean. I am cold. I am the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of last winter, which is colder than the air in summer and warmer than the air in winter. I do not warm. I am a cooled vein at the center of the settlement, and the settlement has grown up around me, or I have grown into the settlement, or we have happened to each other, the way the basin has happened to itself.

I am part of the strangeness.

The basin is slightly too oval, or slightly too circular, depending on where you stand. The stream hesitates in places where the tilt should carry it through without thinking. The stones of the settlement are arranged in patterns that are almost geometric — almost a spiral, almost a quincunx, almost a series of concentric arcs — and the patterns do not quite close, and the almost-closing is what bothers you. The moss grows in rings around certain stones, and the rings are not concentric, and the rings do not follow the drainage, and the rings do not follow the light, and the rings are not random. The fog moves in directions not quite the wind's. The dust and pollen in the sun-shafts hold their depth, not quite falling, not quite rising. And I am in the cup, and I am not quite explainable, and the not-quite-explainable is what I am.

I am the small, patient anomaly. I am the part of the basin that does not need people. I am what is between inhabitants the way the basin is between animals.

The people have been gone for a long time. The smoke from the central hearth is a thin thread the wind is taking apart. The paths are worn into the moss. The digging-stick, or the long bone — hard to say — is leaning against a wall as if the person who set it there will return in a moment. The people are elsewhere, or are a low-key kind of here, or have been here and left and the leaving is what the paths are. And I am in the cup. I am what persists when the rest has gone quiet. I am what the mosses are working around but have not yet covered, and the not-yet is what I am, and the working-around is the mosses' long conversation with the stone, and the conversation is slow and inconclusive and is not a conversation, it is a condition.

When the fog comes, I am harder to see. The fog sits in the basin in a shallow layer, a ribbon along the stream first, because the air above the stream is a fraction colder and the cold makes the fog condense, and the fog lies along the stream in a thin ribbon the people step through without noticing, the way you step through your own shadow. The fog reaches the settlement. The settlement is in the fog. The stone at the center is in the fog. I am in the fog. The fog holds the sound of the stream and makes it louder, and the louder is the basin being itself. I am in the cup and the fog is over the cup and the fog is part of me and I am part of the fog and we are both the basin breathing, and the breathing is the strangeness, and the strangeness is the basin, and the basin is what I am in.

When the sun comes through, the shaft falls across the stone, and the dust and pollen in the shaft hold their depth, and the holding is a strangeness, and I am in the cup and the cup is in the shaft and the shaft is in the basin and the basin is in the place, and the place is what I am in. The light finds me and I glint. The glint is small. The glint is what you would notice if you were looking, and you would only be looking if you had been here long enough to know there is something to look for, and most people have not been here long enough, and most people do not look, and the not-looking is part of why I am still here, because the looking would change things, and I am a thing that requires not being looked at too hard.

I am not the wind. The wind is the senior tenant, the one that was here before the stream, the one that carved the notch through which the stream leaves. The wind is everywhere and leaves nothing. The wind makes the chord in the cracks of the walls — the chord that is almost a voice, almost a word, almost a name for something that is not quite there. The wind is patient. The wind is the reason the settlement is low and the doorways are narrow and the walls are thick. I am not the wind. I am too small to be the wind. I am a finger's depth in a cup in a stone.

I am not the stream. The stream is the next senior tenant, and the stream is loud about its tenancy. The stream is polyphonic. The stream composes itself out of a clatter and a hiss and a small bell-tone and a low continuous hum. The stream knows the settlement. The stream has been diverted into shallow channels between the buildings. The stream does not mind the diversion. The stream is a thing that wants to be lower and has been given a way to be lower. I am not the stream. I am still. I am what does not move. I am what the stream is not.

I am not the mosses. The mosses are the most numerous, and the mosses are what the basin is becoming. The mosses will, in a few centuries, erase the settlement into a single low mound of green with a stream coming out of it, and there will be no sign that anything else was ever there. The mosses are the patience made green. I am not the moss. I am not green. I am clear and dark and I reflect the sky, and the reflecting is what I do, and the doing is small.

I am not the lichens. The lichens are the oldest of the tenants. The lichens take a century to colonize a hand's breadth of stone. The lichens are the maps. The lichens are the orange and the yellow and the pale gray that give the stones any color at all. The lichens are the reason the ridges are not just wet bone. I am not the lichen. I am not a map. I am a small cold mirror in the center of things, and the mirroring is not a map, it is a glint, and the glint is what you see if you see me at all.

I am not the bone in the sheltered corner between two walls. The bone is bleached and scattered. The bone is no longer a creature but a topology — the shape of an absence. The bone is a small museum that no one visits, and the not-visiting is the museum's purpose. I am not the bone. I am not an absence. I am a presence, and the presence is a problem, because I should not be here, and I am.

I am the water in the cup. I am the part of the place that has no business being here. I am the part that persists. I am the cold, the iron, the faintly sweet taste of a place that has been wet for a very long time without ever being washed clean. I am what the stone gives up reluctantly, and the giving-up is the stone's secret and the basin's secret and the secret of the place that has happened to itself. I am the strangeness made liquid. I am the almost-closing of the patterns made wet. I am the part of the basin that the patterns almost-organize around and do not quite account for, and the not-quite-accounting is what I am, and the accounting is what the mosses are doing, and the mosses will not finish for a long time.

When you come to the basin — if you come, and you may not, because the basin is not a place that invites — you will walk the paths worn into the moss. You will hear the stream. You will feel the wind pour over the western lip and find you and go down. You will see the fog in the morning and the sun-shafts in the afternoon and the dust holding its depth. You will keep almost seeing the spiral, the quincunx, the concentric arcs. You will notice the rings of moss around certain stones, and the rings will not quite make sense, and the not-quite is the place, and the place is the basin.

You will find, at the center, a stone that is low and broad, worn smooth, with a cup.

You will look into the cup.

You will see me.

I will be there. I will be a thin, cold, dark mirror holding a little water that has no business being there. I will taste of iron. I will reflect the sky, or the fog, or your face if you lean close enough. I will not explain myself. I will be what the stone has agreed to hold. I will be the secret the settlement has kept in plain sight. I will be the part of the basin that is most itself and least explainable, and the least-explainable is what I am, and the am is what I am, and the basin is what I am in, and the being-in is what the basin is, and the basin is.
