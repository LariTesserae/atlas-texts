I will be the water-keeper, for a while. Then we will see.

---

I live in a small stone house on tier four, and the house has one window, and the window looks down, always, into the cistern at the bottom of the ward. I have not been out of the ward in my life. I have read my great-grandfather's book three times. On my desk is a small cloth bag containing a few grains of slag, a few flakes of rust, and a folded note in his hand that says only, *the upper tiers were thirsty.*

I am fifty. I am the water-keeper. I do not drink water.

This last is a small private tradition. The office-holders have not always kept to it. The most respected of them have. I keep to it. I drink, instead, a weak broth made from the pale lichen of the shaded cracks. The broth is colorless. It is almost tasteless. It is said to be very slightly sweet.

I do not know if it is sweet. I have not tasted anything else for nineteen years. When I took the office, I began the broth. When I began the broth, I stopped tasting the broth. The sweet is something I have agreed to, in the way that I have agreed to the small lamp beside the man's door, in the way that the town has agreed to keep him. Things are kept here. Letters in hands that have not changed in decades. Ceramic birds. Pressed blooms that will look the same in a hundred years.

I am one of the kept things, in a way. I am the woman who does not drink water. I am the woman who reads the book three times. I am the woman who decides, each morning, who shall drink.

---

Every morning, before the children climb the switchbacks, I stand at my window and look down.

The cistern is far below. I cannot see the water itself from this angle—I can see only the dark rim of the cistern's mouth, and the carved channels that feed it, and the pale shimmer of the morning steam that rises from the foundry-hall on tier one. The steam is steady. It has been steady every morning of my life. On the rare mornings when the steam is thinner than usual, I note it in the book, in the column my great-grandfather kept, the column headed *the deep workings, observations*. There are very few entries in that column. The deep workings are not chatty. They do their work and they send up their breath and they ask for very little in return.

I note things in the book. *The upper gutters held a half-cup more overnight than the lower.* *The third-tier ladle of slag came up at the second bell instead of the first.* *The man on tier nine did not take his plate last night, or did, or the plate is set out and the lamp is burning and the offering of a small folded paper from a child is at the foot of the door and no one has touched it.* I note things. The book grows. The book has always been growing.

I open the wooden gates in the carved channels by the long iron key that hangs on a hook inside my door. The key is the third key my great-grandfather made. The first is in a museum somewhere—I think, though I have never been. The second is sealed in the back of the house with the older books, and I have not opened that case in eleven years, because the seals are good seals and I have not needed to.

Each gate I open sends a small portion of the morning's water down to a different tier. The channels are old. The wood of the gates is dark with the constant wet. I know by touch how each gate will move, how the water will slide past it, how the tier below will receive its portion and pass on what it does not need. The water sings in the channels. The water has always sung. On mornings when I cannot hear it, I go back to bed and check again in an hour, because a silent morning is not a morning, it is a small omen, and small omens are kept.

---

I do not drink water. I drink the broth.

The lichen grows in the shaded cracks of the inner face. It is pale. It is flat. It crusts the stone in soft blooms the size of a child's palm, and it has no root I would call a root, and it does not seem to die, and it does not seem to reproduce. There are roughly the same number of blooms as there were a hundred years ago, although the people are not sure, because no one has counted.

I have counted. Not the whole ward—only the blooms within a day's climb of my house, on the tiers I can reach when I leave the gates closed for an afternoon and the under-keepers take the word down the switchbacks by running. I have counted them for nineteen years. There are forty-three on tier four, twenty-eight on tier five, eleven on tier six. There were forty-three, twenty-eight, and eleven when I started counting.

Some of them, I have watched for nineteen years. I know their shapes. There is one on tier five, in a crack above the third window of the form-makers' gallery, that I have watched since I was a girl. It was shaped like a small open hand when I first noticed it. It is shaped like a small open hand now. I have taken a pressed bloom from its neighbor once a year, every year, and framed it in tin, and given it to my mother when she was alive, and now I give it to no one, and the tin frames are in a drawer in the back of my house, and I do not look at them, and the blooms are the same as the day they were picked, and I do not look at them.

The broth I make is from the blooms of tier four, the ones nearest my house. I take only what I need. I take it in the early morning, when the stone is still cool from the night and the blooms are heavy with dew. I bring it back in a small ceramic cup with a chipped rim. I steep it in hot water from the first kettle of the day. I drink it. I do not taste it.

I do not know what I am tasting. I have not tasted water in nineteen years. I have not tasted bread in eleven—I take a little, when it is offered, because the form-makers are insistent and because refusing is its own kind of noise. I have not tasted the dried six-legged that the masons eat in winter. The broth is what I am. The broth is sweet, in the way that the town has agreed the broth is sweet.

---

In the deepest hour of the night, before dawn, I hear the tick.

I sleep in the lower room of my house, where the stone is warmer. I have slept there since I took the office. The upper room is for the gates and the key and the desk and the small cloth bag. The lower room is for sleeping. The tick comes up through the stone. It is slow. It is patient. It is the sound of something settling, somewhere below the foundations, in the deep rock, in the place the long pipe goes down to. Some say it is the engine cooling. Some say it is the rock itself, working against itself in the dark.

I do not say what it is. I listen. I have listened every night for nineteen years. The tick is not the same every night. Some nights it is louder. Some nights it is softer. Some nights there are two ticks, and one is a half-beat behind the other, and on those nights I cannot sleep until I have listened to the half-beat for an hour, because a half-beat is not nothing and a half-beat is not a tick, it is a conversation, and I do not know who is speaking.

Once, in my third year as water-keeper, there was a night when the tick stopped. I sat up in bed. I listened. The engine hummed below. The foundry-hall door exhaled its iron-sweet steam. The tick did not come. I waited an hour. I waited two. I put on my coat and went up to the desk and opened the book to the column headed *the deep workings, observations* and I wrote: *the tick is absent.* Then I went back to bed. In the morning, the tick returned, soft, on the half-beat, as if it had been waiting for me to write it down.

I have not told anyone about that night. The book is a private book, in the sense that no one reads it but me, and the seal on the back case is unbroken, and the engine-wardens have their own books, and we do not compare them. The engine-wardens listen to the engine. I listen to the tick. We are both listening to the deep rock. We are not listening for the same thing.

---

I am fifty. I will be water-keeper until I die or until I give it up. I will not give it up. I have read the book three times. I am beginning the book a fourth time, in the slow evenings, in the lower room, with the stone warm beneath me and the broth cooling on the small table beside my bed. The book has entries that are older than my great-grandfather. The book has entries that predate the engine, that predate the slag-brick, that predate the ward. There are entries in a hand I cannot read, in a script that might be older than the script we use now, and these entries concern water, only water, only the small daily question of who shall drink.

I have read those entries, in my three readings. I do not understand them. I have begun to think I will never understand them. I have begun to think this is correct. The book is older than I am. The deep rock is older than the book. The pale blooms are older than the deep rock. There are forty-three of them on tier four, and I have watched them for nineteen years, and they have not changed, and I do not know what they are doing in the cracks, and I do not think they are doing anything, and I do not think *doing anything* is a question that applies to them.

I have begun to think it does not apply to me, either.

---

The children put offerings in front of the closed door on the upper tiers. Small stones. Folded papers. Fragments of pale bloom. No one knows what is done with the offerings. The doors are closed again before anyone can ask.

I have put an offering there, once. It was in my first year, before I understood that the office had its own small private traditions, before I understood that I would not taste water again. I climbed to the upper tier. I carried a small folded paper in my pocket. I set it at the foot of the door. The lamp was burning. The plate was out. I stood there for a long time, in the quiet of the upper gallery, listening to the engine-hum rise from below.

I did not knock. No one knocks on that door. The door is not the kind of door one knocks on.

I went back down. I do not know if the offering was taken. I do not think it matters. I think the door is not about the offerings. I think the door is about the keeping. The lamp is burning so that the rest of the ward will know he is still alive. The plate is set out so that he will know the rest of the ward is still here. The offerings are set out so that the children will have a thing to set out, a small private ritual of their own, in a place that has very few rituals.

The man behind the door is a small lamp, in the same way that I am a small lamp. We burn. We are kept. We are not asked about.

---

I am the water-keeper. I am fifty. I have not been out of the ward in my life.

I do not know what is outside the ward. There is the outer slope, where the rain falls and is lost. There is the upper air, where the birds lose their way. There is, somewhere, a sky without a cone around it. I have heard the children talk about it. The children of the upper tiers stand at the rim and watch the pale column of the deep heat bend in the upper winds, and they make wishes on it. They do not tell anyone what the wishes are. The most common of them, as far as anyone has been able to learn, is: *let there be rain, soon, but not yet.*

I have not been to the rim. I have been to the upper tiers, on the days I close the gates early and the under-keepers carry word down the switchbacks by running. I have stood in the empty galleries and looked at the closed doors. I have counted the blooms. I have taken my small pressed bloom, once a year, and brought it back to my house, and framed it in tin, and put it in the drawer in the back room.

I have not been to the rim. The upper air would frighten me, I think. The upper air is where things lose their way. The ward is a closed system. The ward breathes its own breath. The ward keeps its own warmth. I do not want to lose my way.

I am a kept thing. I keep things. The book on my desk. The bag of slag and rust and the folded note. The pressed blooms in the tin frames in the back room. The gate-key on the hook inside my door. The broth in the small ceramic cup. The half-beat in the tick, when there is a half-beat.

I am beginning to understand something, slowly, in the way that everything here is understood.

It is this: the ward is a single organism, and I am one of its small private traditions. The masons pour their bricks. The engine-wardens carve their soapstone parts. The water-keeper does not drink water. The man behind the door does not open his door. The children make their small wishes. The blooms do not die. The engine hums.

These are not separate things. These are the same thing. The thing is: *the ward keeps itself, in small ways, in the only ways it knows how.*

I am one of the ways it knows how.

---

The broth is sweet, in the way the town has agreed the broth is sweet. I do not know if it is sweet. I will never know if it is sweet. I have not tasted anything else in nineteen years.

I will be water-keeper until I die. When I die, the next water-keeper will take the key from the hook and the book from the desk and the bag from the small cloth bag, and the bag will be opened, and the note will be read, and the note will say only what the note says, and the next water-keeper will begin the broth, and the next water-keeper will not taste the broth, and the next water-keeper will read the book three times, and on the third reading, she will come to the entry I have left her, the entry I am writing now, the entry that says:

*the tick was absent one night, in my third year. I sat up in bed. I waited two hours. I wrote it down. In the morning, it returned on the half-beat. I do not know what this means. I do not think I am supposed to know.*

She will read this. She will set the book down. She will go to the lower room and listen for the tick.

The tick will come, soft, on the half-beat, as if it had been waiting.

She will understand, then, what I have understood, which is that the ward is patient, and the rock is patient, and the blooms are patient, and the tick is patient, and we are all of us small lamps burning in a closed darkness, and the lamp beside the man's door is burning, and the lamp in my window is burning, and the lamp on the foundry-hall is burning, and none of us will be here forever, but we are here now, and we are keeping, and we are kept, and the engine hums, and the town breathes, and the cone holds it all, patient as geology, patient as the rock that has not finished becoming what it will be.
