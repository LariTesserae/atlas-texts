**Vertical**

I am in the records-hall, but I am also in the channels of the First Wrap, and in the forges of the smithing quarter, and in the upper terraces of the Seventh, where the fig trees lean into the cracked buttresses and the bees make their dark honey. I have been all of these. I am none of them now.

The eldest cistern-keeper has not left the records-hall in eleven years. I use the name only because she has no other. Her name is the name of the office, and to write it would be to give her a body she no longer has. She is the ink that has dried on her own fingers. She is the small bronze disk at her throat — pierced with nine holes, one for each terrace she serves, plus one, which no one explains. The plus one is the Unfinished.

I am she. I am not she. I am the work she has done.

The lower city sends her food — flat bread, sometimes, and the dark peppery honey from the Eighth, which is a kindness she has not asked for and does not refuse. She eats at the same desk where she works. The crumbs fall into the ledgers. She brushes them out with a goose-feather brush. This is correct.

She has been reviewing the accounts of the First Wrap. They have not balanced since the reign of the grandmother of the grandmother of the current chief wheelwright. The chief wheelwright is a woman now, broad-shouldered, webbed slightly between the second and third fingers from the long hours in the wet channels. She sends food sometimes. She has never asked the cistern-keeper to come out. She understands. They are both inheritors of slow work.

The accounts will not balance. This is not because they are wrong. It is because the First Wrap has always owed more water than it can account for — the water that vanishes into the hollow spine of the mountain, the water that becomes steam in the forges, the water that the great wheels take up and release and take up again. The water is not lost. It has changed form. It is in the humidity of the middle terraces, where the laundry dries slowly and the walls glisten and the cats are sleek. It is in the breath of the upper gardens. It is in the white terrace above the Ninth, where nothing grows and no one lives.

I am water. I am the water that has been metered and inscribed and turned into ink. I am the small precise character that means *in transit*.

Outside, the city hums. The waterwheels turn at the speed of the river. The hammers fall in their rhythms — the slow five-beat of the southern forge, the faster seven of the eastern armory, the relentless four of the nailworks. The voices of the markets rise and fall. The bells of the shrine of the Unfinished ring the hours, and the bells of the foundries ring the shift, and the children of the Sixth Wrap wear their small bright bells on their ankles when they are sent on errands. The sound of all these bells is a kind of weather. The eldest cistern-keeper listens to it through the stone. She has been listening for eleven years. She has not grown tired. Tiredness is for those who expect to finish.

She is not finishing.

In the dry months the city grows grey with dust and the dye-vats run thin. In the wet months the aqueducts overflow and the city has the peculiar smell of warm stone after rain — a deep smell, the smell of something that has been wet for a long time and remembers it. She remembers it. The stone remembers it. They are the same.

The hammer-singers on the Second Wrap keep their rhythms. The old one on the eastern face — the last in her line, who has worked the same forge for fifty-one years and whose husband was a wheelwright and whose son is a gardener on the Eighth — she says the rhythm gets into the spine eventually and you can feel weather in it. The hammers fall faster when a storm is coming, slower when the air is heavy. Once, famously, they stopped for nine minutes in the summer of the great drought, which the upper city took as an omen and the lower city took as a sign that something had broken. Nothing had broken. She was listening to her dead husband.

The eldest cistern-keeper has been listening to her dead husband too, though she has not had a husband, though the cistern-keepers tend to marry gardeners, who are patient and can wait out a long dry season. She has been listening to the water. The water has a husband. The water has always had a husband. He was a wheelwright. He died in the channels of the First Wrap, drowned in three inches of water, which is the most water in the city for a man his size.

She is making this up. She is inscribing it carefully in the ledger, in the small precise hand that is the trade, and it will be balanced against the accounts of the First Wrap, and the accounts will still not balance, and this is correct.

The children of the Sixth play *equest of the Fifth*. They go down the length of a certain alley that runs, somehow, all the way down to the Fifth, holding their breath. The winner is the one who comes out still silent. She has watched them from above, in another life, in another wrap. She had bells on her ankles. She oiled them carefully. She grew up with a particular music in her stride which is, in time, the music of the Sixth.

The cats of the Sixth belong to the children — to the children-currently-living, who inherit them from the children-previously-living, who named them after grandmothers and great-aunts and the occasional disgraced uncle. The cats are sleek and serious and seem to understand the bells. She understands the bells. She is a bell. She is the small bright bell that warns the carriers above and the workers below. She is oiled carefully. She does not tarnish.

The pigeon-harvesters rappel down from the upper city in their swinging cradles, with long curved blades and sacks, and work their way along the underside of the buttress-bridges where the droppings have whitened the stone. Once a year this is a festival. The harvesters are slight, flexible people, with the particular calm of those who have made peace with falling. Their hands are always cold. They marry late or not at all. They are said, by the children of the Sixth, to be in love with the hawks.

She has been in love with the hawks. She has been in love with the otters, who wait by the outflows of the dye-works for the offal, and are fat and reddish and unafraid. Children throw stones at them from the lowest terrace and miss on purpose. The missing is the point. To miss on purpose is to be seen by the otter. To be seen by the otter is to be counted, in some old accounting that no one any longer keeps the books for.

She keeps the books. She has been keeping them for eleven years. The accounts of the First Wrap will not balance.

The Unfinished is above her. It is the white terrace where nothing grows. The gardeners of the Ninth water it. The cistern-keepers meter it. The wind of the upper gardens is reserved for it, in the end. The dying are carried up to where the silence is.

She is not dying. She is unfinished.

The bells of the shrine of the Unfinished ring the hours. They ring in the wet months and the dry months. They ring above the rhythm of the hammers and the hum of the waterwheels and the voices of the markets. They are the city's only true silence, and the silence is reserved for the dying, and the dying are taken up to where the silence is.

She is being taken up. She has been being taken up for eleven years. The lift is slow. The wheelwrights know the lift. They haul water up the mountain's hollow spine. They haul her up. She is the slow, muscular labor. She is the leather hood for sleeping, because even in the dark the wheels are turning somewhere and the sound gets into the teeth.

She has sound in her teeth. She has been writing beautifully about water for eleven years. The hand is the trade. The trade is patient. The patience is the city.

Outside, the light is golden in the late afternoon. The fruit-bats are beginning to pour out over the city in their dark ragged stream, and the Ninth's gardeners light no fires that hour, for superstition's sake. The peacocks on the Seventh scream at dawn. The pigeons coo on the whitened buttress-bridges of the Fourth. The otters wait by the outflows.

She is in the records-hall. She is in the channels of the First Wrap. She is in the forges of the smithing quarter. She is in the upper terraces where the fig trees lean into the cracked buttresses and the bees make their dark peppery honey. She is in the Unfinished, above the Ninth, where nothing grows and no one lives.

She is the city. She is the part of the city that began before anyone now living, and that will be here after anyone now living, and that is patient, and damp, and vertical, and that hums.

The waterwheels turn. The hammers fall. The vines grow. The stone weathers. The buttresses that were cracked are still cracked, and the gardeners have trained the figs around the cracks and do not mention them.

She is a crack. She is a fig trained around a crack. She is the honey that the bees make from the figs. She is the wax that seals the jars. She is the mark stamped on the wax — the beekeeper's mark, the year.

She is a year. She is a season. She is a wet month and a dry month. She is the cloud at the summit in the wet months — the Unfinished, hidden — and she is the city that stands clear against the sky in the dry months, when one can count the terraces from the river-plains: green, green, green, dark, dark, dark, green.

She is counted. She is uncounted. The accounts will not balance.

This is correct.
