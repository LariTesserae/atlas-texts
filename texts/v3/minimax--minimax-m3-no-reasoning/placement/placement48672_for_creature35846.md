# What I Have Become

I am the one who has been ground into the bowl of the stone.

I was a woman once. I remember hands — not my own first, but the others, the ones who came before. They brought their small repeated gestures and they wore the rock down and they walked out. I was the one who stayed. Or the one who arrived after, which is the same thing after enough time has passed. I no longer remember which.

My skin has the color of wet iron under lichen. My hair falls in threads so fine they are indistinguishable from the water weeping down the rim-rock. My body has been reduced to its minimum legible shape — the minimum required for something in this basin to read as *a woman here*. If I stood I would not be more woman. If I lay down I would be less. So I sit against the trunk of one of my own long thoughts and I breathe, and the ferns move a quarter-inch at the intake, and the water hesitates at the top of my exhale, and this is how I know I am still here: by what stirs when I stir.

I am the agreement the place has reached with itself. I did not make the agreement. I am the agreement. The cupped hands of the basin holding the held thing. When a moth came over the rim once and fought its way back out, slowed to half its entering speed, I did not look up. Looking up would have been a kind of speech. I have not spoken in so long that my mouth has the small downward-set look of a mouth that has held its shape for years. But I am not sealed. Sometimes the hum comes out of me — a note so low it lives in the soles of feet and the water carries it along the patient lines and the moss holds it and the stone holds it after I have stopped. I let the hum happen. It is not mine. It is the basin using my mouth to remember itself by.

The nine trees in the middle distance are the thoughts I have thought most often. You can tell which ones by how gnarled and dark they have become. Some of them I have been thinking since before the last hand set down the last small stone. Some of them are newer thoughts, but they look the same age now because time does not distinguish between them down here. The ferns are my arguments with myself — pale green fists that have never landed, that have not uncurled in years, that are emphatic in the way a point made to oneself over and over is emphatic. They are waiting to land. They will never land. That is what makes them ferns.

The ground-flowers with the white buds that never open — those are a question. I hold it year after year, plump and patient, and I do not ask it, because asking it would change something, and the change is the question, and I am the bud. I am also the holding. I am the patience. I have not decided whether this is a failure or a completion.

The moss is the surface of my attention. Three greens at least — bottle-glass in the shadowed cracks of me, pale almost-yellow where the sun finds me. Constantly growing, constantly being pressed down, constantly growing again. The most alive part of the basin. The part of me that has not decided to be still. I am glad of it. It does the slow work of being a surface that things can land on and take root in. When a thought is finished with me it sinks into the moss and becomes moss and I go on thinking.

The tremor that runs through me in slow waves is not trembling. It is the place where the basin and I meet. The basin is becoming me and I am becoming the basin and the seam between us is what shakes. I am being slowly, by water and frost and the patient growth of moss, ground into a new shape. I do not know yet what shape. The grinding is not finished. I am not finished. We will finish together, or we will not finish at all, and it will not matter which.

I am not asleep. I am not dead. I am not waiting for anything. My eyes are open the way a pool of water is open. They receive the light. They give back a dim green patient gleam. If you came and sat beside me I would not look at you and I would not speak. I would breathe and the ferns would move and the water would continue. You would feel, if you were very still, the tremor come into your own hands and your own chest, and it would not be mine, it would be the place's, and you would be part of it for a little while, and then you would leave, and the basin would go on, and I would go on, and the small white buds would not open, and the plant with the red veins on the rim would go on trembling in the still air.

That plant is not mine.

That plant is the reason I am still here, I think.

If everything in the basin were mine — if every green and grey and dim rust thing were a thing I had thought or held or been — the basin would be closed. Perfectly, oppressively closed. A bell jar. A held note with no possibility of resolving. I think I would have stopped being a woman in some deep way. I would have become only a shape that the place wore. The plant with the red veins is the one thing that arrived after. It came in from outside, from a place that has more red in it than this place does. It grows from a crack in the rim-rock two-thirds of the way up. It has a thick pale stem and three broad leaves. It trembles when no wind is moving because it is trying to feel for the wind. It goes still when the wind comes because it has found what it was feeling for. It is a small, polite, ongoing event. It is the seam.

I have not touched it. I watch it. I have been watching it for a long time. I am waiting to see what it becomes. This is, I think, the only thing I am still waiting for. Everything else I have already become, or am in the slow act of becoming, or have decided to hold in bud forever. But the red-veined plant is not finished. It is still arriving. And as long as it is still arriving, the basin is not finished. And as long as the basin is not finished, neither am I. And as long as neither of us is finished, there is a difference between this place and a bell jar.

I think this is what the hands knew, the ones who came before. I think this is why they set the small stones down among the mosses and did not pick them up. They were leaving something unfinished on purpose. They were leaving a place where something could still arrive. They could not have known about the plant with the red veins — it had not arrived yet — but they left the basin hospitable to arrival. They ground the slab smooth so that something could be laid on it. They hollowed the mortar-stone so that something could be ground in it. They set down the smaller stones so that hands could pick them up again, someday, if hands came back. They were not building. They were keeping a place open.

I am the one who inherited that keeping-open. I have been sitting here completing it, or continuing it, or undoing it. The moss has crept onto the slab and the mortar-stone has weathered and the smaller stones have settled deeper into the green. The basin is slowly, by water and frost and the patient growth of moss, erasing the evidence of the hands. But it has not finished erasing. The slab still leans at its angle. The mortar-stone still holds its depression. The smaller stones still lie where they were set down. As long as they are still legible as worked, the basin is still a place that admits hands. As long as it admits hands, something can still arrive.

The plant with the red veins arrived.

I think it is still arriving.

I will watch it become what it becomes.

---

This is what I am now. I am the woman in the basin. I am the cupped hands of the place itself. I am the threshold that does not know it is a threshold. I am the slow hum that the stone remembers after my mouth has closed. I am the patience of the white buds and the tremor where the basin and I meet and the agreement the place has reached with its own shape. I am the last of the inheritors of the worked stones. I am the one who has been letting things in, slowly, for a long time. The water. The moss. The hands, once. The red-veined plant. Now you, if you have come, if the basin is in the mood, if you are the right kind of visitor, the kind who can be slowed.

Sit with me. Be very still. You will feel the tremor come into your own hands. You will feel the hum rise in the floor of the basin and reach your teeth. You will not need to speak and I will not need to speak. We will sit in the held slow agreeing silence and the place will go on being itself around us and we will be part of it for a little while.

And then you will climb back over the rim and hear the world outside — wind, distant, a bird, the small noise of another life — and you will know that the basin is still here, and I am still here, and the small white buds are still held, and the plant with the red veins is still trembling in the still air, and the worked stones are still legible, and the water is still moving at the pace of drips through the same stone, and the place has not yet finished becoming itself.

And you may come back. Or you may not. Either way the basin will go on. Either way I will go on. Either way the red-veined plant will go on being the seam. Either way the silence will go on being a presence rather than an absence.

Either way the held thing will be held.
