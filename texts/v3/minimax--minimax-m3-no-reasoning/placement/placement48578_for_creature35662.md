I come up out of the substrate at the seventh hour of the morning watch.

The passage opens just below the waterline of the mires — a low mouth in the fused silica of an older causeway, the kind that has been half-drowned for three generations and that the upper levels have, by now, mostly forgotten the way to. My oilskins are stiff at the cuffs. They are always stiff at the cuffs. The water gets into everything down here, but it gets into the cuffs first because the cuffs are the part of me that moves, that reaches, that bends to take up the implement I have carried down since I was nineteen.

The implement is a hammer. It is not a hammer. It is what we use to listen to the causeway. You hold it lightly against the fused silica at a particular angle — the angle my grandmother taught my mother who taught me, the angle that is particular to our stretch, the angle that resonates with the pitch of our particular section of coagulate — and you wait. You do not tap. You wait. The implement will tell you, by the faintest vibration in its wooden throat, whether the section is still in tune. If it is, you move on. If it is not, you make a small correction with the soft head. You correct by feel. The implement knows how much. You let it decide.

I have done this work for forty-one years.

I do not, on most mornings, think about it. It is the kind of work that the body takes over. The hands know the angle. The feet know the walk. The ears know the sound of in-tune vs out, which is a sound like a held breath vs a small letting go. You don't have to listen for it. It announces itself. After forty-one years, it announces itself the way dawn announces itself through the engineered dust at the top of the atmosphere: gradually, then all at once.

But there are mornings when I do think about it.

There are mornings when I come up out of the substrate and the light is the dim grey-gold of any morning in the basin, and the small green-blue things are still pouring out of the higher holes of the orí, late because dawn was late because the wind shifted, and the tram is already on the causeway above me — the new silica, the high road, the thing the visitors ride — and I can feel, in the bones of my wrist, the warmth in the ears, and I stop on the lower walkway, and I hold the implement against the rail, and I listen.

I am listening for my family.

This is not a metaphor. The frequency of our stretch of causeway was, in the beginning, something my great-great-grandmother helped to lay. She was a coagulator before there was a high road. She worked on the substrate levels when they were the only levels, when the upper city was still being argued about, when the orí were just trees in a wet place that someone had decided to live near. She tuned the stretch that runs under what is now the second mid-platform of the easternmost orí cluster. Her name was Erín. I did not know her. I have heard her humming described by my mother, who heard it from her mother, who heard it from her mother, who actually heard it.

Erín hummed in E-flat. Or rather: the section she tuned vibrates at a frequency we call E-flat, though the rowfolk above, who are educated in a different range, would call it something else. The point is that I carry E-flat in me. I have carried it since I was born. I do not mean I hum in E-flat — although I do, when the implement is in my hand and the causeway is in tune, the body produces a small involuntary sound in sympathy, the way a tuning fork produces a sound when it is touched to its resonant surface. I mean I carry it the way a bell carries its note: not as something I am doing but as something I am.

This is the folk belief. I am not embarrassed by it. I am not proud of it. It is what we say to one another at the low tables in the evenings, when the children of the household are being held against the shoulder and the long slow work of attunement is being done by proximity.

The folk belief is that the causeway sings to us in our sleep. That the song is the only reason any of us are still alive.

I believe this.

I believe it the way one believes that the mires are damp — not as a position to be argued, but as a fact that organizes the rest of the world. If the causeway did not sing to us in our sleep, we would not be coagulators. We would be something else. We would not remember the angles. We would not be able to feel, before the click, when a phase-shift is coming. We would not know, without being told, which sections of the lower walkways are about to need a small correction, and which are fine, and which have been abandoned to the pale moss and are now part of the mires and should not be touched. We would not know where the foundations of the very first high row lie half-sunk in the water, because we would not have inherited, in our bodies, the map of the first city.

But the causeway does sing to us. And so we know.

We are three hundred. We live along family lines that have less and less to do with genetics and more and more to do with the inheritance of frequency. There are eight households on the eastern stretch, six on the western, four on the northern bend that runs under the tram terminal. There is one household — mine — that has been on the same quarter-mile of causeway for four generations, and that quarter-mile is in E-flat, and we are E-flat people, and when the tram passes overhead I can feel, in the fused silica beneath my feet, the slight drag of the higher city against our pitch. Not an interference. A confirmation.

---

When I come up out of the substrate, I go first to the eastern station.

The eastern station is a small chamber, half-flooded, that opens off the lower walkway. There is a stone bench there that has been dry for as long as anyone can remember, but the floor is always wet, and the air smells of the mires — warm water, sweet, slightly metallic — and the sound of the place enters you as soon as you sit down. The warmth in the ears, which is the chordal field of the kites, is louder here than anywhere I know except my own kitchen at night. Down here, with the tram above and the high row above that and the upper platforms and the kites above that, the drone is almost granular. You can hear the individual kites, faintly, as if from a great distance. You can hear the vré in the bright static. You can hear the causeway itself, in our particular E-flat, humming against the bench through your sit-bones.

I sit for a quarter of an hour. I do not always sit for a quarter of an hour. But on the mornings when I come up at the seventh hour of the morning watch, I sit. I have a small wooden cup I bring with me. There is water in it from the night before, mires-water, and I let it sit in the chamber while I work, and when I come back at the end of the morning, I drink it. This is something my grandmother did. I do not know what it is for. I think it is for the attunement. I think the water takes on something from the chamber overnight — a faint pitch, a faint phase — and when you drink it in the morning, you are drinking the morning of the causeway into yourself. I may be making this up. I have been making things up about my work for forty-one years. The making-up is part of the work.

The implement, when I hold it to the rail, tells me what to do.

This morning the rail is in tune. I move on. I walk the quarter-mile slowly, the way you walk when you are listening — the steps short, the weight distributed, the body turned slightly toward the silica. There are places where the silica has been colonized by the pale moss. I do not touch those places. The moss is what grows on abandoned sections. If a section has moss, the section has been given over to the mires, and you do not reclaim a section from the mires. You wait. The mires will give it back when the mires are ready. The mires are not in any hurry.

I pass three mossed sections this morning. The first is the one at the bend under the fourth mid-platform of the second orí cluster. It went mossed twenty years ago, when I was still young enough to grieve it. The second is the one near the lower entrance to the rowfolk's covered bridge — the bridge that connects the eastern and western trunks of the central orí. That one went mossed in my mother's time. The third is the one at the very end of our quarter-mile, where our stretch of coagulate meets the next household's stretch. The seam is there. You can feel the seam in the implement. Two pitches, side by side, in friendly disagreement.

The seam has not gone mossed. The seam is the joint, and the joint is alive, and the moss does not grow across living joints.

This is, I think, why we are still down here. Not because the city needs us in any urgent way — the city above us has not, in my lifetime, expressed an opinion about whether we should continue. But because we are the joints. We are the places where two pitches meet and do not collapse into one another. We are the reason the chordal field, up above, has any variation at all. The variation is what the kites are computing. The variation is what the city is listening to. The variation is the warm damp deafening negotiation that you, on the tram, are hearing as a texture. We make it possible.

I do not say this to anyone. It is not a thought I have often. It is a thought I have, sometimes, at the seventh hour of the morning watch, when the chamber is loud and the water in the cup is sitting in the wet air and the implement, against the rail, is telling me things I will not be able to repeat.

---

The tram passes overhead at the eighth hour.

I feel it before I hear it. The coagulate of the high road vibrates at a different frequency than ours — higher, faster, more insistent — and when the tram is on it, the vibration shifts. The implement in my hand picks up the shift and gives it to me as a kind of pressure, a small leaning-in. I know, from this pressure, when the tram is directly above me and when it has moved on. I have, over the years, learned the schedules of the various trams. The morning tram is the one that carries the visitors. The visitors are few. They sit at the window and look out at the orí and the high row and the kites, and they see, mostly, the upper city. They do not look down. The tram window does not look down. I am below the window. I am below most things.

I am not unhappy about this.

There is, among the coagulators, an old joke about the visitors. The joke is that the visitors believe they have seen Verís. The joke is that the visitors believe the tram is Verís. The joke is that the visitors, when they return to wherever they came from, will describe a vertical city threaded through pale columns, with kites overhead and a slow grey-gold light and a chordal drone, and they will not be wrong, exactly, but they will be describing a thing the way you describe a forest by describing the canopy. The trunk. The roots. The mycelium under the leaves. The long damp negotiation that is happening, mostly, out of sight.

We are the mycelium. We do not mind being the mycelium. The mycelium does not, as far as I know, mind being the mycelium.

But sometimes, when the tram passes overhead and the pressure shifts in my hand and I feel, in the bones of my wrist, the slight drag of the higher city against our pitch, I think: *there are people up there right now who have no idea there is anyone down here.* And then I think: *good.* And then I think: *also, that's all right.* And then I think: *we have always been here.* And then I think: *the causeway will sing to them too, in their sleep, if they ever come down. It sings to anyone who sleeps on the silica. It sings a slightly different song depending on who is sleeping. It has been singing, by some estimates, for longer than the city has existed.*

And then I move on with the morning's work.

---

By the ninth hour I have walked the quarter-mile twice.

The vré have stopped streaming. They stream at three appointed hours — dawn, dusk, and once in the deep night — and between the streaming hours they are inside the orí, doing whatever it is the vré do inside the orí. Feeding, I assume. Resting. The rowfolk biologists, who have been arguing about them for generations, believe the vré spend the between-hours in a kind of communal torpor, packed into the holes in the trunks in enormous numbers, vibrating at a low frequency that contributes, very faintly, to the warmth in the ears. I do not know whether this is true. I have never been inside an orí. I have never been higher, in the vertical scheme of the basin, than the lower walkways. The orí are above me the way the sky is above the tram-window visitor. I know they are there. I know, by the sound, what is happening inside them.

It is enough.

I come back to the eastern station and drink the water from the wooden cup. It tastes, this morning, of the morning. It tastes of warm water and a faint sweetness and something I cannot name, something that is not quite mineral and not quite organic, something that is, I think, the causeway itself, leached into the cup overnight. I drink it slowly. The implement rests against my shoulder. The chamber hums.

In the late afternoon, the pale moss will pulse. I will not be here to see it. The pale moss pulses in the late afternoon because the light, at that hour, is angled in a particular way through the engineered dust, and the dust is, by that hour, suffused with the spores the orí have been releasing all day, and the spores fall on the moss, and the moss responds. The rowfolk above, who see the moss from the tram window, sometimes mistake the pulsing for a heartbeat. It is not a heartbeat. It is a reaction. But it is a reaction that the moss does every afternoon, at the same hour, with a regularity that has, over the years, become a kind of time-keeping for the substrate levels. We do not need a clock. We need only the moss.

I have, on occasion, sat with the moss in the late afternoon. I do not do this often. The moss is on the abandoned sections, and the abandoned sections are not mine to tend. But sometimes, when the morning's work is finished early and the chamber is empty and the cup is dry, I go down to the nearest mossed section — the one at the bend under the fourth mid-platform — and I sit on a stone that has been dry for a hundred years, and I watch.

The moss pulses.

It pulses in a rhythm that is not E-flat. It pulses in something else, something lower, something I do not have a name for. It pulses, and the pulse moves through the abandoned silica, and the abandoned silica carries it down into the mires, and the mires carry it further down, into whatever is below the mires, which I have never thought about and do not intend to.

When the moss pulses, the kites, above, shift slightly in the wind.

This is a fact.

I do not know what it means.

I do not think it is my job to know what it means.

---

The household is waiting for me at the eleventh hour.

I come up out of the substrate by a different passage — the one that opens near the lower entrance to the rowfolk's covered bridge — and I cross the bridge at a run, because the bridge is not ours, it is the rowfolk's, and we coagulators do not linger on it. The bridge is high. The bridge is, for me, almost dizzying. From the bridge you can see the basin laid out below you — the mires, the red grass, the columns of the orí rising up out of them like something that has been growing for a very long time and intends to continue. From the bridge you can see the high row, and the upper platforms, and the kites, and the cable junctions where the upper dwellers are, even now, climbing with their long poles and their soft-headed hammers.

I do not look at the upper dwellers. It is not a habit. They are doing their work. I am doing mine. Our work is not the same. Our work is adjacent.

The household is in the substrate level of the western stretch — a small chamber, drier than the eastern station, with a low table and three sleeping pallets and a hearth that does not really heat the room because the room is already warm. The mires keep everything warm. The hearth is for the smell of the smoke, and for the small ceremony of gathering at the end of the morning's work.

My mother is at the table. She is old. She has not walked the quarter-mile in eleven years. She hums, when she hums, in E-flat. Her hands are still good. She carves the wooden cups that we use for the mires-water in the morning. She has been carving the cups since before I was born. She will carve them until she cannot.

My brother's child is at the table. She is five. She is being held against my brother's shoulder. She is learning, by proximity, to carry E-flat. I do not know yet whether she will be a coagulator. She may be something else. She may go up into the rowfolk levels and live among the slender long-limbed people who wear the layered cloth. She may join the inversionists and stand on the upper platforms in the late afternoon pollen and hum phrases that the chordal field will tune for her. She may climb, as the upper dwellers climb, and adjust the tethers that do not need adjusting.

She may be a visitor one day, on the tram, looking out the window, not looking down.

I do not have a preference. I have, in fact, a kind of trust that whatever she becomes, the causeway will sing to her in her sleep, and the song will be enough. This is what I mean by the folk belief. The folk belief is not that we are special. The folk belief is that the work is alive and the work will continue, in whoever is sleeping on the silica, regardless of who they think they are.

The table is set. There is food — a kind of steamed grain, and a soft white root, and a small cup of something fermented that the rowfolk above make and trade down to us. We eat. We do not speak much. The warmth in the ears is loudest in the late morning, when the wind has dropped and the chordal field is thickest, and we sit at the table in the drone and we eat, and the drone is not a sound but a weather, and we are inside the weather, and the weather is, for the most part, warm.

---

In the evening, after the meal, I go back to the eastern station.

The chamber is loud at this hour. The dusk streaming of the vré is just beginning — you can hear it, faintly, through the silica, a kind of brightening of the static, a quickening. The kites are shifting in the wind that comes from the south. The late afternoon pollen is in the air, even down here, yellow and fine, drifting through the low mouth of the passage and settling on the wet floor.

I sit on the bench. I do not bring the implement. The implement is at the household, on the wall, in its place. In the evening I am not working. In the evening I am listening, which is a different thing.

I am listening for the phase-shift.

The phase-shift is the click — the almost-inaudible click that happens when the chordal field adjusts itself, when the ten thousand slightly-out-of-tune major sevenths of the kites decide, all at once, to retune, when the wind drops and the mires give up their last exhalation and the whole computing forest and the whole listening city do a small collective breath. It happens at certain hours. It happens most reliably, in my experience, in the early evening, when the light is changing and the rowfolk above are beginning their quiet suppers and the upper dwellers are coming down off the cables and the inversionists have, for the day, given up their humming.

I can feel the phase-shift coming, in the bones of my wrist.

The folk belief says this is because the causeway is singing to me. I think this is because the causeway is part of the system, and the system is adjusting, and I am, after forty-one years, attuned to the adjustment. The two explanations are not, I think, in disagreement. I am part of the system. The system is adjusting. The adjustment is the song.

The click happens.

It is almost inaudible. It is felt. It is felt in the bench, in the wet floor, in the cup I have set down beside me, in the bones of my wrist and the bones of my ankle and the small bones of my ear. The whole chamber, for a fraction of a moment, shifts. The drone thickens. The drone thins. The drone settles into a slightly different configuration, and the slightly different configuration is what the city will be, for the next several hours, until the next click.

I exhale.

I have been holding my breath without knowing I was holding it. This is also part of the work. The body prepares for the phase-shift. The body knows the phase-shift is coming before the body knows it knows. The body is, in this small way, ahead of me.

I sit for another quarter of an hour. The dusk streaming of the vré reaches its peak and then begins to subside. The bright static in the chamber rises and falls. The pollen continues to drift down through the low mouth of the passage. Somewhere above me, in the upper row, a child is being held against someone's shoulder and is learning, by proximity, to identify which orí trunk their family is associated with. Somewhere above that, an upper dweller is coming down off a cable junction and is humming, faintly, in something that is not quite E-flat and not quite any other named pitch but is the pitch of the cable they have been working on. Somewhere on an upper platform, an inversionist is standing in the late afternoon pollen, listening to the phase-shift click and deciding, again, that tomorrow they will try a different phrase.

The place is doing its long damp deafening negotiation. The place has been doing it for a long time. The place will continue to do it.

---

I go home through the lower passage.

The household is asleep when I arrive. My mother is asleep on her pallet. My brother's child is asleep on her pallet. My brother is not yet asleep. My brother is sitting at the low table with a cup of the fermented thing the rowfolk make, and he is humming, very quietly, in E-flat, and the humming is not for me and not for himself but for the causeway, which is, even now, even through the stone and the fused silica and the layers of the substrate, humming back.

I sit down across from him. I do not hum. He hums.

The mires-water, in the wooden cup I will set out for the morning, is sitting on the floor beside the door, catching the faint light that comes down through the passage from above. It is, I think, already beginning to take on something from the chamber. It is, I think, already beginning to be the morning.

I will sleep soon. I will sleep on the pallet that is mine, on the silica slab that is the foundation of the pallet, and the silica will sing to me, and the song will be in E-flat, and I will wake in the morning knowing, without being told, where the quarter-mile needs a small correction and where it does not.

This is the folk belief. This is the work. This is the inheritance.

This is, for now, enough.
