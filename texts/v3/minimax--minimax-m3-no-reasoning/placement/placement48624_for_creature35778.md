I am the fire-tender.

My hands know the wood of this place — the dry, resin-poor heartwood hauled down through the narrow places in bundles small enough to pass shoulder-first through the squeeze. My hands know the small dry sticks and the bark that peels in pale curls and the lichen, when there is nothing else, that burns with almost no flame and almost no complaint. I am the one who feeds the small fire in the ring of gathered stones, and I am the one who decides when the fire is too much, and I am the one who carries the cold ashes out through the camp in a hide-wrapped bowl and sets them in the trough between two sleeping ridges, far from any flank that might stir.

I have done this since I was twelve, when my mother’s hands began to fail and the council — though the council does not convene, the council is more a remembered shape than a gathering — decided that I had the lungs for it. The lungs for it is not a metaphor. The smoke must be drawn down before it rises, must be coaxed to lie flat along the floor and slide outward into the lateral passages where the breath of the great ones will take it and carry it away. I breathe the smoke. I breathe shallowly, through the nose, and I hold it low, and my chest has broadened in the years since, and my voice, when I use it, comes out rougher than it should for a man my age.

I know the moods of the fire. I know the moods of the hollow. I know that these are not the same knowledge, though they overlap.

I know which great ones are near. I know it by the breathing I have learned to read, which is not reading, which is more like the way a sailor reads the sea — by the long accumulation of a vocabulary that is not quite words. The shallow-breather in the third passage east has been shallow for six days, and shallow means settled, and settled means safe. The deep-breather in the alcove’s northern wall has been deepening since last nightfall, and deepening means approaching something — a waking, a moving, a rising — and the camp will stay low today and the children will be kept to the inner hides and no one will sing.

I have walked between the limbs of a sleeping great one. I will not say which great one. I will not say what errand. I will say that the limbs were warm and that the breath came in waves, and that the stone under my feet was the exact shape of my feet, and that I did not look at the place where the flank met the trough because to look would have been a kind of trespass. I came back. I did not speak of it for a year. I have spoken of it once, to the keeper of the bone-records, because the keeper needed to know whether that passage was still passable, and I was the only one who had come back from it.

I am not brave. I am careful. There is a difference, and the difference is the difference between the hollow and the surface, between the kind of living that asks you to throw yourself at things and the kind that asks you to thread yourself among them without disturbing. I am a thread. I have always been a thread. My mother was a thread before me, and her mother before her, and somewhere back the line thins into women whose names are not recorded because the records were not yet kept.

I am the fire-tender, and the fire is small, and the smoke is careful, and the great ones do not stir because of me. This is the whole of my work, and the whole of my work is enough.

I have a daughter. She is seven. She has the lungs for it, I think, though it is too early to say. She watches me with the fire. She watches the way I cup my hands around the first small flame and draw the smoke down. She watches the way I read the breathing before I feed the wood. She is learning, without knowing she is learning, the long apprenticeship that will, if she has the body for it and the patience for it and the years of it, make her the next keeper of the small fire in the ring of gathered stones, in the alcove at the far edge of the main vault, in the hollow beneath the root-stones, in the dim warm loud still place where we have lived for as long as anyone remembers.

I will teach her what my mother taught me. I will teach her the fuels and the smokes and the breaths. I will teach her which great ones are the steady ones and which are the unpredictable ones and which are the ones that have not been seen to move in any living memory but that the bone-records suggest once shifted, in the time of the great-grandmothers, and that might shift again. I will teach her the offerings — how to lay them, where to lay them, how to leave them without looking back, because looking back is a kind of asking and the great ones are not asked. I will teach her to read the breathing. I will teach her that the breathing is not a language, and that it is not not a language, and that the space between those two nothings is where we live.

I will not teach her to love the great ones. The great ones are not loved. The great ones are lived with. I will teach her the difference, which is the difference between a god and a housemate, between a thing you kneel to and a thing you step around. I will teach her that we are grateful, and that we are afraid, and that we are careful, and that these are one thing.

I will teach her to step over the ridges in the places where the ridges are lowest, where the stone is worn almost to flat by the slow going of the great ones. I will teach her to walk behind the columns when the breathing is rough. I will teach her to stay in the alcove when the percussive thud comes from deep passages, because the thud means a great one has moved, and a moved great one is not dangerous but is unpredictable, and unpredictable is the only danger there is.

I will teach her the songs. The songs are not for the great ones. The songs are for us. I will teach her to sing them softly, in the alcove, and only when the breathing is steady, and to stop when the breathing changes. The songs are old. The songs are not very old. I will teach her the ones I know, and she will teach the ones she knows to her daughter, and the songs will change a little in the changing, the way the offerings rot a little in the rotting, and this will be enough.

I am the fire-tender. I am a thread. I am a man with rough lungs and careful hands and a daughter who watches me and does not yet know what she is learning.

The hollow is warm. The hollow is dim. The hollow is loud with the breathing of things that do not know we are here, or do not care, or care in a way we cannot read. The smoke from my small fire is sliding along the floor toward the lateral passages, and the breath of the great ones will take it, and the great ones will not stir.

I will feed the fire again in an hour. I will read the breathing again now. The shallow-breather is still shallow. The deep-breather has settled. The children are playing a quiet game in the inner hides. The keeper of the bone-records is making a new notch on a small stick, because yesterday a stone fell from a ridge and was the right size for a hand-axe, and the finding of it should be marked.

Everything is as it was. Everything is as it will be. The place continues.
