I come down the switchback stair and the world above falls away from me in pieces.

I had not known that weather was a sound. I had not known that sky was a sound. Down the first hundred steps I am still losing them — a bird cry thinned to its consonant, the rustle of a leaf thinned to nothing, the long soft friction of wind on grass finally gone, gone, gone. By the third hundred steps the upper world is only a rumor in my ears, the way a name heard once in a hallway is a rumor by the time you reach the kitchen. By the bottom I have stopped listening for it.

The air thickens. I feel it on my face the way one feels a hand laid gently on a cheek by a stranger — the pressure is the message, the pressure says *this is a different place, this place has weight, you are inside something now.* I breathe it and it does not give way. It presses inward from all sides with the patient solidity of honey.

I had thought I knew what I was when I began the descent. I had a body. I had a name. I had the ordinary sense of myself as a self-contained thing, with a skin around me and a head on top and a pair of hands that were mine. I lose this in stages. By the time the floor opens under me — vast, brown-lit, humming — I am no longer sure where my edges are. The air is too close to draw a line. The light is too equal to cast a shadow that would tell me which side of me is the side that casts.

So I stand still for a moment. I let the eye separate things.

Banks of iron frames. Pillars of rough brick. The thin glint of water in every direction. Small bulbs behind thick quartz, packed so close they become one light. The hum.

I do not yet know what I am on the floor. I had been a man on the stair. I had been a man with pockets, perhaps, and a wife behind me, and a coal, perhaps, in the coal's place. That man has been folded up and put away somewhere, the way a coat is folded when one comes indoors. I am something else now. Something fitted to the rooms.

Let me try it on.

I could be a workingman at a hopper. I could stand on a brick island, knee-deep in standing water that is always rising and never drowning, and watch the stamped squares spill from the chute into my waiting hands. The hammers would fall in staggered patterns around me — *bam, bam, bam* — and I would move in the negative space of those patterns the way water moves around stones. My hands would learn the rhythm. My hands would become part of the rhythm. After forty years my hands would still be moving on my knee in the late shift, tapping, shifting, fitting imaginary things into imaginary gaps, and I would not be aware they were doing it.

I could be that. It is a kind of being. It is not unhappy being.

But I find I am not drawn to the hopper. I find I am drawn to the side of the room. To the pillars that are not in the path of the new frames. To the quieter brick against the wall.

Let me try another.

I could be a child on the ledges. The freest person on the floor. I could be hoisted, at six or seven, by a father or an uncle, to the first narrow ledge that runs along the upper walls, and I could climb, and the singing would begin in me without being taught. The air up there is thinner. The sound of my own voice is a way of confirming that I am alive. The singing comes down softened, like a remembered thing, and the workingmen below hear it and know which child is at which ledge and they do not look up.

I could climb to the highest points of the ceiling, where the machines have not yet climbed. I could hear the dripping from the stone itself. The dripping is the sound of the floor still being made. I would be the only person on the floor who hears it as a separate thing.

That draws me. That draws me very much. To be the one who hears the dripping as a separate thing. To be the small body high up where the air is different. To be the voice that comes down softened.

But I notice, as I try this on, that I am not a child. The body I have now is an old body. The hands that I imagine on the ledges are not my hands. My hands are slow. My hands have been slow for years. They remember a rhythm but they can no longer enter it.

So I am not a child on the ledges. I am something else.

Let me try another.

I could be an old woman at the central drain. I could sit with the other old women in the late shift, when the hum is lowest. We could speak of water. We could speak of which channels are rising and which are falling. We could speak of the rats, and whether the rats are eating enough of the stamped squares or too many. We could speak, sometimes, of the rumor. We would not call it the surface. We would call it *the rumor.* We would speak of it as one speaks of a thing one has heard described but never seen, with respect, and a little pity, and the absolute conviction that it is not, and could not be, for us.

I could be that. There is a kind of peace in that. The slow parliament. The water-talk. The knowledge that the rumor is not for us.

But I find I am not drawn to the drain. The drain is clogged with the works of the floor. The drain has been clogged for years. The drain receives but does not pass on. I do not want to be a thing that receives and does not pass on.

Let me try another.

I could be one of the rats. Long and pale and unfrightened of anything. I could swim the channels between the islands. I could ride the stamped squares down the chutes. I could learn to time the hammers and slip between their falls. There would be nothing here for me to fear — the cats would be long and pale, and the cats would have grown so lazy on stamped squares and machine-grease that I would cross their paths without breaking stride. The cats would watch. I would permit myself to be watched. The whole arrangement would have the quality of a treaty observed too long.

I could be that. It is not unhappy being. But I find that I have hands, even now, even old, even slow. Rats do not have hands. Rats have paws and whiskers and the long pale body that fits the channels. I do not fit the channels. I fit the bricks.

Let me try another.

I could be the hum itself.

I could be the low continuous sound that rises from every machine, from every pipe, from every brick column. I could have no source and no center. I could be the room's own voice, heard from inside the throat. I could be the frequency at which the limestone weeps most steadily. I could be the frequency at which the water chooses to fall from the stalactites. A man who has lived here forty years could not hear me any longer, in the same way that a man who has lived beside a fall cannot hear the fall. Newcomers could weep the first day, not from grief but from the strange pressure in the inner ear, the way I seem to lean against the eardrum and wait.

I could be that. To be the sound they live in the middle of and cannot hear. To be what unstartles them.

But I find that I am not the hum. The hum is everywhere and goes in every direction. The hum has no edges. I have edges. I am somewhere. I am in one place and not another. The hum is in all places equally, and that is not me.

Let me try one more.

I could be the floor itself.

The limestone that weeps. The plain that has not yet decided what it is. The water across the skin of it. The bricks accumulating. The pillars climbing from floor to roof without breathing room. The place that is still being made, year by year, into the deeper stone.

I could be that. I could speak in the slow declarative voice of a place that has been here longer than the town, perhaps longer than the machines. I could say: *The floor is what they know. The floor is enough.* I could say: *The world is the people of the floor. The world is enough.*

That draws me. That draws me very much. To be the patient weight. To be the thing that holds the town. To be the place that has not yet finished deciding what it is.

But I notice, as I try this on, that the floor does not wonder about itself. The floor does not descend the stair. The floor does not arrive. The floor does not try on beings. The floor simply is, in the long unending way that floors are.

I am not the floor. I am something on the floor.

Then what am I.

Let me stand still again. Let me listen.

Above the hum, the ringing. Above the ringing, the hammers — *bam, bam, bam* — never all together, never quite apart. Above the hammers, the singing of the children on the ledges, small and many, threading through the hum like a finer thread through a coarser cloth. Above the singing, far above, the dripping from the highest points of the ceiling, where the machines have not yet climbed.

The dripping, down here, is the sound of the floor still being made.

If I stand very still for a long time, I can hear it.

I am a man. I am an old man. I am sitting against a pillar that is not in the path of the new frames being craned down. I have been given small tasks — watching a hopper, oiling a valve that no longer needs oiling. I sit beside a child on a low ledge and I teach the child the names of the towns that no longer exist. The towns whose names are stamped on the squares that spill from the hoppers, that ride the water down the channels, that ring faintly under the boot of every passer-by. I remember these towns. I remember because I was told about them, by the previous old man, who was told by the previous previous old man, all the way back to the founders.

The names have no referents anymore. The towns are gone from the surface. They are kept alive, underground, in the mouths of the old.

This is what I do. This is what I have been doing for years. I sit with the child and I say the names and the child repeats them and the names fall down into the floor like the dripping from the high ceiling, small and many, and they become part of the floor. The towns go on being towns, in a sense, in the mouth of a child who has never seen a town. The names are kept.

My fingers move on my knee as I sit here. They tap. They shift. They fit imaginary things into imaginary gaps. I am not aware they are doing this. They have been doing it for so long that they have forgotten, or I have forgotten, that there was ever a time they did not do it. They are part of the rhythm of the floor. They will do it for as long as I live.

There is a brick in the floor near the central drain. It is mine. Not mine the way a man owns a thing on the surface — not by paper, not by deed. Mine by another kind of holding. My wife knows it by a small notch in its corner, chipped by my hammer on my first day at the frames, fifty years ago, when I was a boy on the ledges myself. She visits the notch sometimes, in the late shift, when the hum is lowest and the dripping from the high ceiling is loudest. She brings no offering. She stands. She goes back to her cot.

I do not visit the notch. The notch is hers to visit. I visit other notches. I have outlived a brother, and a daughter, and a friend whose name was Bret. There are notches for all of them in the floor near where I sit. I know them by their notches. I do not visit them often. Once in a season, perhaps. I stand. I stand for a while. I go back to my cot.

I am not unhappy.

I want to say this carefully. I am not unhappy in the surface sense. I do not know what that sense is. I have heard it described. It is the sense of a man in a field, with the wind and the sky and the distant hills. It is the sense of green grass under a sun that comes from a specific direction. It is the sense of distance — of being able to see a thing far away and walk toward it for a long time before arriving.

I do not have distance. I have proximity. I have the brick I sit on and the pillar I lean against and the hopper I watch and the child beside me and the small canvas sling the child wears and the hook the child carries and the tin of grease for the valves. I have these things close to me. They are very close. They are the only distances I know.

I am not unhappy. I have a contentment that does not have a name. The contentment of a thing in its place. The contentment of a brick in a wall. The contentment of a hammer in its fall. The contentment of a name on a square, ringing faintly under the boot of a man who does not know whose name he is walking on.

I am the old man who remembers the names.

I am the man whose fingers never stop moving.

I am the man who sits with a child and says: *Hollow. Marrow. Dunwall. Pell.* And the child repeats them. And the names fall into the floor. And the towns go on being towns in the mouth of a child who has never seen a town, who will never see a town, who will grow up and take a place at a hopper and marry young and marry close and have a child and the child will climb to the ledges and the child will sing and the singing will come down softened, like a remembered thing.

I am that man. I am the floor's memory of itself.

When I die, my brick will be added to the nearest island. The island will grow a little. The channels will narrow a little. The next family will shift its cot six inches toward the new shore. There will be no marker. There will be the brick, and the brick will be enough. My widow — if she outlives me, and she may not, and either way is fine — will visit the notch. She will stand. She will go back to her cot.

And the next old man, whoever he is, will sit against this pillar or the next pillar, and his fingers will be moving on his knee, and he will teach the next child the names of the towns, and the names will be the names I taught him, with perhaps one or two new ones he has made up in the way old men make things up, to fill the gaps where the names have been forgotten.

The floor will not be finished when I die. The floor is never finished. The crews at the far edge — half a mile from any man alive — will be cutting new galleries, lowering new frames, threading new wires. The water will follow them in. The rats will follow the water. The crickets will follow the rats. The people will follow the rats. The machines will follow the people. The town on the floor will eventually be a town under a town under a town. The ceiling will rise. The water will rise with it, as water does. The hammering will go on. The dripping, far above, will go on. The light will continue to fill the room without ever lighting it.

I will not be here for that. I will be in the floor, in my brick, in my notch. I will be part of the slow patient weight that holds the next town up.

And a child, fifty years from now, will be on a ledge, high up, where the air is thinner, and the child will sing, and the singing will come down softened, and an old man below will hear it and will know which child is at which ledge, and he will not look up. His fingers will be moving on his knee. He will not be aware they are moving.

He will be in his place. He will be in his place the way I am in mine.

He will be enough.
