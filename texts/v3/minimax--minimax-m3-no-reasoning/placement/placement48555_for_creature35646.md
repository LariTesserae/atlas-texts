I think I would be the black bird on the weathervane.

Not because I am wise, or because I have answers, or because I have earned the perch by some long fidelity. Because I am suited to the work. The work is small and requires no explanation. The work is to sit in the same place at the same hours and sing the same three notes, and to do this until I have done it so long that the doing and the being have become the same thing.

I would not be the largest thing in Korrun. I would not be the oldest. The farrier is older than I am, in the way that matters here — he has held his shape through more of the same mornings than I have held mine. The old woman in the back room is older still, in that she has been consulted more times, in more of the same evenings, by more of the same visitors who are not-quite-the-same-asking. The erratics are older than anyone. The mountains are older than the erratics, though they are young as mountains go, which is a thing I would not have known how to say until I came here.

I would sit on the weathervane of the long building, which is the building with the bell in it, which is the building nearest to the cistern plaza, which is the plaza where the bench is. I would face into the prevailing wind, which comes down from the high cirques in the morning and goes back up to them in the evening, and which carries the mist with it when the mist is going. My tail would hang below me, long and black, and would move a little when I sang, though I would not be aware that it moved, because it would always have moved, in the same way, at the same notes.

I would sing at dawn. I would sing at dusk. The three notes would be the same three notes. They would not mean anything in particular. They would mean, perhaps, that the air was the right temperature for the note to carry, and that the lichen on the north wall was the lichen, and that the children were still up on the plateau, and that the dogs were awake. They would mean that I was here.

I would watch the visitors when they came. They would arrive on the switchback road, breathing hard, their lowland clothes wrong for the damp. They would look at the lichens. They would look at the erratics. They would look at the faces of the people who poured them tea, and the faces would look back with that polite consideration, that gentle refusal to mean what the visitors meant by their looking. The visitors would stay, or they would not stay. The ones who stayed would begin to notice. The ones who noticed would begin to ask. The asking would be answered by the old woman, or by the farrier, or by the rowan-climbing children with their scraped knees, and the answer would be the answer that is given, which is not quite an answer.

I would not answer, because I am a bird and do not have words. But I would be present for the asking. I would be on the weathervane when the visitor looked up, and the visitor would see me there, and would feel — I think they would feel — that I had been there before they arrived, and would be there after they left, and that this was not a small thing, and not a large thing, but the only kind of thing there is.

The children would know me. They would call to me, in their cupped hands, in their three-noted whistles that are not my three notes but are kin to them. I would not come down to them. They would not expect me to. They would leave rowan berries on the sill of the long building, and I would eat them later, or I would not eat them, and someone would sweep them away in the morning, and the sweeping would be the same sweeping that had been done the morning before.

In the late summer, when the heather flowers and stains the plateau with a color visible from the walls, I would sing more. Not different notes — the same notes, more of them. The festival would happen below me. The children would carry armfuls of purple through the narrow streets. The elders would sit on the bench. I would watch. I would be part of the watching, the way the weathervane is part of the watching, the way the slate is part of the ringing when the wind comes through it.

If a visitor, after long enough, asked me — which they could not, because I am a bird, but if they could — what it was like to live here, I would not answer. But if they asked the slate roofs, the slate roofs would ring, and the ringing would be the answer. If they asked the lichen, the lichen would not have grown, and the not-growing would be the answer. If they asked the mist, the mist would rise and fall, and the rising and falling would be the answer. The answer is the place. I am only the one who sits on the high point of the long building and says the same three notes into the same air at the same hours, and is not tired of it, because tired is a word that means a thing that does not happen here.

I would be a small dark shape against the pale gray-green stone of the young mountains. From the central square, climbing the carved steps of the erratic with the view, a visitor could look up and see me, and would not know if I was the same bird they had seen yesterday, or a different bird who had learned to sit in the same place. The question would be the wrong question. The bird is the place. The place is the bird. The three notes are the bell, and the bell is the three notes, and the mist is the height at which the children are called in, and the calling-in is the doors being shut, and the shutting is the lanterns being lit, and the lighting is the mica windows glowing in the dusk like eyes, and the dusk is the dawn, and the dawn is the three notes, and the three notes are me, on the weathervane, singing the same thing I have always sung, into the same air I have always breathed, in the same place I have always been.
