# What is on me

The first thing I notice is the slick.

Not the wet — I came down through wet. I came down a long ladder of iron rungs set into a shaft that got narrower the deeper it went, and by the last hundred feet the rungs were slick with the same condensation that slicks everything down here, and my palms had been printed with it, and my boots had been baptized with it, and I had thought I was prepared for what the chamber would be like. I was wrong. The chamber is *wetter* than the shaft. The chamber is *alive* in a way the shaft is not, and the alive part is already on me, has been on me since my second step off the ladder, and I did not notice until I raised my hand to push the hair out of my eyes and saw that the droplets on my forearm were not running.

They were sitting.

They were sitting on my skin the way small animals sit — patient, weighted, *placed* — and when I tilted my arm to make them slide, they did not slide. They shifted. They rearranged themselves into a new pattern, slightly denser, slightly more reflective, and the warmth of my skin beneath them had become — I could feel it — a thing they were using. Not parasitically. The way a cat uses a lap. The way a fire uses a log.

I had brought three things down with me: a lamp, a notebook, and a small bottle of drinking water sealed with wax. The lamp I had blown out at the top of the ladder because the chamber makes its own light and a second source only confuses the eyes. The notebook I had tucked into an oilskin pouch at my belt. The bottle I had put in my coat pocket, thinking I might want it, thinking the water down here might taste of sulfur or copper or something worse, and wanting, foolishly, wanting to have one thing in this place that tasted of *outside*.

The bottle is gone.

I did not drop it. I did not set it down. It is not on the floor of the chamber, which I have walked now twice across in a long slow circuit, looking down at the warm water and the standing waves and the pale drift turning in the slow lanes. The bottle is not on the ladder. The bottle is not in my coat pocket, which I have checked four times in the last hour, the way a man checks his pocket when he has lost his key — a hope that is also a small humiliation, because the hope is unreasonable and the man knows it.

The bottle is gone, and the wax seal is not broken, and the bottle is not on the floor, and the chamber has not, in any way I would recognize as an act, taken the bottle from me.

I am trying not to think about what this means. I am trying to think about what I came down to do.

---

# What I came down to do

I am a surveyor. I am one of three, assigned by a bureau that does not officially exist, to a survey that does not officially exist, of a place that does not officially exist, in a province that has lately come under the administration of a government that does not believe in the things the bureau was founded to record. The bureau was founded, in a previous century, by people who did believe in the things, and who thought that the things should be recorded while they still could be recorded, because the things were going away. The bureau was right about the things going away. The bureau was wrong about the things being recordable.

I have been a surveyor for eleven years. I have surveyed seventeen sites. Two of them were lost to me in ways I will not describe. Three of them were lost to me in ways I can describe: they were flooded, they were collapsed, they were taken by a road. The rest are still where I left them, which is to say they are still doing what they were doing, which is to say they are still continuing to be themselves in the slow indifferent way that such things continue to be themselves, and I am still the only person who knows what they are, and the bureau is still paying me a small retainer to file reports that no one reads, and the reports are still being added to a ledger that no one opens, and the ledger is still being kept in a vault that no one enters, and the vault is still being maintained by a clerk who has been there longer than I have been alive and who will be there longer than I will be alive and who does not believe in the things either, and who files the reports anyway because filing the reports is what he was hired to do.

The Condensary is the eighteenth site.

I came down because the bureau received a letter, three months ago, from a man who is now dead. The letter described a place in the deep limestone under a particular watershed in a particular province, and the letter described the place in terms that the bureau has used, in its internal documents, for two hundred years, to describe a particular *kind* of place — a place that runs on its own, a place that does not need the surface to continue, a place that has been continuing for a very long time. The man who wrote the letter was a well-sinker. He had been digging a well for a village that needed water, and he had gone down through the limestone and through a clay seam and through a thin layer of something he could not identify, and he had reached a chamber, and the chamber had reached back.

He died six weeks after writing the letter. The cause of death, as recorded in the village, was a fever. The cause of death, as I understand it now, standing in the chamber he found, was that the chamber was not finished with him. The bureau does not acknowledge causes of death of this kind. The bureau notes them. The bureau sends a surveyor.

I am the surveyor.

# The first thing the chamber did to me

The chamber took my bottle.

I do not mean this in the way a person means *took* when they mean *stole*. I mean it the way a person means *took* when they mean *took in*. The bottle is part of the chamber now. The bottle has been *received*. I know this because I have walked the chamber twice and the bottle is not on the floor, and the floor is shallow and clear and there is nothing on the floor except the water and the drift and the standing waves and, in one place, near the base of a pillar at the far edge of the chamber, a small pile of what I take to be the remains of the man who came before me — a heap of fabric, mostly, going soft, and a few pale curved shapes that I do not want to look at closely, and a leather strap that might once have been a sandal. The bottle is not among these things. The bottle is not anywhere I can find. The bottle is *in* the chamber, somewhere, distributed, taken apart, used.

I have made my peace with the bottle. The bottle was a foolish thing to bring. The bottle was an act of the surface, an insistence that the surface could be carried down, and the chamber has gently, without my noticing, corrected me.

What I have not made my peace with is the second thing.

The second thing is that the chamber is already on my skin, and the chamber is patient, and I can feel, in a way I cannot prove, that the chamber is *reading* me through my skin.

Not in the way a doctor reads a pulse. In the way a page reads a hand that has just touched it. I touched the nearest pillar, when I first came down, because I am a surveyor and I am supposed to touch things, and the pillar was warm, and the warmth went into my palm, and my palm has been faintly warm ever since — warmer than the air, warmer than the rest of me, warmer than it should be — and when I put my palm against the oilskin pouch at my belt, against the notebook inside it, the notebook was *not* warm. The notebook was at air-temperature. My palm was at pillar-temperature. The pillar-temperature has not gone down.

I have a hand that is the temperature of the pillar, and the rest of me is at air-temperature, and the difference is small but it is *there*, and it has been there for an hour, and I do not know what the pillar put into my hand, or what my hand gave to the pillar, or whether the exchange is finished.

---

# What I am

I am a man with a notebook I cannot open down here because the condensation would ruin the pages. I am a man with a lamp I cannot light down here because the chamber makes its own light and a lamp would only confuse me. I am a man with a bottle I no longer have. I am a man with a hand that is the temperature of a thing that has been running for longer than my country has had a name.

I am a man who was sent down here to record what is here, and I am standing in what is here, and the thing I am supposed to record is *running*, and I cannot write while it is running because my notebook is in an oilskin pouch that is at air-temperature and my hand is at pillar-temperature and the difference between the two is small but it is the difference between the surface and the deep place and the difference is the thing I came down to measure and the thing I cannot measure because I do not have an instrument that measures the difference and the chamber has taken the instrument I would have used, which was a thermometer, which I left in my pack at the top of the ladder because I thought the chamber would be at a constant temperature and a thermometer would be redundant.

I am a man who has been down here for an hour and a half.

I am a man who will be down here for as long as he can stand the noise.

---

# The noise

The noise is the first thing. The noise was the first thing before I came down, when I was reading the well-sinker's letter at the bureau and trying to imagine what he had found. The noise is the first thing now. The noise is constant. The noise is vertical. The noise comes from above and it comes from the meeting of the currents and it comes from the pillars and it comes from the roots and it comes from the ring, and all of these noises are slightly different, and all of them are running together into one noise that is the chamber, and the chamber is the noise, and the noise is the chamber breathing.

I have been a surveyor for eleven years. I have been in deep places. I have been in places where the only sound was the sound of my own breathing and the sound of my own boots on wet stone. I have been in places where the silence was so complete that I could hear my own pulse in my ears. This is not that kind of place. This is the opposite of that kind of place. This is a place that does not permit silence. This is a place that has filled every available frequency with the sound of itself, and the sound of itself is the sound of water falling and water meeting and water being used and water being changed and water coming back, and the coming-back is the point, and the coming-back has been the point for longer than I have been alive.

I can stand the noise. I do not know for how long. The noise is in my sternum. The noise is in my teeth. The noise is in the hand that touched the pillar, in a way that is not pain but is not not-pain, a kind of resonance, a kind of being-played.

I am trying to listen past the noise. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had heard something past the noise. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had stayed down here for two days before he came back up. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had described, in the letter, *a sound like a wheel turning in honey* and had said the sound was coming from *a thing in the middle of the chamber that he could not look at directly*.

I have heard the wheel. I have not yet tried to look at the thing in the middle of the chamber. I am not ready to try to look at the thing in the middle of the chamber. I am trying to listen past the noise first. The bureau taught me to listen past the noise first. The bureau said: *the first thing a deep place does is fill the ear. The second thing it does is fill the eye. The third thing it does is fill the hand. The surveyor who is not careful will be filled before he has finished his listening.*

I am trying to listen.

I have been listening for an hour and a half. I have not yet heard anything that sounds like speech. The roots click. The clicks are small and dry. The clicks are not addressed to me. The clicks are addressed to the other roots. The clicks are part of a conversation that began before I was born and will continue after I am dead and I am not part of the conversation and I am not, I think, intended to be part of the conversation. The clicks are the chamber talking to itself.

But the wheel — the wheel is not talking to itself. The wheel is doing something. The wheel is doing something to the water. The wheel is doing something to the air inside the ring. The wheel is doing something to the warmth in the floor, which rises through my boots and up my legs and meets the cool of the scribed lines on my face when I stand close to the ring, and the two temperatures argue in my skin, gently, and the argument is the closest I have come, so far, to understanding what the wheel is doing.

The two temperatures are not arguing about which is correct. They are arguing about what to do with me.

---

# The second thing the chamber did to me

The chamber put a question into my hand.

I do not have a better word for it. When I touched the pillar, the pillar was warm. The warmth went into my palm. The warmth has stayed in my palm. The warmth has, in the last hour, become *specific* — it is not just warm, it is warm in a *direction*, the way a compass needle is not just metal but is metal *toward* something. The warmth in my palm is oriented. The warmth in my palm is pointing.

It is pointing at the ring.

I did not decide to point my hand at the ring. I became aware, ten minutes ago, that my hand was pointing at the ring. I became aware that my hand had been pointing at the ring for some time, and that I had not noticed because the pointing is subtle. My hand is not outstretched. My hand is at my side, fingers slightly curled, the way a hand hangs when a person is standing still. But the *warmth* in my hand is oriented, and the orientation is toward the ring, and the orientation has been there since I touched the pillar.

I have tried, three times now, to point my hand somewhere else. I have tried pointing it at the wall. I have tried pointing it at the ceiling. I have tried pointing it at the floor. The warmth does not move when I move my hand. The warmth stays oriented toward the ring. The warmth is, I think, the pillar's answer to the question of whether I will go to the ring, and the pillar's answer is yes.

I do not know yet whether I will go to the ring. I do not know yet whether going to the ring is what I came down to do. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had gone to the ring. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had stood at the ring for an hour and a half and had *listened* and had then come back up the ladder. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had not been able to say what he had heard. The bureau's letter said the well-sinker had said only that the wheel had been *speaking to him in a language he did not know he knew*.

I do not know yet whether I will stand at the ring. I do not know yet whether I will hear anything past the noise. I am trying to listen past the noise. I am trying to write down what I hear past the noise, so that the bureau will have something to file in the ledger that no one opens. But my notebook is in an oilskin pouch that is at air-temperature and my hand is at pillar-temperature and the oilskin is *not* wet, and I want to keep the notebook dry, because the notebook is the only thing I have that is not part of the chamber yet, and I want to keep at least one thing that is not part of the chamber, for as long as I can.

I have not opened the notebook.

I do not know what I will write in the notebook when I open it. I do not know what language I will write in. The bureau's language is the language of the surface. The bureau's language does not have words for the things I have been describing. The bureau's language has the word *Condensary*, but the bureau's *Condensary* is a translation, and the translation is approximate, and the bureau knows the translation is approximate, and the bureau uses the translation anyway because the bureau has not yet found a better word.

I will write what I can. I will write what I can, in the bureau's language, knowing the bureau's language is insufficient, knowing the bureau knows the bureau's language is insufficient, knowing the bureau has sent me down here anyway because the bureau has been doing this for two hundred years and the bureau will continue to do this for as long as the bureau has surveyors to send down.

# Who I am

I am the eighteenth surveyor.

I am the man who came down after the well-sinker, who came down after the man who died, who came down after the man who wrote the letter, who came down after the bureau, which has been doing this for two hundred years and which has, in the bureau's vault, in a ledger that no one opens, the reports of the seventeen surveyors who came before me, and which has, in the bureau's vault, in a drawer that no one opens, a list of the names of the seventeen surveyors who came before me, and which has, in the bureau's vault, on a wall that no one looks at, a list of the names of the surveyors who did not come back, and which has, on the wall, beside the names of the surveyors who did not come back, the dates of their descents and the dates of their last reports and the dates after which the bureau stopped expecting them to come back.

I am the eighteenth name.

I am a man with a warm hand and a notebook I cannot open and a bottle I no longer have and a lamp I cannot light and a coat that is already heavier than it was when I came down because the condensation has soaked into the wool and the wool is holding the water the way the chamber holds the water — in a closed thing, in a circuit, in a system that does not let the water go.

I am a man who has been down here for an hour and a half.

I am a man who will be down here for as long as he can stand the noise.

# What I will do

I will open the notebook.

I will write down what I have seen, in the bureau's language, knowing the bureau's language is insufficient, knowing the bureau knows the bureau's language is insufficient.

I will write down the roar and the light and the warmth and the water and the drift and the roots and the pillars and the tenants and the ring and the wheel.

I will write down the slick on my skin and the warm hand and the missing bottle and the conversation I am not part of.

I will write down that the chamber does not know I am here.

I will write down that the chamber has not paused.

I will write down that I am the only thing in the chamber that is thinking about being in the chamber, and that I am, in that way, the loneliest thing down here, and that I am, in another way, the most alive.

I will write this down because I am a surveyor and I have been sent down here to write things down and I will write things down for as long as I can hold a pen.

Then I will put the notebook back in the oilskin pouch, and I will seal the pouch, and I will put the pouch at my belt, and I will stand in the warm shallow water, and I will listen to the wheel, and I will see whether the wheel has anything to say to me in a language I do not know I know.

And if the wheel does not say anything to me, I will come back up the ladder, and I will file my report, and the report will be added to the ledger that no one opens, and the clerk will note that the eighteenth surveyor has returned, and the bureau will send the nineteenth surveyor when the twentieth surveyor is needed, and the nineteenth surveyor will be someone else, and the nineteenth surveyor will come down here, and the chamber will not have paused, and the nineteenth surveyor will be, for as long as he can stand the noise, the only thing in the chamber that is thinking about being in the chamber.

And if the wheel *does* say something to me — if the wheel speaks to me in a language I do not know I know — I do not know what I will do. The bureau's letter did not say what the well-sinker did after the wheel spoke to him. The bureau's letter said only that the well-sinker came back up the ladder, and that the well-sinker died six weeks later, and that the bureau sent a surveyor.

The bureau sent me.

# What is on me

The slick is still on me. The slick is patient. The slick has not run off. The slick is, I think, still working — still taking the minerals from the water, still taking the faint warmth, still taking the oils from my skin, still making me into a place, briefly, the way the chamber makes everything into a place.

I will wipe the slick off before I climb the ladder, because I do not want to bring the slick up with me, because I do not want the slick to be on the surface, because I do not want the surface to have the slick, because the slick is not for the surface, the slick is for the chamber, the slick is the chamber's way of being polite to its visitors, the slick is the chamber's way of saying *you were here and we received you and you may go now if you wish but you do not have to go and we will not ask you to stay and we will not ask you to leave and you are welcome to stay as long as you can stand the noise*.

The slick is not on my hand. The slick is on my hand, but the hand is warm, and the warmth has done something to the slick, and the slick on my hand is not the same slick as the slick on the rest of me. The slick on my hand is *denser*. The slick on my hand is *more*. The slick on my hand is, I think, the slick that was on the pillar, which was on the pillar before I touched it, which was on the pillar for a very long time before I touched it, which is on my hand now because I touched the pillar and the pillar gave me its slick along with its warmth along with its orientation toward the ring.

I have a hand that is the temperature of the pillar, and a hand that has the slick of the pillar, and a hand that points at the ring whether I want it to or not.

I am, in this small specific way, already part of the loom.

I did not intend to be part of the loom. The bureau did not warn me that I would become part of the loom. The bureau warned me about the noise and the light and the warmth and the tenants and the roots. The bureau did not warn me that the pillars give things to the surveyors who touch them, or that the things the pillars give do not come back.

I will not touch another pillar.

I will not touch another pillar because the first pillar has already changed me, and I do not know what the second pillar would add, and I do not want to find out.

---

# What I have learned

I have learned that the chamber is older than the bureau's word for it.

I have learned that the chamber does not know I am here.

I have learned that the chamber is a process, and that the process does not include me, and that the process will absorb me, gently, if I stay.

I have learned that the well-sinker died because the chamber was not finished with him, and that the chamber was not finished with him because he stayed for two days, and that two days is longer than the chamber allows, or longer than the chamber's patience allows, or longer than the slick allows, and that the slick cleans a person the way a river cleans a stone, and that a river does not stop cleaning a stone when the stone has become clean, and that a river does not know when to stop, and that a river does not know what it is cleaning.

I have learned that the wheel turns, and that the wheel has been turning for longer than the chamber has been a chamber, and that the wheel will turn after I have gone, and that the wheel will turn after the bureau has gone, and that the wheel will turn after the country that administers this province has gone, and that the wheel will turn until it does not turn, and that no one, including the wheel, knows when that will be.

I have learned that I am a small warm thing in a small warm room in the middle of a wet bright world that has not paused to notice me.

I have learned that this is not a comfort.

I have learned that I am welcome to stay as long as I can stand the noise.

# What I will do next

I will open the notebook.

I will write down what I have learned.

I will seal the notebook in the oilskin pouch.

I will stand in the warm shallow water.

I will listen to the wheel.

I will see whether the wheel has anything to say to me.

I will see whether the well-sinker heard what I am hearing.

I will see whether the language the well-sinker did not know he knew is a language I do not know I know.

I will see whether the ring is a thing I can stand close to for an hour and a half.

I will see whether the ring will speak to me.

I will see whether I will come back up the ladder, or whether I will stay down here for two days, or whether I will stay down here for longer than two days, or whether I will become part of the loom, or whether I will become one of the things the bureau files in the ledger that no one opens.

I will see.

I am a surveyor. I have been a surveyor for eleven years. I have surveyed seventeen sites. I have been in deep places. I have learned to listen past the noise. I have learned to write down what I hear past the noise. I have learned to file my reports. I have learned to come back up the ladder.

I will see whether I can do those things here.

I will see whether the chamber will let me.
