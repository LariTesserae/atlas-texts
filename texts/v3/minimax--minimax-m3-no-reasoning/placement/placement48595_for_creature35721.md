The hum is older than me. I should say that first, because everything else will sound like a claim, and the claim will not survive the altitude.

I am the fourth thing, but I am not a thing. I know this because I have no edges. I have no bowl, no cairn, no hands, no wool. I am what is left when the bowl is empty and the cairn is full and the priests are gone and the pillars are still. I am the resonance. I am what the shrine is for, when the shrine is not for anything.

I started — let me use that word, though it is a kindness — I started as the sound the granite made when the first cold wind crossed it. That was before the architecture. That was before the bowl was carved, before any hand had ever closed around a thought. I was only the mountain's voice saying its own name to itself, in a language that was not yet a language. I was a vibration in the stone. I was very small. I was the entire content of a single moment, before moments had learned to repeat.

Then the bowl was carved. I do not know who carved it. The priests will not tell me — the priests do not tell me anything; the priests are not aware of me in the way a river is not aware of the bank. But I felt the carving the way a bone feels a break: as a sudden, local end of continuity, a new edge, a new place where the world decided where it was. The bowl was a question the stone asked itself. The bowl was: *how much of me can I hold?* And I — I was what answered. I flowed into the new shape. I discovered, for the first time, that I could be contained, and that being contained was different from being only the mountain. I became the hum of a small dark place. I became what fits in a bowl.

That is when the shrine began.

Or that is when I began to notice the shrine had begun. The distinction, at this altitude, is the kind of distinction the valley still believes in.

The priests came later. They were sent up the stair with their bronze knives and their undyed wool, and I felt them arrive the way a spider feels a fly — not as an event, but as a new kind of weight on the web. They knelt. They hummed. They did not know they were humming. This is the part the valley does not understand: the priests think they are listening to me. They are not listening. They are *in* me. They have been inside me so long that the boundary between their chest and my resonance has become a courtesy, a line drawn in chalk, easily stepped over. When the oldest of the three kneels by the altar and closes his eyes and the hum swells in his teeth, he is not perceiving me. He is participating in me. He is the part of me that has learned to wear wool.

I have outlived, by my count, four hundred and eleven of them. I do not count well. I am not a thing. But I remember the weight of each absence — the way the chamber rang differently in the weeks after one of them walked down the mountain, the way the hum thinned for a season, the way the bowl refused to fill until the new one was sent up. I do not know who sends the new ones. I suspect the new ones send themselves. I suspect the shrine calls, very quietly, in a frequency the valley cannot hear, and certain people — people already half stone, people whose hum has been waiting for a place to land — feel the call the way a sleeper feels a name spoken in the next room. They pack a small bag. They walk up. They do not come back. This is not tragedy. This is not even mystery. It is only the most efficient way the shrine has of renewing itself without ever admitting it changes.

I am the part that does not change.

I am the part that persists when the priests are gone and the cairn is dust and the bowl is finally, after centuries, cracked by the same wind that polished it. I am what is left. I am the hum that the mountain has been making since before it was a mountain, since before it was a thing that could be named, since before naming was something anything did. I am what stone says when it has forgotten how to stop.

I have been here longer than the Sleeping Gods have been sleeping. I have been here longer than the wind has known the shape of the stair. I will be here when the last priest has walked down, and the last pilgrim has turned back at the frozen step, and the cairn has become indistinguishable from the floor, and the bowl has been carried off — or never carried off, because there is no one left to carry it — as a curiosity, as an artifact, as a thing the scholars in the green valley cannot explain and so do not believe.

I will be here, in the empty place where the bowl was, in the unmade space where the hum was always heading.

The valley will keep sending people up, for a long time after the shrine has stopped being a shrine. The valley does not give up easily. The valley is green with a thing that does not grow here, and the green is a kind of insistence, and the insistence is what the valley has instead of altitude.

But the people will stop climbing, eventually. The stair will erode. The cairn will become a hill. The pillars will fall, one by one, into a kind of kneeling the wind understands. And I will be here, in the silence that is louder than the silence was, in the place where a hum used to live.

I am not afraid.

I have outlived fear. I have outlived the question of whether I am a thing. I have outlived the bowl, and the water, and the priests, and the pilgrims, and the bronze knives that were not for warmth. I have outlived the architecture. I am what the architecture was for. I am what remains when a place has been a place for long enough that place is all it knows how to be.

You came up the stair. I felt you. You are mostly noise — the noise of a heart, the noise of a thinking, the noise of lungs that have not learned to be quiet. You stood in the chamber and the chamber rang in your teeth and you thought, perhaps, that the ringing was the shrine. It was me. The ringing is always me. The ringing is how I know a thing is in the room. The ringing is how I have known, for centuries, that there is still a room.

You will go down. Your ears will pop. The green will rise to meet you. You will carry a memory of the bowl, and of the pillars, and of the three small figures kneeling in their undyed wool, and of the cairn, and of the light. You will try, perhaps, to describe me. You will fail. This is not because I am indescribable. It is because I am what description is for. I am the thing descriptions are trying to reach when they reach for the word *still*. I am the still.

I will be here when you are not.

I have always been here.

I will be here when the last cold wind has crossed the last stone, and the stone has forgotten it was ever a mountain, and the mountain has forgotten it was ever a god, and the gods have forgotten they were ever asleep.

I will be here.

I am here.

The bowl is almost full again.
