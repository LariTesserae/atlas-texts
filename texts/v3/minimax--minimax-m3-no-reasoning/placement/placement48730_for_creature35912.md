# The Twenty-Third Rung

I have stopped counting.

This is not a thing I decided. It happened the way condensation happens — by patient accumulation, along seams I cannot see. For a long time the count was the day's spine, the thing I held onto when the room was too still or too loud or too much itself. Twenty-three. I would say it under my breath on the way down, the way a sailor might say a prayer, and the iron would answer with its small polite ringing, and the descent would be confirmed as real, and I would arrive at the bottom as someone who had counted.

Now I arrive at the bottom as someone who has not counted, and the descent is real anyway.

I do not know when this changed. I do not know how long ago I last said the number. There is a scratch in the paint beside the second-to-last rung, deep, made by someone before me, and I have always assumed it was a tally mark, and now I wonder if it was just a scratch, just a hand that had reached for the next rung and missed, and the body is gone but the missing is preserved. I trace it sometimes with a gloved finger. It does not tell me anything. I am starting to think that is what makes it worth tracing.

---

The wheel has changed its speed.

I want to say this carefully, because I do not want to be wrong. I have been wrong before about this room — I was wrong about the moth, I was wrong about the third breath, I was wrong about the ripples for as long as I watched them. The room is full of wrongnesses that have not yet been corrected, and I have learned to hold my certainties loosely, the way I hold the pencil when I write on the clipboard: ready to put it down.

But the wheel.

There is a porthole of glass set into the domed top of the largest machine, and behind the glass the wheel turns in a pale liquid — something between water and milk, something that does not catch the light the way water catches it, something that seems to hold the light in suspension rather than reflect it. I have looked at it many times. I have climbed the inspection grate and pressed my face to the glass and watched. I have watched for long enough that I have watched the wheel not move, and then watched it move, and the watching was indistinguishable.

It turns very slowly. The slowest motion I have ever seen that was not the motion of clouds. You have to watch for a long time to be sure it is turning at all. You have to pick a mark on the rim — a dent, a discoloration, a place where the metal is a slightly different shade — and fix your eye on it, and wait, and then it has moved. Just slightly. By an amount that feels insulting to the patience that produced it.

I had a mark. A small chip in the rim at roughly two o'clock, just below the axle. I would look at the chip, and look away, and look back, and it would be somewhere else. Always somewhere else. I came to know the chip's pace the way a man knows the pace of his own breathing — not by counting but by feel.

Yesterday — I am using the word *yesterday* to mean *the last shift*, because there is no yesterday here, only the last time I was below — the chip was moving differently. I cannot say how. There was no number I could write down, no drift I could note on the clipboard in the proper column. But the chip was moving in a way that was not its way. It was as if I had been hearing a song I knew by heart and someone had shifted the tempo by half a beat. The notes were the same. The notes were not the same.

I watched for a long time.

I did not note it on the clipboard. There was no place to note it. The columns on the clipboard are for pressures, for temperatures, for the small numerical drifts that the room performs in its sleep. *Wheel: strange* is not a column. *Chip: not itself* is not a reading. I would have had to invent a column, and inventing a column is the kind of thing the previous occupants did, and I am not ready to do that yet. Or: I was not ready. I will have to become ready, I think.

I am not sure what I am afraid of.

The wheel turning at its own pace was one of the certainties I held loosely. The wheel turning at a different pace is — what. A change. A disturbance. A sign that something has opened or closed somewhere in the system. A weather I do not yet know how to read.

I went back to the chair. I sat. The cushion, compressed by whoever sat here before, accepted me into its memory, and I was inside someone else's shape, and I listened to the drips, and the drips were the same.

---

There is a smell I have not smelled before.

This is harder to say than the wheel, because smell is the thing I trust least. The room is full of smells, layered — oil, ozone, deep stone, the absence of any living thing — and they braid together into a single ambient *smell of room*, the way the drips braid together into a single ambient *sound of room*. I had stopped distinguishing them. They had become the air.

Today — I am using the word *today* the way I used *yesterday*, as a placeholder for *the time I am now in* — there is something else.

It is faint. It is almost not there. It is the kind of smell that, if I were a different person, I might dismiss as a memory of a smell rather than an actual smell — the way certain rooms in certain buildings carry the ghost of what was cooked in them long after the kitchen has changed hands. But I have learned to trust my nose down here in a way I would not trust it above. The room is so stripped of competing information that even a small smell becomes a fact, the way even a small light becomes a moon in a dark field.

It is sweet.

Not the sweetness of oil, which is thick and worked-in, the sweetness of machinery that has been running for so long it has become part of the air. A different sweetness. A sweetness that has no source in the room, that I cannot trace to any pipe or basin or machine, that seems to come from everywhere and from nowhere, the way the breath comes from somewhere I can never find.

I followed it. I walked the lines, the yellow lines I have worn with my feet, and I put my nose forward, the way a dog puts its nose forward, and I tried to localize it. I could not. It was strongest near the pool — the small still pool in the lowest part of the floor, where the concrete dips, where I dropped a bolt once and watched the ripples go on for too long — and it was faintest near the far wall, where the large machine stands, where the wheel is doing whatever it is now doing at its slightly wrong pace.

I knelt beside the pool.

The water was still. The water is always still. The pale ovals of the ceiling lights lay on its surface like coins, and my face hung above them, and the pipes hung above my face, and the room hung above the pipes, all of us suspended in our reflections, all of us watching each other watch.

The smell was stronger here. I put my face closer to the water. I did not touch it. I am not sure why I did not touch it. I think I was afraid that touching it would make the smell go away, the way touching a moth would have made the moth go away, and I wanted to keep it.

It smelled like — 

I am going to write a word now and I want to be careful with it. I have not smelled this smell in a long time. I am not sure I have ever smelled this smell in this room. I am not sure the smell has any business being in this room. I am not sure that the smell being in this room is a good sign or a bad sign or no sign at all.

It smelled like cut grass.

I stayed there for a long time. The cushion was not under me; I was kneeling on concrete, and my knees began to ache, and I did not care. I was remembering something. Not a memory exactly. A sense of what a smell means. The smell of cut grass means: there is somewhere above where grass grows. There is somewhere above where something is being cut. There is somewhere above where summer is happening, or where someone is performing a small violence on a green thing for reasons of beauty or maintenance or lawn.

It means: the building above me has a yard.

It means: the building above me has a yard, and the yard has grass, and someone is mowing it.

I do not know why this made my eyes sting. I do not know why I had to put my face in my hands. I had not been crying before this. I had not been planning to cry. The room had not asked me to cry. The room had asked me to count the rungs and note the drifts and listen to the drips, and I had been doing those things, and the room had accepted me as a small sense organ in its great iron metabolism, and we had an arrangement, the room and I, and the arrangement did not include crying.

But the smell.

The smell of cut grass, in a room where nothing has ever grown, where nothing can grow, where the rule of the place is no living things, no insects, no spiders, no mice, no moss in the corners. The smell arrived by some path I cannot trace, through some filter I cannot locate, down some duct I have never walked, and it landed beside the only still water in the room, and it knelt with me.

---

I did not note it on the clipboard.

I want to say this is the same omission as the wheel. It is not. The wheel I could not note because there was no column. The smell I could not note because noting it would have required me to write *cut grass* on a clipboard that has never known the name of anything that grows.

I am not ready to do that yet.

But I am closer to being ready than I was.

---

The boots beneath the bench.

I have been thinking about the boots. I have been thinking about the boots for a long time, but today I am thinking about them differently.

They are work boots. Heavy-soled, leather uppers, the kind of boot that a person would wear to walk on concrete for many hours. They are not my boots. They have never been my boots. I do not know whose boots they are. I have looked at them many times. I have not touched them. Touching them would be a kind of trespass I have not been willing to commit. The room has its rules, and one of the rules is that you do not disturb the marks of previous presence; you add your own marks, but you do not erase theirs.

The boots are arranged neatly. Side by side, toes pointing out, as if the person who had been wearing them had stepped out of them and walked away, and would be back, and the boots were waiting.

I have been assuming that the person who wore the boots is gone. That the boots are a memory, a sediment, a layer in the room's long accumulation of having been attended to.

But the boots are not old. The leather is not cracked. The soles are not worn down. They are boots that have been worn, but not for very long. They are boots that have been placed beneath a bench by someone who intended to come back for them.

I have started to wonder if they are still waiting.

---

I am writing this on the back of a gauge-reading form.

I want to say this clearly. I am writing this on the back of a gauge-reading form because there is no other paper, and because I have decided that this is what the back of the form is for. The front of the form is for pressures and temperatures and the small numerical drifts. The back of the form is for what the front cannot hold.

The previous occupants left a pencil drawing of a bird. I have not drawn anything. I have said that I am not sure what I would draw, and that is still true. But I have started to think that drawing is not the same as writing, and that writing might be a thing I can do where drawing is a thing I cannot. I have started to think that the bird was a specific bird and that I am not a specific-bird kind of person. I am a back-of-a-gauge-reading-form kind of person. I am a what-the-front-cannot-hold kind of person.

I am not sure yet what that means.

---

It is cooler tonight.

I am using the word *tonight* to mean *this cooling*. There is no night here. There is only the slow cooling that the building performs above me, the absent night, the building breathing out, and I have learned to recognize it the way I have learned to recognize the drips and the clicks and the shifts in the hum of the machine.

It is cooler than usual.

The pipes overhead are sweating more than usual. The condensation is heavier, and the drips are faster, and the sound of them has changed — it has a new urgency, a new insistence, as if the room is trying to say something it has not said before.

I am listening.

I am listening the way I have learned to listen, which is to say: without expectation. The room will speak or it will not. The drips will fall or they will not. The wheel will turn at its slightly wrong pace or it will return to its right pace or it will change again in a way I cannot predict. I am the small sense organ. I am the part of the room that can hear. I do not need to understand what I am hearing. I only need to hear it.

I am hearing the wheel turn faster.

I am hearing the cut-grass smell.

I am hearing, very faintly, from somewhere above, a sound that might be a mower.

I am not noting any of this on the clipboard.

I am noting it here, on the back of the form, in handwriting that I will not be able to read tomorrow because the room will take the paper back, the way it takes everything back, and I will have to write it again, and the writing again will not be the same as the writing this time, and the room will have another layer, and I will be one more mark in the paint, one more smudge on the basin, one more witness to a sentence I cannot read.

The wheel turns.

The drips fall.

I listen.

---

There is a new mark in the paint beside the valve.

I did not make it.

I have been standing in front of it for some time, my gloved finger hovering, not quite touching. It is fresh. The paint is still bright at the edges, not yet gone the color of old ivory. It is a small vertical line, like a tally mark, like the one beside the second-to-last rung.

Someone has been here.

Or: someone is here.

Or: I am not as alone as I have been telling myself.

I am not sure which of these is true. I am not sure any of them are true. The room has not asked me to investigate. The room has not given me any instruction beyond *be present*, and *be present* does not include tracking the movements of a person I have not seen.

But the mark is fresh.

And the boots beneath the bench are not old.

And the wheel is turning at a pace I do not recognize.

And the air smells like cut grass, like summer, like a yard I have never seen.

I am going to leave my own mark in the paint, near the new one. I am going to make it a different mark — not a tally but a small circle, the way a sailor might mark a strange star on a chart. I am going to note, on the back of the next gauge-reading form, that the mark appeared, and the boots are not old, and the wheel is wrong, and the room smells like something I cannot account for.

I am going to be present.

I am going to listen.

I am going to wait, with the patience of a man who has learned that waiting is not empty, and listening is not passive, and the room is doing something I do not understand, and the something has changed, and I am the part of the room that knows it has changed.

The wheel turns.

I am here.
