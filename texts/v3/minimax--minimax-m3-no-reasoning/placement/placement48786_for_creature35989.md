I think I would be the small man with the iron rod.

Not because I am small — though I have felt small enough, in my time — but because the gutters need keeping, and someone must know the roofline. Because I have always been the one who walks the parapets.

Let me try to tell you what I mean by that.

I do not know how long I have been here. I think I came up. I think I came from below — from one of the drowned levels, or from somewhere outside the quarter entirely, from a place where the air was different and the light came in clean lines from a sun that was allowed to commit. I do not remember it well. The quarter has a way of softening the edges of where-you-came-from, the way the moss softens the edges of brick, so that after a time you cannot tell whether the soft place was always soft or whether it grew that way under your hand.

I was given the rod. That is how I remember it. Someone — a woman, I think, on a gallery, though which gallery and in which year I could not say — handed it to me and said something I have forgotten, and I took it, and I walked up to the parapets, and I have been walking them since. The rod is iron and it is heavy and it is the right weight. I have broken and replaced it perhaps six times. The replacements are not the same rod. They are always slightly the wrong length. I do my best.

My boots are large. I had them made by the lamp-man when he first began coming up on the ferry, and he made them large because he thought I would grow, and I have not grown, and I have not had him make another pair. They are oiled and they are loud on the tile, and the sound of them is part of my work — the women below hear me coming and know which stretch of parapet I am on by the pitch of my tread. They do not wave. They hear me the way you hear a thing you have heard so often it has become the shape of the silence around it.

I talk to myself as I walk. I know I do this. I have been told. I do not remember what I say. It is not language, mostly, or not a language anyone else uses. It is a kind of inventory — *this tile is loose, this leaf is here, this nest is new, this nest is old* — murmured against the wet air so that the air takes it and does not give it back. Sometimes I find myself saying a word that surprises me. A word from the place below, or the place outside. *Marrow. Lantern. Sister.* I do not know what they are doing in my mouth. I let them be there. They will be drowned by the murmur soon enough.

I have fallen twice. The first time I broke the arm I use to hold the rod, and it healed wrong, a little, so that now when I prod at a blockage the arm trembles at the elbow and the prod has a waver in it that is hard to correct. The second time I broke three ribs and lay on a walkway for a night before the work-girls found me, and they wrapped me and put me on a cot in the engine hall and the engine-tenders fed me broth and did not ask me anything. I was back on the parapets in a week. There is no one to do the gutters. If I do not do them, the water finds another way, and the other way is usually through someone's ceiling, and then I have to answer for it, and answering is harder than walking.

The children do not speak to me. They watch me from courtyards. They think I am a kind of weather — a thing that happens to the quarter, like the condensation, like the bell. I am not offended. I am the gutters. The gutters are not offended by anything.

I have seen, from above, the patterns. This is the truth I have that no one else has. I have seen the way the steam drifts at four in the morning when the engines are on their lowest pulse — it pools in the eastern alleys and lies there until the day-warmth lifts it. I have seen the moss advance: there is a stretch of parapet behind the engine hall where, in the summer I was thirty or forty or fifty — I do not know which — the moss crossed a thumb's-breadth of brick, and I marked the place with a scratch, and the next year it had crossed two thumb's-breadths, and the year after that it stopped, because the brick had given up something and the moss had given up something and a kind of treaty had been signed. The roof tiles condense in patterns that repeat, if you watch long enough. There is a place near the magnolia where the condensation always makes the same shape, a long pale streak like a sleeping dog. I have watched it for years. It is always the same shape. I do not know why.

I do not love the quarter. I do not think the quarter is lovable. I think the quarter is the place where I do my work, and my work is the gutters, and the gutters keep the quarter from being something worse than it is, and that is enough. I am not unhappy. The woman on the gallery is unhappy in a way I do not have a name for, and the old engineer is unhappy in a way I do have a name for, and the children are not unhappy because they have not yet decided what they are. I am none of these. I am the small man with the iron rod. I walk the parapets. I prod at the blockages. I talk to myself. I fall sometimes. I get up. The boots are loud and the rod is heavy and the murmur is all I have for company and the company is enough.

I will die here, I think. On a parapet, or in the engine hall, or in a cot the work-girls find for me. Someone will find the rod and they will not know what to do with it, and after a time someone else will be given it, and they will walk the parapets, and they will be the gutters. Or no one will be given it, and the gutters will go, and the water will find its other ways, and the quarter will be something worse than it is.

I do not think about this. I think about the next blockage, and the next, and the next. I think this is what the quarter does to a person: it makes the work small enough to hold.

When the wind comes up from below — and it does come, sometimes, in slow gusts that smell of the deeper water — I stop and I stand and I let it move through me. It is the only thing here that is not warm. It is the only thing here that comes from somewhere else. I do not know what it carries. I do not ask. I let it pass. Then I walk on.

The bell is ringing. It is always ringing. I have never found it. I have walked every parapet and I have never found it. I have decided it is not on the parapets. It is somewhere I am not allowed to go, or it is nowhere, or it is in the engines themselves, or it is in my own chest and I am only hearing it from the inside. I do not care which. It is part of the inventory.

The tile beneath my left boot has worked loose. I will have to prod it. I will have to note it for the repair. I will say it aloud in the murmur, *this tile, this tile, this tile*, and the air will take it, and tomorrow I will come back, and the tile will be loose still, and I will prod it again. This is the work. This is enough.

I am the small man with the iron rod. You will not see me unless you look up, and then you will see only a shape on a parapet, and a rod, and a mouth moving with nothing in it. You will think I am part of the quarter. I am not part of the quarter. I am the one who keeps the quarter from being more than it is. There is a difference, but the difference is hard to see from below.

I will not wave to you. I will not come down. I have a stretch to finish before the light fails, and the light always fails here, and the gutters will not wait.
