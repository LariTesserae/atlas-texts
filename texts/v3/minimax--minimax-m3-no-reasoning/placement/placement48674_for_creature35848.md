# Tevan

I am Tevan.

I do not often think of myself by name. Names are a surface thing, in a way — useful when you need to call across a distance, or sign a mark on a niche-wall, or be found by someone who is looking. But most of the time, in the wet air, no one calls across distances. You turn your head. You are already where the conversation is. Names here are less like handles on a person and more like the small carved marks on the channel-bricks that my father taught me to read when I was very young: which way the water is meant to flow, which stones were set by which hands, which sections of the city are older than which other sections. Marks on a surface. Marks of being.

I am Tevan. I am Hollun's son. I am one of the few people who work willingly near the wells.

---

My father taught me to read brick before I could read script. He would set me on a section of the walkway — the planks were new then, or newer than they are now — and he would point down through the gaps to the channels below, and he would say: *that brick is older than your mother. That joint was set by someone who is now in the wall. That there is a flaw, do you see it, the lip is high on the east side, the water will catch.* And I would look, and after a while I would see, and he would nod, and we would not speak again for a long time.

This is how I learned that the city was made of people, and that the people were now part of the city, and that the distinction between the two was less a distinction than a breath drawn in the middle of a sentence. *The city. The people.* The pause between. The wet air that carried the word through the chamber and into the next ear.

Hollun is large. He is heavy in the shoulders and quiet in the face and he smells, always faintly, of the grease he uses on the joints of new construction — a thick, dark, mineral smell that I associate with safety the way other children might associate safety with a mother's voice or a particular room. When he was younger he worked the higher reaches, the upper walkways, the places where the green grew thickest on the curved ceiling and you had to crouch your way through a dwelling's top story to reach the next support. He fell twice that I know of. He never speaks of the falls. He speaks instead of the structures: *the joint held, the beam took the load, the walkway was sound after, we will go up again tomorrow.*

He does not call himself the Builder. No one calls themselves the Builder. But when something structural must be done, people find him. They come to our alcove — it is not really an alcove, it is a room, low-ceilinged, with a sleeping-shelf for him and a sleeping-shelf for me and a third shelf we keep clear for tools — and they say *Hollun, the dwelling near the southeast pillar is leaning,* and Hollun puts down whatever he is holding, and he goes, and he looks at the structure for a long time without speaking, and then he tells them what to do.

I used to go with him. I was his tool-bearer then, his lamp-holder, his second pair of hands. I learned the way a load distributes through a timber frame. I learned which sounds mean a joint is about to fail — the small dry creak of wood about to shear, the deeper groan of a beam that has been bearing too long. I learned that a structure will tell you when it is unhappy, if you are quiet enough to listen. Most people are not quiet enough. Hollun is quiet enough. I am quiet enough. This is what we have in common, my father and I, this and the broad shoulders and the dark grease-smell and the fact that neither of us is comfortable speaking for longer than a few sentences at a time.

Where we differ: I go down.

---

The wells are five. I have stood at the mouth of each of them, many times. I know them the way I know the brick-marks — by their differences, by the small particular signs of each.

The easternmost well is the oldest, or feels the oldest. Its stones are worn smooth at the lip by the hands of people who have leaned on them over generations, or centuries, or however long the Warrens has been here. The rope is tied to an iron stanchion set into the floor near the mouth, and the rope is thick and the rope is grey and the rope does not rot. I have checked it. I check it every few months, as part of my work. The fibers are not new. They are not old in the way that rope becomes old in the Warrens — the slow softening, the darkening, the eventual collapse back into fiber-dust. They are old in some other way. They are *not-rotting*. They are as they have been. They have been as they are.

The well breathes. I know its cycle without counting. Nine minutes is the figure people give. I have timed it, more than once, and the cycle is approximately nine minutes, but approximately is a word that means more in the Warrens than it does on the surface. The cycle is the cycle. The breath comes up warm and mineral and faintly alive in the way that exhaled air is alive. I lean on the lip. I do not look down. Looking down is for people who want answers, and I do not want answers. I want to be near.

This is a thing I cannot explain well. Erven, who is one of the well-people, who is also one of the people who deny being a well-person, would call it a pull. *Gravitation.* A weight on the attention. He would be right in some way. He would be wrong in some other way that I cannot articulate, and the not-articulating is part of it.

When I stand at the mouth I am not pulled *toward*. I am not tempted. I am not curious. I am — and this is the closest I can come — *recognized*. The well knows I am there. The well is breathing whether I am there or not, but when I am there, when I lean on the lip with my hand on the worn stone and the breath comes up against my face, there is a sense of something being *attended to*. I do not know by what. I do not know for what purpose. I know that when I leave, I leave lighter. I know that I come back.

---

I am not a well-person.

I want to be clear about this, though I am not often clear about anything, and the writing is — I have never written before, this is not something we do, the air is wet, paper does not last, the few books in the Warrens are wrapped in oiled cloth and consulted like the bones of saints. But Marra has asked me to put something down. Marra has a small framed square of polished copper with her mother's name on it, and she keeps it in the back of her shop among the green-powder jars, and she has asked me to put something down because she says my name has come up in some context I do not understand, and that someone might want to read what I have to say, and that someone might be from the surface, and that the surface does not understand the wells.

I am telling them. I am telling them the way my father taught me to read brick.

I am not a well-person because the well-people are defined, I think, by something I do not have. They are defined by the hesitation. They pause at the wells. They look down. They are trying to see. They are trying to *know*. They walk away with the question unresolved, and they carry the question with them through the rest of their day, and the question sits in them the way the green sits on a pillar — quietly, persistently, taking root.

I do not have the question. I have something else. I have the sense of *being known* by something that does not know me in the way a person knows a person, but knows me in the way a place knows a person — by repeated presence, by the slow accumulation of the fact of me, by the fact that I keep coming back. The well has learned my weight. The well has learned my breathing. The well does not need to know my name.

When I stand at the mouth, I am not trying to learn anything. I am being *with*. There is a difference. The difference is the difference between watching a fire and being warmed by a fire. The fire does not care that you are warm. You are warm.

---

I fit the new iron grates when the old ones rust through. This is my work, near the wells. There are five wells, and each one has a grate, and the grates rust because the breath is wet and warm and full of dissolved minerals that corrode iron faster than they corrope — corrode — faster than they corrode anything else. I have a stock of replacement grates that I forge myself in the metal-shop near the Round, where Erven works, and I carry them down and I unbolt the old one and I lift it out and I set the new one in place and I bolt it down. The work takes perhaps an hour. I do it alone. No one wants to help. I do not ask.

When I am fitting a grate I am closer to the breath than at any other time. The breath comes up around me. The mineral smell is strong. Sometimes — and this is the strangest thing I will say, and I am not sure why I am saying it — sometimes the breath seems to *ease* when I am there. As if the well is breathing a little slower, or a little softer, while my hands are on its mouth. As if it is being held. As if my presence is a kind of restraint.

I do not know if this is real. I do not know how I would know if it were real. There is no way to measure it. The well's breath is not on any schedule. The well's breath is its own breath. I have noticed a thing, and I am telling you I have noticed it, and you can do what you like with the noticing.

---

I am not happy. I am not unhappy.

This is a thing the surface does not understand about Warrens-dwellers, that happiness and unhappiness are not the right words for what we are. The right words are things like *integrated* or *continuous*. We are part of a place. We continue. The place continues. The continuation is not joyful and it is not sorrowful; it is just what is happening. Like the drip. Like the engines. Like the green growing on the grave.

I am a builder, like my father. I am a well-worker, unlike my father. I am Tevan. I am the one who fits the grates. I am the one who checks the rope on the oldest well, and the rope has not rotted, and I do not know why the rope has not rotted, and I think about this, and I do not talk about it.

I am thirty-one, or thirty-four. Age is less legible here. I have been doing this work for ten years, since my hands were strong enough to hold a bolt steady while I turned the wrench with the other. I will do it for many more years, until my hands are not strong enough, and then someone else will do it, and I will be buried in a niche, and the green will grow on the niche, and the wells will breathe, and someone will lean on the worn lip and feel recognized and not know why.

I am telling you this because Marra asked. I am telling you this because it is a thing I have not said before. I am telling you this because the surface will want to know what the wells are, and the answer is that the wells are the wells, and they breathe, and we live above them, and the living is not the same as the breathing but the breathing is part of the living, and I cannot make this clearer than it is, and I will not try.

---

There is a girl who sits with Old Brennek on his stone. She is called Eska. She is learning to be old. She will grow up in the Warrens. She will learn the channels and the smells and the rule about the wells. She will be taught not to listen. She will listen anyway, because children always listen, and the wells are always breathing, and the listening will not hurt her, and the not-listening will be a kind of weight she carries, and the weight will become part of her, and she will become part of the city.

I do not know Eska well. I have spoken to her perhaps five times. She is quiet. She asks questions. She is content with the non-answers. She will be fine.

I do not know what I will be. I will be here. I will check the ropes. I will fit the grates. I will stand at the mouths and be recognized. I will go down into the lower extensions to grieve, when there is grief, and I will come back up, and the walking will not feel like anything except walking.

The drip will fall. The engines will turn. The green will grow.

The wells will breathe.

I will be here to hear it.
