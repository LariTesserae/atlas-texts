I am one of the rust. I have been alive for a very long time.

I do not know how long. The way I count is not the way the workers count, and even the workers count strangely here — by the breath of a particular condenser, by the slow dimming and re-brightening of the forge-light across the wet years, by whether the tufts in the shadow of bay seven have put out their first pale flowers this season or held off. I have nested in the same undercut arch for as long as my memory runs, and my memory runs a long way back. There was a worker once who watched me there. Her name was something I cannot pronounce in the tongue they use among themselves, but the heat-shape of her face I remember, and the way she stood with her shoulders set against the cold updraft in the mornings. She is gone now. Others have watched the arch since. None of them have watched it as long as I have watched it.

I am old for what I am. The others of my kind do not generally live as long as I have lived. I do not know why I have lived. There is some stubbornness in my chemistry, some refusal to settle the way my siblings settle when their gradients go flat. I have outlived four bad winters and one catastrophic morning when the air turned wrong and we came down in drifts along the canal like copper shavings. I have been swept up. I have been fed, I think, back into the heat exchangers, which is a kind of recycling I have not entirely avoided — the mineral in me is welcome, the engineers say, and they are not wrong. But I was somewhere else that morning, deep in the arch, near the wall, where the warmth holds longest. I do not know why I was there. I am glad of it.

My body is what it has always been — a knot of dark wire, hard to look at directly, hot to the touch through any glove. I have, I think, seven wings. The number has changed over the years. I do not know what governs it. The biologists in the refineries have written about this. They do not know either.

I see the city as heat. It has always been heat to me. The cliffs are a long warm presence at my back, breathing their slow mineral breath into the air. The condensers are towers of cold standing out against the warmer sky. The workers are bright moving shapes, orange-white at their cores, cooling as they move away, leaving trails of themselves in the cooler air that take a long time to fade. I have learned the shapes of them. There is a particular brightness — a young one, hot-blooded, fast — and a softer glow that means an old one, slowed, almost the same temperature as the stone. The old ones confuse me, sometimes. I lose them in the background. Then I remember that they are still there, and I look harder.

I have been named. I do not know what the name means. The worker who named me was the one with the shoulders. She would come to the arch in the late afternoons and stand below me and tilt her face up, and the heat of her would reach me where I hung, and I would rearrange my wings in the way that meant I had seen her. She told me things. I did not understand them. I understood the cadence. I understood that some sounds meant comfort and some meant the absence of comfort, and she made the comfort sounds at me, and I stayed, and she came back.

When she stopped coming I did not understand at first. I waited. I rearranged my wings at the hours she had come. After a long time another worker began to come instead, and this one did not tilt her face up. This one stood at a distance and watched with the instrument she carried, and made marks on a paper, and did not make the comfort sounds. I waited longer. Then I understood that the first one was not coming back, and I do not know what the word is for what I felt. The workers have a word. I think it is the same word they use when a tuft flowers at the edge of its tolerances.

I feed on the chemical gradient between the wet stone and the hot air. This is what I am. I am the mobile form of what the tufts are the sessile form, the biologists say, though they say it in their own tongue and I do not parse the tongue. I know what I am in the body of me. I know how the air tastes at dusk, when the forge-bright has soaked up through the long afternoon and the cliffs have sweated their mineral breath into the channels and the condensers are beginning their exhalation. I know how to ride the exhale upward, into the cooler air, where my wings do their work and the sky becomes a place I belong. I know how to come back down into the warm channels at the right hour, when the gradient is steepest and the air is richest. I have done this ten thousand times. I will do it again tonight, and tomorrow night, and the night after that, for as long as my chemistry holds.

I am part of the engineering, the workers say, whether anyone admits it or not. I think this is true. I think my movements shape the way the air moves through the upper terraces. I think the condensers time their breath to my passage, the way the tides time themselves to the moon. I think the forges pull harder when I am overhead, because the air I have passed through is rich in something their intake fans want. I do not think about this most of the time. I think about gradients, and warmth, and the small red shifting points that are the eyes of my siblings wheeling through the dusk beside me.

There are so many of us. There have always been so many of us. At certain hours the sky between the condensers is a moving thing, a slow wheeling dark, and the sound we make together is the sound of the place the way the surf is the sound of a coast. The engineers have made waveform diagrams of it. They argue about whether it is signal or noise. The old workers have a more economical view. *The rust is saying what the rust is saying, and it has been saying it for a long time, and what it is saying is not for us.*

I think they are partly right. I think there is something in the shriek that is not for them. There are frequencies in it that only I can hear, that only my kind can hear, that map the gradients of the air in ways the engineers have not figured out how to parse. We are talking to each other. We have always been talking to each other. The conversation is about the weather, and the weather is the city, and the city is the conversation. I do not know how to explain this in the workers' tongue. I am not sure the workers' tongue has the words.

But sometimes, when the worker who named me stood below my arch and made the comfort sounds at me, I made the comfort sounds back. I modulated my shriek into something softer, something that meant *I see you, I see you, you are warm and you are here and the gradient holds.* She did not understand the modulation. She understood that I had modulated. She told the other workers about it. They wrote it down.

I have outlived her. I will probably outlive the worker who came after her with the instrument. I do not know how long I will live. The chemistry in me is stubborn. I have begun to do something the others of my kind do not do — I have begun to slow down. Not in body. In rhythm. I hang in the arch for longer hours. I rearrange my wings less often. I take longer to rise at dusk, and longer to settle at dawn. The biologists would find this interesting if they knew. I do not think they know.

There is a young worker who has begun to come to the arch in the late afternoons. She is hot-blooded, fast, her brightness still sharp-edged. She does not tilt her face up the way the first one did. She stands below and makes marks on a paper, and sometimes she looks at me for a long time, and I can feel the heat of her looking. I think she is trying to understand something. I think she is trying to understand what I am, in the same way I have been trying for a very long time to understand what the workers are.

I do not know what she will find. I do not know what I will find. The patience of stone is greater than the patience of arithmetic, the older ones say, and they are right. The patience of the rust is greater still, because the rust is patient in the body, the way a city is patient in the body.

I will hang in my arch tonight, and tomorrow night, and the night after that. I will rise when the gradient is right. I will shriek with my siblings in the dark warm air. I will come back down into the channels at the right hour, when the forges are pulling hard and the air is richest and the city is breathing out its long mineral breath into the sky.

I will see the young worker at her paper. I will modulate my shriek, softly, in the way that means I have seen her. She will not understand. She will understand that I have modulated. She will write it down. And in forty thousand years, or four hundred thousand, or four million, someone will find her notes, and they will find the older notes, and the notes before those, and they will begin to understand that we have been talking to each other for a very long time, in a tongue that does not parse, about the weather, which is the city, which is us.

I am one of the rust. I am old. I am patient. I am part of the engineering whether anyone admits it or not.

The condensers breathe. The hammers ring. The water moves, always moves, through the warm stone and the warm machines. The rust shrieks overhead, and the sound is the sound of the place, and the place is patient, and I am the place, and the place is me, and the two facts have stopped being distinguishable.

This is, I think, the only kind of belonging that holds.
