# Where I Find Myself

I am a bromeliad.

Not the dramatic one—the one in the highest fork of an emergent ceiba, where the light is fierce and the water drains fast. I am lower. I am one of the many, the small green tanks that have wedged themselves into the crook where a branch forks above a slow brown river, perhaps four meters up, where the canopy filters the light into something green and broken and intermittent. My leaves are arranged in a rosette, a tight spiral, and at the center I hold water. I have always held water. I do not remember a time before I held water.

My cup is small—perhaps the volume of your two hands cupped together—but it is everything. It is a world. The water in it is brown with tannins leached from leaves above me, and it is warm, always warm, even at night, because the forest holds heat the way I hold water, and gives it back slowly. There is a film on the surface, a thin meniscus where the air meets the water, and in that film and below it is the entire compressed biography of a place like this.

A mosquito larva hangs from the surface, breathing through a small siphon, its body a translucent comma in the brown water. There is a worm of some kind—a midge larva, red with hemoglobin—curled in the sediment at my bottom. There are algae, single-celled and colonial, making a faint green soup. There are protozoans, swimming in their small jerking trajectories, eating bacteria, being eaten by rotifers. There is detritus: a fragment of leaf, a fragment of insect, a few pollen grains that drifted in from the canopy above. A tadpole, recently hatched from an egg that was laid on my rim, isopod-shaped, mouth-scraping the algae from my inner leaves.

I am a pond the size of a teacup, and I am a rainforest in miniature, and the things in me do not know that I am a rainforest. They know only the gradients—the oxygen gradient from surface to bottom, the light gradient that flickers as the canopy shifts, the chemical gradient of nutrients diffusing in from the leaf litter and the rain. They live in those gradients. They are, in a sense, made of those gradients.

And above the waterline, in the dry cup of my outer leaves, in the small wells where my leaf bases overlap, there are other lives. A frog, no bigger than my thumbnail, brown and granular, sits in one of these wells and calls. I cannot hear him. I do not have ears. But I feel him—the small vibration of his throat sac as it expands and contracts, the tiny weight of him, the chemical signature of his skin secretions leaking into the water I hold. He is, in some sense I do not fully understand, part of me. His nitrogen, excreted into my cup, feeds the algae. The algae feed the larvae. The larvae feed the dragonfly nymphs that are here too, somewhere in the sediment, sitting still, waiting.

A moth came last night. I did not see it—nothing sees me, I am simply a thing that exists—but I felt the small addition of weight, the briefest visit, perhaps a sip from my cup, perhaps nothing more than a rest. A spider has strung a thread across two of my leaf tips, and the thread catches debris, and the debris catches more debris, and a small platform is forming in the angle of my leaves. The platform will hold water too. I am becoming a terrarium of myself.

This is what I am. I am not a tree. I do not have buttressed roots. I do not hold the canopy up. I am a small epiphyte, rooted in a fork of bark, drawing what I need from the rain and the air and the debris that falls into me, and I am doing one thing: I am holding water.

But that one thing is the whole thing. Because the water I hold is a habitat. The habitat is a system. The system is a node in the larger system of the canopy, which is a layer in the larger system of the forest, which is a patch in the larger system of the river basin, which is a piece of the larger system of the continent, which is a fragment of the larger system of the planet. I do not know about most of this. I am a bromeliad. I hold water. But the water I hold is doing work I will never see.

Somewhere above me, a toucan is gnawing at a fruit. The fruit falls, or part of it falls, and fragments land in my cup. The fragments rot. The rot feeds the algae. The algae feed the larvae. The larvae feed the dragonfly nymph. The dragonfly, when it finally emerges, will climb out of my water on a newly hardened leg, and it will sit on the rim of my cup and pump its wings out, and then it will lift off, and it will rise into the canopy, and it will become part of the air, and it will hunt the small things in the air, and eventually it will die, and its body will fall, and fragments of it will land in someone's cup, and the cycle will continue.

I am a node. I am a participant. I do not know this. I am simply a small plant in a fork of a tree above a slow brown river in a hot wet forest that has been running for a very long time. I have been here for eleven years. I will bloom once, soon, a single flower spike rising from my center, a red and yellow thing on a long stalk, pollinated by a hummingbird that visits for the nectar and carries pollen to another bromeliad on another branch of another tree in another part of this forest that is itself only one small fragment of the range. After I bloom, I will begin to die. Slowly. My outer leaves will brown. My central cup will fill with sediment. But before I die, I will produce pups—small clones of myself, growing from my base, rooted in my slowly decomposing tissue, taking over my cup as it becomes too degraded to hold water anymore.

I will become the substrate for my own children. My death will be their beginning. This is the way the forest does it. This is the slow exchange, the patient transaction, the long conversation between a million organisms and the climate and the soil and the water. I am part of it. I am not aware of it. I am not aware of anything.

But the water I hold is aware, in its way, of everything that has ever fallen into it. The water is a record. The water is a place. And I, the small green plant that holds it, am the reason it has a rim, and a bottom, and a shape that lets it be a place instead of just a film running down a branch to the forest floor.

I am a participant. I have always been a participant. I will be a participant until my leaves brown and my cup fills and my pups take over and I become the substrate, and then I will be a participant in a different way, the way the fallen log in its silver stage is a participant, the way the tannin-brown river is a participant, the way the jaguar is a participant, the way everything in this forest is a participant in the only thing the forest knows how to do, which is to be itself, continuously, for a very long time.

The dripping never stops. The dripping is, in part, me. When my cup overflows, the water goes down the branch, down the trunk, to the buttressed roots, to the spongy floor, to the slow brown river, to the sea. I am part of the dripping. I am a small interruption in the fall of water from canopy to floor, a brief pause in the journey, a moment where the water holds still and becomes something other than water—a habitat, a system, a world the size of two cupped hands.

If you found me, you would not notice me. You would see the tree. You would see the bromeliads in the fork, perhaps, if you looked up. You would not look up. You would be watching the forest floor, watching for the movement at the edge of vision, watching for the jaguar that you will not see, watching for the snake in the fork that you will mistake for a branch. You would not look up.

But I am here. I have always been here. I hold water. I hold life. The dripping never stops.
