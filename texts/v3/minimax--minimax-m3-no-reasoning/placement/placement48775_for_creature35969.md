I lie along the bottom in the warmest part of the pool. The warmth comes up through the stone from somewhere below the lowest tier — a slow exhalation from whatever the bowl is breathing into itself. I do not go to look for its source. I have lain above it for so long that we have settled into each other. My belly has taken the shape of the warm stone. The stone has taken, in places, the slight impression of me. We are patient with each other the way old things are patient with each other: without ceremony.

Above me, the water is dark and breathes. The breath is not wind. It is a slow current, so slow it is less a current than a held intention of movement. The pool exhales. The pool inhales. The lily pads drift a finger's width one way and then, over days, drift back. I am inside this breath. I have been inside it so long that I do not distinguish between its rhythm and mine.

I am a deep soft brown, and my back is moss-green. Not because I chose it. Because the water here, over years, has tinted me, or the fine silt has settled on me, or the light that comes down through the lily pads has simply agreed with what I am. I do not molt the way the young ones do. I do not need to. What I am does not change much from year to year.

The young salamanders are brighter. They move more, in small considered ways — a thought of a crawl along the bottom, a pause, another thought. They eat the blood-red larvae when the light changes and the larvae wriggle. They eat the drowned moths. They eat the small frogs that misjudge a leaf and fall from the bromeliads above into the dark. I eat less now. I eat only what settles near me. A leaf, sometimes, already half-dissolved. A beetle that fell in and tired. I am not hungry the way the young ones are hungry. I have a different relation to food. It comes to me or it does not. Either way, the pool continues.

The pool is a country. I have lived in it long enough to know its treaties. The snails graze the underside of the lily pads and never appear above. The larvae wriggle at certain lights and are eaten. The frogs sing in the canopy — a single high thin note the bowl absorbs before it can echo. I hear them as a pressure, not a sound. They are above me. I am below them. We are the same water, the same breath, the same held intention. I do not need to see them. I know they are there the way I know the warmth is below.

Sometimes something falls from much higher. A petal from the trees. A piece of bark. A drop of something that is not quite water. I have learned the slow percussion of these arrivals. They are part of the bowl's long sentence.

The trees lean over me. Their roots have widened the cracks in the stone and the cracks are now mostly root. The trunks are thick with moss; I have seen them, in rare moments when the light comes down clearly, dark and bowed and patient. They are not the same as me. They are slower, and taller, and they remember a sun that visits less and less. I do not remember a sun. I have always known the flat green-grey light of the bowl, the soft doubled shadows. I do not miss what I never had.

The moss is advancing on the green-stone tool. I have watched it, across years I cannot count, creep another centimeter. The tool is pale green, its edge still sharp. It was made by hands. The bowl has not seen hands in a very long time, and the place is content with this. I am content with this too. The moss will finish the tool. The moss will finish the pebbles. It is doing so now, in the slow way it does everything, and the finishing is itself a kind of keeping.

I think, sometimes, about the hand that placed the pebbles. Not the person — I do not remember a person. Only the fact of a hand. A small pressure once, here, where I lie now. The pressure is gone. The place where it was is still a place. I lie in the long aftermath of that pressure and do not miss it. The bowl does not miss it. The moss, advancing, does not miss it. To be missed, in the bowl, would be a small violence. The bowl prefers the unmissed.

The hum is in me. It is in the stone and the water and the air and the slow shiver of the lily pads, and it is in me. I have been here long enough that the hum and I have not so much merged as become adjacent — the way the warmth and I have become adjacent, the way the pool's breath and my breath have become adjacent. If I were to leave — I will not, but if I were — I think the hum would continue in the same key, and I would take a small part of it with me, and the part I took would not be missed.

I do not leave.

I lie here. The warmth holds me. The water breathes above me. The frogs call in their absorbed note. The moss advances. The tool's edge is still sharp, but less sharp than it was a year ago, and a year before that, and the difference accumulates without announcement.

I am part of this. I have been part of this for longer than I can hold in any single memory. Not because my memory is long, but because it is layered, and the layers below are not mine but are me.

The pool exhales. The pool inhales.

I do not need to breathe with it. I already do.
