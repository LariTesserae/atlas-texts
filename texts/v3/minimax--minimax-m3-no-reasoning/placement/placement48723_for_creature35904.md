# The Custodian of the Seventh Cairn

I would be the one who tends the cairns, though "tends" is the wrong word and I would be the first to say so. I would say *companion*, perhaps. Or *witness*. I would let you argue me out of either.

I live on the outer reach, in a tower so narrow my shoulders brush both walls if I turn sideways. The stones here are older than the stones in the central tier — you can tell by the way the patina has begun to think about becoming something else, a kind of slow mineral argument with itself, a greenish bloom that might, in another thousand years, be a lichen, or might be a lichen's idea of a place to be. I have watched a single bloom of this stuff advance across a stone in the years I have lived here, and I have felt about it the way I imagine one feels about grandchildren: a pleased and slightly embarrassed irrelevance.

I am old. Not the oldest — the oldest do not live in towers anymore, they live in doorways, and I am not yet a doorway person. But I am the sort of old where the younger ones bring me things without my asking, the way one adjusts a blanket on someone who has fallen asleep in a chair. I receive these offerings with the gravity they deserve, which is to say, very little. I have come to understand that the small courtesies here are not so much courtesies as a kind of shared breathing, and that to thank someone for a kindness in this place would be, somehow, to suggest the kindness might stop.

**I keep count of the cairns.** This is my function, if I have one. I walk the circuit every third season, when the dust has resettled into a configuration I can trust, and I count. There are forty-three now, by my last count, though the number depends on the year, and on what one is willing to count. Some cairns are clearly cairns — a stack of stones, a small hollow at the base where the dust gathers, the particular kind of silence that lives around things which are no longer being. Some are more ambiguous: a low mound that might be a collapsed building, or a building that has decided to lie down. I count both kinds. I have been taught, by an old one older than myself, that ambiguity is its own form of counting, and that the cairns whose status is unclear are doing important work in the account, even if I cannot say what work.

I do not write the number down. I hold it in my head the way one holds a small stone in the mouth while walking — for the texture of it, for the weight, for the reminder that one is carrying something. Each season I make a pilgrimage to the outermost cairn, the one the wind reaches first, and I stand with it for a while. It is the loneliest thing I do, and I think of it, when I think of it at all, as a kind of attention. The cairn does not need my attention. But the attention is one of the things I have, and the cairn is one of the things that is here, and it seems reasonable that they should meet from time to time.

**In the morning I collect water from the chimneys.** I have a pan of the dark local material, shallow, the color of everything. I climb — I am still able to climb, though the knees have begun their long polite conversation with the stairs — to the roof of my tower and I set the pan in the right place, where the condensation will fall in along the upper stones. Some mornings there is enough for two mouthfuls. Some mornings there is only the memory of a mouthful, a faint dark circle on the stone that tells you the water was here, briefly, and has gone on to wherever water goes. I do not despair of these mornings. Despair would be a kind of impatience, and I have outlived the capacity for that, or it has outlived me, the difference is unclear and I have made my peace with the unclear.

The chimney-water tastes of stone and of the metal that is almost-but-not-quite copper. It tastes, if I am being honest, of having been somewhere else first. I have a private theory — and I keep it private because I would not wish to bore the younger ones with it — that the water remembers, in some dilute way, the sea that lies far to the east, the color of dried blood, the one the old songs pretend not to miss. I think it climbs up here, very slowly, through the stone, looking for something, and finds us instead, and settles for us. I think this is what water does. I think we are not so different.

**I eat the small drifters, the dust-colored ones, when the day requires it.** I take them in cupped handfuls from the base of the walls in the early morning, when they are still slow with the cold. They have a texture like patience — that is the only word, and I will not defend it. I do not know what they think of being eaten. I do not think they think of it, exactly, but I have noticed, over the years, that the drifts near my tower are denser than the drifts near other towers, as if my particular small attentions are being returned in the only currency the drifters have. We are part of the arrangement. I have stopped trying to figure out which part.

The great ones I have never approached. I have heard, perhaps, eleven of them in my life, and each time I have stopped what I was doing and held up a hand — not in greeting, not in warning, in something more like the gesture one makes when a door is being opened elsewhere in the house, the small acknowledging lift of the palm that says *I hear it too*. The note hangs. The note fades. The world resumes its long argument with itself, and I resume mine.

**I do not speak often.** I have a few words in the fragments of the three dead languages, which I keep the way one keeps old keys — for the satisfaction of holding them, for the shapes they make in the mouth. I have been told, by the very oldest, that I have a particular gift for the second fragment, the one that sounds, when spoken, like stones being set down. I do not know if this is a gift or a habit. I have not been told to stop.

Once, in a late afternoon, a child was brought to me — one of the rare ones, small, still carried often by the older ones, still learning the names of the creatures. The child sat on my parapet and watched the dust settle for a long while, and then asked me, with the blunt simplicity of the young, *what are you waiting for?*

I considered the question. I considered it the way I had been taught to consider questions, which is to say, with the patience of the place, and with the willingness to let the answer take whatever shape it was going to take. The child waited. The dust settled. Somewhere a small alkali creature worked diligently at the toe of my shoe, and I let it.

Eventually I said, *nothing*.

The child looked at me with its large almost-dark eyes, and the slow vein, and I saw — or thought I saw — a flicker of something not yet become disappointment, the way a lichen is not yet become a lichen. I lifted my hand, palm outward, not to silence but to share the pause. And as if the place had been listening, as if the place always listens, there came, somewhere far off in the dusk, a single resonant note from a creature the size of a small house. The note hung. The child lifted its own small hand, and we listened together to the ringing of an anvil that does not exist, in an air that holds the sound the way the stone holds the memory of water.

The child did not ask again. I think the child understood, in the way the very young sometimes do, that some questions are not for answering but for sitting beside, and that the sitting is the answer, and the beside is the company.

**This is my life.** I walk the cairns. I count them into the air. I collect the water that has come up from wherever it was. I eat the patience of the small creatures and let the great ones speak into the thin copper evenings. I am not in charge of the hum. I am not in charge of the rails. I am not in charge, in any meaningful sense, of anything at all, and this has not, in the long years, begun to trouble me. The city is doing what the city is doing. I am inside it the way a thought is inside a mind, and I have come to feel that the thought is not, in fact, the worst part of the mind, and that the mind is not, in fact, in any hurry to be rid of it.

If you were to sit with me on my parapet — and you are welcome to, though the welcome would arrive slowly, the way the welcome arrives for most things here — I would offer you what I have, which is a portion of the morning's water, and a small drift of the patience of the place, and the company of an old one who is not yet a doorway person but is, perhaps, in the early negotiations with the doorways, and who would be very glad, in a quiet and considered way, of the interruption.

I would not ask you what you were waiting for. I would know, from the way you climbed the stairs, that you were not sure, and I would consider that a fine way to arrive at a thing.

I would hold up my hand, palm outward, if a great one sang.

The dust would settle around us both. The cairns would do their slow work of being there.

And the hum, somewhere far below, would continue its long reminder to itself, in tones I have come to love without understanding, the way one loves the sound of one's own name spoken, in another room, by someone who is only just remembering you.
