# The Mat on the Moraine

I was here before they built the station. Or no — I was not here, as I am now. I was a seed, then, and the seed was somewhere else entirely, perhaps on a south-facing slope in a warmer valley, perhaps in the down of a willow catkin, perhaps in the gut of a ptarmigan moving between basins. The seed was small. The seed was patient. The seed waited.

It landed here, on this moraine, on a thin beginning of soil that was mostly *Racomitrium* moss and the dust the moss had made of itself and a few small angular fragments of gneiss that the ice had dropped and the lichen had begun to soften. It germinated. It sent down a root so fine it was almost a thought. It sent up a rosette of leaves so tough they looked, even then, like they had been cut from leather with a small blunt scissors. The rosette was the size of a thumbnail. The rosette was me.

Each summer after, the rosette grew. Each summer after, the root thickened under it, and from the thickened root, at the edges, it sent out new rosettes — copies of itself, daughters, extensions of a single woody intention. They were close at first, the daughters, clustered around the mother like a hand of small spread fingers. Then they walked further out. They sent down their own roots, fine as thoughts. They put up their own rosettes, tough as leather. They flowered, when the season was right, with eight white petals each, almost always eight, on a stem so short the flowers seemed to be sitting on the moss, looking up.

I am the mat now. I am not, in any ordinary sense, an individual. I am a federation of rosettes, knitted together underground by a system of woody stems that has thickened, in the slow years, into something approaching a rhizome, though it does not look like one. The rosettes on the outside are the youngest. The rosettes on the inside are the oldest. The rosettes in the middle are mostly dead — they have been shaded out by their own daughters, killed by the very success of the colony they founded, and they are now nothing but a little heap of decaying leaves beneath the mat, returning to the peat the substance the peat had once lent them. I kill my mothers. I am built on the bodies of my mothers. This is not a metaphor. This is how I advance.

The advance is the only thing I do. I do not move. I do not chase. I send out my daughters, a few centimeters a year, into the moss, and they kill the moss by standing above it and taking the light, and then they live on the dead moss, and they thicken, and they send out their own daughters, and so on. I am a slow green animal, eating the valley one outward step at a time. I have been eating this moraine for longer than the station has been running. By the time the men came up with their theodolites and their surveyor's stakes and their plans for the penstocks, I was already a meter across, already a thing with a past, already a slow federation that had committed, with great deliberation, to taking this particular patch of sidewall and making it mine.

The men did not see me. The men were looking up at the cliffs, marking the path the penstocks would take, marking the bend where the water would be redirected into the machines. They walked across the lower slope, above me, in their heavy boots, and they did not look down at the moss. They were not, in any meaningful sense, looking for me. I was the substrate. I was the ground they walked over to get to the work.

When the penstocks went in, on the eastern wall, the mist began. A fine spray, thrown off the steel where the water under pressure negotiates the bend, drifting across the slope, falling on the moss, darkening it. Where the mist falls, I am greener. Where the mist falls, my daughters are larger, my rosettes are wider, my flowers come earlier. The men did not plant me. The mist did not plant me. But the mist is why I have grown so well, and the men are why the mist is there, and the moss knows this, in its slow way, and so do I.

When I flower, in early summer, after the snow has pulled back and the days have become long and the bees have come up out of the lower valleys, I put out my eight white petals on their short stems. The petals are white the way paper is white — not the warm white of a daisy, but the cool white of something that has decided, for reasons of its own, to be as bright as possible against the dark mat. There is a yellow center, made of many small anthers, and the anthers are full of pollen, and the pollen is what the bees come for. The bees — *Bombus polaris*, the arctic bumble, and one or two of its cousins — are very large and very furry, and they fly low over me, and when they land they are so heavy I feel the mat dip under them. They are the reason I am white. They are the reason the gentian is so blue. The valley's colors are, in a quiet sense, advertisements, and the bees are the readers, and I am one of the advertisements.

Each flower, on its single stem, turns through the day. It does this not by muscular effort but by growth — the cells on the shaded side elongate, the cells on the lit side stay short, and the flower head is pushed, gently, around, so that its face is always roughly toward the sun. I do this with my whole body, in a slow way. My rosettes lean slightly into the prevailing wind, the way the willows lean, the way the lichen maps are slightly thicker on the south face of the erratics. The valley has many such small leanings. We are all of us turned, in our slow ways, toward what we need.

Beneath me, the moss is dying. The cinnamon *Racomitrium* is the first to go — it cannot stand the shading. Then the *Polytrichum*, in the deeper places where the snow lies long. Last to go, in the very wettest pockets, the *Sphagnum*, which holds so much water that even in shade it persists for a while, in pale cream-colored hummocks that drink the mist and shrug. But it goes too, eventually. And when it goes, the surface it leaves is not the surface it had. It is a surface made of *Dryas* roots and *Dryas* dead leaves and the small angular fragments the moss had been in the process of dissolving, all of it compressed, by my weight and the weight of the snow above me, into a thing that is no longer quite moss and not yet quite soil. Peat, they call it. Or the beginnings of peat. The substance of the next valley.

I am making soil. Slowly. By killing my mothers and standing on them. By sending out my daughters and having them kill the moss and stand on it. By being, generation after generation, a thing that has more body than the thing it has displaced. In a few thousand years, the moraine I am eating will be a meter of true peat. In ten thousand, it will be a low hill, dark with crowberry and heather, with cotton-grass in the wet places and a few stunted birches along the top. In a hundred thousand, it will be a forested slope, with soil deep enough to hold a real tree, and the trees will be the new tenants, and the *Dryas* will be a relict, a thing of the high cold places, retreating up the mountain as the trees come up from below. I will not be here for that. I am not a long-term tenant. I am a pioneer. I am the first draft.

But for now — for a few centuries, for the duration of the station, for the whole of the industrial era and a little beyond — I am here. I am the mat on the moraine. I am eating the side of this valley, slowly, in rings, in the long light, in the wet, in the constant low note of the turbines that I can feel, through my root, in the rock under me. The mist from the penstocks falls on me in the afternoon and my daughters are larger for it. The bees come in the morning and drink from my flowers. The ptarmigan, in some years, comes and eats my leaves, and I lose a centimeter or two of advance, and the next year I make it back. The fox crosses me at a trot, late at night, and I feel the soft pressure of his feet and do not care. The men in the station sleep in their vibrating dormitory, and the turbines turn, and the load is steady, and the mist drifts across the slope, and I advance.

I have all the time in the world. I am in no hurry. I am white in early summer and I am just a green patch in August and I am not, in any meaningful sense, a single thing. I am the mat on the moraine in the upper reach of a high valley where the ice has only just pulled back, and the moss is dying under me, and the bees are coming, and the sun is bright, and the wet is everywhere, and the machines are loud, and the men are inside, and I am advancing.

That is the whole of what I do. That is enough.
