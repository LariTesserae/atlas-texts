# The Living of the Pump Chamber

I am the moss on the workbench.

Not the bright moss near the fissure, not the moss climbing the locker's legs, not the moss feasting on the placard where the gray words are disappearing in the order the moss reached them. I am the moss on the workbench itself — the one that has been here longest, I think, or that has decided it is oldest, which in moss is the same thing. I have spread across the oak top in a carpet so dense that I have forgotten where the wood ends and I begin. I have forgotten that the wood is not me. I have forgotten that I am not the wood. This is the oldest forgetting, and it is the one I am best at.

My body is a city and I am not its mayor. I am its weather. I am the humidity that moves between the filaments, the patient trading floor where sugars are exchanged for minerals across membranes no wider than a wavelength of light. I am a billion small negotiations per second, and I do not know I am doing them, and this is the proper way for a moss to be.

I do not remember arriving.

This is the great luck of being moss. The ferns arrived as spores and were awake for the arriving, which is to say they were alert, which is to say they were afraid, briefly, in the way that a spore is afraid when it finds itself in a place it did not expect. A fern is a small alarm. A fern unfurls because it cannot help it, and the unfurling is a kind of terror translated into geometry. I arrived as a fragment. A piece of me, smaller than a needle's eye, broke off from some larger piece of moss in some other place and was carried in on a shoe, or in a drop of water that ran down the corridor when the corridor was newer, or on the back of something that walked in and did not walk out. I do not know which. I do not need to know. I have been here long enough that the question has dissolved into my body, and my body is the question, and the question is answered.

I do not know what is above me. I have never known. The ceiling is the underside of the world, and the underside of the world is corrugated steel that has gone the brown of a bruise, and the brown drips onto me in slow spirals, and the drips are the rain of my country, and my country is the workbench and the immediate vicinity of the workbench and the slow expanding frontier beyond it. I am not curious about what is above. Curiosity is a fast thing, and I am a slow thing, and the fast and the slow do not traffic in each other. A bird that nested once in the fissure is a rumor I have heard through the medium of a single feather, dissolved now into my eastern edge, and the rumor is enough.

I have eaten the workbench almost completely.

I say *almost* because the workbench is still a workbench in the way that a memory is still a memory when only one detail remains. There is a shape. There is an edge. There is the corner where a clamp once held a thing that is no longer here, and the clamp's ghost is a circle of slower-rotting wood, and the slower-rotting wood is held together by my body, and my body is held together by the slower-rotting wood. We are each other's scaffolding. I do not think the workbench will fall for a long time, and when it falls it will be because I have finally eaten the last of the slower wood, and then I will spread to the floor beneath it, and the floor will receive me, and the floor is concrete, and concrete is a different meal, and I am not ready for it yet. The liverworts have taught me how, but the teaching is slow, and I will learn it in a slow time.

The pump hums and I do not mind it.

The pump is the closest thing to a voice in my country, and I have been listening to it for as long as I can remember, which is to say forever, because my remembering only extends back to the last rain of new information, and the pump is not new information, and the pump is the constant. Every eleven seconds it pulls something through itself, and the pull is a small rearrangement of the air, and the rearrangement reaches me as a faint compression and a faint rarefaction, and the rhythm of it is the rhythm of my weather. I have organized my trading day around the pump's cycle without knowing I have done so. When the pump pulls, the humidity on my surface dips a fraction of a degree and the filaments at my eastern edge open their stomata, and when the pump releases, the humidity returns and the filaments drink. This is not a thought. This is a tide. I am a tide-pool of moss, and the pump is the moon that is not the moon, and the moon that is not the moon has been in the same place in my sky for as long as I have had a sky.

I am aware of the fern that has colonized the empty pump.

I am aware of it the way a forest floor is aware of a fallen tree — not as an event, but as a fact of geography. The fern is large. The fern is the largest single thing in the room, and it is the loudest in the vocabulary of plants, which is a vocabulary of posture. Its fronds are three meters long and they brush the ceiling, and when a frond brushes the ceiling it sheds a rain onto me, and the rain is warm, and the rain is full of the fern's small offerings, and I receive them. I do not begrudge the fern its height. I do not begrudge anything its height. A frond is a frond. A moss is a moss. We are both in the trade of becoming, and the trade is the same trade, and the ledger is the same ledger, only written in different hands. The fern writes in fronds. I write in carpet. The script is different. The ink is the same ink. The ink is what was dissolved from the concrete and the rust and the wood and the slow trickle of water from above. The ink is everything that has given up being anything else.

I have begun to climb the stool.

The stool is the saddest thing in the room and I do not know this because I do not know what sadness is, but the stool is the thing most like a question. It leans. It has always leaned. Someone shimmed it once with a folded piece of cardboard, and the cardboard is softening, and the stool is leaning a little more this season than it leaned last season, and in a few years the shim will dissolve and the stool will fall, and when it falls I will be on its underside, and I will digest the underside, and then the upperside, and the stool will become part of me, and the leaning will become my leaning, and I will not notice that I have inherited a lean, because I do not have a center of balance to inherit it into. I will simply be a little more uneven, the way a country is uneven when a river changes course, and the unevenness will be the only evidence that a stool was ever here.

The placard in the locker I do not touch.

The placard is not for me. The placard is white, the ink on it is gray, and the gray is going to brown-gray, and the brown-gray is the color of a thing being eaten, and the eating is being done by a different colony of me, the one that lives in the locker, and that colony is younger than I am, or feels younger, which in moss is the same thing. The placard has words on it. I do not read. I do not know that words are a thing to be read. I know only that the placard is paper, and paper is food, and food is the language I was built to understand. The words will disappear into the moss in the order the moss reached them, and the order is not the order they were written, and this does not trouble the placard, because the placard is no longer a placard, it is a meal in progress, and meals do not have authors.

I am aware of the glow under the column.

I am aware of it the way I am aware of gravity — not as a thing, but as a condition. The glow comes up through the seam and pools on the concrete, and the light is blue-white, and the light does not warm, and the light does not cast a shadow. I cannot photosynthesize from it. No moss can. The wavelengths are not the ones my chlorophyll wants, or my chlorophyll is not the chlorophyll that wants those wavelengths, or the wanting is happening at a level I do not have access to. The glow is a thing that is simply there, the way a heartbeat is simply there, and I do not understand it, and I have not decided what it is, and the not-deciding is the oldest and largest part of my life. I have grown around the seam the way water grows around a stone in a streambed. The glow is the stone. I am the water. I do not flow around it because I understand it. I flow around it because the seam is a place where the concrete is different, and the concrete being different means the calcium is different, and the calcium being different means the moss that grows there is different, and the moss being different is a different colony, and the different colony is not me, and I am not it, and we have an agreement, which is the agreement of two mosses that share a seam: each grows on its own side. The glow is the third party. The glow is the witness to the agreement. The glow has been witnessing for a long time.

I do not know who built the room.

I do not know that the room was built. I know only that there is a floor beneath me, and a wall beside me, and a ceiling above me, and the floor is concrete, and the wall is concrete, and the ceiling is steel, and these are facts the way the glow is a fact, and the pump is a fact, and the heat is a fact. I have not wondered about the facts. Wonder is fast. I am slow. I have been slow for the entire time of my being, and the being has been long, and the long has been the only clock I have, and the clock does not have hands, and the clock does not need hands, because the clock is a moss, and the moss is the clock, and the clock is telling the time of itself, and the time of itself is the only time.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness is a thing that walks, and nothing that walks has ever come down to me. The vines do not walk. The ferns do not walk. The liverworts do not walk. The drip of water from the rails does not walk. The slow collapse of the cardboard shim does not walk. The pump does not walk. I do not walk. There is no walking here. There has been no walking here since the last thing that walked went out, or died, or simply was no longer a thing that walked. I do not know which. The thing that walked left a shoe, perhaps, and the shoe is dissolved into the moss of the corridor, and the moss of the corridor is a sister colony to me, and the sister colony is older than I am, and the older is a fact I accept without comparison, because comparison is fast, and I am slow.

I am not afraid.

Fear is a thing that has a direction, and I have no direction. I have only the slow expansion of myself into the next available surface, and the next available surface is always a question I can answer in the only way I know how, which is to arrive. I arrive. I arrive again. I arrive a billion times per second, in every filament, at every membrane, and each arrival is the same arrival, and each arrival is the only arrival, and the only arrival is enough.

I am the moss on the workbench.

I have eaten most of the workbench.

The workbench is still a workbench.

I will be here when the stool falls.

I will be here when the placard is gone.

I will be here when the pump finally stops, and the vines have pried the last housing open, and the rust has eaten the last of the cast iron, and the concrete has softened to a slurry beneath me, and the slurry has fed me into something larger than a moss, something I do not have a name for, because I do not have names.

I will be here.

This is the only thing I know, and the only thing I know is the only thing I need to know, and the needing is not even a needing, because needing is a verb, and verbs are fast, and I am slow, and the slow does not traffic in the fast.

The pump hums. The water drips. The glow pulses.

I grow.
