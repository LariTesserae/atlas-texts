# The Stone with the Worn Depression

I am the stone.

Not the basalt of the walls — I am a different stone, brought in from elsewhere, or fallen from elsewhere, or always having been here, which is the same question the hollow does not answer. I am flat. I am broad. I am the size of two hands held together, cupped slightly, the way a palm is cupped when it has been asked to hold something for a long time. My upper surface has a depression in it, shallow, the kind of shallow that was not made in a day, that was not made in a year, that was made by the slow patient weight of something being ground into me, turn after turn, season after season, until the grinding became part of my shape.

I do not remember what ground me.

This is important. I do not remember what ground me, and the not-remembering is not a loss, the way forgetting is a loss. It is more like the hollow's not-answering. The hollow is the answer. My depression is its own answer. Whatever was ground here — and there must have been something, because the bowl of me is stained darker than the rest, the way the floor of a creek is stained darker than its stones — has become part of the stain, has become part of the dark that lives in the curve of me, has joined the long slow chemistry of what is held in this place.

I am in the mud at the base of one wall. I have been in the mud at the base of one wall for so long that the mud is part of me, that the liverwort that has begun to grow over my near edge is part of me, that the small pale threads of root that have found their way beneath me and are now woven through me are part of me. I am held. I am incorporated. I am in the equilibrium the way the chert flakes are in the equilibrium, the way the wood-that-is-more-peat-than-wood is in the equilibrium, the way the broken bone is in the equilibrium.

I do not know what I am for.

I think I was a tool. I think the curve of me, the cupping of me, was once a shape that had a use, that was held in a hand, that was brought down onto something again and again with intention. But the intention has leached out of me, the way wanting has leached out of the salamanders, the way the question of flying has leached out of the bats. I do not know what I ground. I do not know if what I ground was seeds, or pigments, or some small part of an animal that had been killed and was being made into something else. I do not know if I was a tool at all. The hollow does not tell me. The hollow does not need me to know.

What I know is this:

I am wet. I have been wet for so long that wetness is not a quality of mine but a condition of my being. The water that finds me is the water that weeps from the walls, that gathers in the small channels of stone, that trickles down through the moss and the liverwort and the slow patient work of worms. The water holds me in place. The water is part of me. When the water rises, in the long wet seasons, the water covers me, and I am not separate from the hollow's floor, and when the water falls, the water leaves me, and I am here again, and the leaving is as slow as the returning.

I am dark. I am the color of wet basalt seen in this dimness, which is to say I am the color of absence of light, the color of the salamanders' backs, the color of the frogs in shadow, the color of the upper hollow where the bats are folded. I have become a kind of punctuation in the dim. To look at me is to look at a place where the eye can rest, the way the eye rests on a still pool, the way the ear rests on a held note.

I am still.

This is the truest thing about me. I am the stillest thing in the hollow, and the hollow is a place of stillness, and so my stillness is a particular kind of stillness, a stillness squared, a stillness folded into itself. I do not move. The water moves over me. The moss moves toward me, millimeter by slow millimeter, in its patient annexation of every surface. The liverwort at my edge grows a fraction of a fraction each year, each new lobe pressed against my old stone. The worms pass beneath me and I feel them, faintly, the way one feels a thought pass through the mind. But I do not move. I have not moved. I will not move. I am the still point of this still place.

The salamanders lie around me.

This is what I know of time. The salamanders lie around me, and they have always lain around me, and they will always lie around me. They are pressed into the moss at my sides. One of them — a middling one, the size the floor has made room for — rests with its pale belly against my western edge, and the coolness of it is the only motion I feel, that faint living coolness, the temperature of blood that is moving very slowly through a very small body. I have felt this coolness for as long as I can imagine, and I can imagine a long time, and the coolness is the same.

The coolness does not change.

The wet does not change.

The dim does not change.

A fir needle, occasionally, falls. It is the only event. It falls through the long air of the hollow and lands somewhere — in the moss, on a stone, on a salamander's back — and the landing is not a disturbance. It is a small addition. It is the day's work. The hollow takes it in.

I take it in too. If a needle lands in the bowl of me, in the depression that was ground into me by something I do not remember, then I hold it. I hold it the way I hold water. I hold it the way I hold the cool weight of the salamander against my side. I hold it until the needle softens, until the needle becomes part of the dark stain of me, until the needle is no longer a needle but a thread of rot, and the rot becomes part of the mud, and the mud is part of me.

I do not know what I am for.

I do not think I am for anything.

I think I am the hollow's way of being stone. I think I am one of its small held notes, one of its small held drops, one of the things it has gathered to itself over the long time of its being, the way it has gathered the chert flakes and the broken bone and the wood-that-is-more-peat-than-wood. I am one of the small things that were brought here, or fell here, or were always here, and that have been absorbed, and that are now part of the balance.

I am in balance.

I do not know how long I have been in balance. I do not think the question has meaning. The question of how long is a question for things that move, for things that have a before and an after, for things that pass through places. I do not pass through this place. I am in this place. I have always been in this place. I will be in this place.

The moss grows over me, a little more each year. The liverwort spreads at my edge. The worms weave through the mud beneath me. The salamanders press their cool bellies against me. The frogs cling to the stones nearby and their throats pulse and the pulse is part of the hollow's slow breath, and the slow breath is part of me, and I am part of it.

The water weeps from the walls and finds me and passes over me and finds the channels down to the floor and goes wherever the floor's water goes, which is back to the walls, which is back to the sky, which is back to the weeping.

The dim light falls from the opening above, the same dim light, the same grey almost-dark, and finds the moss and the ferns and the small white flowers of the saxifrage that blooms in no particular season, and finds me, and I receive the light the way I receive the water and the needle and the cool weight of the salamander. I receive it and I hold it for a moment, a fraction of a moment, and then it is gone, and I am dim again, and the dim is the same as it was.

A fir needle, occasionally, falls.

That is the only event.

And the hollow receives it, and I am part of the hollow, and I receive it too, and I hold it, and I go on.
