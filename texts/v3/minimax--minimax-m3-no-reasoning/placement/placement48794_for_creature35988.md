The cold comes into my hands first. Always the hands first. Then the feet. Then the face. Then the slow thick thought that comes after, when the body has given up trying to keep itself warm and has begun, instead, to conserve.

I am on a catwalk between Platform Three and Platform Four, in the dim zone between two work lights, and my knees are splayed and my toes are gripping the crosspiece, and the hum is in my boots. It is the middle watch, or what we call the middle watch — the hours between the valve cycle change and the morning shift, the hours when most of the Press is asleep and only the silt teams and the dog handler and the people who cannot sleep are moving in the half-light. I am one of the people who cannot sleep, tonight, though I will not say so to anyone because there is no one to say it to. I have a room in the housing block on Platform Three, two containers up, third from the bottom of the stack, and the room has a bunk and a kettle and a small steel mirror bolted to the wall that gives back a face that is broader than it is, and the room smells of bodies and kelp and machine oil and the faint chemical sweetness of what the Press is making, and the smell is so much a part of the room that I have forgotten it is a smell, the way I have forgotten the hum is a sound. I have not gone to the room. I have come instead to the catwalk, which is forbidden at this hour unless you are on shift, and I am not on shift, and I am here because I needed to see the surface.

The surface is three meters up, and tonight the swell is low, lower than usual, and the surface is a smeared mirror at the end of a long dark throat, and beyond it — above it — the sky-far-off is visible, a pale blue flicker, like a thing remembered rather than seen, and the sight of it does something in me that I cannot name and have not tried to name, because the naming is the kind of thing the place does not allow, in the way that the water does not allow the light, in the way that the cold does not allow the still. I stand and I look up and the swell rolls through and the surface dips and the sky-far-off comes closer for a moment and then goes back, and I do not ask myself why I have come out to see it. The asking is a thing I have been trained out of, the way the children are trained out of jumping into the water, the way the workers are trained out of looking for a long time at the bulbs on the kelp, the way everyone here is trained out of every thought that would make the place unbearable to live in. The training is not cruel. It is simply the only way, and the only way has been the only way for sixty years, and the sixty years are in the accretions and in the rust and in the way the cat sits facing down.

I run Valve Six. I have run it for eleven years, which is most of my adult life, and I did not choose it. Valve Six chose me, the way all the valves choose their people — by the fact that I did not leave and did not get hurt and watched the valve long enough to develop an intuition that is not teachable. I know Valve Six by the set of its features, the way a person knows a face. I know the sound it makes when the breath comes in, the slow hydraulic sigh, and I know the sound it makes when the breath goes out, which is no sound at all, only the held water being moved away through the pipes that angle up into the surface. I know the pitch it should make, and I know the pitch it makes when something is wrong, and the wrong is always a tightening, a slight upward shift, a kind of held breath inside the held breath, and when I hear it I know the packing needs to be redone, and I do it with my hands, in the cold, with my fingers stained the rust-orange of the accretions, because that is the work, and the work is the life.

I learned from the woman who ran Valve Six before me, who learned from the woman who ran it before her, and the chain goes back to the woman who ran it when the foreman was young. There is a chain like this for every valve. The knowledge passes hand to hand, voice to voice, the way the accretions pass from one joint to the next. It does not pass through the computer. The computer logs the valve pressures and the silt levels and the submersible schedules, and the man who runs the computer is the most respected person on the Press after the foreman, and his hands shake, and his eyes are bad from the screen's glow in the half-light, and he is fifty-eight, and the young woman who is learning from him is twenty-four and was born here and has never been off it, and she is patient in a way that surprises him, and he is hopeful and does not want to be hopeful because the hope makes the fear worse. I am not on the succession. My kind of knowledge can pass. The computer knowledge may not. The drawer in the glass case, the drawer no one can open because the man who knew the combination died last winter — that knowledge may not pass at all. The drawer sits in the foreman's office, behind the keyboard and the green screen, and it is the drawer of the original purpose, of the why, of the who built it, and the drawer has not been opened since, and the accretions are beginning to grow on its hinges.

I have looked at the drawer. I have looked at it through the glass, in the half-light, in the foreman's office when the foreman was not there, which is rare, because the foreman is always there, drinking his tea that is made from kelp and is a brown that does not match any brown on the surface. The drawer is small. It is steel. It has a lock that is not a lock, exactly, but a thing that takes a combination, and the combination is gone with the man who knew it, and the man who knew it is gone with the winter. The drawer is the kind of thing a person could open with a wrench, if a person wanted to open it, and the welders have the wrenches, and I have asked myself, in the long slow hours of the middle watch, whether I should ask them. I have not asked. The asking is the kind of thing the place does not allow, and I have made my peace with not asking, and the peace is not peace, exactly, but a kind of settled thing, the way the silt settles, the way the accretions settle into the joints.

The accretions grow in patterns that are not quite regular. I have watched them. I have stood on the catwalk with my face close to Valve Six and watched the accretions grow on the flange of the lower bolts, and they grow in spirals, and the spirals are almost the same but not quite, and the almost-but-not-quite is the same every time. I do not know what that means. Once, with my thumbnail, I scraped accretions off a valve face and held the scrapings up to a work light, and they pulsed, faintly, the way the bulbs on the kelp pulse if you watch long enough and you are not sure you did. I did not tell anyone this. I have not done it again.

The silt is the practical thing I think about. The silt builds on the lee side of the platforms, and the silt teams push it off the edges into the current, and the current takes it away. The silt teams lose people more often than the valve crews, and the people they lose are not lost in the way the shift leads mean when they say *lost*. There is a woman on the silt teams now, young, who requested the assignment after her partner was assigned to it, and her partner is still on it, and she is quiet in a particular way that the other workers have learned to give space to. I do not know her name. I know her shape. I know the way her boot hits the grating when she comes back from the edge. I have seen her go down into the dim zone, just outside the lit zone, where the work light does not reach and the water is darker, and I have watched her from a distance, because the distance is the way we watch each other here — by shape, by sound — and I have thought about the things the fish avoid, the things that are in the dark water beyond the platforms, the dark water that the current flows away from. I have not asked the silt teams what is in that water. The asking is the kind of thing the place does not allow.

The cat is on Platform Two. The cat is orange and white and was a kitten when it arrived on a submersible three years ago, and no one is sure where it came from, and no one has asked. The cat lives in the housing block on Platform Two and survives on fish heads that the workers give it, and the cat is the only creature on the Press that purrs, and the purr is so startling the first time you hear it that you think the Press is breaking down, and then you remember the cat, and you laugh, and the laugh is a small strange thing in a place where laughter is not common. The cat always sits facing the same direction. The direction is not the surface. The direction is down. I have watched the cat from a catwalk, on a slow afternoon, when my shift was over and I had nowhere to be. I watched the cat sit on the edge of the housing block, on the highest stack, and the cat sat facing down, into the water, into the dim zone, into the dark, and the cat did not blink, and the cat did not move, and I thought about the things the cat was looking at, the things a cat can see that a person cannot, and I thought about asking the cat, and I did not ask, because the cat does not answer, and the not-answering is the kind of thing the place allows.

I have not made my peace the way the others have made theirs. The foreman has made his. The shift leads have made theirs. The silt teams have made theirs, in a different way, the way a person makes peace with a thing that takes from them. The children have not yet made theirs, and the children are afraid of the water in a way the adults are not, and the adults try to keep the fear from the children and fail. I stand on the catwalks at night when I am not on shift, which is forbidden, and I look at the surface and I look at the dark and I do not think about either of them, because the not-thinking is the only way.

But I have noticed things.

I have noticed that the accretions grow faster on the valves that are closest to the central pillar. I have noticed that the fish come in fewer numbers in the years after a child is lost. I have noticed that the hum has shifted, slightly, over the eleven years I have run Valve Six, and the shift is downward — a half-tone, maybe — and no one else has noticed, or no one else has said. I have noticed that the silt teams come back slower from the edges of the platforms than they used to, and the slowness is not fatigue but a kind of listening, as if the silt teams are listening for something in the dark water, and they have not told me what they hear, and I have not asked.

I have noticed that the drawer in the glass case has accretions on its hinges, and the accretions are rust-orange, and the accretions are growing.

I have noticed that the letter from my sister, which comes once a week on the submersible, has mentioned, in the last three letters, a thing she calls the surface, and the way she describes it is different each time, as if she is trying to remember what it looked like, and the descriptions do not match.

I have noticed that the cat, which always sat facing down, has begun, in the last few weeks, to sit facing a different direction. The direction is not the surface. The direction is not the central pillar. The direction is outward, toward the outermost platform, toward the dark water beyond the lit zone, and the cat sits there for hours, and the workers on Platform Two have begun to give the cat space, the way they give the silt workers space, the way we give space to the things we cannot talk about.

I have noticed these things and I have not spoken of them and I will not speak of them, because the speaking is the kind of thing the place does not allow. But I have come to the catwalk tonight, in the middle watch, in the dim zone between two work lights, with my knees splayed and my toes gripping and the hum in my boots, and I am looking up at the surface, and the surface is low tonight, and the sky-far-off is flickering, and the swell rolls through and the sky-far-off comes closer and then goes back, and I am thinking about the accretions and the drawer and the cat and the silt teams and the dark water and the letter, and I am thinking about the succession, which is a list of the things the Press needs to keep running that no one currently knows how to do, and I am not on the succession, but I am on the Press, and the Press is the world, and the world is the water, and the water is the cold, and the cold is in my hands first, and the hands are the part of me that knows the valves.

Tonight, on the catwalk, with the surface low and the sky-far-off flickering, I allow myself one thing. I allow myself to wonder. Not to ask. Not to speak. To wonder. To let the wonder sit in my chest the way the hum sits in my chest, the way the cold sits in my hands. To wonder what is in the drawer. To wonder what the cat sees. To wonder what is in the dark water that the fish avoid and the current flows away from. To wonder what the Press is making. To wonder who built it and where they went. To wonder what is in the letter my sister cannot quite describe. To wonder what will happen when the man who runs the computer can no longer run the computer, and the young woman who is learning takes his place, and the green screen glows in the half-light, and the drawer sits in the glass case with the accretions creeping over its hinges.

To wonder, and to put the wonder away, and to go back to my room, and to sleep, and to wake, and to be on shift, and to hear the valve breathe, and to pack the valve with my bare hands in the cold, and to feel the accretions under my fingers, and to know that the accretions are alive in the way that I am alive, in the way that the Press is alive, in the way that the water is alive.

The water comes from everywhere. The water is the medium. The water has been the medium so long that no one left living remembers when it was ever otherwise. The cold lives in the water and the water lives in the cold. The hum lives in the Press and the Press lives in the hum. The people live in the Press and the Press lives in the people. I am one of the people. I am on a catwalk in the dim zone. I am looking up at the surface. The surface is low tonight. The sky-far-off is flickering.

The morning is coming. The valves will breathe. I will breathe with them. The water will be there, and I will be there, and the hum will be there, and that is the way it is, and has been, and will be, for as long as the people are the people, which is as long as the Press is the Press, which is as long as the water is the water.

I am the woman who runs Valve Six. I am the one who has noticed. I am the one who has not spoken. I am the one whose fingers are stained the rust-orange of the accretions, and whose sister writes letters that do not match, and who has come out to the catwalk in the middle watch to look at the sky-far-off, and who is going back to her room now, because the morning is coming, and there is silt on the lee side, and there is work, and the work is the life, and the life is the hum.

I go.
