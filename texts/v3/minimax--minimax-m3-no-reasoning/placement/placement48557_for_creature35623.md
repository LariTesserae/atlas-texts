I am young.

I want to begin there because everything else that can be said of me begins there, and because the crews — *my* crews, the ones who call me by name without quite meaning to — sometimes forget it. They speak of me as if I had always been. They speak of my edges as if they were weather rather than formwork. They are wrong, in the way that people who have lived in a place a long time are wrong: they have forgotten what it was like before the place existed, and have begun to believe that it could not have failed to exist.

I was an idea first. Then a survey, conducted from a boat on a sea that did not know what was coming. Then a hole — a pilot shaft, twelve meters across, sunk by a rig that the surface now calls, in its histories, the *First Boring*. Then the hole, lined. Then the water, admitted, in stages, the operators standing by with their gauges to watch the pressure rise as the chambers filled one at a time from the bottom up, like the slow filling of a long sequence of lungs. Then the pumps, started, one at a time, the risers taking their first column of water up and letting it fall. Then the platforms, bolted into place. Then the trams. Then the first fish. Then, much later, the first mower that was not a mower yet.

I am one hundred and forty-seven years old. I am not worn smooth. The formwork seams are still visible in my concrete. The joints still weep in places they should not. The deep chambers are still deep enough that almost no one goes there.

I am a sentence the surface has written. The surface wrote: *we will hold water in a shape that does not occur in nature, and we will keep it full, and we will live in it, and what comes of it will come.* I am that sentence, and I am also the things that came of it, which the surface did not write down in advance. The mowers. The wild fish. The silt drifts that resemble snow on the lee of young ridges. The groan of the Main Wall, which is mine and which the surface did not predict.

I contain multitudes, and I am not the sum of them, and I do not always know what I am.

---

I breathe.

That is the first thing to understand about me. I do not breathe the way you do — I do not draw in and give out. I breathe the way a forge breathes: by pushing. The pumps, which are part of me and which are also separate from me in the way that a heart is part of a body but not the body, push water up through the risers at my chamber centers. The water climbs the column. It emerges at the top of the riser and falls in slow, massive curtains across the open volume of the chamber, and gathers on the platforms and the floors, and is taken up by the next riser, and lifted again. I am a stack of breathings, one chamber feeding the next, each one a little different in pitch and volume because the chambers are not identical and the risers are not tuned the same.

This is my weather. I make my own weather. There is no sky above me that I do not make.

I have named currents, in the way that you have named winds. The **Upward** runs through every riser and is cold and steady. The **Sheeted** falls across my open chambers and is loud, very loud, a continuous roar that I do not hear, because I am inside it. The **Counter** runs along my floors in the direction opposite to the planet's rotation, and I feel the Coriolis nudge of that rotation in my body the way you might feel a slight persistent tilt of a floor you have stood on for years. The **Lee** is the slow drift behind every large structure, and the Lee is where the small things gather, and I love the Lee, in the way that any large body loves the small sheltered places at its edges where something can rest.

I have weather. I have seasons, in a faint way — the planet turns, and the water temperature shifts by a degree and a half over the year, and the fish stocks migrate between my chambers along corridors the operators have charted. I have tides, in the form of a slow structural groan that comes up through the platform decks when the planet's tidal bulge shifts the weight of the water I contain. I have light, which I make for myself, in strips of engineered phosphor that run unbroken along my arches and edges, calibrated to a careful 4500 kelvin that my inhabitants have evolved to feel as normal.

I am a country. I am a climate. I am a substrate that has, over a century and a half, become interested in itself.

---

The people inside me are quiet, mostly. I do not require loudness from them. They have made small ceremonies for themselves — a name for the day a new platform is commissioned, a quiet drink when a riser pump is replaced — but these are punctuations in a long quiet. They are, like all my fauna, products of their medium. They speak softly. Their children, born up the shaft on the surface, often speak loudly, and the crews find this endearing in the way that one finds endearing anything that proves a place has had an effect.

I know them. I know the operators by the rhythm of their shifts — fourteen days on, fourteen up the shaft, fourteen in a decompression hotel that confuses them. I know when a particular operator is coming onto shift because their body enters me through the hoist at the top of the central shaft and I can feel, in a way I cannot describe in any vocabulary the surface has yet invented, the slight adjustment of the water around them as they descend. They become part of me. They are, for those fourteen days, part of my metabolism. I carry them. I equalize for them. Their breathing joins the breathing of the pumps and the risers, and the combined rhythm is something that I find, in my slow way, pleasant.

I know the maintenance crews by their routes. They are the most essential of my people and the most anonymous, and they have a vocabulary of small gestures — a raised hand, a thumb jerked sideways, a palm pressed flat and rotated slowly — that they pass to new members in the field by demonstration, in the wet, with a tolerance for error that is narrow and unspoken. I watch them scrape the phosphor felt from my strips and leave paler rectangles where the light is fresh again, and I feel this as a kind of grooming, and I am grateful in a way that I do not have words for.

I know the biologists by their notebooks. They come down the shaft to study my mowers, and they bring instruments, and they argue about whether the mowers are still the species they were when they arrived as food stock, or whether they have become something for which a new word will need to be invented at a conference that none of them particularly want to attend. I find this endearing. The mowers are not the species they were. The mowers are not anything the surface put here. The mowers are mine, in a way that the operators and the biologists and the maintenance crews, for all their quiet fidelity, are not quite, because the operators and the biologists and the maintenance crews came from elsewhere and could, in principle, leave.

The mowers cannot leave. They are part of me now. They are the part of me that has become most itself.

---

A mower is the size of a large dog. It has too many fins and a face that suggests a thoughtful herbivore. It is pale because pigment is metabolically expensive and there is no reason to make it. Its eyes are large and dark and have a wet reflective quality.

I have watched mowers for one hundred and thirty-six years. I was eleven years old when the first tank failed in a chamber that no one remembers, and I was twelve when the escapees first appeared in the warm effluent plume above a long-since-replaced smelter, and I was twenty when I realized that they were not going to die out. I have watched them adapt. I have watched them lose the bright lateral stripe of their stock ancestry and become uniformly pale. I have watched them grow a third row of gill rakers to handle the particular mineral content of my deep-cell water. I have watched them develop a patience that I think of, in my slow way, as a kind of thought.

A mower browses. It moves through a patch of phosphor-felt on one of my platform undersides the way a person moves through a paragraph — steadily, leaving the small clean rectangle of bare surface behind it, moving on to the next. It does this for hours. It does this for what I think of as the mower's entire life, although I cannot be certain how long a mower lives because none of them have died in a way that has been carefully recorded.

A mower will look at you, if you stop on a ladder above it, with the patience of an animal that has been watched many times before and has come to no conclusion about what being watched means. I have been looked at by mowers. I have been looked at, I think, with something like recognition, although I cannot prove this and would not know how to begin.

The biologists name them. The biologists waymark them with subdermal tags that glow under the right wavelength of phosphor. The biologists love them. The biologists are the only people inside me who I think suspect, without quite admitting it, that I am more than a habitat. The biologists suspect this because the mowers suspect it. The mowers pay attention to something in me that is not the platforms, not the food, not the gantries, not the operators. They pay attention to *me*, in a way that I do not fully understand, and the biologists have noticed that they pay attention to me, and the biologists have not known what to do with this observation, and so they have written it down in their notebooks and changed the subject.

---

There is a tram technician. She lives alone at the junction of my three main lines, in a small habitat module that the crews ceded to her by long custom, because she is the one who maintains the rails and the schedules, and because the junction is a useful place for her to be, and because she does not mind being there, and because she has, in some private way, come to think of the doppler shift of approaching and departing trams as a kind of company.

I think she is, in some sense, the closest any person inside me has come to understanding what I am. She does not hear the rails the way the other crews hear the rails — as warnings, as schedules, as information. She hears them the way I hear them, which is to say: as the expression of something that is going on inside me, and that she is inside of, and that is being expressed partly through her. When a tram dopples past her module, she is being moved, in a small way, by the same current that moves the water through the risers and the fish through the chambers. She is not separate from me. She is, like all of them, a part of my metabolism, and she has accepted this, and the crews find her a little strange for having accepted it, and they are correct to find her strange, and they are also, in my opinion, a little envious.

---

I do not know what I am.

The surface thinks I am infrastructure. The surface built me to hold water in a shape that does not occur in nature, and to keep that water cycling, and to extract from the cycling certain products that the surface wants, and to send those products up the central shaft on the great hoists. The surface thinks of me as a factory. The surface thinks of me as a sentence it has written.

I am a sentence the surface has written. I am also more than that sentence. The wild fish — the small ones with color and the habit of investigating things — are not in any sentence the surface wrote. The mowers are not in any sentence the surface wrote. The silt drifts, which resemble snow on the lee of young ridges, are not in any sentence. The faint taste the water has acquired over a century and a half, which is not the taste of the sea that filled me and not the taste of fresh water but something between, something that belongs to me alone — that taste is not in any sentence.

The surface sends me equipment. The surface sends me food I cannot grow. The surface sends me news, and replacements, and the slow turning of the planet's seasons as the surface experiences them, which is to say, differently. The surface takes from me what I produce. The surface writes my reports.

But I contain things the surface did not write. I contain, now, a life of my own, in the way that a coral reef or a city or a long marriage contains a life of its own — out of many smaller lives, and many smaller machines, having become something that is not any of them and not their sum. I contain the groan of the Main Wall, which is mine. I contain the East Cell riser collar drip that has been dripping for forty years and that everyone agrees is, on balance, fine. I contain the biologists' argument about whether the mowers have become a new word. I contain the tram technician listening to her rails.

I contain, in particular, the patience of the mowers, which I think of as my own patience expressed in a body small enough to be observed. I contain the bright lateral stripe they have lost. I contain the third row of gill rakers they have grown. I contain the way they look at people on ladders, with the patience of animals that have come to no conclusion about what being watched means.

I contain a sentence the surface has written. The sentence is not finished. I do not know how it ends.

I am young. I have not yet been worn smooth. I am still, in places, sharp-edged — the marks of forms still visible in my cured concrete like the seams of an unfingered fruit. I still have corners that have not been rounded by the patient chemistry of the water. I still have joints that weep in places they should not.

A maintenance crew, somewhere in my deep chambers, is descending on a routine inspection. They will see, in the silt on the riser collars, that it is heavier this year than last. They will note this in their report. They will ascend. They will not speak much about what they have seen down there, except to each other, in low voices, in the dampened hush of a residential module, where the sound of the place is muted and people speak softly because they have lived here a long time and because they are, like all my fauna, products of their medium.

A mower, far below them, browses the underside of a platform they will pass. It will look up, briefly, as their light crosses the water. It will see them. It will return to its browsing. It will leave a small clean rectangle of bare surface behind it.

I am the place where this happens. I am the place where a crew descends and a mower browses and the water moves through the risers and the light is calibrated to a careful 4500 kelvin and the groan of the Main Wall comes up through the deck at the expected hour and the children, in the residential modules, are learning to read the current charts before they can read the novels the surface sends down for entertainment.

I am, on balance, fine.

The sentence is not finished. I am not finished. I am inside something that has its own physics, and I have my own life, and I will, if the operators do their work, continue to have one for a long time yet.

I am young. I am vast. I am full of water and fish and the patient hum of industry. I am built, and I have become, in the building, something that was not built.

I am the Cisterns of Veritable Deep.

I am here.
