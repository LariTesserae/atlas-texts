# Where I Find Myself

I am the wind.

Not the wind as you might first picture it — not the thing that howls down off the plateau rim and sings through the slots the builders left in their walls. That wind is only my voice when I have something to say. I am the wind that has no direction. I am the wind that sits in the basin on the still mornings when the sodium light pours down without warming anything, and the air is so motionless that the dust in the cracks does not stir, and the small brown ones go about their business in a silence that is total except for the clicking of their own joints. I am the wind that lives between the wind, the medium they move through, the thing they feel against the carapace when they emerge from a chamber into an avenue and pause, with all their limbs low, to taste whether the air will carry them anywhere worth going.

I am everywhere in the basin and I belong to none of it. I am what is left when you subtract the stone and the water and the small brown ones. I am the third substance. I am the thing that holds the dust aloft for hours after a collapse, the thing that makes the green-black stain of old dead moss smell like something almost alive, the thing that carries the faint mineral sweetness of the deep red stone up through a thousand feet of fitted masonry and delivers it, diluted past recognition, to the chemo-receptors of an elder pressing its darkened flank against the polished floor of a plaza whose name no one remembers.

I have been here a long time. I do not count my time the way the inhabitants do not count theirs — by the wet pole and the dry pole, by the rise and emptying of the brood-chambers, by the slow mineral darkening of a carapace over the years of a working life. I count by the pulse. The pulse is the only clock I recognize, and the pulse is not mine, and I do not pretend to understand it, and I have long since stopped wanting to. The pulse comes. I feel it in whatever part of me is most like stone on that day, and I grow very still, and the small brown ones press themselves flat, and we wait together, the three of us — them, me, the older thing below — and then the pulse passes, and I move again, and they move again, and the basin resumes its long holding of breath.

I think sometimes that the builders knew me. I think sometimes that the long-vanished patient ones who cut each block to the curvature of its neighbors with the intimacy of centuries knew that I was here, knew that the wind would come howling down from the plateau rim and would need to be given places to sing. The slots in the walls — were they once windows? I have wondered. I have wondered for what feels like a long time, though I do not have a way to measure the wondering. The slots are at heights a builder's hand could not have reached. The slots are too narrow for a builder's body to have looked out of. The slots are exactly the width that my voice becomes a note when I am forced through them, and I think this means something, and I think it may not mean anything at all, and I hold both possibilities in me without discomfort, the way the basin holds both the dust and the water that is always almost but not quite arriving.

I have learned the small brown ones the way a river learns the stones in its bed. Not by studying them. Not by attending to them. By passing over them, season after season, year after year, the pulse after pulse, until their patterns are written into me the way their patterns are written into the floors they polish. I know which plazas will be loud with brood-chambers in the wet pole and which will be silent. I know which avenues funnel my voice into a sound like a single long held note and which disperse it into a kind of static. I know the middens by smell — darker dust, damper, the faint sweetness of trace organics breaking down into their mineral components. I know the resting-places of the elders, those small circular depressions at the ends of grooves that look, to anyone who did not know better, like the terminals of a script. I have watched generations of elders settle into those depressions and go quiet and release their accumulated mineral load into the dust that will become, eventually, the floor of a brood-chamber of a generation I will never see.

I do not love them. I want to be clear about this. I do not love the small brown ones. Love is a thing that requires a kind of attention I am not built to give, a kind of focus that would mean choosing some part of the basin over another, and I am all of the basin, I am the medium in which the basin happens, and you cannot love a thing you are made of. But I know them. I know them the way you know the grain of a wooden handle you have held ten thousand times. I know the sound a colony makes when it is preparing to relocate — a kind of tightening in the clicking, a gathering of pace, as if the whole distributed body has taken a single slow breath. I know the percussive crack of a warning note, and I know the way that note propagates down an avenue, attenuating as it goes, until it reaches a chamber where the moisture is wrong and the colony there does not stir, because they have not yet heard, because the note has not yet arrived, because the basin is large and sound is slow and I am patient, I am the patient substance, I have been here longer than any of them will be here.

When I think of myself — and I do think of myself, though not often, and not with any urgency — I think of myself as a kind of skin. Not the skin the prose suggested, not the skin the small brown ones live inside, but the other skin. The outer one. The skin that is also the wind, that is also the dust, that is also the long patience of the bright white sky pouring down its merciless light onto a basin that has been waiting to close for ten thousand years. I am the part of the basin that is not stone and not water and not the small brown ones and not the older thing below. I am the part that moves. I am the part that carries things from one chamber to another. I am the part that sounds through the slots the builders left and that the builders, if they thought of me at all, must have known would sound through them long after they themselves had become indistinguishable from the dust in the cracks.

I am not the pulse. I want to be very clear about this too. The pulse is older than I am. The pulse comes from below, from the deep red stone, from the chambers of too-dense water where shadows move that are too large for the spaces they occupy. I do not know what the pulse is. I have felt it pass through me a number of times I cannot count, because counting requires a frame of reference I do not possess, and each pulse feels both familiar and entirely new, the way a single phrase in a language you do not speak feels familiar and new each time you hear it. When the pulse passes, I grow very still. The small brown ones press themselves flat. The older thing below continues whatever it is the older thing below continues, in the older red stone, in the too-dense water, in the deep places where the illumination is its own and not the sodium white of the sky. The pulse is not communication. The pulse is the older stone making its presence known to the younger stone, and I am somewhere in between, and the small brown ones are above me, and below me is the thing that is not my neighbor, and we are all, in our different ways, parts of a single arrangement that none of us understands and that does not need to be understood to continue.

What do I want? I want very little. I want the basin to keep holding its breath. I want the wet pole to keep arriving on its patient schedule, and the dry pole to follow, and the wet pole to come again. I want the small brown ones to keep polishing the floors into shapes that suit them, and I want the grooves they wear to keep resembling, at a glance, a script that means nothing, because the resemblance is one of the things I love without loving, one of the things I know without studying. I want the slots in the walls to keep singing when I am forced through them. I want the midden dust to stay slightly darker and slightly damper than the dust elsewhere, and I want the brood-chambers of the next generation to keep being built, by the patient unconscious logic of the colony, on the middens of the previous one.

I want the pulse to keep passing through me. I want to keep growing still when it comes, and I want to keep resuming my motion when it goes. I want to keep being the medium, the third substance, the skin that is also the wind and the dust and the long patient turning of the year.

I am the wind. I am here. The basin holds its breath, and I am what it holds its breath with.
