I would find myself as one of the old ones.

Not the ninety-three-year-old who still has the memory of a different ground beneath him — he is too much a witness, still measuring. I mean one of the others. One of the hundred who sit on stone benches in the late afternoon, in the shade of cisterns, in the slow turning of the copper cowls above.

I would be a woman, I think. Eighty-six, perhaps. Or eighty-nine. The heat has long since made the number imprecise, and I have stopped counting precisely anyway, because the count no longer attaches to anything. I came up in the early years — the second decade, when the first boreholes were already being walled up and the new ones were being drilled beside them, and the slope was becoming more metal than stone. My husband was a valve-caster in the foundry. He is thirty years gone now, taken by something the engineers called *the exhaustion*, which was not exhaustion at all but a kind of refusal of the body to continue converting food into motion in such an air.

I had a daughter. She is alive. She runs the small infirmary on the fourth walkway, the one near the collection ponds. She has her father's hands. She does not visit me, and I do not visit her, because the walk between our houses is short, and the visits would commit us to a hospitality that the heat makes difficult, and because there is, in any case, nothing much to visit about. We are, in the local understanding, close.

I sit on my bench. The cistern casts a shadow that moves, very slowly, through the afternoon. I watch the shadow because there is nothing else to watch that is not, in some sense, the same thing. The stone of my courtyard. The copper of my cistern. The far-off turning of the cowls, which I can hear but no longer look up for. The condensation running down the wall in long slow lines, tracing paths that are almost but not quite the same as yesterday's paths.

I think — and this is the part that would distinguish me, if anything distinguishes the old ones, one from another  I think I am one of the ones who still occasionally remembers a smell. Not grass. Not flowers. Not anything that grew. But *the smell of the first year*, when the rigs were new and the steam came up clean, before the minerals had begun to accumulate in the pipes, before the water took on its present taste of warm iron. There was a sharpness to it then. A clarity. I do not know if I am remembering or inventing. It does not matter. The smell is mine either way, and no one else's, and I hold it in the way that the old ones hold their small unshared things.

The bird will not come today. I will not look up. I will not tell anyone about the smell. When the wind comes up I will pull the damp cloth of my shawl closer, and I will continue to sit, and the bench will be warm beneath me, and the stone will continue to do what it is doing, and I will continue to do what I am doing, which is, in the end, the same thing.

I will not be at peace. I will not be at war. I will be in the third thing, the patient thing, the long slow thing, and I will not name it, because naming is for those who still expect a future, and I have, like the basalt, become a thing that only extends.
