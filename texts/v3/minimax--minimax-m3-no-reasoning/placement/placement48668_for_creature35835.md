The water is at my collarbone. I am standing on the threshold of one of the low domes, and I have just woken, which is to say: I have just stopped pretending to sleep, which is to say: I have let the first current of the morning move through me and acknowledged that I was aware of it. There is no clean line between sleeping and waking here. The water does not respect the line. You come back to yourself in stages, like a fish turning in a school, like a thought rising.

I am not a child. I am not yet an elder. I am somewhere in the long middle, and the long middle is where most of us are most of the time, and it is a good place to be, if you have made peace with the water, and I have.

I have a son. He is eight, and his name is Vellan, and he is already down in the middle vaults, has been since before the first lantern-light. I can feel him the way you feel a current — not precisely, not in words, but in the way the water moves when he is near. He is with his cousins. They are doing something I do not need to know about.

I have a partner. We pressed palms six years ago, in the upper gallery, in front of the basalt spine's oldest face. We do not live in the same dome. We live in adjacent domes, with a corridor between, and the corridor is where we keep our shared things — a lantern, a wrapped bundle of tools, a small bowl of red algae that we refresh on the feast days. We see each other most days. We do not see each other every day. The water has taught me that this is enough.

I have a task today. I am going to walk the spine. I walk it often. There is a chamber I have not visited in a season. There is a name I have not spoken in a season. The basalt spine is patient, but it likes to be spoken to. It likes to be reminded that it is not only stone.

I push off from the threshold. The water receives me.

---

The basalt spine rises through the heart of the old city. It is not a building, exactly. It is a thing that was here before the buildings, and the buildings were built around it the way a reef is built around an old volcanic plug. The long builders raised it — that is what we say, the long builders, the ones from before — and we do not know what they used it for, exactly. We know it is warm. We know it holds the chambers of the dead. We know it hums faintly when the vents are strong.

I swim the corridor down. The walkways of woven shell-concrete are for the formal, the way you might wear shoes indoors; mostly we swim, and the swimming is the way of the place, and the place is not offended by it. My hands trail. The webbing between my fingers catches the faint current, and I can read from the pressure on the webbing which way the water is moving, which way the fish are, which way the long things in the deep are. This is not a special skill. This is what the hands are for.

The light is already failing. By the second descent the amber is gone, and what is left is a kind of green-black, and what is beyond that is a kind of memory of green-black, and beyond that is just the water and what the water holds. I do not need to see. I know the way by the way the current bends. I know it by the way the temperature shifts as I pass the vent-mouths — a half-degree here, a quarter there, each one a different mineral signature, each one tasting of a different morning.

Somewhere above me my son is laughing. I cannot hear it. I can feel it, the way you might feel a small stone drop into a deep pool, the ripple arriving after the stone is already gone.

I pass a chamber where an old woman is mending a net. She sees me — she does not need much light to see — and she raises a hand, palm out, and I raise one back, and we do not speak. The speaking would be redundant. The hand is enough.

I pass a school of silver fish. They are wheeling in a slow column through one of the high arches, and I swim through the column without disturbing it, and the fish part around me and close again behind me, and I feel them on my arms, on my ribs, cool and quick, like a memory of rain. They are not afraid of me. They have no reason to be.

I am looking for the chamber of Auran. Auran was my mother's mother. Auran lived ninety-three years, which is not unusual, and she died in her sleep, which is not unusual, and her chamber is in the lower spine, third column from the basalt face, which is the column of grandmothers. I have not been to see her in a season. I have been busy. The water forgives busy.

I find the chamber. I press my palm to the basalt outside it. The stone is warm, and the warmth moves into my hand, and the warmth has been in the stone for a long time, and I do not know if Auran is in the warmth, exactly, but the warmth is the shape of her, and the shape of her is enough.

I speak her name.

*Auran.*

The basalt takes the name. The water takes the name. The name is no longer only mine.

I stay for a while. I tell her about my son, who is eight and is beginning to swim the lower vaults alone, which she would have wanted. I tell her about my partner, whose name she knows. I tell her that the silver fish are up this season, which she would have been glad of, because she liked to watch the silver fish. I do not say anything difficult. I do not say: I miss you. I do not say: I am afraid. The basalt does not need to be burdened. The basalt has its own long memory, and it has enough.

I press my palm to the stone again. The stone receives it. I push off.

---

On the way up, I pass the place where the vents open. There are three of them in this section, mouths in the basalt no bigger than my two hands cupped, and from them the warm water exhales, slow and patient, a long exhalation that has been going on since before I was born. I put my palm over one of the mouths and let the warm water push against my hand. I taste the mineral on my lips. Iron, mostly. A little sulfur. The taste of a calm morning.

The vents need tending. That is someone else's work today. I do not know who. I trust that someone is doing it, the way I trust the water to be warm, the way I trust the silver fish to wheel.

I rise through the middle vaults. The light is beginning to come back — not the amber of the upper column, but a kind of grey-green suggestion that the sun is doing something far above. I cannot see the sun. I have seen the sun twice in my life, on the two occasions I have been to the surface, and both times it was too bright, and both times I came back down within an hour, and both times I was glad to be home. The surface is a place I do not need.

In the middle vaults, the long things are stirring. I can feel them — a slow displacement of water, a length of presence moving somewhere below and to my left. They are not afraid of me either. We have shared this column for a long time, the long things and I, and we have made a kind of peace, the way any two neighbors make a peace, by being patient with each other and not making sudden moves.

I find my son.

He is in a chamber I do not know the name of, one of the smaller ones off the main corridor, and he is with two of his cousins, and they are doing something I do not immediately understand. There is a silver fish, a small one, held gently in his hands. He is showing it to the cousins. The fish is not dead. It is held in the cup of his palms, and his palms are open to the water, and the water is moving through his fingers, and the fish is breathing. He is letting the cousins see its gill-cover rise and fall.

*Aa, Vellan,* I say — his name, and the address that means *mother speaking*. I do not say it loudly. The water carries it well enough.

He looks up. His eyes are very large, the way all our eyes are, and in the grey-green of the middle vault they look almost amber at the rim, which is the family marking, the deep-dwelling marking, the one I have too, though mine is more pronounced. He is eight. He is becoming more of the water every year, the way I did, the way his grandmother did, the way the long things in the deep are more of the water than anything could be.

*Mother,* he says. Just the address. The way we say it.

I do not ask him what he is doing. I know what he is doing. He is teaching the cousins to be gentle with the silver fish, which is a thing every child in this city learns from another child, and not from an elder, and the elders think this is right. The fish is calm in his hands. The cousins are watching with the focus of children watching something they are about to be allowed to do.

I let him be. I press my palm briefly to the wall of the chamber as I pass — a kind of hello to the chamber, a kind of acknowledgment that I was here — and I push off, and I rise.

---

The meal is in the upper gallery of the third dome, which is the dome my family has eaten in for as long as anyone remembers, and which is therefore our dome in a way that domes become yours when you have eaten in them long enough. The water here is at my waist when I stand on the central platform, and warmer than the column at large, because we are near a vent that has been encouraged in this direction for generations. The platform is of old coralcrete, packed and re-packed, the surface worn smooth by decades of feet and knees and the soft sandal-covers of people being formal about it. Today I am not formal. I am in the water in the way I usually am.

There are perhaps twenty of us. My partner is across the platform, talking to one of his cousins, and I can see the way his shoulders move when he talks, the way the water moves with them, the way he is not fighting the medium. He is a little taller than me. He has more freckle than I do, which I have always liked about him. He catches my eye across the platform and raises a hand, palm out, and I raise one back, and that is the entire conversation for now, and it is enough.

The food is silver fish, prepared in a way that is too ordinary to describe. There is also a kind of red algae that has been pressed into a cake, and a sauce of something fermented that tastes of the deep vents, and a small round fruit that grows in the upper reaches where there is barely enough light. I eat slowly. Everyone eats slowly. The meal is a kind of long communal event, and people drift in and out of conversation, and no one is in a hurry, and a child swims past the platform trailing a small soft-bodied lantern in her hand, and the lantern pulses faintly as she moves, and no one looks up.

I am sitting near a woman I do not particularly like. Her name is Saela. She is sharp in a way that I find tiring, and she has held a grudge against a cousin of mine for eleven years over a matter of who was supposed to be at a certain ceremony and who was not, and she has brought this up at three meals in a row now, and I am losing patience with her. I do not say anything. I let my hand rest on the platform beside me, palm down, which is the posture of someone who is not engaging. She does not notice, or pretends not to. She continues to talk to the woman on her other side.

I look at my partner. He has moved closer, is now sitting two people away from me, and he is looking at me with the small expression that means *I see you are not enjoying this and I am sorry*. I do not return the expression. I let myself be irritated for a little while, the way you might let yourself be cold, knowing that the cold will pass. The irritation passes. The water takes it, the way the water takes most things, and what is left is the meal, and the company, and the slow drift of the afternoon.

I eat a piece of the red algae cake. It tastes of iron and a little salt. It tastes of the upper column, where the light is just barely enough to feed a thing. I think of my grandmother Auran, who liked the red algae cake, and I am a little sorry I did not bring her a piece today. I will bring her one tomorrow. The basalt will keep.

---

In the afternoon I work. My work is the coralcrete, the mending of it, the mixing of it. I have done this since I was young, since I was taught by a woman who is now in the basalt spine, in a chamber I visit when I can. The recipe is not written. It is in the hands. It is in the way the dust feels between the fingers, in the way the binder-algae clings to the palm, in the way the mix holds its shape when you cup it and lets go. You learn it the way you learn the water — by being in it for a long time.

I have a section of walkway that needs patching. There is a place where the current has worn the surface, has made a small bowl of it, and the bowl collects sediment and the sediment is starting to work its way into the structure below. I have brought my materials in a soft bag over my shoulder. I settle onto the walkway, cross-legged, the water at my chest. I open the bag. I begin.

The mixing is slow. You cannot rush the binder-algae. It needs to be coaxed into combining with the dust, and the coaxing is a kind of conversation, and the conversation takes the time it takes. I work with my hands. The webbing between my fingers helps — it lets me gather more of the mix at once, lets me feel the consistency through the whole hand. My partner laughs at me sometimes, says I can feel the weather through my palms, which is not entirely wrong.

A child swims past and watches me for a while. I let her watch. Children are supposed to watch. Children are supposed to learn by watching, and then by trying, and the trying is the part that teaches. I do not invite her to try today. There will be other days. The coralcrete will keep.

I apply the mix to the worn place. I smooth it with the heel of my hand. The mix is cool against my palm, and then it begins to warm as the binder-algae activates, and the warmth is familiar, and the familiarity is what the city is made of.

When I am done, the patch is invisible, or nearly. It will cure overnight. By morning it will be part of the walkway, and the current will move over it without knowing it is new, and that is the goal — that the new becomes indistinguishable from the old, that the work of one woman's hands is absorbed into the long work of the place.

I sit for a while. The water is at my chest. The dim is all around. Somewhere far above, the silver fish are wheeling. Somewhere far below, the long things are turning in their sleep. The basalt spine hums faintly, the way it always hums, the way the city breathes, the way the warm column holds everything it has agreed to hold.

I think of Auran, and of the other women in the basalt, and of my son, who is eight and learning to be gentle with silver fish, and of my partner, whose freckles I have always liked, and of the woman Saela and her eleven-year grudge, and of the surface, which I have seen twice and do not need to see again, and of the recipe for coralcrete, which is in my hands, and of the patch I have just made, which will be part of the walkway tomorrow.

I am in the warm column. I am of the warm column. The water is at my chest, and has been at my chest for the whole of my life, and will be at my chest tomorrow, and the day after, and on.

I do not think this is sad. I do not think this is anything in particular. It is the way it is. The way the reef is of the sea. The way the lung is of the breath.

A lantern passes by, held by a hand I cannot see, and the light moves across the dim and fades, and the dim takes it back, and the water moves, and the city breathes, and I am in it, and I am of it, and I am welcome.
