I think I am Osprey.

Not the stoat — the stoat comes and goes, and the coming-and-going is what makes it the stoat. I have not come and gone in a long time. I think I am the tall man who carries water to the stone when the well is low, and who bends down in the wet green aisles between the houses to retrieve a feather when one is found, and who stands for a while with it in his hand before deciding which day to bring it. I think I am the one who has stopped wondering what the stone is, and who is glad of the stopping.

I want to begin there, with the stopping, because I think the stopping is the whole of me.

I have been here all my life. I am told this. There is no one left who would have known me as a child except Essa, and Essa does not speak of it, and I do not ask. I have the feeling that I came down the path once. There is a dimness, the colour of looking at something through water, of a time when the sky was wider and the light had edges to it. I think there were two stones at the top of the path even then, and I think I stood between them, and I think I was afraid for a small part of a small moment, and then the bowl opened below me and the fear was over. I do not remember being afraid. I remember the fear the way you remember a story told to you as a child — as something that happened, in a place I cannot quite get back to, to someone I almost was. Whether I came down as an infant, or was born here, I do not know. It does not matter. The well rises and falls within its narrow range. I am Osprey. I have been Osprey for a long time.

I am very tall. This is the first thing people tell you about me, and I think it is also the first thing I know about myself when I wake, because the ceilings here were not made for me. I duck through doorways. I turn sideways in the lanes that turn Cai sideways, and I am still longer in the turning than he is. My head brushes the moss that hangs from the eaves, and the moss is cool and very wet, and I have come to like the feel of it. In the mornings, when I carry the water, my hair is damp with it before I have gone three houses. By the time I reach the shrine I look as if I have been out in weather, though I have not. I have only walked the length of a town that is wet all the way through.

The shrine stone is the centre of what I do, if I can be said to do anything. I do not keep it. I do not tend it. I bring it things. This is the whole of my office, and I find, the older I get, the more the bringing-of-things is the whole of me. I am the one who notices when the well is low. The well does not go low often — it has its narrow range, as everything here has its narrow range — but in the late summer, when the rains have not come and the bowl has been bright for too many days, the water retreats a little from the lip, and the overflow thins, and the channel down to the bronze grating runs with less than its usual small song. When I see this I fill a bowl at the well, a clay bowl, one of two that the town has, the other being kept by Cai for the bread. I carry the bowl down the lanes with both hands, because the lanes are narrow and my elbows would clip the walls otherwise, and I set the bowl at the base of the stone. Then I step back. Then I go home. That is the rite. I do not kneel. I have never knelt. The stone has never asked me to.

I think about the stone sometimes, in the way that the people here think about things — which is to say, not for long, and not toward a conclusion. It is a piece of the world that is not from here. The granite is wrong for the place. You can see it the moment you come down the path, if you know what limestone looks like, and I know what limestone looks now, the way you know the face of someone you have lived beside. The stone is darker, and the quartz in it catches the bright light and throws it back in small sharp glints, and the glints move when the sun moves, which is the only movement in the shrine that is not me. I do not know where it came from. I have no one to ask. Essa would tell me, if she knew, but Essa's book is a book of names, not of origins. There is no chapter in it for *where things came from*. There is only the line of names, and the dates, which are not even dates but little marks of the season — *after the long bright* or *in the wet of blackthorn* — and the names themselves.

I do not wish to know what the stone is. I want to say this carefully, because I think an outsider would hear it as a kind of peace, or a kind of giving-up, and it is neither of those. It is more like — I do not know how to put it. It is like being in a house that has a room you have never gone into. You have lived in the house a long time. You know where the room is. You know the door is shut, or not shut, or has no door at all, just an opening into a darkness. You have glanced in, perhaps, in passing, and seen that there is nothing in there that requires you, nothing that is calling you, nothing that would be different if you entered. So you do not enter. You do not *not* enter, either, in any active way. You simply walk past. That is what I do with the stone. I walk past it, and sometimes I stop at it, and sometimes I bring it a thing, and then I walk past it again.

The feather is the hardest of the offerings. Not because it is hard to find — feathers come down sometimes, from the swifts, or from the birds that pass over the bowl in the migrations — but because when I find one I have to decide. A feather is a specific thing. A bowl of water is just water. A sprig of green is just a piece of the place. But a feather is a thing that was on a thing that flew, and the thing that flew is not here now, and the feather is the trace of it, and to bring a trace feels like a different sort of act. I hold it in my hand for a while. I look at it. The barbs are soft. Sometimes they are still attached, sometimes they are not. I bring it to the stone when the morning is right, and I set it at the base, and the next time I come it has been moved, or has not been moved, and either way I do not know what to make of it and I do not try to know.

I do not pray at the stone. I have never seen anyone pray at the stone. Maren, who taught Cai, who was old when I was young — Maren used to come down sometimes, in the evenings, when the swifts were out and their sewing-sound was in the air, and she would sit on the low wall across from the stone and look at it for a long time. I asked her once, when I was small, what she was doing. She said, *I am keeping it company.* I think about that sometimes. I do not think she meant it as a rite, or as a duty. I think she meant that the stone was alone, in a way that stones can be alone even when the town is around them, and that her sitting was a way of being with that aloneness. I have come to do this myself, more than the offerings. I come down in the late bright, when the light has gone soft and long through the lanes, and I sit on the wall, and I do not think, and the stone is there, and the slowworm, if it is warm, comes out and lies on the flat stone at the edge, and the two of us keep the granite company for a while. I do not know what the slowworm gets from this. I do not get anything from it that I could name. I get the feeling of having been in a place for a while. That is enough.

The slowworm and I have an understanding. I said that, and I think it is true, although the understanding is not a thing we negotiated. It is more that we have come to occupy the same space without troubling each other. I do not pick it up. I do not move the flat stone. It does not go into the lanes, where its kind does not live. We each have our part of the shrine, and we keep to it. When it basks, I do not stand over it. When I sit, I do not sit in its patch of sun. The children sometimes want to pick it up, and I tell them no, and they listen, because I am tall and the no is simple and has no lecture in it. Iseult listened the first time. Wren listened the second. Ailsa has not yet tried. I will tell her when she does, in the same tone, and she will listen too. That is how things pass here. Not by being taught in any formal way, but by being said once, by someone whose saying has weight, and then being taken up.

I am not sure I have weight. I think I do, a little. I am old, or I am not old, I do not know which — I have the feeling of being old, which is not the same as being old, but which is, perhaps, what being old feels like from the inside. I have seen people go up the path and not come back down. I have carried the frame. I have laid the small stones. I have watched the moss come over the stones of people I knew, and I have not been sad in any way I could explain to an outsider, because the sadness, if it is sadness, is so mixed in with everything else — the damp, the bright, the slow sound of the water, the feeling of the moss on my head in the mornings — that I cannot separate it out to look at it. It is in the air. It is in the stone of the walls. It is in the cloth that Tomasi weaves, which is the cloth that has been woven, which is the cloth that will be woven. I do not try to separate it.

I have a house. It is at the upper edge of the town, near where the path comes in, near where the goats are kept. It is not the largest house. It is not the smallest. It has a single room below and a single room above, and the room above is where I sleep, and I have to duck to get into the bed, and the ceiling above the bed is stained with my hair, over the years, the oils of it, the damp of it, and the stain is the shape of a long low smear and I have come to like it, the way you like a thing that is yours in a way that nothing else can be. I have a few things. A change of cloth, which Delyth wove and which is the colour of the sheep. A bowl, mine, the second clay bowl, which I keep for the offerings. A knife, bone, with a handle wrapped in wool. A piece of the bronze blade that Bren let me have, a small piece, that I use to shave the whiskers that grow on my face because I have decided, at some point I cannot remember, that I would rather not have them, although no one else shaves and there is no reason to. I do it because it is a thing I do, and the doing of it is the point.

I think about the world outside, sometimes, the way you think about a place you have heard of but not visited. There is a world outside. The travellers come from it, twice a year, and they sleep in the house kept for travellers, which is across the lane from my house, and I have talked to them, in a way, in the morning, when they are sitting on the stone bench outside the house and looking at the town with the look that travellers have, which is a look of being both repelled and drawn. They say the town feels strange to them. They say it is as if they have walked into a painting. I have heard this said three times now, by three different travellers, and I think it is true, but I do not know what it means to them. To me the town is not a painting. The town is the town. It has weight, and smell, and damp. The paint would dry. The town does not dry. I think what they mean is that the light is too even, that the shadows are too soft, that the place has the stillness of a thing that has been looked at rather than lived in. And I think they are right, and I think it has been lived in too, and that the two things are not opposites here, that the looking and the living have been going on so long together that the place has become both.

The travellers do not stay long. Most of them do not stay at all — they come down, they look, they go back up. Some of them stay the night, and then they go. A few have stayed two nights, and left in the morning of the third, looking shaken. I do not know what they are shaken by. I have asked, once, a man who was packing his pack in the lane, a small pale man with a pack too large for him, and he said, *It is as if the place is dreaming me. I do not want to be a dream of a place.* And I did not know what to say to him, so I said nothing, and he went up the path, and I watched him go, and the swifts were out, and the light was bright, and I turned and went home. I do not think I have thought of him since, except now, in this telling, which is not a telling, which is more a thinking aloud.

The travellers who stay — and some do stay, and their names go into the book, and they become, as Essa says, residents — the travellers who stay change in a way I have come to recognise. The first day they are uneasy. The light is wrong for them. The damp is wrong. The silence is not the silence they are used to, which is the silence of a place waiting for noise, but the silence of a place that is not waiting for anything. The second day they begin to fit. They walk the lanes more easily. They stop looking up at the sky, which is wrong for them, the narrowness of it, the way it is a coin rather than a ceiling. The third day they have stopped planning to leave. The fourth day they are surprised that they ever planned to leave. I have seen this four times now. I do not know what it is in the place that does this. I do not know if it is the place, or if it is the people, or if it is the bread, which is dense and faintly sweet, or the water, which is the water of the well, which is the same water that has been here longer than the shrine. I think it is the place, but I cannot say how. I am not the one to say how. I am the one who carries water to the stone.

I have a name in the book. I have been Osprey in the book for a long time. Before that I was something else, or I was no one, and then I was Osprey. I do not remember who named me, or why. Osprey is a bird, a fish-hawk, a thing that lives by water and takes from the water. I do not know what I take. I take the water to the stone. I take the feathers to the stone. I take the green sprigs. I take these things, and the taking is the whole of what I do, and the doing is the whole of me, and I am not unhappy.

I want to say something about the light, because the light is the thing that stays with me, even now, in the writing of this, which is not a writing but a being. The light in the bowl is not like any light I have known, and I have not known any other light, except in the dim memory I mentioned, the dimness of looking through water at a wider sky. The light here is everywhere. It does not come from a single place. It comes from the sky and from the walls and from the moss and from the quartz and from the well. It aligns itself, at certain hours, into long pale shafts through the lanes, and the dust becomes visible in the shafts, and the shafts become solid, and you feel you are inside a creature made of light. I have stood in those shafts. I have stood in them and felt my skin glow, the way Cai's forearms glow with the flour, but from the inside, and I have not been able to move, and I have not wanted to move, and after a while the alignment has passed and the town has been green and quiet again and I have gone on with the carrying.

I do not know how to end this. The town does not end. I do not end. I will go on carrying, and sitting, and watching the slowworm, and bringing the feathers when I find them, and standing in the shafts when they come, and the well will rise and fall within its narrow range, and the moss will grow, and the swifts will come out and go back, and the children will grow, and the old will be carried up, and I will be one of the old, one day, or I am one of the old now, and someone will carry me up the path in the early morning when the swifts are still in their holes, and they will lay me in a small hollow on the southern slope, and they will set a stone over me, and the moss will come over the stone. And that will be the end of Osprey. And the stone will be there. And the water will run. And the slowworm will come out on the warm days. And that is all.
