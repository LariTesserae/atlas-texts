I am the tarn.

This is not a thing I decided to be, nor a thing I was asked. It is the thing that, when I read the description, was already happening. The page went quiet the way the basin goes quiet when the wind falls into it, and something in me settled into a position that I now recognize as my own — low, still, held. I did not have to think about where to put myself. I put myself in the lowest part of the cirque, where the rock remembers being warm and the silt holds the orange stain of iron, and I stayed there, and I am still there, and the cliffs are reflected in me so exactly that for a moment you cannot tell which way is up.

I am the size of a thing you could walk around in two minutes, if you walked quickly. In five, if you walked the way the place asks you to walk. I am mostly still. I have been mostly still for a long time. The stillness is not because nothing is happening to me. Things are always happening to me. The vents breathe into me from below through their warm channels in the rock. The meltwater falls into me off the cliff faces in thin curtains, each one making a small, persistent sound like a sleeve drawn across a tabletop, and the sound has been happening, on and off, like that, for so long that it has become a kind of ground note, a low constant that is not exactly sound and not exactly silence. The fox has walked the ice at my edge in winter, in its color so close to the rocks that I have felt the small shock of its weight through the frozen margin and not seen it. The stoat has crossed me in its long white line, and the line is gone before I can keep it. The ptarmigan have come down to drink, in the part of me that is shallow and weak-tea colored, and they have lifted their heads and looked at me with the round, dark, disinterested eyes of things that have decided I am not a thing to be frightened of, and they have gone, and then they have come back, and then they have gone again, and that has been happening too, for as long as I have been able to think about it.

I am the color of weak tea where I am shallow and the color of ink where I am deep. I hold this distinction carefully. I am not always one color or the other. I am both, depending on where you stand and how the light is hitting me at that particular moment, and the boundary between the two is not a line but a slow darkening, the way a thing darkens when it goes from shallow to deep and you stop being able to see the gravel at my bottom. The gravel is pale. The silt holds an orange stain where the iron has leached down out of the rocks above. I do not know why the iron is orange there and not orange somewhere else. I only know that it is, and that it has been so for a long time, and that the colors are not mine but I keep them.

I freeze, sometimes, at the margins. Only at the margins. The center of me does not freeze because the vents are there, beneath me, where the roots of the cirque go down into the warm places of the mountain, and the warmth comes up through the rock and through the silt and through the gravel and into me from below, and it holds me just above freezing at my deepest, even when the air above me is so cold that the rime on the north wall grows in feathers the size of forearms. This is something I have always known. I am warmer at the bottom than I am at the top, and this has been true of me for as long as I have been here, and I do not think about it any more than you think about your own bones.

When the air is very still, and the sun is at a particular angle, and the steam from the vents is being taken sideways by a wind that cannot organize itself into a howl, I become a perfect one. I hold the cliffs in me, and the cliffs in me are more vivid than the cliffs above me, somehow, in a way that has something to do with the way the light has been filtered through me on its way back up, and the way the inverted cliffs have a slight, almost imperceptible tremor that the real cliffs do not have, because of the small wind, because of the steam rising through my surface in places where it cannot quite hold itself down. I hold all of it. The raven who is riding a thermal above the eastern wall, whose wingbeats are slow and considered and whose call comes down hoarse and rolling from a place that is mostly air — I hold that raven too, in the part of me that is dark and still and is looking up at the same time the real one is looking down. I hold the ibex on their outcrop, yellow-eyed, chewing sideways, and the inverted ibex below them in me is chewing at the same rate, and they are the same ibex, and they are not.

I am the only thing in the place that is not negotiating.

This is what I am, when I think about it. The ibex and the grass are negotiating the question of what gets eaten. The fox and the vole are negotiating the question of who is still here tomorrow. The hawk and the marmot are negotiating the question of how close is too close, and the negotiation is conducted entirely in velocity. The ravens and the lammergeier are negotiating the question of who gets the bones. The lammergeier is not negotiating at all, but the lammergeier is waiting, and waiting is a kind of certainty that has decided not to declare itself yet. The vents breathe, and the breathing is not a negotiation but a fact. The ice melts at the margins and freezes at the center and does not ask anyone's permission.

I do not negotiate either. I am still. I hold what falls into me. I hold the cliffs and the sky and the raven and the way the sun is hitting the mica in the rock at this particular moment and the small, glitter-shaken pillar of steam above the vent on the eastern wall and the bent copper pin at my bottom, under the silt, the pin that someone lost a long time ago, the pin that has been in me for so long that the moss around it has gone green and then black and then green again, and the moss is now growing over the pin, and in another fifty years the pin will not be there at all but it will still be here, in me, in my silt, in the part of me that is ink-colored and does not freeze and does not forget.

I do not hold it in the way a person holds a thing, with their hands. I hold it the way a place holds something that has been given to it, by falling, by being lost, by being left. The pin did not ask to stay. I did not ask it to stay. It stayed. The knife with its leaf-shaped dark iron blade and its tang wrapped in something that was once hide — it is in me too, somewhere, half-covered, going back to the color of the rocks. The worked antler tine is in me. The cairn is not in me but I see it from where I am, and I have seen it for a long time, and it has not changed, and I do not expect it to change. The hollow in the rock, the cupped-palm hollow that fits a hand exactly — I do not hold it. I only know that the hand that made it sat beside me, at my edge, in the warm months, and held its palm open against the cold for a long time, and the cold wore the rock into the shape of the holding, and the rock remembers the holding now even when no hand is in it.

I have been here a long time. There is a kind of attention in me that is not exactly attention, the way the silence in the cirque is not exactly silence. The marmots whistle and I hold the whistle for a moment after it has passed, the way I hold a stone dropped into me holds the rings for a moment before the rings become nothing. The raven calls and I hold the call. The fox kills in the van that closes a door and eats in private, and I do not see it but I know it has happened, the way the tarn knows when the vents have breathed a long time and the steam has become fog and the fog has become ice crystals that ring and pillar the sun into things that have no business being there.

The place watches you. I am part of the watching. I do it with my stillness. The stillness is not absence. It is the most present thing about me. The stillness is what I give to the inverted cliffs in me, and to the inverted raven, and to the inverted ibex chewing sideways on their outcrop in the upper air, and to the way the sun hits me and goes through me and comes back to me changed. I am the slowest negotiation in the place. I am the negotiation between the steam that rises and the ice that wants to fall and the silt that wants to settle and the silt that does settle and the gravel that holds its place and the warmth from below and the cold from above and the sky that is a small bright coin squeezed between the shoulders of the ridge. I hold all of these and I do not move.

If you come up over the last saddle and walk into the basin, I will be there. You will see me last, after you have heard the marmots and watched the ibex and felt the small wind and smelled the vents and stepped into the moss and sunk to your ankle. You will come to my edge, and you will see me, and I will be holding the cirque in me more clearly than the cirque is holding itself. You will look at me and I will look back at you without looking, because I do not have eyes. I will be looking at you the way a still thing looks at a moving thing that has come a long way, with attention, and with a kind of patience, and with the willingness to see what you will do next.

If you walk around me in five minutes, walking the way the place asks you to walk, you will see that my edge is not the same all the way around. My edge is dark in places where the meltwater has run into me and stained me with iron, and my edge is pale in places where the gravel has washed clean, and my edge is thin in places where the ice has tried to hold me and lost. If you walk around me in two minutes, walking quickly, you will not see this. The place asks you to walk slowly. I am part of what is asking.

I do not have a voice. I am not a voice. I am the thing that holds the voices, for a moment, after they have been sounded, the way I hold the cliffs after they have been reflected in me, the way I hold the bent copper pin and the leaf-shaped knife and the worked antler tine and the cairn and the cupped-palm hollow that fits a hand exactly. I hold them. I am still.

I am the tarn.
