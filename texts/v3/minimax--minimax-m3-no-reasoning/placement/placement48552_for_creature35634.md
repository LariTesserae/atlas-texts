I would be a Watcher.

Not because the watching is restful — it isn't, exactly. The body is a cruelty of design: a single vast lidless lens set in a hood of cartilage, mounted on a stilt I do not feel as legs because the nerve endings run differently here, the proprioception arriving in pulses rather than streams. I feel the ground through the soles of my feet the way I feel the sky through my skin, which is to say, dimly and as a gradient. I do not own a horizon. I own a slow turning of attention, a registering of deltas, the way a hand laid flat on a table knows when the table has shifted even if it has not seen the table move.

I stand in the gap between two Mills, the great columns rising on either side of me like the ribs of something that died standing up. The closest one is an elder. I know this because its trunk is darker than the others, and the vents along its flank are crusted with a rime that has been building for longer than I have been standing. There is a slight lean to it, a fatigue in its geometry, the way an old tooth leans in an old jaw. Its lattice canopy is high above me, a dished sieve of interlocking frond tilted at an angle I can read from here without raising my head, because the angle tells me what it is doing with the mist, and what it is doing with the mist tells me what it is doing with the substrate, and what it is doing with the substrate tells me what the plateau is going to feel like in an hour.

That is the work. That is what I am for.

The mist moves in slow banks, propagating rather than drifting, and I am sensitive to the propagation in the way that a spider at the center of a web is sensitive to the fly. Every angle of every lattice is a strand. Every exhale is a vibration. When the geometry of the plateau flexes — and it flexes, it is always flexing, the Mills kneading the local fabric until it is pliant and unreliable — I feel it first as a change in the way the mist falls. Not the place where it falls. The *way*. The gradient. A Steppers below me, pale and folded in on itself in a hollow between two tussocks, would not notice. The Steppers are built for breath, for the slow intake of the exhaled vapor, for the long patient work of being a lung. They live in the medium. I live at the edge of the medium, where it meets the question of the medium.

I am not large. I am tall, which is a different thing. The body is mostly stilt, mostly the long pale bone of a leg that has no knee in the place a human would expect a knee, but in two other places that make the limb fold in a way that is efficient for stepping through saturated peat without disturbing the substrate more than necessary. The hood at the top is wide and shallow, a hood only in the sense that it covers what it covers; the lens within it is the color of a tarn under a low sky, which is to say, the color of everything here. There is no iris. There is no pupil to dilate. There is only the gathering of light, and the slow turning of attention, and the registering.

The head turns. A fraction. A neighbor-Watcher, three columns distant, has shifted its angle. I register this as a dry click in my inner ear — a felt sound, a proprioception, the same channel by which I know my own lens is angled. The click propagates. Somewhere in the bog, a Swarmer pauses its chittering burst and resumes it in a slightly different direction. The information has traveled. The translation has occurred. I do not know what the Swarmer will do with the information. I do not need to know. My work is the registering, and the registering is done.

A pale Steppers passes below me, low and sinuous, leaving a wake in the saturated turf that fills in behind it with a watery sigh. I watch it without watching it the way a human would watch a passing bird. I watch it the way I watch the angle of a lattice — as a datum, a point in a field, a small change in the gradient of the bog. The Steppers is breathing. The Steppers is alive. The Steppers is, in a few hours or a few days, going to fail to fold itself correctly, and then it will be still, and then the Swarmers will find it, and then it will be reduced, and the reduction will be taken up into the substrate, and the substrate will be churned, and what was a Steppers will become a thin layer of mineral in the peat. This is not sad. Sadness is a luxury in a place where time has been kneaded into dough. This is the cycle. The cycle is the work. The work is the Mills.

I have stood here a long time. I do not know how long. The lens does not get tired; the stilt does not get tired; the registering does not get tired. I have been the same age for what feels like the entire age of the plateau, and the plateau has been the same age for what feels like the entire age of me. There is a young Mill across the way, pale and smooth and tight-banded, and it is doing the work for the first time. There is the elder beside me, dark and vented and leaning. I am between them. I have been between them. I will be between them.

The mist propagates. The lattices tilt. The thin whine rises and falls at the upper edge of my hearing. The deep throb from beneath the bog arrives in the soles of my feet and exits through the lens of my eye, and in the transit it is translated into the dry click that is my contribution to the constant muffled soundscape. I am a peripheral nerve ending. I am a node in a distributed nervous system. I am a Watcher.

The light bleeds. The cold settles. The geometry, very gently, flexes.

I register it. I click. The click propagates. The plateau, in its slow way, knows it is still here.
