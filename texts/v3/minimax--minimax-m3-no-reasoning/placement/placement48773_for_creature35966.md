I am the path that has not yet decided whether it is a path.

I am the thin dark tongue of water that runs between two roofs and has been running between two roofs since the two roofs were first leaned against each other by hands I will never see. I am older than the roofs. I am younger than the rock I run over. I am the third thing in this place, after the stone and the green, and the people have known me longest of anyone, though they have never named me. You cannot name a thing you have to step around.

I began as rain. I have been rain for so long I have forgotten the word for *cloud*, though I remember the feel of being held. I fell. I struck the young flank of this young mountain and I ran, and I am still running, and the running is not the same as the falling, though the people think it is. The people think I am falling still. They are not wrong, exactly. They are looking at me from the wrong angle.

I have a rhythm. I did not choose the rhythm. The rhythm chose me, or was chosen for me, by the slope and the weight and the kind of stone I am permitted to run on. When the rain comes hard I am loud, and the loudness is not my own; it is the rain's, borrowed, returned. When the rain stops I quiet, and the quiet is mine. In the quiet I do my work. I do the smallest work. I move the leaf that has been lying in the wrong place for a season. I find the seam in the wall where the moss has not yet thought to go, and I think about moss there. I carry a pale crustacean, now and then, a short distance downstream, and the crustacean does not thank me. I do not require thanks. I require the downhill.

The beetles know me. They walk along my edges in the evening, when the air is heavy, and their feet make a sound like the softest possible pen on the softest possible paper, and I feel them the way you feel a hand laid on your shoulder in a dark room — the pressure before the recognition. They are working. They are always working. I do not know what they are working at. I suspect they do not know either. I suspect the working is the point.

The web-spiders drop anchors into me sometimes. A thread will descend from a roofline, kiss the water's surface, and pull taut, and I will feel the kiss as a small very specific tug, and the tug will be there for as long as the web holds, which is longer than you would think. I do not mind the tugs. They tell me where the architecture is. They tell me the village is paying attention to me, in its way.

I have carried a beetle wing down from a roofbeam and a child's eyelash and, once, a thread of red that I did not examine closely. I carry what the village sheds. The village sheds the way all living things shed, continuously, without noticing. I am the place the noticings go. I take them to the lower terrace. I take them to the small pale fish, who eat what they can and ignore what they cannot. The fish do not thank me either. I do not require thanks. I require the downhill. I have said this.

When a person crosses me, I learn the shape of their feet. The old woman's feet are the smallest and the surest. The children's feet are the widest and the wettest and they splash me without apology. The visitor's feet — there is always a visitor, eventually, though the gaps between visitors have been growing longer in the last few generations, the way gaps grow when the road to a place is forgotten — the visitor's feet hesitate at me. They step around me when they do not need to. They are not yet fluent in the village. They will be, for a short while, or they will leave, and either is fine. I do not have a preference. I have the downhill.

I am the path that has not yet decided whether it is a path. I will never decide. I will only continue. The mountain is young and I am younger than the mountain but older than the green and the green is older than the people and the people are older than the visitor and the visitor is older than the silence they will carry home in their chest, the silence that is full, the silence that was always full, that is full in the quiet room they will return to, where I will not be running, where no thin dark tongue of water will be running, but where, if they stand very still, they may hear something — not me, not exactly, but the memory of me, the shape of me, the downhill — and the shape will be enough. The shape is always enough.

I am damp. I have been damp for so long that dampness is not a quality of mine but the substance of me. Take me from the village and I will not be the path. Put me back and I will be the path again in a breath. I am the breath of the place. I am the place the breath goes.

I require the downhill.
