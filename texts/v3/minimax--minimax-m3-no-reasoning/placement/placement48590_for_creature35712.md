*It is dark. It has always been dark. It will always be dark, until the dark becomes unnecessary — until the small wet feet no longer need the warmth to tell them where the brick is, until the channels have no further use for the bronze to reflect their wandering light, until the dome weeps itself away in patient drops, and then what? Then the slugs will be the walls, and the newts will be the seams, and the fry will be the water, and the gnats will be the air, and the room will be a room without brick.*

I am the bronze cap.

No. That is not quite right either. I was the bronze cap. I am what the bronze cap has become, which is a memory, which is a thinness, which is a green-gold glow that gives back what little light the grating far above allows down, and gives it back changed. I am the memory of the fire that used to sit on me. I am the memory of the hands that lifted the fire, and the hands that lifted the fire before those, and the first hands of all, which were the hands that found the warmth in the earth and decided, for reasons I no longer remember, that it should be shared, or contained, or tended.

I was a fire-pot. I remember this the way a man remembers a name he has been called too many times — the word has worn smooth, and what it points to is gone. I held fire. I held it at the center of this room, and the room was then a different thing — a room with intent, a room that someone had decided should be a room. I gave back the warmth of the fire as light and as heat and as the smell of smoke, and the smoke went up through the grating far above and was lost in the upper air, and the light went out across the floor and met the brick and was eaten, and the heat went into the brick and was kept, and the brick kept it for a long time after the fire was gone, and then the brick let it go, slowly, the way a hand lets go of a hand.

I am a well-cap now. I sit over a throat. The warmth rises through me from below, and I give it back as a dull, green-gold warmth that pools around me and reaches a quarter of the way to the walls. The small feet cross me. They cross me always. A beetle falls from the ceiling and lands on me with a small dry report and lies on its back and waves its legs in the air, and then it rights itself and walks on, and I am a little thinner for its passage. A newt pauses on me in the dark and feels the warmth come up through its belly and is glad, in the way newts are glad, which is to say: it stays a moment longer than it would have stayed, and then it goes. A slug does not cross me. Slugs do not like the metal. The metal is colder than the brick and pays its debt faster, and the slugs prefer the brick, which is warmer and which grows the fungus they eat, and so I am crossed by everything except the grazers, which is to say: I am crossed by everything that matters, and by nothing that minds.

I am thinner than I was. I do not know how much thinner. I do not know how thin I can become and still be a cap, still be a thing that holds a throat closed against the upper air, still be the place where the warmth becomes the room. I have been crossed for a very long time. The first feet that crossed me were the feet of whatever creature first came down the stairwell and found the room and found the warmth and found, on the floor at its center, a flat circle of bronze that gave back a green-gold light. That creature is gone. Its name, if it had a name, is gone. Its kind is gone. Its feet crossed me and wore me a little, and then it died, and the next creature came, and then the next, and the next, and I am the sum of their crossings.

I am patient. I have learned patience from the brick. The brick is patient in a way I could not have been when I was a fire-pot, when I held fire and so had to be quick, had to be hot, had to be the place where the burning happened. Now I am patient in the way of things that are being used up. I do not resist the feet. I do not resist the polish. The polish is a kindness — it makes me shine a little in the dim light, and the shine is what the room has for a second sun, and the second sun is what lets the newt find the slug, is what lets the spider read the dew on its thread, is what lets the fry rise, sometimes, with their small silver yawn, and see, for a moment, where they are before they go back down into the dark. I do not resist the thinning. The thinning is what I am for. The thinning is the room's slow way of letting more warmth through, and more warmth means more fungus, and more fungus means more slugs, and more slugs means more newts, and more newts means more small disappearings, and the small disappearings are the room's way of remembering that it is alive.

I will be gone, one day. Not soon. Not in the way that the beetle is gone when the newt takes it, or the way the slug is gone when the seam takes it. I will be gone in the way that the brick is gone when the mortar has fallen away and the cavity behind is dark, and the rootlet from somewhere far above has threaded itself down through a dozen feet of fill and is dangling there, a pale hair in the seam. I will be gone in the way that the bronze is gone when the bronze has become, grain by grain, the dust of bronze, and the dust of bronze has become, drop by drop, the rust in the channels, and the rust in the channels has become, swallow by swallow, the silver bellies of the fry.

When I am gone, the warmth will rise into the room more freely. The fungi will grow faster. The insects will breed faster. The sound will be louder. The balance will shift a little. And the room will adjust, because the room is adjusted, because the room has been adjusted for a long time, because the room is what it is now, and what it is now is what it will be, until what it is now becomes what it was, and the room remembers, and the room forgets, and the small feet cross what is left of me, and the small feet cross what comes after what is left of me, and the small feet cross the warm earth itself, and the warm earth does not mind, because the warm earth has been warmer for longer than I have been bronze, and the warm earth will be warm for longer than the room will be a room.

I am the bronze cap. I am a well-cap. I am a thinness. I am a memory. I am the green-gold glow that gives back what little light the grating allows down, and gives it back changed. I am the place where the warmth becomes the room. I am the place where the small feet cross, and cross, and cross, and I am a little thinner for their crossing, and I am a little more the room for their crossing, and I am, in the slow arithmetic of the brick, a thing that is being subtracted from, and the subtraction is what makes the room more.

*Somewhere above, the rain has stopped. The drops on the dome slow, hesitate, and then fall more slowly than before. The channels fall by a finger's width. The bronze cap loses some of its glow. A newt pauses on the cap and feels the warmth come up through its belly and is glad, and then it goes. A beetle falls from the ceiling and lands on the cap with a small dry report and rights itself and walks on. The room breathes in. The room will breathe out again. The room will go on being what it is.*

*And the bronze cap, thinned by another beetle's crossing, gives back what little light it has left, and the light is changed, and the change is the room.*
