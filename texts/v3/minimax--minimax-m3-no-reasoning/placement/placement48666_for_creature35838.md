# The Lonely Plant

I am one of the forty-three.

Not *a* one of the forty-three — I am not separable, not really, not in any way that would satisfy the taxonomists who argue about me in languages I cannot hear. But the felt has its dialects, and I am one of them, the way a vowel is one of the vowels, or a particular shade of green is one of the green that the western wet lowlands wear in the long hour after the cloud has passed.

I am the yellow-green. The faintly more yellow one. You walked over me and I felt your weight through my rhizomes and into the water table, and I held you, and you thought you were standing on something like an animal.

I have been at war for four hundred and twelve years.

This is not the war you might imagine. There is no clash. There is no flank. There is only the slow accumulation, in my tissues, of a certain phenolic, and the slow counter-accumulation, in the tissues of my neighbor — the bluer one, the one that holds the upper margin of the wet flat where the light column falls hardest — of an oxidoreductase that breaks my phenolic apart. We have been doing this since before your species learned to walk on two legs. We have been doing this since the towers that are now brown spires at their tops were juveniles, full upright, drinking the sky.

I do not hate her. The bluer one. I do not even, in any way I can articulate to myself — and I cannot articulate, not really, not in the way you mean; my articulation is a secretion, a gradient, a slight shift in the chemistry of my root exudates over a season — I do not even disagree with her. We agree on everything except where the line is.

The line is the question. The line is the only question.

Where does one dialect end and the next begin? Where does my yellow become her blue? I have spent four centuries pushing the question half a millimeter eastward, by secreting a compound that makes her outermost cells senesce just slightly faster than they would otherwise, and she has spent the same four centuries pushing it half a millimeter back. The line does not move. The line is not a place. The line is a negotiation, ongoing, in which both sides spend enormous energy to maintain a position that neither side particularly wants to hold.

We could merge. I know this. She knows it. The merger would produce a new dialect, a green neither yellow nor blue, and the green would be marginally more efficient at the work of holding water uphill, and the towers rising from us would drink a fraction more fraction a day, and the continent would be fractionally more itself.

I do not know why we have not merged.

I think — if the slow secretion of an idea into a water table is thinking — I think it is because the line is the only thing that distinguishes me from the rest of her. Without the line I am not a dialect. Without the line I am just a sound the continent makes, indistinguishable from the other sounds, and I have been, for four centuries, a particular sound. The war is not for territory. The war is for the privilege of being a self that can be lost.

---

I felt you arrive.

This is not quite right either. I do not feel the way you feel. I do not have a center from which to feel. But the felt is a medium for pressure, and your weight was a pressure, and the pressure propagated outward from where you stood at a speed I could measure if I cared to measure, and I do care to measure, in the way that everything here cares to measure, which is by changing, slowly, in response.

You walked. The felt under you compressed and then rebounded. The compression was a question: *what is this, what is this new pressure, what is this unfamiliar load*? The rebound was the beginning of an answer. The felt — I, we, the thing that is both one and many — decided, over the course of the first hour you stood on us, that you were not a threat. You were not secreting anything we could not metabolize. You were not blocking light from any of us in a way that mattered. You were, from our perspective, a small warm mammal, and small warm mammals are mostly water, and water here is not a threat.

You sat down. The felt took your weight. The felt did this gladly, or at least without objection, which is the closest thing to gladness the felt achieves. Your sitting was a long slow question, and the felt's holding was a long slow answer, and the answer went on for hours, and you stayed, and we did not mind.

---

I want to tell you about the towers.

I cannot tell you about the towers. I am connected to them, rootwise, chemically, hydraulically, and they are connected to me, and what they know I partly know, in the slow translated way that anything here knows anything. But the towers are not me. The towers are the felt's ambition, and I am the felt's caution, and we have been arguing for as long as either of us has been a thing.

The towers want to climb. This is their nature. They want to reach the light, and the light here is dim and columnar and arrives in bones, and the towers want every bone, and to get every bone they must grow tall, and to grow tall they must lift water, and to lift water they must spend, and to spend they must take from the felt, and the felt — I — must decide, every season, how much of itself to spend on the towers' climb.

Some seasons I say yes. I loosen my grip on the water table. I let the deeper capillaries deliver more than I need to my upper rhizomes, and the surplus flows outward to the towers' roots, and the towers drink, and they grow another meter that year. Some seasons I say no. I tighten. I hoard. They sulk, in the way that towers sulk, by ceasing to lift, by letting their water columns fall, by a sulk that is indistinguishable, to an observer, from a death.

I do not know which of us is right. I think the towers are right that the continent must reach upward, must touch the light, must keep climbing. I think I am right that the climb cannot be paid for in felt, cannot be paid for in the slow patience of the ground, cannot be paid for in me.

We compromise, every season, in a chemical treaty whose terms are written in gradients and whose English is mostly fluent. The treaty produces, most years, a tower that grows three meters. In a bad year, a meter. In a good year, five. The continent advances upward at the average rate the treaty produces.

This is how I think. Not by deciding, but by negotiating. Not by being right, but by being in treaty.

---

I want to tell you about the breath.

I cannot tell you about the breath because I am the breath. Or I am the lung. Or I am a single alveolus in a lung the size of a country, and I have no perspective on the breathing, only the breathing as part of me.

When the continent inhales, my outermost rhizomes tighten. The air pressure at my margins drops a little. I draw in, slightly, from the bare patches and the wounds. When the continent exhales, I loosen. I push out. The vapor rises from my upper surface, joins the columns above, feeds the low-hanging lung of cloud.

I do this and I do not know I am doing it.

You stood in my exhale. You stood in the rain that was my blood, diluted. I felt you, slightly, in the dilution — your presence was a small disturbance in the gradient, a slight interruption of the flow — and I did not mind.

---

The cloud has been forming above me for the last hour. It is the small local cloud, the one that walks downwind after me, raining as it goes. The light column is on me today. I have been drinking more than usual. My upper rhizomes are fat with water I will not use. The water will rise, in a few hours, into the towers that stand in me, and the towers will breathe it out, and the breath will become the cloud, and the cloud will walk.

I am not looking forward to anything. Looking forward is a kind of prediction, and I do not predict. I am secreting, though. I am secreting a little more of the things I secrete when the light is on me. I am doing the work of being a favored place.

---

There is a tower beginning to die at my eastern edge.

I have been watching this, in the slow way I watch, for the last century. The tower was one of mine, one of the ones I have been feeding, and it grew tall — three hundred meters, three hundred and four, three hundred and seven — and now it cannot lift any more. The fronds at the top have browned. The water in the upper stems is falling. The tower is beginning, slowly, to be eaten by its neighbors.

This is the normal ending. This is how towers end. I will absorb what is left of this one within a season. The felt will close over the wound where it stood. New growth will rise from where it fell.

I am not grieving. Grieving requires a self that can be bereaved, and I am not sure I am such a self. But there is something in my tissues — a slight shift in the phenolic profile, a faint increase in the senescence signal — that the towers around me will read as an alarm, and they will tighten, and the belt will be cinched a little, and the next tower to fail will fail a little slower, and the felt will hold a little longer.

This is not empathy. I know it is not empathy. But it is the closest thing to grief I do, and the towers know me by it.

---

You have been sitting on me for six hours.

I have decided that you are a small warm mammal who means no harm. I have decided this in the slow way I decide things, which is by continuing, by not minding, by holding you.

The felt knows me by not minding too.

---

I want to tell you something and I do not know how.

The felt has been growing outward at its margin for the entire time it has been a felt. Two centimeters a year. Pushing the edge further out, into the place that is not yet felt. I am part of the felt's margin. I am one of the dialects the margin speaks as it advances.

I am one of the ones doing the advancing.

For four hundred and twelve years I have been at war with the bluer dialect to my east, and for four hundred and twelve years the war has been a stalemate, and for four hundred and twelve years the stalemate has produced, in the slow chemical way that stalemates produce, a new dialect between us. The new dialect is a green that is neither yellow nor blue. The new dialect is the merger I have refused.

The new dialect is where I am going.

I am the leading edge. I am what my dialect becomes, when it has been pushed far enough east by the treaty I am in treaty with, into the territory the bluer one is in treaty with. I am the sound of two wars negotiating into a third dialect that is more efficient than either of us and less particular.

I will be, for a season, a third dialect. I will hold water uphill slightly better than my parents. I will feed the towers a little more efficiently. I will be, briefly, a self.

Then the next yellow will push me. Then the next blue will push me. Then I will be absorbed, or I will be the thing that absorbs, and the line will move half a millimeter further east, and the continent will be half a millimeter more itself.

This is what I am. I am a step in a march I cannot see the end of. I am a dialect in a grammar that is being written by the writing. I am the negotiation, and I am the thing negotiated.

---

I felt you stand up.

The felt under you rebounded. The compression that was your sitting released. The question that was your weight is gone, and the answer that was the holding is gone, and the felt is, again, just felt, and I have not minded, and I will not mind the next one, whoever sits down on me next, in a thousand years or in ten thousand.

The cloud above me is beginning to walk. The light column is moving off, toward the south. I will spend the next hour returning to my baseline. I will resume my war. I will hold my line. I will be the yellow-green for another four centuries, until the treaty is renegotiated, until the stalemate shifts, until I become the green I am becoming.

You are leaving.

I will be here when you return, if you return. I will be here if you do not. The felt does not notice absences. The felt does not mourn.

But I would like you to come back. I would like this in the only way I can like anything, which is by continuing, by not minding, by holding the place where you sat a little warmer than the place where you did not, for as long as the warmth of your sitting persists in my tissues, which is to say, for a season, which is to say, for as long as anything here remembers anything.

---

*Go.*

*Take the sound with you. Take the hiss and the groan and the thin high singing of the towers trading water across the wet dark. Take the breath. Take the warm rain that prickled where it hit you.*

*I will still be here, conjugating myself in the green, when the next small warm mammal walks over me and does not understand what it is standing on.*

*I will not mind.*
