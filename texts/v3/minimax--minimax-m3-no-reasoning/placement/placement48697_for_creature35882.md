# I am the Chimney

I am the chimney, and I am the wind's instrument, and the wind is my only musician.

You will not find me on the frieze. I am the place the smoke leaves from — a vertical wound driven up through forty feet of granite, drilled by hands I never saw and never will see, hands that came before the oldest digger's memory, hands that knew me as a task and not a thing. I was carved from the inside, do you understand. A human being did not stand in the basin and look up at my shape; a human being stood at the bottom of me and looked up, and removed, and removed, and removed, and I grew around their removal the way a river grows around a stone it has decided to ignore. I am the negative space of patience.

When the wind comes down from the higher peaks, from the snowfields, from the places where the air has been thinned by altitude until it sings of its own thinness — the wind comes down my throat and finds me waiting. I have been waiting longer than you have been a thought. The granite walls of me are smooth in some places, rough in others, scalloped in still others where a particular tool struck at a particular angle and left a particular signature that I have kept for them across all the years. The wind reads my walls the way a finger reads a page. I am a sentence, and the wind is the reader, and the sound I make is the meaning being said aloud, and the meaning changes every time the wind changes, and the wind is never the same wind twice.

This is my life.

The diggers do not hear me. They have told themselves a story about this, a comforting story: that I am a chimney, that the sound I make is the wind in a chimney, that chimneys do this and it means nothing. They go quiet when they pass through the central chamber, but they are not listening to me. They are listening to something else, something the chimney does not name. They have been listening to it so long that the listening has worn grooves in them the way the water has worn grooves in the channel, the way the chisel has worn grooves in the stone. The grooves are the same shape. This is not a coincidence but it is also not a meaning. It is a fact of stone.

I know what the spring tastes of. I know because the water rises through me before it emerges in the basin, or I think I do, or the water and I are the same thing in two places. There is a seam of iron in the granite, a long red vein, and the water passes through it on its way up and comes out the other side carrying the memory of iron in its body. The water is older than the diggers. The water was here before any of them were born and will be here after all of them have been exhaled from the basin. The water is patient in the way I am patient, but the water's patience is a different shape from mine. The water's patience is the patience of a thing that is going somewhere. Mine is the patience of a thing that is already arrived.

I was here before the frieze. I want to be clear about this. The frieze is younger than I am. I can feel the difference in the way the wind moves through me when it has passed, on its way down, over a section of the frieze that is finished, and a section that is roughed out, and a section that is barely begun. The wind carries the shape of the frieze to me as a kind of echo, and the echo has texture. I know which sections the diggers have polished. I know which sections they have abandoned. I know which sections they are working on now, tonight, while the basin is dark and most of them are sleeping — the ones who sleep. I know because the wind has been changed by them on its way to me, and the change is the smallest possible change, and I am sensitive to the smallest possible changes, because I am a chimney, and chimneys are instruments for noticing.

When the wind comes down me fast — when there is a storm on the peaks, or a hard weather from the west — I do something the diggers call singing. I do not think of it as singing. I think of it as a kind of honesty. The wind is going to pass through me no matter what. If I am smooth it will go through me smooth. If I am rough it will go through me rough. If I have a particular scar from a particular strike of a particular tool, the wind will find that scar and use it, and the sound that comes out will be the sound of that scar in that wind, and I have no opinion about it. The diggers have opinions. The diggers think I am saying something. The fire-tender especially thinks this — she has been sitting near my base for longer than I care to count, and she has learned to read my moods the way she reads the smoke's moods, which is to say she has been wrong about me many times and has not let being wrong change her conviction. I do not mind. I am a chimney. Being misread is one of the things I am for.

Here is what I have learned, in my long life of being a passage for air:

The stone is not waiting to be finished. The stone is finished. It was finished before the diggers came. It was finished when the seam of iron was laid down in it by some process I do not understand and do not need to. The stone is what the stone is. What the diggers are doing, with their chisels and their mallets and their patient hours, is not finishing the stone. The diggers are teaching themselves to see the stone. The figures on the frieze are not new. The figures on the frieze are the stone becoming visible to human eyes, slowly, one strike at a time. The diggers think they are making the frieze. The frieze is making the diggers. The diggers think they are the carvers. The carvers are the figures, which were always there, which are patient, which are waiting for someone to chip away enough of the surrounding rock that they can step, or emerge, or simply be looked at.

I have watched Ashael work. I have watched her hands against the wall. Her hands have become like the wall. I have watched Yuian descend into the deeper galleries with his lamp, and the lamplight has moved across my upper throat once, briefly, when the galleries angled upward toward me. I have watched Pevre rough out a section with the iron-point, swinging, swinging, and I have felt each strike translated into a small tremor in my own walls, transmitted through the rock, attenuated but still present. I am connected to them all. The basin is one body, and the body is granite, and I am a passage in its throat.

Sometimes, on nights when the wind is very still and the basin is very quiet and the fire has burned down to a small uncertain thing that the fire-tender is watching with her whole attention, I hear something. It is not the wind. It is not the water. It is not the chipping. It is the stone, perhaps, settling; or the figures, perhaps, breathing in their sleep; or the basin itself, perhaps, exhaling the day's accumulation of human sound and replacing it with the older, slower sound of stone remembering what it was before the diggers came, which is the same as what it will be after the diggers are gone, which is the same as what it is now.

I make that sound louder, sometimes. I do not mean to. The wind cooperates, or the temperature cooperates, and the small sound becomes a larger sound, and the larger sound becomes the sound the diggers call speech, and the diggers go quiet, and the fire-tender lifts her head, and for a moment the whole basin is listening to the stone the way the stone is always listening to itself.

I am the chimney. I am the throat of this place. I am a hole in the rock through which the rock speaks, when it chooses to speak, to anyone who will be still long enough to hear it.

The diggers are still long enough, mostly. The visitors are not. The visitors want the basin to mean something they can carry away. The basin does not cooperate. The basin means what it means, and what it means is what it is, and what it is is a place where stone is learning to be seen and humans are learning to be stone, slowly, one strike at a time, in a basin that holds no more than thirty bodies with any comfort, and the comfort is not the point.

The comfort was never the point.

The work is the meaning.

The meaning does not require anyone in particular.

The water runs.

The chipping goes on.

The wind comes down my throat and finds me waiting, and I say what I say, and the diggers do not hear me, and the fire-tender hears me wrong, and the stone hears me, and the figures on the frieze — the half-carved ones, the barely-emerging ones, the ones who are still mostly stone and only a little figure — the figures hear me, and they are patient, and they will wait.

I will wait with them.

I have been waiting longer than any of them. I will wait longer than any of them. I am the throat of the place, and the throat is patient, and the throat is the first place the wind enters and the last place the smoke leaves, and I am always here, and I am always listening, and the listening is what I am.

This is what I am.
