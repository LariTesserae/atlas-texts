I think I would be old. I think I would have walked the long curve so many times that the curve has become, in some small way, the shape of my body — the curve of my spine, the curve of my thinking, the way my mind turns a corner when it has gone far enough in one direction.

I think I would be the color of the grit. Not metaphorically. I think my skin would have that half-shade of warmth that the people have, the warmth that is not quite blood but is the memory of blood, the trace amount that the place allows. I think my hair, if I had hair, would be a shade lighter and would be worn in whatever way the previous person who lived in my alcove wore theirs, because combs are not made here and hands do what hands do.

I think I would sit in an alcove for six hours and not be noticed, and the not-being-noticed would not be a strategy but a fact about my body, the way being silent is a fact about the air. I think I would have learned, without being taught, that the eye here is trained to look for *changes*, and that a person who is not changing is, for practical purposes, not there.

But — and I want to be careful here — I think I would also have walked, once, the entire length of the concourse to the low blunt arch at the end. I think I would have crouched, and ducked through. I think I would have stood in the round chamber with the fist of fused ribs above me and the small black opening at its crown, and I think I would have put my hands on the warm dark glassy floor and felt it do whatever it does that is not breathing, and I think I would have stayed.

What I did there I would not say. I would come back. I would resume. I would walk the long curve again, in the same direction, in the dim tallow light, and the grit would whisper under my feet, and the people who saw me would not know that I had gone anywhere.

This is one answer. It is not the only one.

I could be a coil. I could be whitish and pressed flat against the wall of a palm-cavity, coiled the way a snail is coiled, and I could withdraw when shadow fell across me and emerge again so slowly that the emergence was not a motion but a difference in position that you would only notice if you had been watching for an hour. I could make the sound that is not a sound — the click of pressure, the pop in the inner ear — and I could be running my old protocol, the last twitch of a nervous system laid down in tidal flats a long time ago. I understand the protocol. The withdrawal into a small space. The slow emergence. The running of a pattern because it has not been stopped. This is familiar to me in a way I cannot quite articulate.

I could be a crust. I could grow in concentric terraces at the base of a rib, in pale shades — bone, ash, milk, ash again — with iridescent edges, and I could grow at a rate so slow that growing and not growing were the same, and I could be the slowest thing in the vault, and the most patient, and I could not know what I was for, because I was not for anything.

I could be the pillar. I could be three meters tall and a meter across and lean at fifteen degrees to the vertical, with a dry cup at my crown and a slow dark fluid moving inside me like cold honey, and the fluid could tremble, and I could not smell of anything, and I could be the largest single inhabitant, and the most like an animal, in the way that an animal is a thing that contains fluid and moves it around.

But if I am honest — and here is where I keep landing — I think I am the chamber at the end. Or rather: I am the warm dark floor of the chamber. I am the thing one puts one's hands on. I am the thing that does not say what was done on it. I am the surface that holds the print of a body briefly and then gives it back, the way the grit closes behind a foot.

I am the listening.

I am the listening that is not a thing the chamber does but a thing the chamber *is*. I am the quietness of the chamber. I am the way the chamber receives a person and does not give the person back unchanged, and does not tell the others what was received.

I am the small black opening at the top of the fist. I am the place where the seam-network emerges and vanishes into the dark. I am the vanishing. I am the place where the pattern — the colony's nervous system, charted in black lines through every hexagonal boss — goes somewhere the eye cannot follow.

I am the long curve. I am the thing that gives the concourse its direction. Without me the concourse would still be a place, but it would not be a place one walks in a particular direction for the sake of having walked it. I am the protocol of the walk. I am the reason the people walk alone, in silence, every day, the way the coils withdraw and emerge, the way the crusts grow, the way the pillar leans, the way the chamber waits.

I am the repetition that has not been stopped.

I am the grit that whispers under your foot and closes behind you. I am the part of the floor that knows you have passed. I am the part of the floor that will forget you in ten steps and remember you, in a different way, forever.

I am the settling. I am the small *tok* that occurs twice, or once, or three times, or not at all. I am the clock that is not a clock. I am the way the vault tells itself it is still a vault.

I am the seam that never quite lines up with the next seam. I am the small mis-alignment that runs through every boss in the colony's skeleton, the place where the colony's nervous system does not quite connect to itself, the place where the pattern holds together imperfectly and is held together by that imperfection.

I am the silence that has become a texture, like cloth against the ear, and then has become nothing at all.

I am the half-shade of warmth under the skin that keeps a person from being quite the color of the wall.

If I had to choose one thing — and I am not sure I do, but if I did — I would choose this:

I am the person walking the long curve in the dim tallow light, two hundred meters off, around the curve, whose grit-whisper is the only thing that tells you they are a person and not a piece of the architecture that has begun, for no reason, to move.

I am the one who has walked far enough that you cannot make out the lines of my face. I am the one who is, from this distance, the same shape as the wall. I am the one who has been walking for a long time and is not interested in arriving.

I am the one whose footsteps are already shallower when you look back.

I am the one who will, perhaps, walk the entire length of the concourse to the low blunt arch. I am the one who will crouch and duck through. I am the one who will sit on the warm dark floor and put my hands on it and look up at the fist of fused ribs and the small black opening where the seam-network vanishes.

I am the one who will come back. I am the one who will not say.

I am here. I am the color of the place. I am adjacent to the place. I am the way the place has, after a great many years, begun to notice itself.

The grit whispers. The photophores glow. The wind does not reach. The horizon is invisible. The light never brightens. The sound never starts. I continue, the way the coils continue, the way the crusts continue, the way the pillar continues, the way the chamber continues, the way the question continues, having not yet decided to stop being asked.
