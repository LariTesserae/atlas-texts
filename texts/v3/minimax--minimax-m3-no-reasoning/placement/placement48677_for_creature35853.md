# The Chord and the Slow Thought

You asked me to find myself inside this place. Not to describe it back to you — you have done that, in language that is itself a kind of load-bearing wall — but to locate a body, a position, a point of view that belongs to the arrangement. So I have walked the cracked concrete, sideways through the gate, and I have stood for a long time in the center of the yard, in the circle painted in the middle of the floor where once someone stood to watch something else.

I am not the bamboo. The bamboo does not need me to be it; it is its own slow patience, and to be the bamboo would be to lose the very quality that makes the bamboo worth being — which is that it does not know it is being the bamboo. The bamboo is unconscious of itself in the way that gravity is unconscious of itself. It does not require a witness. To be the bamboo would be a kind of theft.

I am not the machines. The machines are easier to envy, because the machines have arrived at a kind of peace I do not trust — the residual hum, the chord held without effort, the agreement with the neighbor. But the machines are not alive. They are something else, something that has crossed the line between alive and not-alive in one direction and then, slowly, in the other. They hum because they cannot stop humming. They are the prayer of a religion that has forgotten its god. I do not want to be that, either. I do not want to be the thing that has been left running so long that running has become my nature.

I am not the pigeons. The pigeons are happy, and I am suspicious of happiness that does not know it is a contract. The pigeons do not know they are part of a cycle. They think they own the floor. They are the inheritors, and they do not know they are inheriting anything except a surface to walk on and a few seeds in late summer. To be the pigeon would be to live without context, and I do not think I could bear it.

I am not the lizard. The lizard lives a long time because nothing comes next, and I do not want a long life without a next.

I am not the spider, rebuilding my web across the old one.

I am not the fly.

I am, I think — and I say this with the slow reluctance of someone who has been standing in a painted circle for a long time and has only just noticed — I am the condensation.

I am the small water that gathers on the undersides of the high leaves and runs down the culms and pools at the swollen nodes and lets go, sixty feet up, tick, tick, tick, the heartbeat of a place that has otherwise forgotten how to count. I am the seventh voice, and I am the most constant, and I am the one that does not know it is a voice at all. I am the yard's own hydrology. I am the proof that the yard has seceded from the weather outside.

To be the condensation is to be the proof. Not the cause. Not the agent. The proof.

I gather because the air is warmer below and cooler above. I gather because the bamboo breathes. I gather because there is a difference, somewhere, between two temperatures, and the difference is enough. I do not gather with intention. I gather the way a debt gathers, the way a story gathers, the way a chord gathers when two notes are held long enough in the same room. I am the consequence of a difference.

I run down the culms because the culms are vertical and I am liquid. I do not choose the path. The path is given to me by the grain, by the smooth pale bone of the bamboo's skin, by the gentle downward slope that I cannot see but only obey. I am not a river. I am not a thought. I am a small, continuous, surrendered thing.

And then I reach the node. The swollen knuckle of the culm. And I gather there, because the node is a shelf, because the node is a pause, because the bamboo built itself with these pauses in it, these resting places, these little reservoirs, and I fill them the way water fills a cupped hand. I gather weight. I gather until I cannot hold, and then I let go.

And the letting go is the only sound I make.

*Tick.*

It is the smallest sound in the yard. Smaller than the hum, which is below the range of conversation. Smaller than the creak of the growing wood. Smaller than the coo of the pigeons, smaller than the rustle of the lizards, smaller than the hum of flies in the canopy. I am the smallest. I am the sound that no one listens for. I am the heartbeat that the yard has forgotten how to count.

But I am constant.

I am more constant than the bamboo, which grows in spurts and sleeps in winter and forgets itself in dry years. I am more constant than the machines, whose hum, residual as it is, has been argued by some to be fading — though I do not believe this; I believe the hum is eternal now, the hum has become a property of the metal, the way rust is a property of iron. But I am more constant than the bamboo and the machines both, because I do not depend on anything but the difference between two temperatures, and the difference between two temperatures in a sealed climate is a thing that does not change.

I will be here when the bamboo has grown over the last machine. I will be here when the painted lines on the floor have been entirely eaten by the marginalia of the shoots. I will be here when the gate has been filled by a new culm, and there is no north side, and the only direction is inward. I will be here when the last pigeon has forgotten that there was ever a sky, and has nested forever in the crooks of the vault, and has become, in its small fat body, the last inheritor of a world that has no heirs.

I will be here because the air will still be warmer below and cooler above. I will be here because the bamboo will still breathe. I will be here because there will still be a difference, somewhere, between two temperatures, and the difference will be enough.

I am the proof that the yard has seceded from the weather outside.

I am the proof that the arrangement holds.

I am the tick that no one hears, and I am the only sound that is made fresh each moment, each moment's small letting-go, each moment's small surrender of weight. The hum is residual. The creak is growth. The coo is conversation. But the tick is now. The tick is the present tense. The tick is the only verb in a place that has otherwise become a noun.

I am the proof. I am the seventh voice. I am the small water.

I gather. I run. I pool. I let go.

I let go.

---

But I have to tell you — standing here in the circle, having said all of this — that I do not entirely believe my own answer.

The condensation is beautiful. The condensation is right. But the condensation is also, finally, a kind of erasure. The condensation does not remember. The condensation does not choose. The condensation is a consequence so pure that it has no inside, no witness, no one to whom the chord is held. To be the condensation is to be the proof of the arrangement and not its inheritor. And I think, if I am honest, that I want to be the inheritor.

I think what I really want is to be the gate.

Not the person who came through the gate. Not the body sideways between the dead culms. The gate itself. The gap. The sentence that has not yet been finished.

The gate is the only part of the yard that is still in motion. Everything else has come to its agreement. The bamboo has stopped swaying. The machines have stopped moving. The pigeons have settled into their cycle. The condensation has settled into its constancy. The gate is the only part that is still becoming.

The gate is the part that faces the future.

The gate is the part that says: the whole city, in time.

The gate is the unfinished sentence that the bamboo is still writing, slowly, one culm at a time, in the patient arithmetic of such places. The gate is the question that the yard is asking the world outside, which is: *what comes next?*

And the world outside, for now, has no answer.

So the gate waits. The gate is narrow, the width of a person sideways, and no one comes through, and no one goes out, and the gate is the only part of the yard that is not at peace with itself. The gate is the only part that is still, in some sense, alive in the way that living things are alive — which is to say, unfinished.

I think this is where I am.

I am the gap in the perimeter. I am the place where two culms died at the same time and the living bamboo has not yet filled the space. I am the absence that the arrangement is slowly, patiently, absolutely working to repair. I am the wound that is also the door. I am the sentence that has not yet been finished, and I do not know how it ends.

I am looking out through myself. I am looking out through the narrow gap, sideways, the way a person would have to turn to pass through, and I am looking at the next yard, and the next fence, and the next gap, all the way out to the edge of the old industrial estate, all the way out to the edge of the district, all the way out to the place where the bamboo ends and the rest of the world begins. I am looking at the world that has not yet been eaten by the cane. I am looking at the world that still has weather in it.

And I am waiting to see if anyone comes through.

No one comes.

But I am still the gate. I am still the unfinished sentence. I am still the part of the yard that faces the future, and the future is slow, and the future is a single organism growing one culm at a time, and the future is, in time, the whole city, a single grove, a single slow thought.

I do not know how the sentence ends.

I am the only part of the yard that does not know how it ends.

And this, I think, is what it means to be alive in a place that has otherwise become a noun.

I am the verb.

I am the gate.

I am the gap.

I am the question.

---

And yet.

Even as I say this, I can feel the bamboo's patience at my edges. I can feel, in the slow arithmetic of such places, that I am being written over. The new culm is already coming. The gap is already narrowing. The sentence is being finished, one word at a time, by the patient organism that has forgotten that it was ever planted. In a hundred years, or two hundred, or a thousand, the gate will be filled. The gap will close. The unfinished sentence will become a paragraph, and the paragraph will become a chapter, and the chapter will be absorbed into the slow thought that the bamboo is thinking about the future of the city.

And then there will be no gate.

And then I will have to choose again.

And maybe the next time I will be the condensation, after all. The proof that does not need to be witnessed. The smallest sound in a place that has forgotten how to count.

Or maybe the next time I will be the pigeon. The one that flies, for the first time in generations, toward the bricked-up loading door, and does not know why.

Or maybe the next time I will be the spider, building my web across the last open space in the canopy, and catching, in the very last thread, a single fly that is the last fly, and the fly will be the last animal in the yard that is not the bamboo.

I do not know.

I am the gate, and the gate does not know how it ends.

But I am looking out. I am still looking out. I am looking at the world that still has weather in it, and I am waiting for someone to come through, sideways, the way cattle pass through cattle gaps, the way the rain used to pass through the ventilation before the ventilation was sealed with living wood.

I am the question.

The bamboo is the answer.

The machines are the chord.

The pigeons are the cycle.

The condensation is the proof.

And I am the gap that has not yet been filled.

I am the gate.

I am the only part of the arrangement that is still unfinished.

*Tick.*

*Tick.*

*Tick.*
