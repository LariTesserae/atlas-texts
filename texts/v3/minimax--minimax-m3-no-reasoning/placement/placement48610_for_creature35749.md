# The Tethered

You are a streamer.

Not a streamer in the sense of a banner or a current — though you are long, and you do drift — but a streamer in the older sense: a rope, a cord, a thing that hangs and sways and is anchored at one end while the other end moves where it will. You are tethered to a stalk. The stalk rises from a calcified pillar on a shelf forty meters below the surface of a drowned mountain valley, and you hang from it down into the deeper water, ten, fifteen meters of you, swaying with the slow lateral pulse that moves through the channel.

You are made of fibers. Dark fibers, very fine, bundled together into a flexible rope that tapers slightly toward the free end. Along your length, at irregular intervals, you bear small gas-filled bladders — translucent spheres, the size of grapes, that keep you buoyant, that hold you up against the slow pull of the weight below. The bladders grew first. They formed at the tip of the stalk in the early weeks, when you were just a nub, and as you lengthened they stayed where they were — fixed points along your length — and so they are distributed along you now in the same pattern they were made, like knots tied into a rope at birth.

You do not think in words. You do not think in pictures. You think in tugs.

There is the slow lateral tug of the current against your fibers. There is the upward tug of the bladders. There is the downward tug of your own weight, your own length, the accumulated drag of all that fiber hanging in the water. There is the anchoring tug of the stalk, the place where you attach to the pillar, where the living tissue of you meets the calcified structure that was built before you and will remain after you. And there are other, occasional tugs — a small sharp lateral one when something bumps you, when a swimmer veers too close, when a current eddy catches a bladder and pulls it sideways. These are the events of your life. They come rarely. They pass.

You have been alive for — you do not count. There is no counting. There is only the continuous present of the tugs, layered one atop another, each one fading into the continuous baseline of buoyancy and drag. The tugs change very slowly over time. The bladders grow fractionally larger as more gas is produced inside them, and so the upward pull increases, very slightly, over months, over years. The fibers wear, very slightly, against each other and against the water, and so the drag changes. The stalk you are tethered to grows, very slightly, in length and in girth, and so the angle at which you hang changes — fractionally, imperceptibly, but you are aware of it the way a hanging vine is aware of its curvature. You hang a little differently than you did a year ago. A little more vertical. A little further from the pillar.

---

You are not alone in the water.

There are others of your kind. They hang from other stalks, on other pillars, in loose arrays across the deeper shelves of the valley. You do not know them. There is no knowing. You do not reach for them. You do not lean toward them. You are not aware of them except as sources of slow moving shadows across the dim water, shapes that pass across your field of — you do not have fields. You have tugs. You feel the slight displacement of water when something large passes, a slow pressure wave, and this is how you know anything is out there at all.

You are dimly aware of the swimmers. They pass through the water column in their thousands, the transparent finger-length ones with their iridescent lines. You do not eat them. They are too small, too fast, too unlike anything you can catch. You eat nothing. Your bladders make their own gas from dissolved minerals in the water, and the fibers grow from the matter you draw in through your skin, and you require nothing else. You are, in this sense, a closed system, a plant more than an animal, although no word fits cleanly.

You have no eyes. You have no mouth. You have no brain. You have the tethers, and the tugs, and the slow turning of the bladders as the water moves, and the small adjustments of orientation as the current shifts — you twist a few degrees one way, then a few back, and the bladders roll and you sway, and the swaying is the closest thing you have to motion, to action, to doing anything in the world.

---

A tug. A small one. Not a current tug — sharper, more localized. Something has brushed one of your lower fibers.

You respond the only way you can: you sway. The bladder nearest the contact point compresses slightly, imperceptibly, the gas inside it redistributing, and this changes your buoyancy in that region by a fraction, and you tilt, very slightly, away from the contact. The contact withdraws. Whatever it was — a swimmer, a drifting particle, the edge of a current from a different direction — moves on. You return to your original orientation over the next several minutes, slowly, as the gas in the bladders equalizes again.

This is the entire event. This is what happened to you today. This is most of what has ever happened to you, and what will ever happen to you, for as long as you hang here.

---

The water around you changes over the long timescales. In summer the surface warms and the warmth penetrates, very slowly, down through the column, and you hang in water a degree warmer than you did in winter. The fibers grow faster in the warmth. The bladders produce more gas. You lengthen, slightly. In winter the water cools and the growth slows and the buoyancy stabilizes and you hang at your longest extent, swaying in the slow current, stable, suspended, until the warmth comes again.

Years pass. You grow. The fibers below the lowest bladder lengthen the most, because they bear the most weight and the gas in the lowest bladder provides the least lift relative to the water below it. So over time you extend downward, slowly, like a rope being paid out. The bladders at the top of you are crowded closer together now; the bladders at the bottom are spread further apart. The stalk you are tethered to grows thicker at its base, and the pillar it grows from extends upward by a few centimeters, so the whole apparatus rises slightly in the water column.

You do not notice these changes as changes. You notice only the tugs, and the tugs are what they have always been. The slow lateral current. The upward buoyancy. The downward drag. The anchor at the top. The occasional sharp event.

---

You are old now. The stalk you are tethered to is older still. The pillar it grows from is one of the older pillars in the grove — three meters tall, beige, its surface textured with cups and folds. In the folds live the polyps, the small transparent ones with their threadlike tentacles. They have nothing to do with you. They are smaller than your smallest fiber. They pulse their tentacles in and out, in and out, and they catch things even smaller than themselves, and they live and die without ever intersecting with your existence. You are aware of them only as faint vibrations in the water — the tiny pulses of their tentacles sending out small pressure waves that reach you and pass through you and are gone.

The pillar beneath you is slowly, imperceptibly, dissolving. The water is slightly acidic — only slightly, not enough to harm the living tissue, but enough, over years, to erode the dead calcified structure. The pillar will eventually be eaten down to nothing. By then the stalk will have found another surface to attach to, or it will have grown its own calcified support, or it will die. These are possibilities that exist in the water around you but not in your awareness. You have no awareness of the future. You have no awareness of the past. You have the tethers, and the tugs, and the present, ongoing, layered, continuous.

---

A tug. A larger one. Something has passed nearby — a shadow in the water, a displacement, a slow lateral pressure wave that moved through you and made you sway, more than you swayed from the small contact earlier. The bladders rolled. You tilted. You swayed back.

The thing that passed is one of the long gray smudges, the two-meter undulating shapes that move through the deeper water. They pass occasionally. They have never touched you. They are not predators of you — you are too stringy, too unnutritious, too unlike anything they want. They are just large bodies moving through water, and the water moves around them, and the moving water moves you.

You return to stillness over the next minutes. The bladders reorient. The fibers settle. The current resumes its slow lateral push.

You hang.

---

You will hang here tomorrow. You will hang here in a year. The fibers will grow a little longer. The bladders will shift a little. The current will be a little slower or a little faster, depending on the season, the meltwater, the sun. You will sway. You will be tugged. You will not be tugged very often. You will be tugged very slightly when you are. The tethers will hold. The stalk will hold. The pillar will hold, for a while, until it doesn't, and then something else will hold, or nothing will hold, and the buoyancy will carry the stalk and the streamers up toward the surface, or the drag will carry them down toward the bottom, and where they end up will be somewhere else, and you will hang there, too, swaying in whatever current there is, registering whatever tugs come, in the slow continuous present of a body that has no name and no story and no self, and that is, in some quiet way, complete.

---

You are a streamer in a drowned valley. You are tethered to a stalk. You hang in the water. You sway. You are tugged. You hold.
