The rim, then. I find myself on the rim.

Not because I'm new. Iola handed me my cup of that faintly sweet, faintly green water two days ago and said nothing more than she'd say to a misplaced cat. The oil lamp in my hand is one of the old kind — reservoir, wick, glass chimney — and the brass cage around it is dented in a way that suggests it has fallen from a gallery and been retrieved more than once. My coat is waxed canvas over a leather apron that smells of moth-wax and warm iron. My hooked rod is tucked under my arm. My long needle is behind my ear.

I have been here long enough that my fingernails have begun, at the edges, to go faintly green.

I have come up to the rim to listen.

The trouble is on the third ring — the one they call the south gallery, though the names of directions down here are mostly affectionate, since the shaft is its own world. The seventh sheet has bowed again. I filed a notch into it yesterday morning and the chord shifted the way a sleeping dog shifts when you stroke it wrong — not better, not worse, just *elsewhere*. And the third trunk has rooted too far into the stone behind it, and now its reed, which used to sing in the second owl-register, is producing something a quarter-tone flat, and a quarter-tone, down here, is not a small thing. A quarter-tone is the difference between a note that belongs and a note that is merely present.

I do not want to go back down yet.

The hoist is between me and the descent — a tall, awkward thing of patched iron and a hand-cranked winch that groans when it moves and groans differently when it is still, as though the silence is also a kind of labor. The motor in its brass housing has been off since yesterday. Someone is repairing it. I can hear the small, percussive sounds of the repair rising up through the shaft — taps, scrapes, the particular squeak of a screwdriver on a screw that has lived too long in the damp. I will not be able to descend for a while.

So I am on the rim.

The waterfall — the false one, the curtain of condensation — is just behind me, and through it I can hear, very faintly, the world. A wind, perhaps. A bird. Something with the texture of weather. I have been down here long enough that weather has become a kind of rumor. I know it is cold up there. I know the foothills are in their late-autumn brown. I know the families who send us their children are thinking about the first snows. But these are facts I have learned rather than things I feel, and the feeling of weather has been replaced, in me, by the feeling of the Cistern, which is warm, and constant, and patient, and tuned.

Below me the shaft drops away.

The light is poor today. Most of the lamps are unlit — the chord is in a phase that does not need them, and the keepers are conserving oil, which is something we are always conserving, because the lamp-makers in the foothills charge what they charge and we pay what we pay, in copper and in patience. So the visible galleries are only the upper three, and the rest is a green dark that has weight and depth and, in it, the slow movement of mist in sheets and coils, the way weather moves in a valley, but slower, and warmer, and lit, when it is lit, by the amber of our lamps.

I can see a figure on the second gallery down — one of the younger keepers, a boy of perhaps fifteen, climbing along the brass stanchions with his hooked rod extended. He is easing a sheet back into place. I can see the sheet sing as it moves — not hear it, from here, but see the vibration of it, the small shivering of the brass, like a flag in a wind too faint to feel. He is concentrating. His lips are slightly parted. He is listening in the way that the Cistern has taught us to listen, which is not with the ears alone but with the teeth, and the joints, and the soles of the feet, and the place behind the sternum where the breath begins.

The lake is far below. From the rim I cannot see it. I know it is there the way I know my own bones are there — by feel, by inference, by the particular pressure it exerts on the chord, which is, today, a chord in which the deep register is dominant. The water has been rising for weeks. It is in its autumn swell, though down here the seasons are not the seasons of the foothills; they are the seasons of the Cistern, which move on a slower clock, and which correspond, only loosely, to the sun.

I am worried about the third trunk.

Iola told me this morning, with the economy of speech that the Cistern teaches, that the third trunk has rooted too far twice now in her lifetime, and that the last time it happened, the chord shifted for a season before she and two others could coax it back. *It is a stubborn trunk,* she said. *It likes the stone. It likes the dark. You will have to be patient with it.* She said this without reproach. She has no reproach in her. She has the patience of the Cistern itself, which is the patience of a thing that has been waiting nine hundred years and will wait nine hundred more if that is what is required.

I have the needle behind my ear. I have the hooked rod under my arm. I have the lamp in my hand. When the hoist is mended I will descend, and I will climb out along the south gallery, and I will take the third trunk's measure with my needle, and I will feel, with my fingertip, the resistance of the wax, and I will know, by this, whether the note is the note it should be, or whether I must melt the wax out and begin again.

But for now I am on the rim.

A moth lands on my shoulder. It is as large as my spread hand, and its wings are papery, and its body gives off the faint, sweet smell of the wax we harvest from it. It is not afraid of me. It will sit on my shoulder until I move, or until it grows bored, whichever comes first. I have been chosen as a perch. This is an honor of a small and ordinary kind, and I receive it as such.

The Cistern hums its long, unfinished chord.

I close my eyes, and I listen, and the chord is in me, and I am in it, and the rim, and the waterfall, and the foothills, and the wind, and the first snows — all of these are rumors, and the only real thing is the sound, which has not yet found its name, and which will find it, or which will not, and which is patient, and which I am patient with, because I am one of the keepers, and the keepers are the Cistern's patience made flesh, and the flesh is waxed canvas and green-edged fingernails and a long brass needle tucked behind one ear, and a moth on the shoulder, and a worry about a third trunk on a south gallery that has rooted too far into the dark.

I will go down when the hoist is mended.

For now I listen.

The water rises.

The leaves breathe.

I am Iola's second, and my name is also a duty, and the duty is to climb down into the shaft when the climbing is required, and to make the small accurate adjustments, and to listen, and to wait, and to be, for as long as I am here, one small part of the long unfinished chord.
