If you stay on the slate shelf long enough for the dry cold to stiffen your knees, watching the dark bowls below, you begin to notice a flaw in the randomness of the valley.

At first, the forty-foot spire-condensers seem to discharge like weather—each one building static from the thin upper wind until it reaches capacity and snaps a silent, blinding-blue corona into the gloom. But the longer you watch, the less it looks like weather. A spire on the far western terrace flashes; three slow heartbeats later, two answer in the central basin. A long pause follows, and then a deliberate ripple of five blue ticks races along the northern ridge, skipping every other spire.

Something down there is holding the charges back. Something is letting them go.

Look closely at the base of the nearest spire, where the braided carbon-reeds grow waist-deep. A patch of the matte-black carpet detaches itself from the taproot and unfolds.

It is tall only when it uncurls from its crouch—long-limbed and multi-jointed, built low-slung enough to hug the damp hollows where the heavier mist pools, yet capable of stretching upward to tend the lower plates of the spires. At a glance, you would swear it was just another growth of the valley floor. Its hide is the same matte-charcoal, ribbed and rubbery like heavy insulation, and across its shoulders and the crown of its head grows a thick, velvety mantle of cultivated ferro-lichen that swallows the starlight. Even its spine is camouflaged: a ridged row of tarnished, biological copper plates, dull as old pennies, running from the base of its skull to its pelvis.

There are no nostrils on its face, and no outer ears. In air this thin, nostrils would freeze dry in an hour and ears are useless for anything except barometric ache. Instead, its head is dominated by two large, lidless eyes covered in thick, smoky-clear domes—built to drink the faint starlight without being blinded by the sudden arc-flashes of the spires—and a broad, heavy, strangely flattened lower jaw.

That jaw is how it hears. And the basin floor is how it breathes.

Watch it move through the reeds on wide, padded feet that make no more sound than the grazers. It does not pant in the near-vacuum. Instead, it kneels beside one of the muffled piston-vents, where the fungal gaskets choke the geothermal steam. Extending a pleated, fleshy collar from beneath its jaw, it presses its face directly against a warm fissure in the fungus and *drinks*—a slow, measured, rhythmic inhalation of warm, mineral-heavy vapor. Its ribbed thoracic sacs swell like dark bellows, extracting oxygen from the steam and binding trace sulfur into its bloodstream. Then it seals the collar shut, stands, and walks away. On that single warm draft and the slow trickle of current in its tissues, it can work in the freezing gloom for hours.

They are not builders. They have never cut a stone, drawn a wire, or laid a road, because the valley is already an engine, and they are simply its living switches.

Follow one down to the oily-black catch-basins where the refiner-sponges hum in silence. It kneels at the water’s edge and gathers the waxy, crystalline ribbons of pure sulfur and copper-salt extruded by the acid-kelps. Some of the sulfur it chews slowly, feeding the layered chemical batteries in its gut. The soft, pure copper ribbons it carries in the crook of its long arm, walking out onto the bare basalt saddles between the basins. There, it lays the unworked metal flat into the natural meltwater grooves of the rock—not a road, not a boundary, just a temporary bridge to bleed excess voltage from an overcharged basin into a starved one. Over a season, the wind and frost will oxidize and scatter the ribbon; the tender will simply lay another.

They do not hunt the fauna, either, though the six-limbed predators give them a wide berth. A tender knows how to ground a patch of wet sedge with the heel of its palm, sending a sharp, localized pulse through the roots that leaves a stalking predator skittering backward into the dark, its copper-tipped mandibles tingling. With the blind, hound-sized grazers, they are gentler. A tender will step in front of a grazing beast, crouch, and touch two fingers—tipped not with nails, but with dark, dense calluses of naturally deposited graphite and copper-sulfide—to the pale plates along the grazer’s back. The tender siphons off a fraction of the beast’s stored electrical charge, a quiet meal of raw current, leaving the grazer lighter on its rubbery feet and drowsily content.

Sometimes, two of them meet on a narrow terrace of dark slate.

There is no greeting in the dead air. No shout, no whisper. They kneel opposite one another and lean forward until their broad, flattened jawbones press flush together—or they place their bare palms flat against the same slab of wet rock, a few inches apart. Through bone and basalt, they speak. To your ear pressed against the slate above, it would register only as a sudden, intricate tightening in the earth’s hum: rapid trains of bone-conducted clicks, rhythmic micro-currents, and faint, modulated buzzes, like a pair of telegraph keys chattering beneath the frost. When they part, a tiny blue spark—no brighter than a struck flint—sometimes snaps between their knuckles as their charges equalize.

Across the wider miles of the amphitheater, they use the spires. By wrapping damp, conductive kelp-ribbons around a forty-foot taproot, or pressing their copper-calloused palms against the woody graphite fiber to bleed voltage into the subsoil, a tender can delay a spire’s discharge by minutes, or coax it to snap early. What looks from the shelf like distant lightning is a slow, majestic conversation—news of a clogged piston-vent, a rich bloom of acid-kelp, a shifting earth-current, passing from basin to basin in silent, blinding-blue flashes beneath the stars.

And when the work is done, or when the upper winds grow too thin and cold even for their insulated hides, they do not retreat to caves or huts. They find a warm hollow beside a rhythmic piston-vent, curl down into the waist-deep mats, and pull living, braided stalks of hollow-stemmed sedge over their backs like heavy blankets. Resting their jaws against the warm basalt, they let their dorsal copper plates trickle-charge from the earth-currents, sleeping inside the hum of the world.

From the slate shelf, if you didn't know to watch the timing of the blue coronas, you could stare down into that vast, hollow dark for hours and never know anyone was there. You would see only the black carpets, the mist, the drifting bladders, and the unbuilt machines of root and stone—never realizing that the valley itself is a limb, and the quiet things curled in the sedge are the nerves that move it.
