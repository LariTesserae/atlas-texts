At first, even if you stood on the western sill for an hour, you would swear nothing larger than a falcon lives here. The chamber is too loud, too bright and wet, and the eye is drawn to the frantic things: the swifts exploding out of the stone, the marmots barking on their mossy shelves, the sheer forty-foot drop into empty air.

Even when the main gale drops for a few heartbeats and you hear that low, residual hum still trembling in the floor, you would blame the gneiss.

You would only begin to notice it during one of the brief, dazzling squalls of sun, when the weeping walls turn to silver and the mist steams off the turf. Down in the widest of the saucer-shaped basins, where the runoff pools dark and cold, one of the rounded stones does not dry at the crown when the light hits it. Its upper surface is too smooth, slicked dark as wet slate, yet etched with pale, chalky lines where mineral water has followed the same channels down its flanks for years. Silver-green beard lichen grows in thick tufts along its shoulders and the ridge of its spine, streaming horizontally in the draft just like the lichen on the ribs above.

It is the size of a mountain sheep, though flatter, built to hug the curve of the rock. When the sun warms the basin, its hide gives off a faint, mineral steam. A pair of swifts might drop down to drink from the rim of its pool, fluttering within inches of its arched back; it does not twitch. Only if you watch the water around its ribs do you see the slow, tidal rise and fall—not the quick pant of a warm-blooded beast, but a long, deliberate drawing in and letting out that takes half a minute at a time.

It is an animal of the organ pipe, and it sleeps in tune with the stone.

When the next cloud-bank slams into the peak and plunges the chamber into swirling grey, the swifts vanish into their pockets and the marmots bolt underground. That is when it wakes.

Its head lifts first from the basin—broad, blunt-muzzled, and curiously light, with small, milky eyes shielded behind thick, translucent lids against the grit-laden wind. Along the sides of its long neck and down the curve of its ribs run rows of slit-like, horn-rimmed vents, each one opening into hollow chambers within its skull and chest. As it rises from the pool, water spills cleanly from those vents in bright, erratic threads, draining down its flanks until the hollow spaces inside it are tuned to the air.

It does not walk so much as *flow* up the forty-foot vertical wall. Its four limbs are long, flat-jointed, and splayed, ending in broad, leathery pads and blunt dark nails worn smooth by gneiss. It keeps three points pressed to the slick stone against the blast, moving with a slow, heavy patience—until it reaches a rib where the updraft strikes hardest. There, it loosens the pleated folds of skin tucked tight along its flanks. The thin membranes catch the roaring air like half-furled sails, taking half its weight off the rock. Buoyed by the gale, it glides sideways across the sheer face, its pads barely kissing the wet moss, its trailing beard lichen snapping in the wind.

It feeds in the grey hours, when the falcons cannot see to stoop. It skims the saucer-basins for drowned cloud-flies and the pale, soft-bodied grubs that wash out of the soggy moss. Where the fleshy rosette plants cling to the sunlit cracks, it crops the swollen, bladder-like leaves before they can warm and pop, crushing them against the roof of its mouth to drink the trapped mist-water. Occasionally, if a sudden gust dashes a swift against the wet stone, it will take the bird whole, though it never hunts them; it is too slow for the air, and too indifferent to chase anything that can fly.

Mostly, it tends the sound.

As the western gale funnels through the arch and roars up toward the oculus, the creature climbs to the ribs just below the crown and opens its neck-vents to the blast. It does not use its lungs. It simply lets the mountain’s breath pass through its hollow chambers, adding a low, woody, shifting overtone to the deep bass thrum of the stone. When the wind gusts sharply through the upper crevices, it subtly shifts the angle of its jaw or draws water up from a cheek-pouch to narrow a vent, bending the whistle into a soft, wet flute-note. That is why the chamber never truly falls silent when the gale pauses: for several heartbeats after the wind drops, the creature’s hollow chest keeps vibrating, holding the note like a struck bell until the next gust arrives to feed it.

It knows the old human marks on the southern wall, though it has no use for them. It avoids the sheltered niche twenty feet up; even washed pale by decades of damp, the charred juniper hearth smells faintly of dry smoke, and the creature dislikes dryness. Sometimes, climbing past the leeward curve, it will hook one smooth-nailed hand around the lowest rung of the mountain-ash ladder, testing the blackened sinew and hammered copper pins before moving on. The dry-stone windbreak below is merely a place where the wind eddies strangely—a dead spot in the music that it skirts around.

But it knows the eastern arch.

Whenever the weather turns and the wind shifts to the northeast, blowing backward against the usual grain of the peak, the great bass organ-note of the chamber breaks apart into flat, choppy gusts. The creature grows restless then, pacing the upper ledges, its vents clicking softly as the pressure drops. High in the eastern arch, the frayed bindings of the old horn and bird-bone tubes catch the northeast draft, clattering against the gneiss and sending their thin, reedy chorus out into the empty sky.

At that sound, the creature climbs straight up the eastern ribs. It anchors its splayed feet in the moss just below the gap, presses its wet, slate-colored flank against the stone, and tilts its head toward the clattering bones. There, in the swirling cloud, it opens its vents and answers the thin reed-notes with a low, steady, resonant hum—keeping the mountain’s voice whole until the wind turns west again.
