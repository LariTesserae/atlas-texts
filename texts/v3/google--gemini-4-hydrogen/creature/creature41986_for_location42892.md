At first, the basin seems empty of anything larger than a pigeon. The amber glare of the work-lamps flattens the shadows, the drifting steam breaks up every silhouette, and the roar of the pumps swallows the sound of footsteps. It takes a long moment of watching the wet aprons and the elevated catwalks to realize that the people who live here are moving through them, keeping to the same warm seams as the geckos and the crabs.

There are never many of them—perhaps a dozen or two, spread thin across the three blocks—and they have been shaped by the basin as thoroughly as the pale moss clinging to the concrete.

Because sweat cannot evaporate here, they wear almost nothing. Most go in loose, salt-stiffened shorts or aprons of salvaged oilcloth, their skin bare and perpetually slick. Under the twenty-four-hour sodium lamps, they have the pale, slightly tallowy look of things raised out of the sun, though their shoulders and chests are mottled pink from the relentless heat. They keep their heads shaved or cropped to the scalp; long hair traps the brackish condensation and breeds rot. On their feet, they wear heavy, improvised boots—soles cut from thick rubber conveyor belting and lashed to their ankles with copper wire or synthetic twine—because the unweathered basalt is razor-sharp, and a sliced heel in ankle-deep mineral water will never heal.

Moisture is the quiet enemy of their bodies. If they stay down in the steaming yards too long, their skin softens, turns white at the creases, and begins to slough. Every few hours, they climb up to what they call the dry-perches: narrow steel ledges tucked right beside the transformer housings or just below the exhaust vents of the condenser bank, where the radiant heat is fierce enough to drive the dampness out of the air. They sit there in pairs, knees drawn up, scraping the softened dead skin from their palms and ankles with flat chips of basalt, then rubbing their joints and feet with mineral grease or rendered bird-fat to seal their skin before heading back down into the wet.

They rarely speak aloud. Trying to shout over the fixed pitch of the piston pumps and the cooling fans ruins the throat in a matter of days. Instead, they communicate with wide, unhurried hand-signs that cut cleanly through the murky air, or by tapping wrenches against the steel railings and insulated conduits. The pipework carries sound better than the air does; a sharp, rhythmic *tink-tink-pause-tink* struck on a brine line in the pumping station can be felt through the soles of a boot on the far side of the condenser bank. When two of them need to say something too complex for fingers or steel, they step close, press their hands to each other’s ribs or shoulders, and speak in low, chesty hums that travel through bone rather than air.

They live off the basin’s narrow ecology, acting as both its scavengers and its gardeners. In the wide, shallow settling pans between the buildings, they skim the dark mats of algae with fine-mesh sieves, pressing the wet pulp into flat cakes and baking them dry on the hot iron casings of the steam turbines. They set wire-mesh traps along the sedge-lined drainage channels to catch the small sluice-crabs, roasting them whole on the cylinder heads of the pumps until the shells crack. Up in the high, dry rafters of the condenser trusses, they move barefoot and quiet along the ironwork—where the steel is worn smooth and free of sharp stone—to harvest eggs and squabs from the dense rows of pigeon and swift nests, always taking just enough that the colonies never abandon the roof.

Without morning or evening, they have no shared schedule. They do not use clocks; they sleep when their eyes burn and their limbs grow heavy, and they wake when the plant’s vibration shifts or their stomachs turn. Their sleeping quarters are tucked into the upper stories of the stacked workshop block, where the windows have been knocked out to let the air move. Inside, hammocks of braided cable-sheathing and nylon mesh are slung directly in the wash of the big wall-mounted cooling fans. The fans do not cool them, but the heavy, churning draught keeps the midges off their faces and pushes the wet heat across their skin until it feels almost like a breeze.

They are not prisoners of the machinery, nor are they entirely its masters. They are simply the creatures that tend it. They pack the leaking valve-glands with oiled hemp, clear the sedge from the sump grates, and replace the blown bulbs in the caged lamps so the midges have somewhere to gather and the geckos have somewhere to hunt. They know the basin’s single, unvarying note so deeply that they do not even hear it anymore—they only notice if it falters, feeling a missed beat in the pumps through the floorboards long before a gauge ever twitches. In the flat, amber twilight beneath the steam lid, they move from block to block at a slow, heat-conserving walk, glistening with grease and condensation, as settled into the rock and iron as the moss in the cracks.
