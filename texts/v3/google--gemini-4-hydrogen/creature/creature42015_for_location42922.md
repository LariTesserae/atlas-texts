At first, if you stepped through the rim of interwoven willow and alder, you would not see it at all. The flat, shadowless daylight and the relentless roar of the basin flatten everything into a single vibrating plane, and the creature’s pale, slick hide matches the silvered lichen and the wet, peeled alder-wood so closely that it reads as just another bulge of root or a tarp draped over the machinery.

It spends most of its hours pressed tight against the cast-iron housings of the three pumping assemblies. In the cellar-like chill of the hollow, the exposed induction motors and humming transformer coils are the only sources of warmth. The creature drapes its long, heavy torso across the oily iron, its broad, hairless belly flattened against the metal to drink in the heat, its chin resting on the warm copper-humming casings. Over years without number, its skin—thick, damp, and the color of leached tallow—has taken on the same dull, greasy patina as the pump plates, smudged along the shoulders and ribs with dark oil and feathered lightly at the ankles with clinging moss.

It is roughly the bulk of a large, crouched man, though its proportions belong entirely to the flat, springy floor. Its limbs are thick and low-centered, ending in wide, spatulate hands and broad, flat-soled feet with tough, leathery pads. When it moves across the clearing, it does not stride so much as slide and press, distributing its weight so evenly that it never tears the ancient sphagnum. It is this slow, unhurried pacing—century after century of heavy, flat-footed circuits between the rim and the pilings—that has worn the peat floor smooth and level as a threshing floor.

Its head is smooth, slightly flattened, and entirely without external ears. In an acoustic chamber this loud, outer ears would be useless; instead, wide, pale plates of bone and taut membrane sit flush along the sides of its skull and jaw. It hears the world through the soles of its feet, its palms, and the bone of its cheek pressed to the iron. To the creature, the deafening, overlapping roar—the sixty-hertz thrum of the transformers, the high metallic whine of the bearings, the steady *chug-hiss* of the centrifugal pumps—is not noise. It is a solid, reliable wall of vibration that holds the hollow in place. It is the closest thing to silence the basin knows.

Only a change in that chord can rouse it.

If a mat of decayed sedge drifts against an intake beneath the peat, or a drowned toad fouls one of the buried float-switches, the pitch of the machinery shifts by a fraction of a cycle—a faint flutter in the bearing whine, a slight stammer in the *chug-hiss*. To a human ear, the change would be lost beneath the din of the frogs and cicadas. To the creature, it is a sharp, intolerable grit against the bone of its skull. It slides down from the warm iron, drops to its knees on the wet moss, and thrusts a long, pale arm elbow-deep into the black, freezing seepage beneath the root-mat. Its slit nostrils pinch shut; its small, dark, heavily lidded eyes stare blankly into the flat daylight while its blunt, broad-nailed fingers feel blindly through the dark peat-seams until they find the clog and tear it free. As soon as the pump’s RPM settles back into its pure, unvarying hum, the creature withdraws its mud-blackened arm, wipes it along its flanks, and climbs back onto the iron.

Without knowing it—and certainly without caring about the distant, unseen grid that feeds the armored cables—it is the reason the hollow’s impossible balance holds.

It is also the reason the center stays empty. The creature is slow-metabolizing, suited to the cool air, and it grazes the clearing with methodical patience. Whenever a runner of ivy from the perimeter wall tries to creep out across the moss, or a willow root sends up a pale, ambitious sucker through the compressed peat, the creature shuffles over, pinches the tender shoot between its flat teeth, and chews it down to the root-mat. It crops the moss-runners that try to climb too high on the pump housings, leaving only a neat fringe along the base plates, and it rubs its heavy flanks along the black, armored power cables, keeping them free of encroaching lichen and polishing their rubber sheathing to a dull sheen.

Because there is no stone or mineral soil anywhere in the hollow, the creature suffers a quiet, perpetual hunger for salt and grit. It satisfies this by licking the iron-scale and the mineral-rich condensation that beads along the cold discharge hoses, and by scraping its tongue across the damp, rust-freckled bolts of the pilings. Its teeth are stained the dark reddish-brown of oxidized iron.

The teeming life of the margins knows its boundaries well. The swallows that dart through the high, thin canopy pay it no mind, and the thousands of frogs, crickets, and newts packed into the rotten heartwood of the perimeter trunks are safe so long as they stay in the crevices. The creature is too slow and cold-blooded to hunt the rim. Yet if a frog or a salamander wanders too far out onto the bare, open peat, or slips into the shallow condensation-pools around the timber pilings, a broad, wet hand drops onto it with sudden, heavy finality, pinning it flat before it is swallowed whole. The margins teem because they are untouched; the center is vacant because the center is its floor.

At night, when the flat daylight drains from the canopy and the air grows no colder than its perpetual cellar chill, the creature retreats to one of the five or six massive, hollow-hearted trunks that hold up the high web of branches—or else it simply stays where it is, draped over the humming transformer coils, eyes closed behind thick, translucent lids. The sixty-hertz current vibrates through its ribs, matching the slow, heavy beat of its own heart.

If you were to stand at the edge of the clearing and watch it, it would eventually notice you—not by the sound of your breathing, which the pumps swallow, but by the slight, unfamiliar tremor of your weight compressing the peat. It would lift its smooth, oil-smudged head from the iron, its ear-plates turning toward you to read the strange, soft rhythm of your pulse. It would not charge, nor would it flee into the alder walls. It would simply slide down from the pump and plant its broad, heavy bulk between you and the humming cables, waiting in the cool, deafening brightness with infinite, motionless patience until you stepped back into the trees and let the hollow return to its single, unbroken chord.
