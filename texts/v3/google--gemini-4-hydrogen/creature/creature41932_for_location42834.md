If you stand on the open avenues, you will never see her. The glare off the wet concrete is too flat, the distances too long and straight, and your eyes are drawn down to the dry, shivering carpet of crabs or out to the motionless white sentries of the herons in the bays.

To find what else lives here, you have to leave the avenues and follow the sound of the machines.

Inside the largest pump-house, behind a chain-link fence whose gate has been unbolted and re-hung with loops of copper wire, the air is even thicker than outside—thick with the smell of warm machine oil, ozone, and boiled silt. Here, the amber status lights are not merely surviving; their glass domes have been wiped clean with a thumb. Beside the humming transformer, a dented tin funnel is wired beneath a pneumatic valve, feeding a slow, clear trickle of condensed steam into a plastic jug. Every few minutes, when the valve vents its soft sigh, the jug shivers and takes on another spoonful of warm, flat, saltless water.

She lives in the margins the tide cannot reach and the crabs do not bother to claim: the upper tiers of the pipe-racks, the hollow sheet-metal housings of idle turbines, and the dark, submerged sumps beneath the sluice gates.

She is small, lean, and the color of the basin itself. Thirty years of pewter light and mineral damp have leached any sharp contrast from her skin, leaving it a pale, mottled grey-brown, stained at the shins and forearms with the same olive algae and rust-colored slime that borders the puddles. Her hair is hacked short with a pair of tin snips so it won't hold the sweat. She wears almost nothing—only a pair of cut-down canvas waders, stiff with salt and grease, and a webbing belt hung with a brass wrench, a short iron hook, and a bundle of salvaged copper wire. Her palms and the soles of her feet are callused to the thickness of horn from decades of gripping pitted I-beams and walking across spalled aggregate.

She does not walk the avenues. She knows, without ever having thought it through in words, that the open floor belongs to the crabs, and that to cross it in the glare would only stir a useless, clicking panic. Instead, she travels the basin by its skeleton—stepping lightly along the upper pipe-bridges, straddling the warm steam-lines, or moving through the shadowed service tunnels that run beneath the loading bays.

When she does enter the water, she moves at the pace of the herons.

At slack tide, when the canals sit brim-full and still, she slips down a rusted ladder into the warm, brackish dark of a pump-house intake. The water closes over her shoulders without a splash. She does not swim so much as walk along the submerged concrete sill, using the iron hook to feel along the bar-screens that keep the sea from choking the impellers. Her fingers work blind in the murk, pulling free mats of olive algae, shed crab carapaces, and the occasional drowned eel. A few feet away, a grey heron stands knee-deep on the submerged ramp; it turns one yellow eye toward the slow, algae-draped crown of her head breaking the surface, decides she is only another piece of shifting timber, and goes back to watching the killifish.

Even the crabs barely register her. When she climbs the wet concrete pilings, her wet skin smelling of iron, mud, and grease, the crabs wedged into the pipe-joints do not drop into the canals. They merely sidestep an inch or two, their stalks clicking softly against the steel, and close back in behind her heels.

She eats what the basin offers, taking no more than a large bird would: a handful of pale eels trapped in a wire basket hung beneath a weir, a few killifish scooped from a shrinking puddle when the tide drops, the sweet meat from the claws of the larger mud-grey crabs, cracked cleanly with the flat of her wrench. She leaves the shells on the edge of the canal, where the next tide washes them down into the silt. For water, she trusts only the pneumatic valves and the clean, high condensation that beads along the undersides of the highest corrugated roofs, far above the salt spray.

Her whole life is tuned to a vibration too low for most ears to catch. She feels it in the soles of her feet and the enamel of her teeth—the steady, sixty-cycle hum of the transformers, the deep, rhythmic thrum of the main dewatering pumps kicking on as the tide rises through the grates. If a bearing begins to run dry in the north pump-house, she feels the change in the tremor of the catwalk half a mile away. She will spend the next afternoon crouched in the dim amber glow, packing heavy grease into a worm-gear or scraping salt-crust off a relay with the point of her hook, keeping the razor-thin margin between the concrete and the sea intact.

She does not do it out of duty, or memory, or hope that anyone will return to thank her. She does it because the pump-house is her shell, just as the flanged joints belong to the crabs and the warm turbine casings belong to the geckos. If the pumps stop, the sea takes the basin, and there is nowhere dry left to sleep.

At the end of the day—marked not by sunset, for the pewter sky only deepens slowly into a bruised charcoal, but by the sudden, leathery rustle of the bats waking in the cooling towers overhead—she climbs up into the gutted housing of an elevated gantry crane. There, thirty feet above the wet asphalt, the air is a fraction drier, warmed slightly by the rising heat of the transformers below. She stretches out on a pallet of folded conveyor-belting, listening to the basin breathe: the *tick-tick-tick* of ten thousand legs on stone, the steady drip of condensation into warm puddles, the distant groan of a sluice gate adjusting to the dark tide.

Sometimes, in the middle of the night, a few of the smaller olive crabs climb the crane's grease-slicked chains and gather around her ankles, quietly picking the dead, water-softened skin from her heels. She does not brush them away. In her sleep, her breathing matches the slow, wet sigh of the valves.
