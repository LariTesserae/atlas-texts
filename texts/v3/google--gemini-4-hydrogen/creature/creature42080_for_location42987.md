If you stand in one of the narrow chutes long enough for the roar to flatten into a wall of pressure, you begin to notice that the stone is not entirely consistent. Every few minutes, if you press your palm hard against a wet calcite fin, the vibration beneath your skin hiccups—a brief, sharp deadening in the hum, as if a hand had been laid across the mouth of an organ pipe three alleys over.

Something is wedged in the flutes.

They are easy to miss, even at arm’s length. In the fierce, bouncing whiteness of the alleys, anything pale and wet disappears into the glare. Their skin is the color of leached travertine, mottled along the spine and ribs with a dull, greasy slate-gray—not camouflage, at first glance, but the same mineral-eating bacteria that stain the rock, thriving in the shallow creases of their hide where the runoff slows. To the touch, they are cold and slick as wet tallow, insulated beneath the skin by a dense, rubbery layer of fat that keeps their core just warm enough to function in the perpetual chill.

They are built for the width of the gaps, not the open sky. Their ribcages are narrow and deep, compressed side-to-side like a fish turned on edge, with long, double-jointed shoulders and hips that fold flat against their flanks. They rarely touch the slick chute floors. Instead, they move five or ten feet above the runoff, chimneying between opposite walls—pressing broad, flat-padded palms, knees, and heels against the wet stone. The skin on those pads is thick and scored with deep, branching channels that shed the water-film instantly, locking onto the smooth gypsum with a wet, leathery suction. They travel along the grain of the valley, sliding sideways through slots barely wider than a handspan, never turning, never hurrying.

Where you would look for eyes, there is almost nothing to see. In a world without shade, open eyes would burn out in an hour; theirs are sealed behind thick, milky membranes that never retract, admitting only the faintest distinction between the white glare of the sky-slit above and the dimmer, wet gray of the fused bases below. They have no external ears—an ear canal here would only bruise and bleed under the wind’s steady weight. Instead, the bones behind their jaws and along their collarbones are unusually dense and broad, fused close to the surface.

To them, the valley is not blinding, and it is not deafening. It is legible.

Every calcite blade in the miles-wide labyrinth has its own pitch, fixed by its height, its thickness, and the weight of the water sheeting down its face. When one of these dwellers pauses in a chute and presses a cheek or a palm to the stone, it feels the entire surrounding thicket humming through its teeth and sternum. It can feel the dull, muffled thrum of a leeward crevice where the wind drops out, or the slight, heavy drag on a fin where a cluster of blind limpets has grown thick enough to thicken the water-film. It can feel the sudden, sharp *blank* in the vibration when one of its own kind wedges itself into a gap fifty yards away.

That is how they speak—not by making noise, which the wind would swallow an inch from their mouths, but by stopping it. By pressing a flat chest or both palms hard against the thin, vibrating edge of a travertine fin, a dweller can choke off a single note in the valley’s roar. To another, braced against the same connected rib three alleys over, the sudden absence of that hum registers like a tap on the bone: a rhythm of crisp, deliberate silences carved out of the noise.

They live on what the stone gives up. Most of their hours are spent in the quietest, tightest seams where the fins fuse together, prying the waxy, thick-shelled limpets and pale arthropods from the weeping rock with broad, chisel-edged fingernails that grow back as fast as the stone wears them down. They scrape the greasy bacterial sheen from the slate-gray chutes with wide, rasping tongues, swallowing the mineral grit along with the slime.

And once in a great while, the sky sends down something richer.

Far overhead, the pale gliders ride the steady updrafts without beating a wing, their hollow bones light as dry reeds. Most never descend. But occasionally a downdraft clips a wingtip against the razor-thin crest of a high calcite tower, or an old bird simply folds in midair and drops like a white rag into the teeth below. It wedges ten or fifteen feet up in a narrow slot, trapped by its own wingspan, unable to open or fall.

The dwellers feel the impact in their teeth before the feathers settle—a brief, messy flutter in the standing wave of that fin. They converge slowly along the alleys, bracing upward through the wet chutes, and strip the bird down to its pale, hollow bones. Nothing goes to waste. They suck the marrow, swallow the tendons, and sometimes wedge the long, hollow wing-bones upright into the cracks of the leeward crevices—marking a rich limpet bed, or simply tuning a favorite fin to a slightly different hum.

When they sleep, they do not lie down. There is nowhere to lie down. They find a seam where two blades pinch together just tightly enough to hold their ribs, lock their knees and shoulders against the damp stone, and let the runoff sheet past their flanks. Their breathing slows until it nearly matches the unvarying trickle of the rock.

In the relentless glare and the unbreakable roar, they have no reason to hurry, and nothing to wait for. They are simply the part of the valley that moves slowly along its grain—pale, wet, and tuned to the stone, listening to the small silences they make inside the wind.
