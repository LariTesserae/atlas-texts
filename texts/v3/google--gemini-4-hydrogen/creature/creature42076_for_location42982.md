At first, there appears to be nothing larger than an eel in the valley. The eye—what little use it has in the bruised, murky grey—slides right over them. They look like just another rounded block of dark basalt, tumbled from a colonnade and wedged into the lee of a pillar.

Even the bacteria treat them like stone. The same pale, slick film that coats the sheltered flagstones grows across their broad, ridged backs, and the same chalky mineral crusts settle into the creases of their hides. Only when a freezing gust of current shifts, or a distant retaining wall gives way, do the boulders unclench.

They are heavy, low-slung things, built like wedges against the water’s violence. Roughly the length of a grown human, they are far broader across the shoulders and flat-bellied, tapering to a thick, muscular tail that ends in a blunt, paddle-like fluke. Four splayed limbs—short, massive, and calloused—extend from their sides, ending in broad pads and stubby, iron-hard claws worn down to the quick by ten thousand years of gripping slate. When they press themselves flat into the eroded seams of a plaza, their shovel-shaped heads tuck down into their shoulders, turning their entire bodies into smooth ramps that deflect the horizontal gales up and over their backs.

They have no use for sharp sight. Set deep beneath heavy brow-ridges are tiny, milky eyes that can tell light from dark, little more—enough to register the shadow of a passing shoal overhead, or the sudden brightening when a silt-cloud thins. Instead, they see with their ribs and their jaws. Pale, recessed grooves run along their flanks, their undersides, and the edges of their flat snouts, lined with pressure-sensitive membranes that read the water’s tiniest eddies. Through those grooves, the valley is not dim or empty at all; it is a sharp, roaring map of jets, vortices, and shear-zones, every sunken doorway and broken stair humming with its own distinct turbulence.

Because the open water is a freezing storm, they rarely swim above the plazas. They are crawlers and anchors, creatures of the floor. Moving against a current, they haul themselves forward one heavy limb at a time, hooking their blunt claws into the mortarless joints between flagstones, pausing to let a sudden upwelling pass before taking the next grip. When they want speed, they slip into the drowned aqueducts and sunken culverts, tucking their limbs tight to their sides and letting the accelerated jets shoot them through the dark like stones from a sling, flaring their tails at the last instant to catch the lee of a pylon.

They live off the sudden, scattered abundance the currents bring. In the quiet pockets behind fallen lintels, they work their blunt claws along the eroded seams, prying up the pale, eyeless crustaceans by the handful and crushing them—shells, grit, and all—between flat, stone-like plates at the back of their mouths. When a cloud of chalky silt erupts from the floor and swallows a colonnade, they hunt the silver-bellied fish. Blinded by the grit, the great shoals lose their tight unison and scatter against the pillars; the dwellers surge out of the grey just far enough to snap them from the churn before anchoring back down to the slate. 

And they watch the eels. The translucent, grey-white ribbons know every shift in the valley’s geometry before anything else. When a collapse blocks an old channel or opens a new seam in an embankment, the dwellers follow the faint wake of the eels to learn where the safe passages have moved.

Their sharpest sense, though, is reserved for the stone itself. 

In a world where water muffles sound to a heavy quiet, the vast, fitted plazas of slate act like a single continuous drumhead. When an ancient retaining wall finally dissolves and tumbles in slow motion a mile away, the dwellers feel the dull *thump* through their bellies long before the bloom of silt reaches them. Across the valley, dozens of dark, motionless shapes lift their heads at once. A collapse is a feast—a sudden release of trapped silt, displaced crustaceans, and fresh, un-scoured crevices—and they converge on the tremor from every sunken courtyard, crawling steadily up the rippled stair-ramps toward the sound.

They speak to one another the same way. They have no voices, and their armored gill-chambers—tucked behind heavy plates at the sides of their necks, flushing out swallowed silt in sharp, rhythmic coughs—make no sound that carries in the gale. Instead, they strike the floor. A dweller anchored in an amphitheater will lift a heavy forelimb and bring its calloused heel down against the slate in a slow, deliberate pattern. *Thud. Thud-thud.* The vibration travels straight through the fitted paving, skipping across empty plazas and down stepped causeways, felt in the sternum of another dweller half a mile away: a warning of a shifting boulder, a claim on a newly fallen wall, or simply the steady, cold-blooded announcement of presence in the dark.

The only places they ever truly relax their grip are the low, thick-walled cisterns. 

While the freezing currents scream across the open roofs above, the interiors of the cisterns hold pockets of near-stillness, their floors deep with settled, chalky mud. These are their nurseries and their graves. In the deepest corners, shielded from the undertow, females anchor clusters of large, leathery, translucent eggs to the damp lime-mortar, curling around them for months at a time and sweeping their broad tails in slow, hypnotic arcs to keep the settling silt from suffocating the brood. The hatchlings emerge pale and soft-shelled, no larger than a finger, spending their first years darting among the cistern walls like the eels before their hides thicken enough to brave the open plazas.

And when the oldest among them grow too slow—when their claws have worn down to useless nubs and the mineral crusts around their joints stiffen so they can no longer wedge themselves against the winter meltwater—they crawl back down into the cisterns one last time. They settle into the deep, cold silt at the bottom, pull their limbs tight beneath their chins, and let the quiet take them, until the pale bacteria coat their backs and they become, at last, indistinguishable from the stone they lived upon.
