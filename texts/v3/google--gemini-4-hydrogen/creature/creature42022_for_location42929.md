In the glare of the White Hollow, you almost never see them before they see you. Against thirty miles of shadowless, frost-crusted white, the eye expects emptiness, and so it skips over a figure crouching in the lee of a pale pith-ridge—until the figure shifts, and you realize that a patch of dark, soot-rubbed leather is moving across the ice.

They have no single name for themselves that translates cleanly, though outsiders who have glimpsed them and returned to the surface call them the **Pith-Walkers**, or simply the **Slit-Eyed**. They are human—or close enough to it that the difference is mostly what the Hollow has pared away. Generations of cold, thin rations, and constant vertical climbing have made them small, spare, and sinewy, with broad, calloused hands and the deep, barrel-chested lung capacity of mountaineers.

Everything about their appearance is an argument against the light.

Uncovered skin in the Hollow burns raw within hours under the actinic glare, blistering and peeling as if exposed to high-altitude sun. So the people go wrapped from crown to heel in layered hides of pale membrane-glider, stitched tight with bone needles and twisted plant-cordage. Because white leather vanishes against the snow-bright terrain—a fatal hazard if a climber slips into a mist-filled chasm—they rub their outer garments with dark, smoky ash scraped from their own pith-fires. From a distance, they look like smudges of charcoal drifting across a sheet of luminous paper.

Their faces are hidden behind close-fitting hoods and heavy, carved visors of blackened, hardened resin. Each visor has only a pair of hair-thin horizontal slits, no wider than a fingernail-paring. Through these, they see the world as a narrow, manageable strip, spared the blinding scatter of the sap-light. The visors give them an eerie, insectile stillness; when one turns to watch you from across a vascular ridge, there is no gleam of an eye, no flicker of expression—only a flat black mask tilted slightly to catch the sound of your boots.

### Living on the Edge of a Breaking World

To survive here is to live without a floor. The ground beneath a camp can swell fifty feet in a night, or shear away into the abyss before dawn. Because of this, the people of the Hollow have developed a kind of tactile literacy—a constant, unconscious reading of the plant-flesh beneath them.

They walk with long, light poles of hollow bryophyte-stem tipped with sharpened glider-bone, tapping the crust ahead of every step. A dull, woody *thump* means old, dry pith: safe enough for a night’s rest. A high, glassy *ping* means pressurized sap trapped just beneath a thin skin of frost, waiting for a boot-heel to split it open. Most telling of all is warmth. In a world that stays bitterly below freezing, any patch of moss that feels even faintly tepid through a resin-soled boot is dangerous; it means the chemical luminescence is surging, driving a new shoot upward from below. When the ground grows warm, you pack and move.

They travel in small, nimble bands—rarely more than six or eight together—because a larger group puts too much weight on brittle bridges and draws too much heat into one place. When they must sleep, they seek out the rare stretches of **Old Wood**: ancient, slow-growing root-cables as wide as rivers, whose bark has thickened into something nearly as hard and dead as timber. Even there, they do not trust the silence. Inside their low domes of stretched bladder-skin, they string a *tell-tale*—a taut line of plant-cordage anchored to three separate root-knuckles outside and tied to a small bone clapper suspended above the sleeper’s face. If the living ground shifts by an inch while they dream, the cord snaps tight or goes slack, and the clapper wakes them in time to cut their way out.

Nothing they own is heavy, and nothing is left behind. When a camp breaks, they pull up their bone stakes, coil their fibrous ropes, and even scrape the half-burned pith-coals into hollow, insulated stem-tubes to carry the embers alive to the next shelter.

### The Gifts of the Ceiling

A sharp eye examining their camps notices a puzzle right away: in a cavern with no stone, no gravel, and no exposed ore, they carry flint axes, stone knives, and hammered copper hooks.

They do not mine these things. They wait for them to fall.

Miles overhead, the ancient ceiling of permafrost-bound roots and frozen silt holds the locked-away debris of the surface world—boulders, river-cobbles, nodules of chert, and rare veins of native copper torn from the earth when the cavern was young. Every few years, a slow shift in the ice or a distant tremor shakes something loose. It falls for minutes through the vast, sunlit emptiness, screaming down through the spore-plumes until it punches through the fibrous canopy below with a thunderous *crack* that echoes for miles.

The Pith-Walkers know that sound. A "Roof-Fall" is the closest thing they have to a miracle, and bands will cross thirty miles of shifting chasm to reach the impact site before the fast-growing moss swallows the prize. A single fist-sized nodule of flint is flaked with excruciating care into blades that are passed down for generations. A lump of raw copper, beaten cold with stone hammers over a hardwood root, becomes a climbing hook, an awl, or a needle—treasured above almost anything else, lashed to the wrist with cordage so a slip on the ice cannot rob the band of it.

Where flint and copper are scarce, they use **bone**. The three-yard wings of the membrane-gliders yield long, hollow spars light enough for spear-shafts and tough enough for tent-stakes. And when one of their own dies, there is no earth to bury them in and no fuel to spare for a pyre. The body is weighted with hardened resin and lowered into the mist-filled depths—except for the long bones of the arms and legs, which are cleaned, polished with frost-sand, and carved into needles, toggles, and crampon-spurs. To sew a torn bladder-skin coat with an ancestor’s rib is not morbid to them; it is how the dead keep moving.

### Hunting the Quiet

Food in the White Hollow is scarce, pale, and mostly hidden beneath the skin of the world.

The Pith-Walkers do not hunt the membrane-gliders if they can avoid it; a glider’s meat is stringy, ammonia-sharp, and tainted by the chemical sap it drinks. Instead, they hunt the **borers**—the hare-sized, segmented creatures that chew slow tunnels through the warmer, sap-rich cores of the giant bryophytes.

A hunter will lie flat on the frost-rimed moss, pressing an ear against the bare plant-skin, listening past the creak of expanding fibers for the wet, rhythmic *rasp-rasp-rasp* of a borer’s jaws below. When they hear it, they drive a bone-tipped lance straight down through the crust, pinning the creature in its glowing burrow before it can retreat.

Borers are rich in a dense, pale fat that glows faintly even after death. Eaten seared over a smoky pith-fire, it tastes faintly of pine resin and copper, but it keeps the cold out of a climber’s marrow. Rendered down in cupped leaves, that same fat becomes a thick, waxy tallow that burns in hollowed bone lamps with a steady, cold blue-white flame—used not for light, which is already merciless, but for the tiny, precious pocket of heat it casts inside a sealed tent.

For water, they cannot rely on the glowing sap, which scours the throat and brings on a trembling sickness. Instead, they harvest the brackish, half-frozen pools in the hollows of giant cupped leaves, or slice open young, swollen bladder-sheets and hang them in the freezing air. As the sap freezes from the outside in, the bitter, luminescent chemicals concentrate in the slush at the center, leaving a rind of clear, sweet ice around the edges that they chip away and melt in their mouths.

### The Rest of the Hollow

The Pith-Walkers are not alone in the glare, though they move as if they wish they were.

The **membrane-gliders** drift above their camps like pale, silent kites, riding the cold thermal currents that rise from the abyss. They are mostly blind—their eyes shrunk to wet, scarred slits behind flaps of leathery skin—and they navigate by the faint heat of the sap-veins and the clicking echoes of their own throats. Usually they ignore the dark, soot-rubbed humans below, mistaking them for dead knots of bark. Only when a band’s fire is burning bright, sending up a thin ribbon of warmth, will a glider bank lazily downward, hoping for scorched borers or dropped scraps, its leathery wings whispering like torn paper in the freezing air.

And far below, in the misty depths where the Pith-Walkers never go—where the towering plumes of luminescent spores rise from unseen thermal vents—something else lives in the warm, wet dark. The climbers sometimes haul up ragged strips of shed skin caught on deep-hanging tendrils, or hear slow, heavy *slaps* echoing up from the abyss that are too rhythmic to be collapsing ice. They have no names for what dwells at the bottom of the Hollow. When a trail leads too close to a deep vent, they simply stack a tall cairn of amber resin beside the drop, click their tongues twice in warning, and climb back toward the blinding white.

### A Culture of Breath and Notches

Because the Hollow is so vast and silent, and because loud vibrations can snap brittle, frost-loaded arches overhead, the Pith-Walkers rarely shout. Around a camp, they speak in a low, clipped murmur—a language full of soft consonants and breathy clicks that carry only a few paces before the freezing air swallows them. Across chasms, they communicate with thin, high whistles blown through hollow borer-shells, sharp enough to pierce the mist without shaking the ice.

They keep no histories in books or carved walls; nothing stays standing long enough to hold a monument. Instead, their memory lives in **routes and marks**. A row of three slanted notches hacked into an Old Wood trunk means *water in the leaf-cups above*; a cairn of amber resin stacked four-high beside a fresh chasm means *this bridge fell yesterday—do not trust the crust*. Every band that passes adds a notch, or repairs a cairn, leaving behind a quiet trail of breadcrumbs for strangers they may never meet.

If you ever share a bladder-skin dome with them while the wind-mist hisses outside and the ground groans beneath your ribs, you will notice how little they sleep, and how often they pause to listen. They are not masters of this place. They do not farm it, or tame it, or dream of building walls against it. They are simply small, dark, patient things clinging to the underside of a frozen world—reading the creak of the fibers, hoarding fallen stones from a sky they have never seen, and moving on before the ground beneath them forgets their weight.
