At first glance, the city looks empty. In the bruised amber twilight, against walls the color of sun-bleached wasp-paper and pale pewter, the inhabitants do not stand out. They are built of the same dry vocabulary as the plateau itself: hollow, articulated, and astonishingly light.

They are tall—often seven or eight feet from heel to crest—yet a strong gust of wind could lift one off the ground if they were not careful. Their bodies are bundles of fluted, woody struts and overlapping plates of pale bark-chitin, joined not by wet muscle or glistening cartilage, but by braided plant-sinew and dry, self-lubricating sockets packed with fine graphite dust. Where they kneel on the felt-like floor, they leave behind faint, dark smudges of mineral powder.

They have no faces in the soft, fleshy sense. Their heads are narrow, swept-back husks, smooth as river-worn bone, studded along the brow with clusters of matte-black mineral lenses that drink in the dim light without blinking. Inside their ribbed, lantern-like chests, behind slats of translucent parchment, you can sometimes catch the pulse of a faint blue flicker—tiny triboelectric sparks snapping across pleated, paper-thin vanes. They do not breathe to wet their blood; they draw the thin, cold air through those vanes to keep an internal static charge alive, and to sift the drifting mineral dust that fuels them.

Because the plateau is deafening, they do not speak into the wind. A voice thrown into the open air would be shredded immediately by the groaning ground and the brassy hum of the towers. Instead, they speak through *resonance*.

Along their forearms and collarbones grow thin, curved ridges of dense lignin, notched like the frets of an instrument. When two citizens meet on a sagging transit-ribbon, they hook their curved, multi-jointed toes into the woven plant-chitin to anchor themselves, reach out, and press their wrists together—bone to bone, ridge to ridge—drawing a keratinous spur across the notches. The conversation passes directly through their frames as a crisp, rapid sequence of woody clicks and harmonic shivers. At greater distances, they stomp their hollow heels against the ground-crust, sending tight, percussive codes through the pressurized nitrogen pipes beneath the felt, reading the replies through the soles of their feet.

To move through the city is to watch a choreography of wind and weightlessness. They walk with a buoyant, skipping gait, leaning sharply into the breeze, their pleated rib-folds tucked tight. On the edges of the disconnected spires—where the upper third of a tower hangs six feet adrift in open air—they do not hesitate. They step straight into the roaring, translucent column of compressed atmosphere, letting the pale blue static lift them like dry leaves and deposit them on the far ledge, or onto the invisible shelves of folded space that ring the matte-black anchors. Many carry small plumb-weights of dense, copper-tasting stone slung at their hips, not as weapons, but as ballast; in a sudden squall, a citizen who loses their ballast can be blown miles out over the empty plain, tumbling across the felt like a broken kite.

Their lives are governed by two things: the **squalls** and the **pitch**.

Every few hours, when the barometric pressure drops and the living floor exhales its miles-wide mist of silvery spores, the city comes fully awake. As the tall collector-spires flare and begin to scream like boiling kettles, the inhabitants emerge from the fluted slits of the towers and climb the exterior walls—dozens of spindly, pale-ochre figures clinging effortlessly to eggshell-thin lignin thirty stories up. They open the slats along their chests, spreading wide, papery membranes from their ribs to catch the swirling vortex. They drink the spore-cloud directly out of the air, letting the dry, silvery dust coat their internal vanes, where tiny sparks crack it down into fresh nitrogen pressure and structural pith.

When the squall passes and the spires drop back to their steady brassy drone, the work of tuning begins.

A city built of dry paper and stasis-threads cannot afford to go out of tune. As the wind shifts and the ground settles, the eggshell towers drift toward fatal frequencies: too flat, and the thin walls will buckle inward; too sharp, and the dry lignin will shatter into chalky dust. Teams of **Tuners**—often the oldest citizens, whose shells have darkened to the color of burnt cedar—patrol the bases of the buildings with long, forked staves of resonant ash. They strike the towers and press their narrow heads directly against the vibrating walls, listening to the overtones. When they find a sour note, they crouch beside the matte-black knots that hover near the foundations, using hooked tools to pluck and twist the shimmering stasis-threads like harp strings, nudging the local geometry back into harmony until the copper taste in the air sharpens and the dust motes resume their neat, horizontal orbits.

Other citizens—the **Listeners**—rarely climb the towers at all. They are heavier, having deliberately packed the hollow chambers of their lower limbs with silvery mineral ash so they can lie flat against the undulating plant-floor far out between the districts. Pressed chest-down to the dry felt, they map the *scritch-thump, scritch-thump* of the unseen borers chewing through the dead lower strata miles below. If a borer-swarm chews too close to the surface, it can collapse a district’s wind-pipes, muting the ground and starving the foundations of pressure. When a Listener detects a rising rhythm, they signal the nearest spire with a series of sharp, ground-borne heel-strikes, and crews march out across the plain to drive long, hollow spikes into the crust, venting the nitrogen pockets or flooding the lower tunnels with choking clouds of dense mineral dust.

Even the smaller creatures of the plateau are woven into the city’s dry economy. Along the stasis-threads, tiny, silver-shelled **thread-mites** nest in the orbiting rings of dust, spinning fine, conductive filaments that the citizens harvest to mend the sagging transit-ribbons. On the outskirts of the districts, herders guide nervous flocks of *static-grazers* across beds of woven copper-weed, harvesting the triboelectric charge from their snapping hooves to keep the matte-black anchors fed. And tethered to the high balconies of the towers, domesticated *clackers* sit in wicker cages, their articulated seed-husk heads twitching at the gloom; the citizens have bred them to rattle in specific, rhythmic patterns whenever a storm-front of barometric change rolls in from the edge of the plateau.

What they fear most is not the wind, nor the borers, nor the thinness of the air. It is the rumor of what lies three miles below, beneath the bruised amber haze.

In their deepest archives—etched onto dry, rolled scrolls of pressed bark—there are cautionary records of *wetness*. To a people made of paper, static, and hollow bone, water is not life; it is a slow, silent rot. A single drop of rain would turn their eggshell spires to paste, short-circuit their lung-vanes, and weigh their limbs down until they drowned in mud. So they keep their city high on the planed-off crown of the world, humming and clicking in the cold, dry dark, forever tuning the wind so the silence never catches them.
