To live three thousand meters up in a world without wood, grass, or dry ground is to belong to stone, water, and bone.

The people of the plateau are built for the height and the damp. Generations in the thin, warm, mist-heavy air have made them broad-chested and deep-lunged, with slow, steady pulses and a heavy, planted way of walking. Their skin rarely chaps; instead, it carries a faint, perpetual sheen, kept oiled with rendered fat from the canal-feeders so the warm rain beads off their shoulders and the mineral water cannot soften their hands into rot. Their hair is worn cropped short or bound tight in greased silk-cord, often dusted pale at the temples with lime-scale from the cisterns.

Because there is no timber to plane, no cotton to spin, and no grain to mill, everything they wear, eat, and hold comes from the rock, the foundries, or the swarming life that crusts the brick.

A foundry-worker setting out at dawn wears a heavy coat woven from the silk curtains harvested beneath the iron viaducts—boiled in powerhouse runoff, beaten flat, and rubbed with reptile oil until it is as supple and waterproof as slate. Beneath it are tunics of fine, pale leather tanned from the hides of the flat-bodied canal amphibians, stitched with sinew and fastened with buttons carved from the discarded plates of the great shelled grazers. Their boots are thick-soled, shod in iron or layered horn, built to grip wet cobblestones and to let the wearer feel, right through the heel, the faint, rhythmic *scritch-scritch* of a grazer scraping the courtyard stones nearby.

No one here speaks quickly, and few bother to shout. In a city where the drop-forges pound, the turbines hum, and a billion insects whine in the steam-thick air, raising one’s voice is a quick way to ruin a throat. Instead, the plateau-folk converse in a slow, economical mix of chest-deep humming tones and crisp, unmistakable hand-signs that cut cleanly through the drifting clouds of midges. A crane-operator on a viaduct fifty meters up does not yell down to the rail-crew; she flashes two fingers and a flat palm, or blows a single, low note on a brass reed-whistle tuned to sit cleanly beneath the treble of the swarms and above the bass of the pumps. In the tenements, where the windows are screened with oiled copper mesh rather than glass, families talk close together over the rattle of the rain, their voices unhurried, leaving wide, comfortable silences for the machinery of the city to fill.

Their kitchens smell of steam, salt, and roasted chitin. There are no ovens fired with logs; instead, every terraced tenement is plumbed into the warm return-lines of the district powerhouses, feeding cast-iron steam-chests set into the brick walls. Inside these chests, dense loaves of pressed slime-fungi—scraped from vertical slate beds kept wet by trickling mineral pipes—simmer for half a day beside earthenware crocks of fermented beetle-meal and rich, milky broths thickened with the fat of whiskered bottom-feeders. Meat from the great meter-wide grazers is a rare, communal feast. No one slaughters a healthy grazer; they are the city’s living street-sweepers, keeping the wild, slippery fungi from choking the stairs and plazas. Only when an elder’s shell cracks with age, or it finally stalls against a retaining wall, do the neighborhood crews pry the heavy muscle from its calcified plates, slow-braising it for three days in mineral water and salt while the shell itself is cleaned, cut, and bolted onto a leaking roof.

Even the animals have learned the city’s patience, and the city has learned theirs.

The grazers are not pests or livestock, but slow, armored neighbors. Workers stepping off the morning rail-cars weave around them without breaking stride, sometimes pausing to tap a familiar lime-mark on a broad grey shell—the neighborhood tally of how many years that particular beast has been circling the same foundry yard. If a grazer’s underside clogs with iron filings or lime-grit, a passerby might crouch with a bone pick to clear the edge of its skirt, waiting calmly while the blind, pale crustaceans underneath scatter into the wet shadow, before standing and walking on.

Up on the viaducts, the silk-harvesters move with the same unhurried care. Suspended in greased-leather harnesses among the riveted girders, they work beside the papery communal hives, cutting away only the empty, hatched curtains of silk so the colonies inside are never roused. Down in the warm discharge basins, old men and children sit on the wet stone steps with wide-meshed copper nets, scooping up the sluggish, whiskered bottom-feeders that crowd the powerhouse pipes, tossing the smaller ones to the pale-feathered aerialists that perch along the iron railings like damp, watchful gargoyles.

At dusk, when the afternoon rain thins to a warm mist and the amber glow of low-voltage filaments warms the screened windows, the gliding reptiles fold their oiled membranes and drop onto the brick chimneys of the tenements to sleep in the rising heat. They pack together along the soot-darkened mortar, clicking softly in their throats as they cool, keeping the midges off the laundry lines where silk coats hang—never quite drying, only steaming gently in the night air.

Nothing on the plateau is built to last forever, because the warm, mineral-heavy water eats iron and softens mortar without rest. Yet nothing is thrown away in haste, either. The foundries cast new boiler plates and rail-ties at the exact, steady pace the old ones rust through. The nests thicken on the brickwork; the grazers inch across the plazas; the people patch the pipes, tend the slate fungus-beds, and step over the wet stone in their oiled silk, part of a single, slow-breathing metabolism that has long since forgotten the difference between what was built and what grew.
