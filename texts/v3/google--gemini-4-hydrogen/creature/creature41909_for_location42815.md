At first, even in the blinding white glare, you do not see them.

They cling to the terraces like part of the gypsum itself—lean, sun-bleached figures dusted from crown to heel in fine white mineral powder and sealed against the dryness with a thin, glassy coat of rubbed plant-wax. In a place without water, nothing can afford to leak. Their skin has no pores that weep, no sweat to darken their tunics of woven pith; it is taut, pale, and faintly translucent at the knuckles and collarbone, like cured bladder-skin stretched over dry wood.

When one turns its head toward you, you see why they are so hard to spot against the chalky stone. Their eyes are narrow, deeply recessed slits, guarded by heavy, chalk-dusted lids and a second, amber-tinted membrane that slides across the cornea to cut the selenite glare. To look at them is to feel watched by statues that have learned to squint. Their mouths, too, are small and perpetually shut—lips thin, dry, and stained dark brown from the resin-oils they chew. They have no use for breath wasted on air that is already screaming.

In fact, they do not hear the roar the way you do. Generations of living inside a giant’s whistle have taught their bodies to abandon the outer ear entirely; the openings at the sides of their heads are either scarred shut in infancy with warm plant-wax or reduced to tight, leathery folds. Instead, they listen with their skeletons.

They go barefoot on the salt-brick ledges, their heels calloused to the density of horn, reading the chamber’s shifting chords through the soles of their feet. When two of them meet on a shoulder-wide stair, they do not look at each other’s faces to take the measure of the other’s mood; they place their bare soles flat against the same step, or press the tips of their fingers to the same handrail, feeling the tremors of weight and pulse travel through the stone.

Every inhabitant wears copper. In the dim, muffled alcoves behind the double walls of packed pith, they spend hours drawing soft copper pins out into hair-thin wire with bone pliers, then coiling it around their wrists, their ankles, and the ridges of their jawbones. A few weave bright copper filaments directly into their stiff, chalked hair. These are not ornaments; they are tuning forks. As the sun climbs and the chamber’s pitch rises from dawn’s belly-deep bass to noon’s white-hot shriek, different gauges of wire begin to hum against their skin—a silent, tingling clockwork that tells them the hour, the wind-pressure outside the northern rim, and the exact moment the selenite roof is about to snap with heat.

Out in the blinding, shrieking alleys, where the standing waves of sound would tear a shouted word apart before it left the throat, they speak in two languages: one for the eye, and one for the bone.

For the eye, they stain their palms and fingertips black with charred resin-soot. Against the shadowless white walls, a dark hand flashes like a striking bird—crisp, angular signs flicked at shoulder height, sharp enough to be read three tiers down through the drifting chaff.

For the bone, they use the railings. The taut sinew-lines strung along every catwalk and staircase are not merely warning bells; they are a city-wide nervous system. A climber coming down from the upper terraces does not just tug the cord once—she plucks it in a rapid, stammering triplet that travels fifty paces along the cliff-face, vibrating through the palms of everyone holding the rail. By varying the tension with a pinching thumb, they send whole sentences down the sinew: news of a cracked salt-brick on tier four, a cluster of bladder-plants ready to burst, or a stranger stepping down through the northern lip.

Their survival in a room without a drop of moisture is a slow, patient alchemy, borrowed directly from the walls. They do not eat wet meat or drink clear liquid. Instead, they farm the dry chemistry of the rock. With flint scrapers, they peel back the thick mats of amber lichen and grind them into a dense, oily meal that smells faintly of heated wax and cloves. They tap the hollow, honeycombed stalks at dawn, collecting a thimbleful of sharp, volatile mineral oil—bitter enough to burn the throat, yet rich enough to keep their dry blood moving.

In the root-tangles beneath their balconies, they keep herds of armored salt-ticks, rattling them out of the dust with bone rakes and roasting them over sun-focused shards of selenite until their shells split, yielding a drop of salty, tallow-thick fat. When noon arrives and the bladder-plants pop open all at once, the inhabitants lean out over their narrow terraces with wide nets of braided fiber, scooping the glittering, weightless chaff-scales out of the air before they can be lost to the violent updrafts of the center. Pressed into cakes with lichen-meal, the chaff becomes a dry, sweet bread that keeps for decades without ever growing mold.

Nothing here spoils, and nothing hurries. Even their childhoods are shaped by the acoustics of the blister.

Infants are born inside the deepest, quietest pockets—windowless chambers lined with three layers of woven matting and stuffed pith, where the exterior roar is reduced to a bearable, womb-like thrum. For their first three years, until the soft plates of their skulls fuse and harden, children never set foot on the open terraces; their heads are wrapped in thick, sound-deadening caps of felted lichen-fiber. In the quiet dark, mothers teach them by pressing their foreheads together, humming low in their throats—not to make a noise in the air, which is useless, but to send a steady, reassuring vibration straight through the frontal bone. Even among adults, this remains the deepest form of intimacy: to sit knee-to-knee in a pith alcove, skulls touching, sharing the quiet thrum of a pulse without a single sign or tugged wire between them.

Once a child’s bones are dense enough to bear the noon shriek, they are brought out onto the sunlit ledges and given their first copper wrist-coil. They learn to walk the tightrope catwalks when the wind-throats slacken at dusk, balancing with long bone poles, and they learn to respect the empty center of the room. No one builds bridges across that three-hundred-pace void; no one throws trash into it. The standing waves there are an invisible, roaring wall. If a climber loses their footing on a high tier and falls outward toward the middle, the violent air catches them, spins them like a dropped seed-pod, and flings them back against the stone—or holds them suspended for a sickening heartbeat before dropping them to the salt-floor below.

Yet they are not entirely alone in the air. They keep a quiet truce with the blind, papery gliders that ride the wall-drafts. Sometimes you will see an elder sitting motionless on a high terrace, holding a long, notched bone rod out into the roaring current. A glider, sensing the rod’s vibration, will bank sharply and settle onto the wood for a rest, its translucent wings humming like plucked parchment. The elder never harms it; instead, they gently brush the dust from its blind head with a fingertip, reading the strength of the high-altitude gales in the tremble of its wings before letting it peel back into the glare.

Because nothing rots in the blister, death here is simply a final drying.

When an inhabitant grows too brittle to climb, and their skin begins to crack like old gypsum, they retreat to a quiet alcove and stop taking the bitter stalk-oil. Within days, their body gives up its last hidden moisture to the warm, dry air. What remains is astonishingly light—a pale, hollow shell of bone and parchment-skin, odorless and clean.

They do not bury their dead in the rock, nor burn them among the dry, resinous plants. Instead, the family carries the lightened body up to the highest, windiest ledges near the northern wind-throats, where the exterior gales tear into the dome. There, they uncoil the dead one’s copper wires and take the long bones of the arms and legs. The rest is wedged into the crevices beside the honeycombed stalks, where the ceaseless friction of the sound slowly wears the dry husk down into fine white powder, returning it to the terraces below as dust.

The long bones are carried back down to the quiet alcoves. Scraped clean with flint and drilled with tiny, precise holes, a grandmother’s femur becomes a new handrail-peg, or a bone chisel, or a wind-pipe wedged into the outer wall—tuned to catch the evening hum as the crust cools, so that even after she has turned to chalk, her bones keep singing in the dark.
