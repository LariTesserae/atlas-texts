You can see eighty paces from one wall to the other, and at first you would swear the hollow is empty of anything larger than a bladder-weed. 

That is only because the light is too bright, and because you are looking at the water instead of the stone.

Pressed flat into the leeward notches behind the slate cairns, wedged tight against the sharpest ribs of basalt where the current splits, they are almost impossible to separate from the rock. Their skin is the exact wet, unstained gray of pale slate, mottled across the shoulders and spine with dark, jagged streaks like basalt seams. Even their stillness looks geological—until a shift in the eddy lifts a flap of pleated skin along their ribs, or a milky, translucent eyelid slides back to reveal a dark, wide eye staring out into the blinding blue void.

There are never many of them in a single blister—perhaps three or four, rarely more than a handful, sharing the eighty-pace courtyard with the moss and the blind, armored crawlers. They are compact, heavy-boned beings, roughly the size of a crouched human, built with the dense, stubborn geometry of things that refuse to be torn loose. Their arms are long and corded with thick muscle; their hands and feet are broad, flat-nailed, and calloused into pale, leather-hard pads that can grip wet, glassy quartz without slipping.

They do not have external ears. Where ears would be, there are only smooth, scarred plates of cartilage set flush against the skull. In a chamber where cavitation roars like an avalanche and trapped air detonates in the ceiling fissures, delicate eardrums would rupture in an hour. Instead, they hear through bone. Their skulls are thick, their jawbones heavy and wide, and their collarbones sit close to the surface of the chest. They feel the mountain’s thunder directly through the sternum and the teeth, and to them, that continuous, bone-rattling roar is simply the silence of home.

They breathe the water itself. Along their flanks and the sides of their thick necks run deep, overlapping pleats of pale, thin-skinned tissue, rich with blood. In the violent churn of the hollow—cold, thin meltwater beaten full of silver-white micro-bubbles—there is more than enough oxygen to sustain them. When they rest in the dead-zones behind the quartz blades, those pleats open slightly like the pages of a wet book, drinking the freezing current. When they dart out into the open wash, the pleats clamp shut, sealing smooth and tight against their ribs.

Even so, they cannot swim the center of the chamber. Nothing with flesh can fight the invisible shear-zones where colliding currents tear at anything caught between them. To cross from one wall to the other, they wait. They wear harnesses of braided sinew strapped tight across their chests and hips, hung with rough chunks of raw copper ore for ballast—without that dead weight, the pressurized updrafts from the floor-fissures would fling them straight into the detonating ceiling. Anchored by short leather tethers to the bone hooks driven into the stone, they watch the translucent bladder-weeds spin and shred in the middle of the room, reading the invisible geometry of the eddies. Only in the brief, three-heartbeat lull after a ceiling pocket explodes—when the shockwave momentarily flattens the cross-currents—will one of them unhook, kick off hard from the stone, and ride the violent arc across the blinding void, limbs tucked tight, copper weights dragging them down toward a distant seam before the shear-zones snap back together.

Their days are spent tending the instruments that keep them alive.

The cairns of flat slate are not monuments; they are farms and shelters. By stacking the sharp stone and binding it with sinew and copper, the dwellers create the only pockets of shadow in a chamber that otherwise burns with bounced daylight. On the shadowed undersides of those cairns, the tough, pale-green moss takes hold—and the dwellers graze it slowly with flat, heavy teeth, never tearing the rubbery crusts loose from the rock. 

The knotted cords whipping horizontally from the bone hooks are their gauges. Every few hours, one of them will haul themselves along a rib of stone, take a taut cord between their teeth or grip it with a broad hand, and close their eyes. Through the vibrating root-fiber, they can feel the exact pitch of the current—whether a floor-fissure has widened by a finger’s breadth, or whether a snowfield vent above is choking shut with ice. As the cords fray in the relentless wash, their shed fibers drift down into the notches to feed the pale, foot-long trilobite-creatures; in return, the dwellers occasionally pluck one of the blind crawlers from the stone, cracking its flexible armor with a chunk of copper ballast.

And in the quiet corner behind the blade of quartz, where the hollowed-out stone bells clack furiously against one another, there is almost always at least one of them crouched in the wash. The bells are tuned by thickness, each hollowed from a different slab of slate. When the mountain shifts—when the whole flooded bell tilts a fraction of a degree as it climbs into the sky—the dead-zone shrinks or widens, and the outer bells catch the edge of the current, changing their frantic tempo. The dweller crouching there presses their chin to the stone wall and listens to the clatter, reading the slow, violent growth of the peak.

Only in the deepest winter nights, or when a black storm buries the skylights above in fresh snow and turns the blinding pale-blue lens dark, do they leave the water. One of them will unbuckle their copper weights, haul themselves up through the slick, vertical ice-chimneys into the howling dark of the outer peak, and descend miles below to the ragged edge of the timberline. There, in the freezing wind, they scavenge the carcasses of fallen mountain goats for sinew, leather, and heavy bone, dig tough, frost-bitten roots from the scree, and pry fresh copper from the outer fractures—moving awkwardly on dry land, squinting against the open air, eager to return before dawn turns the ice above their home back into glass.

By the time the first high-altitude light pours down through the quartz skylights, they are already back in the cold, deafening roar, tethered behind their cairns, with their milky lids drawn shut and their chins pressed to the rising stone.
