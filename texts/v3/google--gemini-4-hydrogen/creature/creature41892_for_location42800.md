If you drift through the valley without knowing what to look for, you might think the city abandoned—a monument left behind by builders who have long since risen to the surface or sunk into the abyss. In the bruised-amber twilight, the inhabitants do not stand out against their own architecture. Their skin is the color of wet slate and bruised plum, matte and fine-grained, with pale, bone-colored mottling along the inner arms and throat. Where the warm, silica-heavy water lingers in the creases of their shoulders and along the ridges of their spines, a faint, pearlescent crust of mineral precipitates directly onto their hides, as if the valley is slowly claiming them the same way it claims the raw basalt.

They are creatures of the vertical column, built not for speed in open water, but for patience among sharp edges. Their bodies are long-waisted and supple, with limbs better suited to wedging, bracing, and holding than to swimming. Their fingers are long and heavily knuckled, tipped not with claws but with thick, horn-like pads, and their palms, knees, and the soles of their feet are armored in dense, leathery callouses. In a newborn landscape where a careless kick could open a vein on fresh obsidian, they never thrash. They move with a slow, deliberate economy—a gentle push from a basalt step, a hand sliding lightly along a braided kelp-cable—adjusting their depth by compressing or expanding internal gas-sacs nestled behind their ribs. Without a ripple or a kick of a fin, one of them can rise six feet beside a hanging reed-pod, pause motionless in the warm gloom to tighten a sinew lashing, and sink back toward the terraces like a stone settling through oil.

Their faces are quiet and broad, dominated by large, pale-amber eyes adapted to the permanent dusk. When they approach the cooling rock seams or chip at a fresh lobe of volcanic glass, a heavy, translucent third eyelid slides sideways across each eye, protecting the surface from stray splinters and the shimmering distortion of rising heat. 

Down on the lower terraces, where the honeycombed slate walls are dry-stacked into windowless alcoves, you find them tucked into the stone like hermit crabs in a reef. They do not use doors; they wedge themselves into tight, shoulder-width crevices where the warm rock presses against their backs and the stagnant water sits still. Here, a knapper might spend an entire afternoon crouched on a mineral shelf, holding a core of raw obsidian between calloused feet while striking it with a stone hammer. Each blow produces that dull, muffled *clack*, and the newly sheared flakes—black and razor-thin—do not fly, but drift downward through the heavy water like slow, dark leaves.

Higher up, in the hanging neighborhoods buoyed by tethered gas-bladders, their strangest adaptations come into play. Because their breathing organs are not in their throats, but are arranged as feathery, sheltered frills tucked beneath the lower edges of their ribcages, they can work effortlessly at the boundary between water and gas. Beneath the inverted domes of oiled bladder-skin, where warm volcanic vapor collects in shimmering, humid pockets, a builder will hover upright—hips and lower torso submerged in the bath-warm sea, head and shoulders rising cleanly into the trapped gas above the meniscus. In that dry, dim air, their pale-amber eyes narrow against the unfamiliar lightlessness, and their long fingers move swiftly, braiding stripped kelp-fiber, curing hides, or drawing a bone needle through oiled skin without the drag of the water to slow them.

They are a sparse, unhurried people, rarely gathering in crowds. A single family or kin-group might occupy an entire cluster of hanging pods, tending the nearby gardens of leathery fronds and pruning the amber-veined gas bladders as carefully as orchardists. Their voices are seldom heard as speech; the water carries low frequencies best, and they communicate across the valley through deep, chest-resonant hums—long, mournful notes that vibrate through the fluid—or by plucking the taut kelp-cables that span the ridges, sending a coded tremor humming directly into a neighbor’s floorboards.

Only when a great slab of volcanic slate must be moved from a quarry-seam to a retaining wall do they assemble in numbers. Five or six of them will gather in the gloom around a fallen plate of stone, wedging levers of dense, waterlogged root-wood beneath its edge. There is no shouting, no rhythmic chanting—only the soft *creak* of wood under strain, the silent exchange of glances, and the slow, collective leaning of their bodies until the weightless water takes the burden and the stone glides into place.

Sharing the valley with them are the pale, slow-moving grazers—thick-bodied, slug-like beasts the size of calves, with translucent, milky hides and heavy internal ribs of cartilage and bone. The builders neither fence them nor hunt them in haste. The grazers drift through the hanging gardens like living clouds, rasping the mineral crust off the fronds and the slate walls with stony tongues, keeping the settlement’s cables and terraces from choking under the creeping moss. When one of the beasts grows old and sinks to the basalt floor, nothing is wasted: its tough sinew is soaked and shrunken to bind the corners of new slate chambers, its heavy bones are ground into needles and awls, and its pale hide is scraped clean with obsidian knives.

Everything in the valley lives by the same unhurried clock. Even the elders—whose skin has grown thick and pale with decades of precipitated silica, until they look nearly carved from the mineral shelves themselves—move as if they have all the time of a cooling world. They sit in the dimness beneath the swaying canopy of lanterns, listening to the distant *bloop* of a rising bubble and the faint, sharp *tick* of fresh glass fracturing in the dark, tending a city that is still, millimeter by millimeter, rising out of the stone.
