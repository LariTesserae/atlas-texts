At first, you don’t see them at all. You see the steam, the ferns, the slow turn of the Archimedes screws, and it is easy to assume the basin runs on its own, a wet clockwork left behind by someone who forgot to turn it off. Then a hand reaches out from behind a bundle of drying hemp to crack a brass petcock, or a silhouette crosses a clerestory catwalk thirty feet up, and you realize the place is full of people. They simply move at the same pace as the slurry, and wear the same colors as the iron.

They are small-boned, mostly, or perhaps they only look that way next to the buttressed cypress trunks and the thickets of cast-iron piping. Generation after generation in the greenhouse heat has left them lean, with skin the color of steeped tea and a permanent, glistening sheen of sweat and machine oil across their forearms and collarbones. Nothing here stays dry, and after a few years in the basin, the people seem to stop trying. Their hair—men’s and women’s alike—is cropped short or braided so tightly it looks like tarred twine, tucked under caps of oiled canvas to keep it out of the overhead drive-belts.

Their clothes are built for shedding water and surviving splashes of hot, acidic bark-liquor. Most wear loose trousers rolled to the knee and heavy, sleeveless aprons cut from the same wide canvas used for the line-shaft belts, cured in pressed tree-resin until they are stiff as leather and dark as old blood. On their feet they wear slatted wooden clogs strapped on with hemp cord, which keep their soles an inch above the wet root-mat and let the water drain straight through. When they walk the perforated steel catwalks, the clogs make a dry, hollow *clack-clack-clack* that is one of the few sharp sounds in the whole soft, roaring valley.

Nobody shouts here. Shouting is useless against the bass hum of the steam-dynamo and the wet slap of the belts; it only shreds the throat. Instead, the basin has its own quiet, practical languages. Within arm’s reach, they speak right against each other’s ears, or use a brisk system of hand-signs—fingers flicked to indicate pressure, a flat palm tilted to show the level of a vat. Across a channel or through the tangle of pipes, they talk with wrenches. A valve-tender needing more head from the upper flumes will rap a specific cadence—three short, one long—on the copper steam-line. The sound travels cleanly through the metal, bypassing the air entirely, and a minute later a distant tap answers back from behind a wall of philodendrons.

If you watch them work, the strangest thing is their stillness. Outside the dike, factory work is a matter of keeping up with a machine; here, the machines are slower than a sleeping dog. A press-watcher might spend four hours sitting on an overturned grease-keg, whittling a replacement wooden pin or chewing on a bit of dried sweet-flag root, glancing up every twenty minutes to check a brass gauge whose needle has moved the width of a hair. They do not fidget. They have the heavy, unblinking patience of people who measure time in *steepings* and *curings*. A batch of marsh-fiber takes three weeks to ret; you cannot hurry a fungus, and you cannot bully steam into condensing faster than the air allows. When an outsider speaks to them, they take a long, unhurried breath before answering, as if waiting for their thoughts to settle like silt in a tank.

They live tucked into the upper third of the basin, above the worst of the damp and the splash of the sumps. There are no houses in the usual sense. Instead, the "town" is a scatter of dry-lofts, sleeping galleries, and narrow, tin-roofed cabins wedged into the forks of the strangler-figs or bolted straight to the steel I-beams alongside the pump-houses. Nobody sleeps on the ground; the root-mat is too spongy, too wet, and too close to the vibration of the dynamo. They sling hammocks of knotted marsh-hemp from the iron brackets, up where the warm air rises and the skylight clerestories let in a cool draft from above the canopy. Children grow up knowing how to walk a wet beam before they know how to read, and they learn early which pipes are cold-water intakes and which ones will take the skin off your palm.

Because the settling ponds are soured with copper and bark-tannin, the basin people are meticulous about their water. Every tin roof has its own crooked system of bamboo and copper gutters leading to covered wooden cisterns perched in the tree-crotches, filtered through layers of crushed charcoal and gravel hauled in from outside the dike. They cook almost everything with waste steam—lowering lidded tin pots of rice, greens, and river-fish into perforated baskets hung inside the vents of the boiler-houses. On the mossy, sun-dappled roofs of the machine sheds, they tend shallow wooden troughs packed with leaf-mold, growing thick crops of hot peppers, climbing beans, and pale, fleshy mushrooms that thrive in the shade of the drying racks.

They have a peculiar, unsentimental intimacy with the plants and the iron. You see it in the *tree-wrights*—a small, respected guild of older workers who carry both pruning saws and grease guns. A tree-wright knows exactly how much weight a cypress bough can carry before it begins to sag, and how to notch a strangler-fig’s aerial roots so they wrap around a brick retaining wall for support rather than crushing a steam-jacket. They treat the trees less like scenery and more like slow, stubborn coworkers. When a trunk swells over a bolt-head, they don’t pry it loose; they chalk the date on the bark and move on, satisfied that the joint is good for another thirty years.

Down at the very bottom of the hierarchy—or perhaps at its center, depending on how you look at it—are the dynamo-keepers. They live in the half-submerged brick vault near the dike, where the air is hottest and the hum is so thick you feel it in your back teeth. They rarely come up into the green-gold light of the canopy. Their skin is paler than the rest, their hearing mostly gone from decades beside the flywheel, and they communicate almost entirely by touch and the flicker of oil-lamps. When a new worker comes down to check the main manifold, the keepers don’t look up; they just reach out, lay a broad, grease-blackened palm flat against the visitor’s chest for a second to feel their heartbeat against the machine’s rhythm, and nod toward the right valve.

Outside the dike, people say the basin workers are half-rotted themselves—that if you took one of them out into the dry wind and the open sun, they’d crack like a seasoned stave. There might be some truth to it. On the rare occasions a worker has to climb the gap in the limestone wall to haul in a load of coal or salt, they never linger. They stand on the rim for a moment, squinting against the flat, un-filtered glare of the sky, unsettled by the silence and the strange, dead stillness of the air. Then they hitch up their oilskin aprons, step back down into the green steam and the roar, and let the canopy close over their heads.
