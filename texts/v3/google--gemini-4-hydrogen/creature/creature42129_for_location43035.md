They are built for a world that has never known a sharp edge to be smoothed by water.

The upright ones who shape the basin—the makers of the tapered cylinders and the braided bridges—are lean, long-limbed, and weathered the color of the raw crust itself. Their skin is dry and matte, naturally dusted with pale gypsum and fine volcanic ash that settles into the creases of their elbows and knees, giving them the look of figures kneaded from the same silt as their walls. They wear nothing woven on a loom, for there are no looms; instead, they drape themselves in loose, knotted aprons of papery sedge and strips of cured succulent skin, worn only to shield their shoulders from the midday glare or to carry bundles of cut stalks.

Their hands are their only tools, and generations of work have shaped them accordingly. Their fingers are long and slightly spatulate at the tips, backed by thick, horn-like nails that can score a groove in drying clay, strip the bark from a woody vine, or pry open the waxy skin of an ice-plant without a blade. Their palms and the soles of their bare feet are plated in dense, pale callus—so thick that they can walk across the jagged, unweathered limestone at the basin’s margins without flinching, and press wet, caustic gypsum-mortar into place without their skin cracking.

They have no voices. Their throats are smooth and narrow, suited only to slow, economical breathing that wastes no moisture. Instead, the basin’s silence is thick with their conversation. They speak in a continuous, fluid grammar of posture, touch, and scent. A builder crossing a suspended reed-bridge announces their intent from twenty paces away by the tilt of their chin, the angle of their elbows, and the precise placement of their fingers against their ribs. Up close, in the shaded corridors where sightlines are short, they press the backs of their wrists or the hollows of their throats against one another, exchanging dry, concentrated scents—mineral-sharp when alert, sweet as crushed acacia-pod when content, bitter and chalky when tired or thirsty. Even the walls carry their speech: every hand-smoothed surface holds the faint ridges of the palms that shaped it, and a blind or hurrying traveler can read the age, haste, and mood of a corridor simply by trailing their knuckles along the clay as they pass.

They do not live alone in the city-basin, nor do they build it alone. The settlement is a slow, ceaseless collaboration between species that have forgotten how to live apart.

Moving through the middle galleries alongside the upright builders are the *tampers*—compact, heavy-shouldered herbivores with broad, cloven hooves and dense coats of silver-ochre hair. They are neither quite wild nor quite owned. By morning, they graze the upper terraces on thorny acacia shoots and dry sedge, their tough, leathery lips indifferent to the spines; by afternoon, the builders guide them with gentle taps of a bare palm across newly laid galleries of damp subsoil. Walking in tight, patient circles, the tampers pack the earth solid with their hooves, leaving behind a stippled, dimpled floor and droppings rich in undigested plant fiber, which the builders immediately knead back into the mortar to bind the next story.

Below them, in the cool, dark veins where the taproots plunge into the fractured bedrock, the pale ants work without pause. They are large—nearly the length of a finger-joint—translucent as peeled quartz, with broad, shovel-shaped mandibles. They never come out into the direct sun. Instead, they mine the deep, dampened subsoil from the edges of the sealed aquifer, hauling it up the root-shafts in endless, silent columns. Where the upright builders shape the broad cylinders and terraces, the ants swarm the seams, packing their saliva-moistened pellets of clay into every hairline crack left by the drying heat. In return, the builders leave the hollowed pith of pruned succulents stacked near the shaft-mouths, where the ants cultivate pale, dust-dry fungi in the dark.

No one here farms in rows, and no one hunts with traps. Everything is gathered from the vertical tangle of the city itself.

The builders drink without ever seeing open water. At dawn, before the thermals rise, small teams descend into the root-shafts—narrow, cool chimneys lined with braided vine-ladders that drop straight through the raw limestone. Far below, where the air smells of wet chalk and old stone, they score the thick, woody taproots of the candelabra succulents with their thumbnails, catching the slow, clear seep in cupped gourd-shells, or they harvest the swollen, water-heavy leaves of the deep-shaded ice-plant. Water is never poured or splashed; it is drunk slowly, or mixed drop by drop with crushed succulent mucilage and silt to make the mortar that keeps the city growing.

Up in the dappled light of the fourth and fifth stories, the air belongs to the small, soft-feathered birds and the dust-colored lizards. The birds are plump, wren-like creatures with muted, ash-and-ochre plumage and unusually thick, curved beaks. They move through the thorny acacias and across the tops of the succulents in tight, soundless flocks, pruning away dead buds and picking silent beetles from the waxy crevices. Their feathers are edged with a fine, velvety fringe—much like an owl’s—so that even a sudden flight from one rooftop to another makes no more sound than a drifting leaf. The lizards, thin as dry twigs and patterned like cracked gypsum, share the same sunlit ledges, freezing when a bird’s shadow crosses them, then darting to snatch a pale moth from the air before vanishing into the mortar.

Along the braided walkways between them threads the long-bodied hunter—a fox-like creature with oversized, leaf-shaped ears, padded feet, and a coat the color of sun-baked clay. It hunts entirely by scent and the faint vibration of footsteps carried through the woven stalks. Because the basin absorbs all sound, the hunter does not stalk so much as it *drifts*, flowing low along the suspended bridges, nose brushing the reed-mats, invisible against the ochre walls until it lunges. Even a kill is quiet: a brief, muffled scuffle in the leaf-litter, a puff of dust, and then the hunter slips down into a shaded alley with its prize. The upright builders rarely interfere with it; it keeps the nocturnal grazers from gnawing too deeply into the living root-lashings that hold the upper stories together.

By mid-afternoon, when the heat rising off the miles of packed earth begins to build into vast, invisible thermals, the entire city slows. The upright builders retreat into the lower, thick-walled chambers where the secondary canopy blocks the sun and the porous clay holds the lingering chill of the bedrock. They sit shoulder to shoulder in the dimness, grooming the fine mineral dust from one another’s backs, mending frayed reed-ropes with their teeth and nails, or resting with their palms pressed flat against the cool walls. Outside, silent dust-devils spin lazily across the empty rooftops, lifting shed lizard-scales, dry pollen, and golden chaff into the pale sky.

When the sun drops and the light turns thick and amber, the builders begin to settle, tucking themselves into alcoves lined with dry grass, and the night-shift rises from below.

Out of the deep, sharp crevices of the bedrock come the owls—broad-winged, silent, with eyes the color of wet flint—lifting off from the lower foundations to sweep the cooling terraces. Behind them emerge the nocturnal grazers: shy, round-bodied creatures with huge, dark eyes and splayed, padded toes, looking something like giant, long-tailed jerboas. They climb the rough clay walls with effortless grace, feeding on the night-blooming succulent flowers that only open after the thermals collapse, and pollinating them with faces dusted thick in pale yellow powder.

Down in the deepest chambers, where the oldest walls have been buried beneath four or five generations of newer terraces, the dead of the upright builders are laid to rest. There are no graves dug into the unweathered rock; instead, the old and the fallen are placed in narrow niches along the root-shafts and sealed behind a thin wall of fresh silt and mucilage. Over the years, the taproots of the great succulents find the niches, threading through the porous clay to draw up the last moisture and mineral salt of the makers, lifting them back into the silvery-olive canopy above.

By the time the night-dwellers return to the stone at first light, the evening’s settled dust has buried every footprint on the rooftops, leaving the basin clean, silent, and ready to be kneaded upward another inch.
