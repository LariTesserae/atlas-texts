If you kneel at the edge of a grove and stay still long enough for the wind to forget you, you begin to see that the silence is not emptiness. It is simply life operating at a frequency the thin air cannot carry.

The thickest congregation of living things hides where the sunlight rarely reaches: beneath the overlapping skirts of the *plate-vines* and deep within the silver-grey cushions of *dust-moss*. Here, in the dry, acid-scented dark, live the *bract-borers*—blind, segmented creatures no longer than a finger, sheathed in overlapping plates of pale, waxy chitin. They have no moisture to spare and no mouths for drinking; instead, they shear away the dead, papery bracts that fall from the towering *fan-spires* above, grinding the silica-stiffened fiber into a fine meal. As they tunnel through the litter, they line their passages with shed husks, building a labyrinth of dry, insulating tubes beneath the moss. When the afternoon thermals rise and the basin warms, you can sometimes hear them—not a sound, really, just a faint, dry *shivering* in the moss beneath your hand, like paper settling in a drawer.

Higher up, clinging to the broad, suede-like leaves of the fan-spires, are the *spark-mites*. They are tiny, no larger than grains of coarse salt, with translucent, amber-colored shells that catch the fierce noon light. They do not feed on the plant itself. Instead, they graze along the forest of microscopic, electrostatically charged hairs that coat the foliage, harvesting the finest motes of nitrogen and sulfur the moment the leaves snatch them from the passing dust-clouds. Because the dry air builds a constant static charge across the swaying fronds, the mites live in a perpetual, invisible crackle. If you brush a leaf with your knuckles, a hundred of them will scatter at once, leaping across the gaps between bracts on tiny bursts of repelled charge, flashing for a fraction of a second like sparks struck from copper.

At the boundary where the dark velvet of the grove gives way to the blinding chalk of the mineral pan, the world becomes exposed, and the life there is built for speed and armor.

This is the territory of the *chalk-skimmers*—the small, burrowing grazers that the *gliders* watch for from the heights. A skimmer looks less like an animal than a wedge of wind-carved ochre rock that has learned to move. Its back is plated in thick, interlocking scutes of dull silica, matching the fluted ribs of the surrounding stone, while its underside is smooth and slick as polished gypsum. It has no eyes on its face, which ends in a blunt, serrated spade for driving through the talc-like powder drifts. Instead, a row of dark, glassy pits runs along its spine, tuned only to sudden changes in the fierce overhead light.

For most of the day, the skimmers swim beneath the surface of the white drifts, leaving only faint, meandering ridges in the powder. They surface at the edges of the basins just long enough to shear mouthfuls of waxy root-flesh from the outermost plate-vines, then dive back into the talc before the razor-sharp shadow of a circling glider can cross their backs.

High above them, the gliders themselves are creatures of pure leverage and patience. Seen up close—if one ever drops low enough—a glider is startlingly fragile: a narrow, keel-chested body no heavier than a bundle of dried reeds, supporting two vast, translucent membranes stretched over hollow, calcified ribs. They have no feathers, no fur, and no muscles built for flapping. In air this thin, powered flight would burn more energy than the barren flats could ever repay. Instead, a glider spends its mornings hooked by its hooked, bone-white talons to the windward flutes of the highest mesas, flattened against the rock like a scrap of shed parchment. Only when the sun heats the white pans and the first silent pillars of rising dust begin to climb into the cobalt sky does it let go, spreading its ribs to catch the updraft.

It can circle for six hours without a single wingbeat, its shadow sweeping miles of empty stone. When it spots a chalk-skimmer breaking the surface of a drift, it does not swoop with a cry. It simply folds its ribs inward, turning itself into a narrow, bone-tipped dart, and falls. In the thin atmosphere, there is no rush of air to warn the prey—only a sudden, silent impact in the powder, a brief thrashing of ochre plates, and then the glider catches the rebound of the thermal, rising back into the glare with its catch locked beneath its keel.

Out on the vast, open corridors between the groves, where even the gliders rarely bother to look, move the *striders*.

They are the only things on the plateau that seem to ignore the scale of the landscape. A full-grown strider stands taller than a man, its narrow, boat-shaped body balanced on six jointed stilts that end in broad, padded discs to keep it from sinking into the talc. Its shell is the color of sun-bleached limestone, matte and pitted by decades of blowing grit, tapering to a sharp prow that parts the continental wind without resistance.

A strider lives by a slow, relentless calendar. It walks in a straight, unhurried line from one sunken basin to the next, covering ten or fifteen miles of blinding white pan in a day, its six legs moving in a silent, pendulum rhythm. At the edge of a grove, it lowers its long, articulated neck and uses a pair of heavy, shearing mandibles to crack open the tough, silica-armored spore-cones that grow along the outer skirts of the fan-spires. It swallows the dense, mineral-rich cores and leaves the shattered husks behind. Without knowing it, the strider is the groves’ only courier: as it brushes against the lower fronds, golden-grey spore-powder clings to the dry, bristled joints of its legs, riding across miles of sterile stone to be shaken loose in the next hollow.

Yet even the strider does not stay in the groves after dark. As the sun drops toward the dusty horizon and the light turns to piercing amber, the strider turns back out onto the open stone. In the lee of a yardang or a low ochre rib, it folds its six stilts neatly beneath its shell, presses its narrow belly flat against the cooling rock, and extrudes a quick-drying, waxy seal around its leg-joints, locking in the day’s heat against the coming freeze.

And then, very rarely—so rarely that you could cross three basins and never see a trace of them—there are the *tenders*.

They are not beasts of burden, nor are they quite like the striders, though they share the same patience for distance. Tall, gaunt, and slow-moving, they are native to the roof of the world, shaped by the same dry laws as everything else that endures here. Their skin is pale, matte, and tough as cured gypsum, cracked into fine, wind-smoothed plates along their shoulders and forearms, and they wrap themselves from throat to ankle in heavy, layered cloaks stitched from the felted leaves of dead fan-spires and the shed membranes of gliders. The outer surfaces of their cloaks are dusted with silver-grey moss-powder to reflect the fierce noon glare; the inner surfaces are dark, holding whatever warmth their bodies can hoard.

They have no spoken language. In air so thin that a shout dies twenty paces away, breath is too precious to spend on noise, and there is no water in them to spare for wet throats. When two tenders meet—perhaps once in several years, crossing paths on the blinding expanse of a mineral pan—they do not speak. They stop a few paces apart, let the wind settle the talc around their wrapped feet, and then step forward to press their bare, chalky palms together. Through the dry contact of their skin passes a faint, tingling snap—the stored electrostatic charge gathered by their felted cloaks over days of walking through the dust-clouds—and in that brief, silent exchange of current, they read where the other has come from, how rich the dust was in the northern basins, and which groves are flourishing or failing.

They live as quiet custodians of the archipelago. Moving from hollow to hollow on cycles that span years, they clear away the choked, dead husks of fallen fan-spires so the younger spirals have room to turn toward the sun. They carry small, tightly sealed pouches of spore-powder from thriving groves to fresh fractures in the gypsum, pressing the seeds into the stone where the dry acids can take hold. In return, they harvest the dense, amber-colored mineral resins that form in the deepest cores of the oldest spires—lumps of solidified sunlight and sulfur that, when struck with a spark of flint at dusk, do not burst into flame, but glow with a steady, smokeless, flameless heat.

At night, when the wind drops to a whisper and the dust settles back onto the pans like snow, the groves fold their heavy suede leaves inward, sealing their warmth into their hearts. Out on the open flats, the striders sit locked in their waxy shells, and the gliders hang like dead leaves from the stone fins. And deep within the sheltered lee of a mesa, beside a glowing coal of resin that makes no sound and casts almost no light, a tender sits wrapped in felt and membrane, waiting for the cobalt sky to brighten again, as patient and settled as the rock itself.
