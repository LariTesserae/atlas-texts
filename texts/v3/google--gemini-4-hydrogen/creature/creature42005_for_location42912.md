Look down at the ground between your boots, and you will see that the emptiness is only a matter of scale.

Wedged into the hairline fractures of the cracked red clay—where no root has ever pried a stone apart—sit neat, leaning stacks of coin-sized discs. They look like flakes of oxidized tin and salt-crusted ceramic, dull and lifeless, baked into the dirt for years at a time. Wait, though, until one of the six-legged copper coils drags its belly past, leaving a fresh, smoking ribbon of fused glass across the mica-schist. As the local electrostatic charge spikes, the stacks in the clay *unfold*. They do not crawl; they snap upward with a dry, metallic *tick*, flipping three or four inches through the parched air, drawn by the residual field toward the cooling glass. Dozens of them gather along the fresh track, pressing their flat undersides against the vitrified crust, clicking softly like cooling engine blocks as they leach the trapped heat and stray ions from the stone. When the glass drops back to the ambient hundred-and-twenty degrees, they fold themselves back into the nearest crack and go still.

Nothing here rots, because there is no moisture to soften it, and nothing here is eaten in a hurry. When one of the cathedral-sized stilt-beasts finally wears out—its tendon-joints seizing, its internal grease carbonizing into black graphite—it simply locks in place. The humming warp inside its ribcage fails, and the thirty-foot quartz wheel drops straight down, shattering the porcelain ribs with a crack that rings across the basin for ten seconds.

Within an hour, the white salt around the wreckage begins to stir.

Out of the dry powder beneath the crust emerge flat, six-foot ribbons of articulated, overlapping bronze scales, moving like blind, sluggish eels. You never see them while they travel—only a faint, straight-line ripple in the salt-dust. They drape themselves over the fallen porcelain and the fractured quartz, their undersides lined with thousands of tiny, diamond-hard corundum teeth arranged like a wood-rasp. Then, in unison, they begin to saw. Back and forth, at the pace of a shifting dune, they slide across the bones with a dry, rhythmic *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* that goes on for months, milling the carcass down into fine, conductive dust—swallowing the metal filings to plate their own armor and leaving behind neat, wind-planed drifts of sterile white silica.

Look up at the walkers that are still standing, and you will see they are not alone inside their own frames. Clinging to the inner curve of those bleached, porcelain-hard ribs—right where the blue-white static arcs strike—are limpet-like crusts of black, sintered carbon. They have no mouths and no eyes, only dense clusters of fine, silver-white whiskers—metallic dendrites—grown inward toward the spinning quartz wheel. Every time a bolt of static leaps from the stone teeth, it grounds itself into those whiskers. The carbon limpet drinks the surge, glowing a dull, sullen cherry-red for three seconds before cooling back to matte black, shedding thin, iridescent flakes of vitrified ash that drift down through the empty ribcage like dry snow.

Higher still, above the mesa rims where the barge-sized bladders drift on the thermals, live things that never touch the ground at all. They are rigid, triangular kites of translucent mica-paper and balsa-thin ceramic, no larger than a hawk, with articulated tail-flaps of hammered copper foil. They have no muscles to flap with. Instead, they hang effortlessly in the scalding, ozone-thick updrafts directly above the bladders’ brass exhaust pipes. Every few minutes, when the valley-wide chorus of *HISS-THUMP* shakes the dust from the rocks, the kites catch the superheated blast and shoot hundreds of feet higher into the bleached indigo sky, glinting like shards of mirror, before banking into a long, lazy spiral back down toward the pipes. They feed on the unburned mineral soot and sulfur crystals blown out with the gas, combing the dust from the air with electrostatic hairs along their leading edges.

And beneath your heels, in the pitch-black, oven-hot void under the vitrified salt-crust, the hollow plateau has its own ceiling-dwellers. Hanging from the underside of the basin like stalactites of braided, tar-black glass and asbestos-fiber are long, pendulous counterweights of heavy galena and iron-pyrite, suspended on taut cords of mineral-silk. Every time a stilt-beast takes a step miles above, the crust flexes downward by a fraction of a millimeter, and thousands of these subterranean pendulums begin to swing in the superheated dark. They swing for hours without stopping, their cords humming like cello strings—passing the vibration of a single footstep across miles of empty underground air, feeding on the friction of their own suspension sockets, grinding hot silica and sulfur into a dry paste that keeps their cords supple.

Yet the strangest inhabitants of this plateau are not the ones that cling to the machines, or dismantle them, or ride their exhaust. They are the ones that live in the *intervals*—the thin, frayed stretches of space between every moving part.

Stand still on the salt-pan for long enough, watching a mesa that refuses to get closer, and you may notice a single vertical line against the bleached sky—so thin it looks at first like a scratch on your own vision. Watch it for five minutes, and the line tilts, lifts, and plants itself a few yards to the left.

It is a creature composed almost entirely of a thirty-foot spire of hollow, fluted basalt, tapered like a lightning rod and balanced on three short, articulated caliper-legs of blackened brass and bone-china. Around its middle sits a slow-turning, horizontal collar of gear-toothed slate, studded with dozens of milky-white quartz lenses facing outward in every direction.

It has no head, no mouth, no hands—only an immense, patient attention. Where the stilt-beasts stride blindly on their minute-long rhythms, this spire-walker stops. It screws its three caliper-legs half an inch into the salt, grounding itself. Its slate collar rotates with a faint, gritty *shhh-click*, orienting its milky lenses toward the empty air where nothing appears to be.

It is looking at the seams where the plateau’s geometry has come unstitched. Where a displaced shadow sweeps across the salt, or where the *clank* of a joint three miles north arrives out of order and collides with a stray magnetic wake from the slag-tangle below, the air itself develops a tiny, localized eddy of ungrounded potential—a pocket that hums a half-step higher than the basin’s drone and bends the daylight like a drop of oil.

The spire-walker steps into that shimmer. Slowly, from the tip of its thirty-foot basalt mast, it unfurls a cluster of fine, copper-plated filaments like a dry, metallic dandelion. When the filaments touch the eddy, there is no flash—only a long, papery *hiss*, like water hitting a skillet left on the stove for an hour. The shimmer in the air flattens out. The displaced shadow of the distant quartz wheel snaps back, for a few seconds, toward its true silhouette. And deep inside the hollow basalt spire, a heavy internal flywheel of meteoric iron—the creature’s heart, its stomach, its only organ—picks up speed by a fraction of a revolution per minute.

Then it retracts its filaments, unscrews its brass feet from the salt, and begins its agonizingly slow march toward the next frayed edge—one solitary, grazing spire among miles of empty, shimmering nothingness, keeping the great, deafening, bone-dry engine from shaking itself loose from the world.
