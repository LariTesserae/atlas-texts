If you walk far enough out onto the flats, past the first ring of basalt chimneys, you begin to notice that the hum in the soles of your boots is not the same everywhere.

Over bare shale, it is a thin, steady buzz, like a wire strung tight. Near the exposed veins of quartz and copper, it sharpens into a stinging tingle that makes your ankles ache. But every so often, as you skirt one of the low, rust-stained mounds half-hidden in the knee-high mist, the sound beneath your feet drops an octave. It thickens into a heavy, purring thrum—the unmistakable sensation of a current meeting a load.

At first glance, the mounds look like nothing more than slumped heaps of iron-rich clay or old, weathered slag. They are four or five feet long, broad and low, sitting flush against the wet mud beside the copper veins. Only if you stand still beside one—long enough for your eyes to adjust to the flat daylight and the drift of warm steam—do you realize it is not rock.

It is breathing. Or at least, it is keeping time.

Every three seconds, when the nearest basalt chimney lifts its ironstone plug with a deafening *THUMP* and vents its plume of white steam, the heavy, mud-colored ridge before you visibly flattens. A row of small, wet vents along its flank opens to expel a faint, silent puff of gas. Then, as the plug drops back with a metallic *clack* and the three-second pause begins, the creature slowly swells again, drawing the warm, damp air back in.

It has no head, no eyes, and no face. Its hide is thick, leathery, and stained the same dull rust and verdigris as the valley floor, except along its spine, where it has packed its own flesh with heavy, rounded lobes of black iron sand. Bound together by a clear, salty mucus, the iron grains have sintered over decades into a matte, metallic crust. Beneath the thinner, translucent skin of its lower flanks, you can see the faint, dull-orange gleam of coiled vessels—thick, pulsing tubes filled not with blood, but with a dense, copper-rich brine.

This is what the natural sluice-boxes of the basin are feeding.

When one of these creatures needs to move, it does so with a patience that makes the bronze-shelled crustaceans look frantic. You will not see it creep; you will only notice, if you watch for an hour, that the smooth, wet smear in the clay behind it has lengthened by the width of your hand. Using a broad, muscular lip at its leading edge, it sifts through the gleaming pockets of sorted grit left in the shallow channels—drawing iron sand up into the pockets of its back to weight and shield its core, and taking in raw copper granules to replace what slowly corrodes away inside its conductive channels.

Once it has fed, it drags itself across an exposed vein of native copper, presses a wide, pale grounding-pad at its trailing end flat against the damp rock, and settles in to complete the circuit.

Running current through itself keeps it warm—warmer than the lukewarm brine pools, warmer than the mud. In the flat, featureless stretches between the chimneys, these creatures are quiet islands of heat. If you look closely at one, you will almost always find three or four of the palm-sized bronze crustaceans clinging to its iron-crusted back, grazing methodically on the sulfur-yellow films that precipitate there. They do not seem bothered by the deep vibration running through their host; in fact, they keep its hide clean of mineral scale, preventing the heavy creature from shorting out or calcifying into actual stone. Now and then, one of the pale, leathery-winged flyers will flutter down out of the mist to rest on the warm, humming ridge, sipping the pure condensation that beads along its cooler edges before launching back into the three-second rhythm of the vents.

For a long time, you might assume these creatures are entirely solitary, locked in their own slow, silent circuits.

Then, while you are standing on the wet shale, one of them will speak.

It starts as a subtle shift in the buzz under your boots. The creature beside you tenses its heavy flanks and lifts the edge of its pale grounding-pad a fraction of an inch off the copper vein. The circuit breaks, and a jagged, blue-white spark snaps across the wet gap with a sharp, wet *CRACK*—the exact sound you heard from the ridge, smelling suddenly of ozone and scorched brine.

The pad settles back down. The deep purr resumes.

And a quarter-mile away, across the misty, empty flats, another low, rust-colored mound lifts its edge and answers with a bright *crack* of its own, sending a brief, stinging pulse straight through the rock beneath your feet.

They do not fight, and they do not flee. There is nothing in the basin to hurry for, and nothing to hide from. A single one of them may creep along the same hundred yards of copper vein for a century or more, growing heavier with each passing decade as more iron sand packs into its back and more mineral crust settles into its hide. Eventually, it simply slows until it stops. Its grounding-pad fuses to the vein; its iron core hardens into solid, sintered stone. Even then, it does not rot or vanish—it merely becomes part of the valley's clean, ancient geometry, still humming softly in the mist, still conducting the endless current for whatever crawls past it next.
