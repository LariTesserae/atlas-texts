If you were to press your face against the outside of the quartz oculus—assuming you could survive the thin, freezing air at that altitude—you would not see it at first. You would see the mustard-colored tiles, the slow sweep of the kelp curtains, the silver flash of the siphon-shrimp, and you might assume the room was entirely given over to small, mindless things.

Then the forty-second *clack* would sound, the floor-vent would erupt in its roaring column of bubbles, and one of the heavy shadows wedged beneath the sagging catwalk would uncoil.

It has been in the room so long—or its kind has, generation after silent generation—that the gyre has shaped it the same way it shaped the bolt-heads and the intake-flanges. Every sharp angle on its body has been rubbed down. It is roughly the length of a tall human, though it never stands upright; there is no reason to stand in a room with no air and a spinning impeller beneath the floor-grate. Its limbs are long, lean, and strangely boneless-looking in the current, ending in broad, flat-nailed hands and feet whose toes are thick and splayed for hooking into the diamond gaps of the iron grating.

Its skin is the color of tallow steeped in weak tea—pale enough to catch the trembling shafts of daylight from the window, yet stained along the shoulders, knees, and spine with faint, permanent washes of verdigris and iron-oxide rust. Where the water is warmest, near its ribs, three pairs of dark, feathery gill-slits open and close in a slow, unhurried rhythm that does not match its heartbeat. Instead, they pulse in strict time with the *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the brass impeller below.

When it rests, it tucks itself into the hammock of the catwalk, tangled among the translucent fern-vines, letting the current rock it like a pendulum. A thin, milky membrane slides over its large, round eyes, shielding them from the blinding noon glare through the quartz. In that state, with emerald sponge-moss growing in small, tolerated patches across the calloused pads of its elbows and heels, it looks like nothing so much as an old piece of drowned rigging.

It is not a beast, though it has no language. You cannot speak in water, and even if you could, the room is far too loud. Instead, it has *listening*—the same full-body listening that the thread-eels use, though sharper. Its pale skull is heavy and dense, built to take the vibration of the turbine through the teeth and jawbone without going numb.

It wakes when the rhythm changes, or when the room needs tending.

At the metallic *clack* of the ceiling valve, it drops from the catwalk—not fleeing the superheated column of bubbles like the shrimp, but slipping right up to the roaring edge of it. It turns slowly in the violent upward draft, letting the scalding froth scour the slick biofilm and stray algae from its back and flanks. The copper-bellied skates peel off the tiles and glide out of its way as it passes, though the blind thread-eels ignore it, weaving right between its ribs to snap at the scattered shrimp. It never harms the eels unless one grows long enough to tangle near the floor-grate; then it catches the creature behind the jaw, breaks its neck with a single, practiced twist, and feeds it to the turbine.

Its real work begins in the quiet seconds after the bubbles shatter against the dome, when the organ-barnacles along the three bronze standpipes begin their wet, hollow, three-note whistling.

It swims to the north wall with long, effortless strokes—more like a seal than a swimmer, using the flat of its palms to steer against the heavy gyre—and hooks one foot around a standpipe. Slowly, methodically, it runs a horn-thick thumbnail along the fluted rims of the barnacles. It scrapes away the excess calcium that threatens to choke their mouths, and clears out the pockets of iron-oxide silt that settle in their bases. If a barnacle has cracked or gone sour in pitch, it crushes the tube cleanly in its fist and lets the current carry the fragments down to the kelp roots.

Sometimes, while it clings there in the dimness behind the pipes, its throat swells. A deep, wet, rattling hum vibrates out from the hollow of its chest—a fourth note, low and bronze-colored, that threads itself into the barnacles' dissonant chord until the *clack* strikes again and shatters the music.

It is the only creature in the chamber that keeps the room from choking on itself. Once a day—measured by the angle of the sun-shafts cutting from the western wall—it swims along the underside of the catwalk and harvests the thickest curtains of warm-water kelp, tearing them free with its teeth and hands before they can grow heavy enough to drag the iron grating down into the turbine. It eats the translucent bladders from the fern-vines, popping them between its molars for the sweet, mineral-rich jelly inside, and occasionally pins a fat skate against the mustard tiles, cracking its cartilage with a smooth, palm-sized river-stone of vitrified rust it keeps wedged in a pipe-joint.

And always, before it returns to the catwalk to sleep, it swims to the western wall.

At the base of the convex quartz oculus, it kneels on the rounded lip of the bronze sluice, careful not to block the outflow. With one hooked finger, it rakes away any stray kelp or dead shrimp caught in the drain, freeing the narrow stream so the faint, continuous snow of gold mica-scale can keep drifting out toward the sky.

Then it rises a few feet and presses both palms—and sometimes the cool, smooth curve of its forehead—against the glass.

The quartz is warm on its side, warmed by the boiler-core miles below, and bitterly cold on the other. Through the thick lens, it looks out at the thinned, blinding blue of the upper sky and the white tops of the thunderheads drifting far below like spilled wool. It does not know what air is. The only air it has ever met is the violent, scalding, silver thing that tears up from the floor-vent every forty seconds and shatters against the dome.

So when it looks out through the window, it must think it is looking into another room—an impossibly vast, still, blue cistern with no walls, no kelp, no *thrum*, and nothing at all to keep clean. It watches the clouds drift for the span of three or four valve-strikes, its gill-slits pulsing steadily in the bath-warm water, its milky eyelids half-lowered against the glare.

Then the impeller grinds, the pressure builds, the ceiling valve *clacks* like an anvil, and it pushes off the glass—sliding back into the bright, loud, endless circle of the only world it knows.
