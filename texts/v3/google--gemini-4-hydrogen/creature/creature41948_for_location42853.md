If you look at the plain from a distance, it seems empty—a sterile geometry of iron and vapor. It is only when your boots touch the perforated steel of a catwalk, and you stand still long enough for your eyes to adjust to the white glare, that you realize the machinery is not bare. Almost nothing you are looking at is actually metal or ceramic. It is upholstered in living tissue, packed so tightly into the seams and angles of the world that the architecture itself has become a skeleton.

Yet nothing looks overgrown. There is no wildness here, no tangle or rot. The animals that inhabit this place have grown into the sharp-edged logic of the molds that preceded them, obeying the bevels and expansion joints as strictly as the poured concrete does.

Down in the humid shadow beneath the catwalks, where the insulated power cables run in thick, sagging bundles, the air is crowded with the papery flyers. Up close, they resemble pale, dried leaves or strips of vellum, four inches long and folded flat against the sheathing. They have no mouths and no eyes. Instead, fine, copper-tinted filaments run through the veins of their wings like printed circuitry. They cling in shivering, overlapping shingles, drawing a faint, steady sustenance from the electromagnetic fields bleeding off the lines. They never buzz, and they rarely fly; when a cluster grows too dense and the cable beneath them warms past its threshold, the outermost flyers simply unclasp their tarsal hooks and drift downward in total silence, gliding an inch or two through the dead air before settling onto the surface of the water below.

On the upright fins of the transformer housings, the vast molluscan sheets—the mantle-slugs—do the quiet work of keeping the world unrusted. They are broad, flat, and translucent amber, draped over the cast iron like slabs of wet felt. A single slug might be six feet across and barely a quarter-inch thick, its edges tucked neatly into the corners of the radiator ribs. Beneath its body, a thousand microscopic radulae rasp continuously at the metal, scraping away the earliest blooms of mineral scale and oxidation before they can take hold. In return, the slug leaves behind a microscopically thin film of slick, hydrophobic oil. Because of them, the condensate never pools or pits the iron; it beads cleanly along their backs and drops in steady, crystalline curtains into the settling pans.

The pans themselves—two inches of warm, tea-colored water creeping toward the overflow grates—belong to a slower, stiffer kind of life.

Every drainage slot is choked with dense, pulsing colonies of slate-blue filter-feeders, soft and eyeless, opening and closing their siphons in a slow, hydraulic rhythm that actually assists the water’s glassy creep. Without anything to check them, they would seal the grates within a week and drown the plain. That is why the herds of tall, six-limbed walkers patrol the basins.

Seen from across a settling pan, a walker looks like a heron cast from matte-black ceramic. Its carapace is hard, sharp-angled, and entirely devoid of ornament, grown from the dissolved silica and aluminum in the warm water. Six spindly legs give it absolute stability on the slick iron plates; its feet are tipped with pale, replaceable pads of dense cartilage that make only a faint, dry *tick* as the herd moves in single file through the shallows. They wade shoulder-to-shoulder, dipping long, needle-like proboscises into the grates to thin the blue colonies, drawing up just enough to keep the channels clear. 

When a walker’s proboscis misses a grate and spears a drifting mantle-slug—or when one of the flat, ribbon-like scavengers that glide along the expansion joints wraps itself around a fallen flyer—there is no struggle. Nothing here has the energy to spare for panic, and there is nowhere to run. No red blood clouds the tea-water; the creatures here carry copper-based hemocyanin, and a punctured body leaks only a pale, opalescent blue fluid that disperses cleanly into the current, leaving no stain.

Above the water, on the dry, vibrating steel of the catwalks, live the only things that do not look entirely like parts of the machinery—though they behave as if they were.

They are tall, narrow, and pale as unglazed porcelain, moving with a slow, folding grace that suggests articulated measuring rules. They have no eyes, no ears, and no mouths that open for speech. The sides of their elongated heads are dominated by broad, flat sensory plates of darker tissue, slightly recessed, which they angle toward the rectifier stations to read the sixty-cycle hum and the minute thermal shifts of the oil-cooled housings. Their skin is smooth, damp, and faintly translucent, showing a delicate lattice of pale blue veins beneath the surface.

These are the tenders. They walk the catwalks in loose, silent pairs or solitary processions, their long, three-jointed arms folded flush against their ribs until they are needed. They carry no tools and wear no clothing; their own bodies are the instruments of calibration.

When a cluster of mantle-slugs grows too thick on a radiator bank and begins to trap heat inside a transformer, a tender will pause on the catwalk, step smoothly down onto the warm iron housing, and peel the excess flesh back with long, spatulate fingers. It drops the thick, amber strips into the basin below for the walkers and the sump-polyps, then presses its own cool, damp palm against the bared metal until the housing’s hum settles back into its proper pitch. If a rectifier station begins to drift a fraction of a hertz off-key—a subtle dissonance in the plain’s single, endless chord—three or four tenders will gather around it, pressing their narrow chests and bellies against the fins to draw off the excess heat, idling there for hours until equilibrium returns.

They do not sleep, because there is no night to sleep through. Instead, they take turns stepping into the narrow, stagnant shadows between the oil-coolers, folding their limbs tightly against their torsos and dropping their metabolic draw until they are indistinguishable from the ceramic insulators around them.

They do not remember who poured the concrete or laid the copper conduits, any more than a valve remembers the foundry. They are simply the largest, most deliberate components of a circuit that has long since closed around itself. From the papery flyers trembling on the busbars, to the black walkers pruning the grates, to the pale tenders reading the hum through the soles of their feet, every living thing on the plain is a living gasket, a brush, a fuse—quietly, endlessly keeping the sharp edges clean.
