They do not call themselves anything, because a name spoken aloud would be a death sentence, and in the perpetual twilight of the basin, even a whisper is an unnecessary risk. To one another, they are known by the weight of their step on a shared ledge, by the particular knot-pattern braided into their wrist-cords, and by the rhythm of the tugs they send across the mist.

They are a small, lean, pale-skinned people, shaped as much by the basin’s silence as by its cold. Generations of living beneath the pewter cloud-lid have left their eyes wide and pale-irised, perpetually dilated to catch the bruised light and the faint, sickly amber rising from the fungal beds below. Their skin is cool and faintly waxy from constant dampness, their fingertips permanently stained dark plum from the sap of bladder-fronds and yellow-brown from fungus-paste. They move with a deliberate, unhurried grace—knees slightly bent, weight rolling silently from the outer edge of a moss-soled boot to the ball of the foot, testing every slab of splintered slate before committing to it. A loose shard that shifts and *clacks* underfoot is as fatal as a frayed rope.

Even their clothing is engineered for quiet. Their heavy cloaks of matted, oiled moss-felt are weighted along the hems with small, smoothed pebbles of slate so the fabric cannot snap or flutter in a sudden draft. Beneath the felt, they wear supple tunics of cured stilt-predator hide, greased to shed the relentless condensation, and fingerless mitts wrapped around their knuckles to keep bare bone from scraping against volcanic glass.

Because they never speak, their throats are stiff, unused instruments, and their greatest communal terror is not hunger or the sheer drops, but a winter cough. In the cold, wet air, a simple chest-rattle is a mortal threat to an entire terrace; a single barking cough sends ripples through the mist that draw the thread-jellies drifting in toward the doorways. Every household keeps jars of a bitter, numbing paste rendered from boiled dew-moss and the pith of bladder-fronds. At the first tickle in the throat, they chew it until their tongues and windpipes go dead and slick. If a sickness takes hold anyway, the afflicted person retreats—or is carried—into a *muffle-chamber*: a windowless, double-walled slate dome packed thick between the stones with inflated bladder-fronds and hung inside with dense curtains of damp moss. There, sealed in total acoustic isolation, they wait out the fever in the dark, coughing into wet rags until they either heal or suffocate.

Children are raised in these same moss-stuffed domes. For the first years of life, an infant never sees the open ledges; they are kept swaddled, nursed at the slightest twitch, and soothed before a cry can gather in their lungs. They learn to read the world through touch before they learn to walk—feeling the pulse of a mother’s fingers against their collarbone, learning the slow, fluid grammar of hand-signs in the dim glow of a fungus-wick lamp. A child’s first test on the outer walkways is not climbing, but carrying a clay bowl of cistern-water across a narrow, cantilevered slab without spilling a drop—not for balance alone, but because a splash makes a sound.

Up close, inside the dry-stone huts sealed with resinous paste, they speak with their hands—not in the air, where quick gestures might stir a draft, but skin-to-skin. Two people sit knee to knee, clasping forearms or pressing their palms together, tapping and sliding complex sentences into each other’s wrists. Across the misty voids between terraces, they rely on the pale cloth-sweeps and the rhythm-cords. The cords are greased with glider-oil so they do not hum in the damp air, and each household keeps a hanging bone knot beside the doorframe. When a neighbor tugs a line three hundred feet away, the knot twitches silently against the stone. The elderly—those whose knees have grown too stiff to trust on the wet glass—spend their days seated by the threshold with one hand resting lightly on the bone, acting as the house’s listener, translating the faintest tremors from across the chasm.

Their daily labor is a slow, surgical pantomime. To harvest a bladder-frond without letting its trapped gas *pop*, a harvester presses a pad of sopping moss against the rubbery skin and slides a hollow bone awl through the center, letting the gas hiss harmlessly into the wet fiber until the leaf goes limp. To kill a trapped moss-crab without cracking its matte shell, they slip a thin needle of stilt-predator bone beneath the carapace joint, or lower the whole wicker basket into a covered cistern until it drowns. They keep no fires that might crackle; instead, they warm their huts and steep their fungal stews over smokeless, slow-smoldering cakes of compressed moss-fiber and glider-fat, buried deep in stone hearths.

They do not fight the thread-jellies; they shepherd them. When too many of the translucent sacs gather around a mist-comb or a counterweight lift, a warden creeps out onto a distant, uninhabited spur of rock and sets a *tock-lure*: a clay cup with a pinhole in its base, suspended over a flat shard of volcanic glass. The warden retreats silently into the mist. Half an hour later, when the cup fills with condensation and a single heavy drop strikes the glass—*tock*—the filaments whip taut toward the empty spur, and the jellies drift away from the walkways.

There is no wood here, and no earth deep enough to dig a grave. When someone dies, their family wraps the body in their weighted felt cloak, binds it with braided frond-fiber, and carries it to the nearest counterweight lift. Slowly, without a creak from the greased sinew cables, they lower the bundle down through the pewter twilight, past the grazing moss-crabs and the silent gliders, into the cold, pooled air at the very bottom of the basin.

There, in the thick amber gloom, the gourd-fungi quickly take hold of the damp felt. For a few seasons afterward, the household on the upper ledges can look down into the dark at the base of the cliff and pick out a slightly brighter, swollen patch of sickly gold—the quiet, steady glow of their kin, still keeping watch in the mist.
