At first, looking down into the bright, silent hollow, it is easy to miss them altogether. Their layered coats of grey felt and moss-dyed wool match the pewter and emerald of the cushion-plants so closely that a figure kneeling on a roof-terrace looks like another swell of turf, and someone resting on a dished threshold blends into the pitted chalk behind them. Only the thin, unmoving plume of breath hanging before a wrapped face gives them away.

They are small, compact people, round-shouldered from generations of ducking through low doorways and turning sideways in shoulder-wide alleys. Most of their lives are spent bundled inside garments that are scarcely ever removed—layers of felted wool, scraped vole-pelt, and woven sedge, patched and re-stitched with sinew until each person wears a kind of soft, thickened shell. What little skin shows above their high, fat-greased collars is pale as weathered limestone, untouched by windburn, though the cold keeps a faint, permanent flush high on their cheekbones.

Their eyes are shaped by the bowl’s relentless, shadowless glare. Beneath heavy, pale-lashed lids, their pupils stay pinned to sharp dark points, and most of them smear a thin line of dark algae-paste from the water troughs into the hollows under their eyes to dull the white bounce off the selenite seams. When they look up at the flat sky, or down into the clear cisterns, they do so through a perpetual, patient squint.

Their hands, by contrast, are bare whenever the work requires it—broad, short-fingered, and toughened by damp chalk and freezing water. Their fingernails are thick as horn from parting dense mats of moss and prying pale tubers out of the mortarless joints. Even in the still chill, their fingers move with steady, unhurried certainty, whether scraping a hide with a flint flake or threading a bone needle through a torn hem.

They do not speak. Perhaps their throats are still capable of it, in the way a bird’s throat holds a cry it never uses, or perhaps centuries of living stacked six tiers high in a windless, echo-less pit have bred the habit clean out of them. In a place where moss swallows every footstep and even water slides without a sound, a raised voice would feel like a stone thrown through thin ice. Instead, they communicate in weight and inclination. Passing on a stone-slab bridge, two figures turn sideways and press the backs of their hands lightly against each other’s ribs—a quiet acknowledgment of balance and passage. A pair of fingers tapped twice against a collarbone asks if the upper cisterns are full; a palm resting for three slow heartbeats over a friend’s wrist reads the warmth of their blood and the ease of their pulse. Even their disagreements are silent: a turned shoulder, a door-latch tied shut with a double knot of gut instead of a single loop.

Without chimneys or hearths—for smoke would foul the dead air, and open flame would break the bowl’s delicate frost-balance—warmth is their rarest possession, hoarded as carefully as the condensed water. Inside the thick-walled chalk cells, behind the scraped rawhide shutters and cloudy panes of split gypsum, the rooms are dim, close, and low-ceilinged, smelling of lanolin, damp peat, and old wool. They sleep heaped together on deep mattresses of dried sphagnum and sedge, sharing heat beneath mountains of patched pelts. They have trained their bodies into a slow, economical rhythm, breathing shallowly and moving without waste, burning very little. They eat cold, dense foods: tubers chewed raw until their starches turn sweet, tart berries softened by the perpetual near-freeze and crushed into rendered fat, dried snail-meat, and the occasional strip of salted blubber.

They do not rule the hollow so much as they form one more layer of its quiet ecology, bound tightly to the smaller lives that share the stone.

The thick-furred voles that slip through the chinks in the dry-stone walls are neither pests nor pets; they nest in the warm spaces between the inner rawhide linings and the outer chalk, fed on tuber-parings and crushed seeds. In return, their small, warm bodies help insulate the dwellings, and when they die, their pelts are scraped and sewn into the linings of boots and mitts.

The round-bodied birds with soft, owl-like plumage roost undisturbed beneath the curtains of waxy liverwort. They glide silently between the upper terraces to snap up the tiny pale midges that hatch over the water troughs, and a bird will sometimes settle on the wool-wrapped shoulder of a gardener kneeling in the moss, waiting motionless beside the bone needle to snatch any grub uncovered in the roots.

Along the hand-carved moisture grooves, the people tend the pale, translucent snails like slow cattle, gently lifting them from bare stone to patches of over-thick algae so the water-films keep sliding down into the troughs. And in the sunken cisterns themselves, they watch the cold-blooded newts with quiet care. No one dips a clay pot into a basin without first looking down through the clear water to see the newts hanging suspended there, their red-feathered gills pulsing faintly. As long as the newts are steady and bright-gilled, the water is good.

Only rarely—when timber is needed for a ladder, or a copper pin, or fresh hides to replace those worn thin—do a few of them climb the dished steps to the lip of the bowl. They go only when thick sea-fog smothers the gravel plain, slipping out into the windless grey to scavenge driftwood, bone, or seal-carcass from the distant tideline, returning as quickly and silently as they can before the gales rise.

To an outsider peering over the rim, they might look trapped, or diminished—small, silent figures huddled in a cold well. Yet down among the cushioned roofs and glowing chalk alleys, there is no sense of want or haste. Outside the bowl, the world is scoured by salt wind, violent thaws, and sudden freezes; things break, blow away, and rot. Inside, they have found the exact, bright stillness where nothing spoils and nothing shatters, living at the same patient pace as the condensing water and the slow-growing moss.
