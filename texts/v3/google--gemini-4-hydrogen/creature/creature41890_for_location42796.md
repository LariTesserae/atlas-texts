If you stay on the upper gantry long enough for the vapor to soften the creases in your collar, you begin to notice the small, irregular interruptions in the house’s rhythm.

The machinery has its own steady grammar: the *tock* of a brass float-valve, the wet sigh of a felt-baffled vent, the slow, greased tick of a counterweight chain paying out an inch at a time. Against that, every few minutes, comes a sound that is neither water nor iron—a crisp, double *snick* of shears, or the scrape of a flat blade against ceramic, followed by the soft *plop* of a severed stem dropping into a sump.

Someone is down there in the green.

You won’t see them clearly from above at first. The foliage is three tiers deep, and the light pouring through the clerestory baffles breaks into damp, shifting shafts that turn every oiled truss and broad elephant-ear leaf into a silhouette. But if you follow the catwalk down past the heat-exchangers, where the air grows thick enough to taste of iron and warm celery, you find the keeper of the place.

He is not an old man, though the damp has given his skin the pale, slightly puckered look of someone who has spent months underwater. He wears only heavy-soled rubber boots, faded canvas shorts, and a sleeveless leather apron darkened at the chest with boiler-sweat and green sap. A pair of brass-handled shears hangs from his hip on a length of tarred twine, alongside a small oilcan and a stubby scraping knife. His knuckles are crosshatched with fine, red nicks—the signature of the extruded terracotta troughs, whose edges haven’t yet been dulled by time or salt.

He moves along the narrow grated walkways with a curious, sideways economy, ducking under low manifolds without looking up, his shoulder brushing the wet leaves with the unhurried familiarity of someone walking through his own curtains. He doesn’t fight the jungle, because there is too much of it and it grows too fast. Instead, he edits it.

Right now, the whole building is in its first, reckless season, and everything green is trying to eat the plumbing. He pauses by a flanged joint where a cluster of pale, chlorophyll-tinted roots has wrapped itself three times around a warm feed-pipe, prying gently at the gasket. With two deft turns of his knife, he unwinds the thickest tendon, trims it back to the ceramic lip, and tucks the loose end toward the fiberglass mat where it belongs. Farther along, he stops at a pressure-valve whose stem is choked with the black, wire-stiff stems of mineral ferns. He pulls them out by the fistful—his palms are permanently stained graphite-black from the valve-packing—and drops them through the grating into the sump below, where the Archimedes screws will eventually chew them into slurry and lift them back to the roof.

He knows the house by its minor rebellions. He knows that the third cistern on the west wall trips two seconds late because a link in its counterweight chain is stiff with fresh paint; he knows that the bladder-vines near the south glass are swelling faster than the ones in the shade, and will burst before sundown if he doesn’t prick them with a needle to bleed off the weight.

His name doesn’t matter much here—there is no one else to call it, unless you count the steelworkers who rode the crane-skip down through the clouds six weeks ago, leaving him with three crates of tinned provisions, a coil of spare packing-twine, and a notebook full of pressure tables. He is a *commissioner*: the kind of solitary mechanic-gardener hired to live inside a newly-built station during its first year, before the concrete finishes curing and the plants learn where the pipes end.

He has done this before, on other piers above other cloud-decks. He prefers places that still smell of fresh mortar and bright rivets. Once a conservatory settles—once the iron takes on an honest coat of rust, the terracotta rounds off, and the canopy grows thick and polite enough for a regular crew to manage—he usually packs his kit and goes looking for the next empty frame.

Because nothing here was built for him to sleep in, he has made a dry island for himself in the only place the vapor can’t reach: a narrow shelf of marine plywood slung directly above the main boiler-casings, tucked between two insulated copper busbars. The radiant heat from the housings keeps the air there crisp and toasted, just dry enough that his blankets don’t mildew. Up on that shelf, inside a soldered copper chest, he keeps the things that belong to the cold world outside: two dry wool shirts, a tin of loose tea, a whetstone, and a stack of paper notebooks whose pages have gone wavy at the edges despite the silica packets tucked between them.

He eats mostly out of tins, heated on the flat top of a steam-trap, though lately he has begun to supplement his rations with the house itself. He boils the tender, curled tips of the sump-ferns in salted water and chews the pale, crisp stems of a trailing vine that tastes faintly of sorrel and wet copper. He drinks the condensate straight from the lead-lined gutters—not the mineral-heavy water from the sumps, which tastes of slag and dead leaves, but the clean, silver threads weeping off the upper copper mesh, cool from the mountain air outside and soft as melted snow.

He is not entirely alone, though the company is quiet.

In the mornings, while the boilers are still humming up to full heat, he checks on the few creatures that have found their way into this suspended room. There is the tiny, nearly translucent frog that lives under the brass bezel of the third-tier pressure gauge; he never wipes that particular dial clean, leaving a film of condensation so its skin stays slick, and in return it picks off the minute midges that breed in the wet fiberglass. There are the pale, flat-shelled snails he brought up himself in a damp rag from the valley—two dozen of them, seeded into the lower sump-troughs to keep the algae from filming over the Archimedes screws. Already they have laid clusters of clear, jelly-like eggs along the dark iron ribs, multiplying in the warm, mineral-rich dark.

And once in a great while, when he is up on the clerestory catwalk clearing drowned moths from the gutters, the little long-legged wren slips through the upper louvers. For a few seconds, they share the same shaft of high-altitude light—the man with his stained hands resting on the warm iron rail, the bird cocking its head from a copper baffle, sharp-eyed and impatient, smelling of ozone and thin, freezing air. Then it snatches a beetle from the lead channel and vanishes back into the sky.

He watches it go, wipes the sweat from his forehead with the back of a forearm, and climbs back down into the green.

Down below, a float-valve gives its polite, brass *tock*. A counterweight drops an inch. Somewhere in the dark under the grates, the screws turn slowly, lifting the rich, black water back toward the light. He walks on, shears ready, listening for the next small thing that needs mending—settled, for now, in the warm, wet middle of a machine that is still learning how to live.
