If you descended into the vault with a dive-light, you would not see it at first. You would see the dead lamps, the bruised amber glow of the living one, the pale pollock hanging in the eddies, and the long, slow turn of the turbine below. Only after your eyes adjusted to the silt and your chest grew used to the *thrum-scrape* of the warped bronze vane might you notice that one of the concrete-colored humps draped over the frozen gantry crane is breathing.

It has lived in the upper third of the vault for so long that it has taken on the room’s complexion. Its hide is thick, slack-folded, and the color of wet, aggregate concrete, mottled with the same colorless biofilm and frost-like patches of branching bryozoans that coat the piers. Along the ridges of its broad shoulders and the backs of its forearms, small clusters of gooseneck barnacles have anchored themselves, their shells clicking faintly when it shifts its weight. It does not look like something that swims. It looks like something that was poured into the chamber with the foundations and left behind when the forms were stripped.

Yet it is alive, and it is the reason the third brass cage-lamp still burns.

Hanging from the corroded rails of the gantry crane, its long, heavy-jointed limbs wrapped around the rusted box-girder, it occupies the only warm place in the room. The third lamp’s slight thermal plume—barely a degree or two warmer than the numbing eight-degree current—rises straight up through the dark water, carrying with it the scent of heated brass, red algae, and the frantic, flickering clouds of mysid shrimp. The creature sits directly in that rising column, suspended like a sloth in the canopy of a drowned forest. Its head is blunt and tallow-pale, almost featureless where a face ought to be, save for two small, milky eyes sunk deep beneath heavy brow-ridges and a series of sensitive, fluted pits along its jaw that tremble in time with the turbine’s twelve revolutions a minute. Behind thick muscular flaps at the base of its neck, dark-red, feathery gills pulse in a slow, deliberate cadence—not the rhythm of a frightened animal, but the heavy, patient tempo of the machine below.

It has no concept of electricity, or of the auxiliary dynamo coupled to the main shaft, or of the men who wrapped the umbilical in tar centuries ago. It knows only that the amber cone is its hearth, and that the hearth requires tending.

Every few weeks, when the pale calcium carbonate in the seawater begins to film over the third lamp’s thick glass—threatening to choke the light into the same dead grey as its two neighbors—the creature unhooks itself from the gantry. It descends the rusted chain-fall hand over hand, its broad, calloused digits gripping the slick iron links with a slow, crushing certainty. Wrapping two limbs around the warm brass cage, it takes a flat shard of broken shell it keeps wedged in the crane’s dry grease-cup and methodically scrapes the mineral scale from the glass. The silver-grey rockfish scatter into the gloom while it works, and the dark red algae brushes against its pale knuckles, but the mysid shrimp simply orbit its shoulders, drawn to the newly brightened glow. When the tar-wrapped umbilical catches a drifting length of snapped cable or a mat of shed kelp washed down from the intake shaft, it climbs down into the roaring hiss of the main current, bracing its massive, barnacle-crusted back against a concrete pier while it teases the tangle free.

How it came here is written only in the shape of its body—built for crushing pressure and total dark, too dense to float, too heavy-boned for the open sea above. Most likely it slipped up through the deeper fault beyond the sump grate when it was small, drawn by the endless conveyor belt of plankton and stunned fish pouring down from the ocean’s floor, and grew too large—or too accustomed to the *thrum-scrape* and the amber warmth—to ever squeeze back down into the dark below.

It rarely descends to the floor grates when the tide is slack. There is little need. When the tidal surge peaks, however, and the ocean’s weight drives the great turbine faster, the whole vault changes pitch. The *thrum-scrape* quickens into a low, continuous groan; flakes of rust shiver down from the gantry rails; the white anemones on the pipe flanges bloom wide. Below, the schools of pollock and rockfish drop lower, pressing themselves into the dead-water eddies behind the piers.

Then the keeper drops down with them.

It does not chase its prey. It moves along the vertical iron ladders and the slack haulage cables like a shadow detached from the masonry, hanging upside-down in the lee of the east manifold. Its milky eyes are nearly useless in the gloom, so it hunts by the vault’s other clock: the massive, counterweighted check-valve that seats and unseats every forty seconds with a dull, metallic *boom*.

The creature waits, its gill-flaps still, its jaw-pits drinking in the vibration of the water. When the *boom* strikes—sending a visible shudder through the floor silt and momentarily scrambling the lateral lines of every fish in the eddy—it lunges. A single long arm shoots out with the sudden, heavy violence of the intake water itself, snatching a plump, disoriented pollock out of the current before the echo has even faded from the concrete ribs.

The large brown crabs scuttling along the catwalks do not flee it; it rarely bothers with their armored shells, leaving them to pick at the scraps that drift down toward the sump. The conger eels, coiled deep inside the open junction boxes and the hollow bearing-housings, merely watch it pass with blunt, rhythmic indifference, knowing it will not reach where the bronze vanes graze.

When it has fed, it climbs back up the ironwork, past the twenty-foot edge of the amber light, and settles once more into the rusted cradle of the gantry crane. The current combs its pale biofilm; the warm plume rises past its chest; the turbine turns, and turns, and turns beneath it. In the bruised dark of the sunken cathedral, the machine and its tenant keep each other alive, bound together by a single filament of light and the endless, roaring weight of the sea.
