At first, from the rim, you might mistake the people for another kind of hydraulic component—dark, steady shapes moving along the catwalks at the same unhurried pace as the overhead trolleys. Only when you come down into the canyons between the buildings do you see how densely the basin is inhabited, and how thoroughly the place has shaped the bodies and habits of the people who keep it running.

They are small-stepped, broad-shouldered, and perpetually layered against the cold damp. Everyone wears the same practical livery of the climate: thick wool underneath, oiled canvas over it, and boots soled in compressed, tarred felt that swallow the sound of every footfall. Because there is no wind to speak of in the low basin, their faces are not weathered in the usual way; instead, they have the pale, tight-grained skin of people who spend their lives in cool shadow and brief pockets of machine-warmth. Their hands are the most expressive part of them—broad, calloused across the palms from winch cable and quarried stone, the knuckles permanently darkened with graphite grease and the fine white dust of chalk.

They do not speak aloud if they can help it. A cough or a dropped wrench draws a dozen sharp, disapproving looks, not out of anger, but the way a sudden glare hurts eyes adjusted to the dark. Over generations, their speech has retreated into their fingers and their slates. A rigger’s hand-sign is brisk, clipped, and designed to be read one-handed while the other hand holds a spanner or steadies a swinging beam; it uses the whole upper body—a tilt of the chin for *tension*, a flat palm pressed to the chest for *anchored*, two fingers tapped against the collarbone for *rising floor*. At every belt hangs a small, iron-framed slate and a stub of soapstone. In the crowded, silent canteens, you hear no voices at all—only the soft *shhh* of felt soles on duckboards, the dull thud of ceramic mugs set onto rubber coasters, and the dry, rapid *skritch-skritch* of slates being wiped and passed across trestle tables.

Because the basin floor refuses to stay level, the people have no concept of a permanent address. A family does not live at a street number; they live inside a *module*—a bolted, three-room box of timber and riveted iron with a brass coupling-plate riveted beside the door. You might go to sleep with your kitchen window looking out onto the steam-mist of a conservatory roof, and wake to find the whole block has been jacked onto dollies overnight and winched two hundred yards east to clear a buckling shelf of basalt, leaving your window facing the humming, moss-furred flank of a dynamo hall. 

Nobody complains about this. To live here is to accept that the ground is still making up its mind. Furniture is bolted to the floorboards or built to fold flat into the walls. Heirlooms are small, dense, and unbreakable: cast-iron kettles, brass plumb-bobs, heavy woollen blankets, and slate-backed mirrors. Children learn to read a spirit level before they learn their letters. They are raised in the velvet hush so completely that even infants rarely wail for long; they are swaddled tight in felt, rocked in slings that hang from the roof-trusses to absorb the micro-tremors of the rock, and taught by age three to point, to tap the floor twice with a toe to announce their approach, and never to startle someone working near a live busbar.

Their days are governed by a quiet, unsentimental choreography. There are the **Riggers**, who spend their shifts suspended in leather harnesses between moving buildings, unbolting anchors and splicing copper conduits without a spark; the **Masons**, who work in teams behind the automated crushers, laying quick-setting mortar that they know will be broken back down into aggregate before the season is out; and the **Listeners**, usually older workers whose knees and wrists have grown sensitive to the barometric and seismic shifts of the valley. You see the Listeners sitting on upturned crates near the junction boxes, eyes half-closed, one bare palm pressed flat against an exposed shelf of raw basalt or watching the ink-black meltwater in a crevice for the faintest tremor. When a Listener lifts a slate and chalks a single arrow, an entire crew of twenty riggers will converge on the nearest tenement without a word, already unfastening the corner bolts before the stone beneath it has even finished heaving.

Feeding thousands of people in a city that exports nothing and sits on barren, unweathered rock requires its own quiet ingenuity. That is the work of the **Warmth-Tenders**, who cultivate every square foot where the machinery bleeds heat. Up on the terraced glass roofs and along the damp, sheltered alleys beside the coolant pipes, they pack crushed basalt grit mixed with shredded bramble, moss, and night-soil into shallow wooden trays. In that thin, mineral-rich warmth, they raise dense crops of frost-tolerant greens, bitter root-tubers, pale cellar-mushrooms, and thick-stalked sedges whose seeds are ground into a dark, nutty flour. The diet is heavy, earthy, and endlessly recycled, washed down with steepings of dried air-fern and bramble leaf. Nothing goes to waste—not the ash from the drying kilns, not the condensation collected from the transformer fins, not even the shed fur of the winch-nesting rodents, which is gathered and felted into boot-liners.

The animals and the people share this crowded, heat-hoarding truce with an intimacy born of tight quarters. The mute, slate-gray birds that pack the conservatory eaves are never shooed away; the Warmth-Tenders leave cracked seeds for them on the gutter-ledges, and in return the birds serve as an early-warning system. When a deep fault is about to shift—hours before even a Listener’s palm can feel it—the birds lift off the glass roofs all at once in a single, soundless gray cloud, wheeling in tight circles until the tremor passes. Down in the damp cable trenches, riggers lift the pale, hand-sized salamanders gently by the belly with gloved fingers whenever a conduit needs re-routing, setting them carefully onto a warm transformer fin while they work and returning them to the moss once the splice is sealed. The salamanders keep the trenches clear of the blind white mites that would otherwise chew through the rubber insulation; in return, they get a steady, electric summer in the middle of a cold basin.

Even the automated freight carts—squat, rubber-wheeled boxes that nose silently along buried copper induction lines—are treated less like tools and more like slow, blind livestock. Workers step aside for them without looking up, pat their dented bumpers as they pass, and chalk small, affectionate tallies on their flanks to track how many tons of fresh stone each one has hauled from the rising floor to the crushers.

Perhaps the strangest inhabitants, though, are the **Ballast-Crews**—the people who work the lowest, wettest tier of the city, down where the buildings end and the raw, fractured geology begins. They rarely come up to the glass roofs. Dressed in heavy rubberized aprons that shine black with condensation, they spend their shifts guiding the long hydraulic rams that shove pulverized aggregate, cracked mortar, and scrap stone back down into the fresh fissures opening in the valley floor. They work by the dim glow of caged electric lamps, in a cold so sharp it turns their breath to mist before their faces, packing the city’s own waste back into the earth’s wounds to keep the basin from tearing itself open.

If you ask one of the Basin-folk—on a slate, of course—why they stay here, why they pour all this tireless, silent labor into a city that sells nothing to the outside world and builds nothing meant to last a decade, they will likely look at you with mild, unblinking surprise, as if you had asked a heart why it bothers to keep beating when it isn't going anywhere.

To them, the basin is not a place you leave, and the city is not a monument. It is a living splint strapped across a broken, still-growing bone. They are the ones who hold it steady—tightening the bolts, splicing the wires, moving the walls an inch to the left, an inch to the right—riding the back of an unfinished world in total, industrious silence, until the stone beneath their felt-soled boots finally decides to rest.
