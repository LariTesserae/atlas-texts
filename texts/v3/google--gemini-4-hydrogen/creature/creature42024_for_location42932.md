You do not see them first; you feel the smallness of their absence. You notice that the wax plug of a root-cistern is fresh, still tacky with pine-gum in the white glare, or that a bundle of acoustic reeds lashed to a watch-tower has been turned slightly to catch the afternoon crosswind. 

If you wait long enough beside a seep-basin—past the thrashing chaos of the midday watering, past the shrieks of the needle-jacks and the shrill descent of the pith-runners—you might finally spot one of them standing motionless in the shadow of a condenser-tree, so still and so thoroughly the color of weathered pith that your eyes slide right past them.

They are lean, sinewed people, cured by the high, unfiltered sun to the shade of old cedar and ash. Their skin is dry, fine-grained, and tough as seasoned bark, dusted pale along the shins and forearms with the powdered cellulose of the plains. To look at their faces is to see what the plateau demands: narrow, deep-set eyes shielded beneath heavy, overhanging brows and thick, dark-rimmed lashes that sift out the drifting gnat-chaff; wide, flat nostrils lined with stiff hairs; and no outer ears to catch the punishing wind—only small, baffled slits behind the jaw, rimmed with muscular flaps they can pinch shut with a deliberate twitch when the midday roar rises to a physical weight.

In a world that never stops screaming, they have learned to hear with their bones.

They walk barefoot, or in thin, supple sandals stitched from cured sail-ray membrane, so that the broad, calloused soles of their feet remain pressed directly to the fibrous crust. Every step is a reading. Through the soles of their feet and the butts of the long, hardwood staves they carry—often rested squarely against the point of the chin or the collarbone—they feel the plateau’s hidden architecture. They know by the pitch of their own footsteps whether the vascular tubes fifty yards down are hollow and dry, or swollen with morning seep; they can feel the heavy, six-legged gait of a husk-boar lumbering through the sedge half a mile away, long before its grinding jaws cut through the ambient drone.

Their day belongs to the edges of the light.

In the brief, sharp chill before dawn, when the wind drops and the drum-mites lie dormant in the pores of the ground, the plateau falls as close to silent as it ever gets. This is when the inhabitants emerge from the dark, carved mouths of their canyon huts. They move swiftly, without wasted gesture, wearing loose, hooded cloaks of mottled sail-ray skin that rattle faintly like dry paper. 

Dawn is the harvest. They fan out across the glistening, silver-haired turf to tilt their resin-sealed catchment trays, funneling the cold, precious dew into gourd-flasks and hollowed root-cisterns before the sun clears the horizon and drinks it back into the thin air. At this hour, they speak—if speaking is the word for it. Without the midday din to fight, they use a low, rapid language of throat-clicks, soft whistles, and resonant hums that carry cleanly through the damp air without waking the bellow-worms anchored in the canyon pipes below.

By mid-morning, as the crust warms and the ground begins to buzz with emerging beetles, the people vanish downward.

Their dwellings are not built *on* the plateau so much as hollowed *out* of it, carved deep into the dense, deadwood walls of the canyons or beneath the braided root-arches of long-dead trees. Inside, behind heavy curtains of layered membrane and packed peat-felt, the roaring continent dims to a distant, rhythmic pulse—like blood moving through a thick vein. The air is cool, still, and smells strongly of warm resin, tannin, and smoked fat.

Here, in the dappled light filtering through thin-scraped gourd panes, they spend the blinding hours of noon. They mend sinew nets, scrape the inner bark of condenser-twigs into pliable cordage, and shape tools from whatever the plateau yields: scrapers of notched husk-boar tusk, needles of pith-runner bone, and—most prized of all—small, hammered-copper awls, worn bright at the tip from generations of use, traded up from the unseen, stone-ribbed edges of the world. Near the entrance of a dwelling, a wind-chime of hollow bone tubes hangs in the hot draft; its furious, erratic clatter is not music, but a watchman, telling those resting in the cool dark when the afternoon thermals shift or a sudden gust heralds a rising swarm of click-locusts.

Yet they are not merely huddled beneath the skin of the world. They are woven into its rhythms, and they know how to turn the plateau’s violence to their own quiet ends.

When the bladder-oaks swell in the late afternoon heat, groaning like strained hulls, the inhabitants watch them from the shade. They know the exact pitch of a tree about to vent. At dusk, when the temperatures plunge and the great pale-green sacs violently expel their hot gases with reports like cannon fire, hunters are already crouched near the roots with wide-mouthed leather bladders, ready to catch the sudden, brief condensation that weeps from the tree’s cooling core before the bark seals tight for the night.

To hunt, they play the plateau like an instrument. A lone walker, stalking a pack of needle-jacks or trying to drive a dangerous, armored husk-boar away from a seep-basin, does not rely on stealth alone—stealth is easy in the deafening roar, but distance is hard. Instead, they carry bundles of dried acoustic reeds, each cut to a precise length and notched at the lip. Pressed to the mouth and angled into the hot wind, a reed can throw a sharp, piercing note—tuned to the exact frequency of a needle-jack’s whistling nostril, or the territorial rasp of a giant beetle—cutting straight through the organ-drone of the plains. With three notes, a hunter can scatter a pack into confusion, or call a companion perched two miles away on a bleached root-rib watch-tower.

They are few, and they are scattered thinly, bound by the harsh arithmetic of water. A single seep-basin and its surrounding catchment ridges can sustain only a small cluster of kin—three, perhaps five, tending their cisterns and peat-cairns like keepers of a lonely lighthouse. 

Yet they are never truly isolated. Across the vast, heat-hazed expanse, they maintain a slow, patient conversation through the substrate itself. At dusk, when the mites quiet and the wind steadies, a watcher will climb a lashed root-tower, press a hollowed hardwood horn to the main timber, and strike the base with a weighted mallet—sending a deep, rhythmic thrum down into the continental mat of braided roots and petrified pith. Miles away, sitting barefoot on the floor of a carved canyon hut, another family feels the faint, unmistakable cadence rise through their heels: news of a dry cistern, a sighting of copper-traders coming up from the rim, a death, or simply the steady, reassuring pulse of neighbors still holding their small corner of the roaring world.

When the rare travelers do cross the plains—walkers draped in layered membranes, carrying sealed gourds of water and small pouches of salt or metal—they move like ghosts through the sun-bleached badlands. They follow no roads, only the wind-worn cairns of compressed peat and the subtle acoustic landmarks of the terrain: the brassy, unmistakable honk of a particular bellow-worm colony echoing from a hidden canyon, the faint, hollow drum of an ancient vascular tunnel underfoot.

They pass through the deafening heat without hurry, leaving behind only a re-waxed plug in a root-cistern, a freshly notched reed tied to a tower, and the faint, stubborn proof that even inside a continent that screams from dawn to dusk, something small and watchful has learned how to listen.
