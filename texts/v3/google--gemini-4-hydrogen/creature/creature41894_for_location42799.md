At first, it is easy to mistake them for part of the stonework. They are the color of slate that has just begun to drink the morning damp—a pale, matte grey, cool and unblemished—and they possess the same uncanny stillness as the birds on the eaves. You only notice them when one shifts its weight on a cantilevered balcony, or when a figure detaches itself from the shadow of a narrow doorway and steps out onto a moss-padded stair.

They are tall and spare, built with the same vertical economy as the towers they inhabit. Their limbs are long, their shoulders narrow, their fingers slender and faintly stained at the tips with the dark green of bruised moss and the dull, metallic rub of copper. They go barefoot across the wet stone, their soles pale and broad, gripping the slick slate with a quiet, practiced certainty. Even in air cold enough to stiffen a visitor’s hands, they do not shiver, nor do their breaths plume in the chill. If you were to touch one’s forearm, it would feel no warmer than the rim of a stone cistern.

They dress in heavy, loose-hanging tunics and layered wraps woven from the tough, silver-edged vines and lashed reeds that drape the balconies. The cloth is dense and matte, weighted along the hems with small, pierced discs of slate and beads of dull bronze so that it hangs straight down in the windless air, never billowing, never rustling as they walk. They fasten their garments with needles of scraped bone, and those with hair—fine, straight, and the color of wet ash—bind it back tightly with braided plant fiber so that not a single strand trails into the water channels.

Their faces are smooth and unlined, impossible to read for age. You cannot tell an elder from a youth here; like the bronze pins and the freshly chiseled stone, their skin shows no sign of weathering, no scars, no creases of time. Their eyes are large, pale grey, and almost entirely unblinking, reflecting the crisp, high-altitude light with the flat clarity of the mirror-still pools.

They do not crowd together. Though there are hundreds of them scattered across the league-wide basin, they occupy the city with the same deliberate sparseness as the buildings themselves. Two or three may share a cluster of towers, moving silently past one another on the narrow bridges or sitting at opposite ends of a terraced plaza. When they cross paths on the elevated causeways, they do not speak. Instead, they incline their heads by a fraction of an inch, or briefly press the flat of a cool palm to the other’s shoulder, and then continue on.

The basin has no need for speech. There is no storm to warn against, no harvest to rush before a frost, no rotting beam to point out for repair. What little communication they require is left in the quiet arrangement of things: a single fern frond laid across a threshold, a copper chisel set point-east on a dry-stone ledge, a shallow bowl of condensation water moved from the shade into the light.

They spend their days in a slow, absorbed tending of the city’s permanence. You will find them kneeling beside the bedrock channels, using a splinter of scraped bone to clear a stray beetle from the path of the creeping water, or sitting cross-legged on the upper terraces, braiding endless lengths of damp plant fiber with fingers that move at the pace of uncurling ferns. They do not hammer the stone anymore—the noise would shatter the basin’s quiet—though they still carry their stone hammers and copper chisels tucked into their fiber belts, as if the work of building were only paused for a moment, waiting for a signal that never comes.

Around them, the wildlife moves without fear or haste. A heavy, iridescent bird will perch on a weaver’s shoulder for hours, its feathers brushing a slate-colored cheek; a pale salamander will rest undisturbed across the instep of a standing figure’s bare foot. The inhabitants treat the animals neither as companions nor as quarry, but as fellow fixtures of the same cool equilibrium. Now and then, one will kneel at a cistern with a cup of hollowed stone, dip it gently beneath the surface, and drink. When the cup is lifted, the water settles back into stillness, still brimming to the exact edge, as if nothing had been taken at all.

Nothing in them hurries, and nothing accumulates. They do not light fires, for smoke would stain the clean-cleaved rock and stir the motionless air. They do not sleep in the way warm-blooded creatures do, though at times they will sit in the dim, unglazed rooms of their narrow towers for days on end, hands resting palm-up on their knees, watching the unchanging shadow of a timber beam on the floor.

They are the builders of the city, yet they seem less like its masters than its caretakers—held, alongside the unrusted bronze and the uncrumbling peaks, in the same bright, damp, and endless late morning.
