To survive here, a body must first surrender air.

In a sea hammered by forty hertz of constant bass, a gas-filled lung or a swim bladder would be a fatal flaw—an internal drum that would bruise and rupture in minutes. Everything that lives in this bright, freezing volume is solid flesh, fluid, and mineral, pressurized to match the water around it. Nothing floats by trapping a bubble; nothing rises to gasp at the flat mirror overhead.

At first, the clarity makes it seem as though nothing could possibly hide, and nothing could possibly die without leaving a stain. Yet the water stays as clean as cut glass because the scavengers here work like surgical seals.

Drifting low over the black basalt are pale, flexible sheets—creatures like three-meter panes of wet mica, thin enough that the quartz veins in the rock show straight through them. They have no eyes and no mouths in the usual sense. When a pelagic eel exhausts itself and sinks, or a ray strays too close to the high-voltage arc of a damaged stanchion, one of these sheets settles over the body before it can begin to cloud the water. It presses its muscular rim against the stone, pulls a vacuum, and floods the sealed pocket beneath it with clear, corrosive enzymes. Within hours, it lifts away, faintly pink for a little while before its gut clears. What remains on the basalt is not carrion, nor any drifting shred of rot, but a skeleton scrubbed as white and dry-looking as porcelain.

Closer to the machines, where the water loses its brittle edge and warms by a few degrees against the riveted steel, life turns heavy and metallic.

The cathedral-sized copper stator rings and the concrete footings of the electrolysis towers are encrusted with creatures that look, at a distance, like nothing more than blistered welds. They are massive, slow-moving mollusks the size of flagstones, clamped so tightly to the machinery that not even the hydraulic *thud-thud-thud* can pry them loose. Over decades, they draw leached copper and trace iron from the electrified boundary layer, weaving the metals directly into the ridges of their shells. They do not move from the stations that feed them; they grow in tarnished, verdigris tiers along the intake grates, grazing on the gray chemotrophic crusts and using the stray voltage of the pylons to drive their own sluggish metabolisms. If you press a hand to one, you feel no pulse—only the same steady, mechanical hum as the steel beneath it.

Out in the open water, far from the stations, there are no warm boundary layers and no iron shells. There is only the twenty-kilometer light, the cold, and the sound.

Because the machines never alter their speed, and because the basin is a rigid box of unyielding stone beneath a windless ceiling, the industrial chord does not wash through the sea at random. It forms standing waves. Across the vast, empty plateau, the forty-hertz bass and the metallic whine cross and cancel one another in a fixed, three-dimensional geometry. Between the walls of crushing acoustic pressure lie pockets of eerie, localized quiet—motionless rooms of still water, some no wider than a doorway, others as broad as a house, suspended hundreds of meters above the stone or tucked into the lee of a granite shelf.

These silent nodes are where the listeners live.

They are long, slender beings—four meters from snout to tail-fin, pale as skimmed milk, with a faint, icy-blue sheen along the ribs where copper-rich blood runs close to the skin. They have small, lidless eyes suited to the endless daylight, but they do not trust sight alone. Along the crown of the head and down the length of the spine, they carry delicate, branching plumes of soft tissue—feathery organs tuned to the faintest stir of water, the tiniest shift in mineral taste, the softest whisper of touch.

In the open, roaring void, those plumes would be shredded by vibration in seconds. The listeners cannot bear the noise.

So they inhabit the invisible architecture of the nulls. You might drift past one from fifty meters away and take it for a trick of the light—a pale, motionless ribbon hanging vertically in a column of refracted sun, its plumes unfurled like frost-ferns in the three-meter bubble of peace. In these quiet rooms, they gather in pairs or small, patient clusters. They speak to one another by brushing their plumes together, or by clicking their narrow jaws in tiny, intricate patterns that die completely at the wall of sound just beyond their tails.

When they must travel—to follow the bone-white eels along the power cables, or to find a new sanctuary when too many gather in one pocket—they prepare like divers plunging into a furnace. They fold their feathery crests flat into deep, bony grooves along their skulls and spines, sealing the channels shut with muscular flaps. They tuck their long, clawed pectoral limbs tight against their ribs. Then they launch themselves out of the silence.

Crossing the loud water, they are effectively blind to everything but the sharpest silhouettes, deafened by the physical blow of the bass against their skulls, navigating by memory and the distant landmarks of the ridges until they burst back into the next pocket of stillness.

Over generations, they have learned to mark the safe harbors.

If you swim low over the abyssal plain, far from any factory, you will sometimes see them from kilometers away: small, deliberate towers of bleached eel-ribs and ray-cartilage, stacked neatly on the black basalt and wedged against the quartz. They sit directly beneath the motionless pockets of quiet overhead. Because there are no storms to topple them, and no currents strong enough to scatter them, the bone cairns stand on the stone floor unchanged—white signposts in a blue-white desert, pointing upward to the only places in the world where a creature can hear itself think.

And above it all, in the upper hundred meters where the light is brightest and the water is coldest, drift the vast, silent shapes that belong to neither the machines nor the nulls.

They are solitary ctenophores the size of houses—completely transparent, boneless cathedrals of living gel, trailing no stinging threads, moving at a pace you can only measure against the distant mountains. They have no ears to bruise, no nerves to rattle. The forty-hertz chord passes straight through them as if they were made of water itself. You will not see one coming until the straight, blue-white shafts of daylight suddenly bend and shimmer, like heat rising off summer stone, and you realize the emptiness in front of you is alive, slowly straining the microscopic mineral-grazers from the clear, freezing roar.
