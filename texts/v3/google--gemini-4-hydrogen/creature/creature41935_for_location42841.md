Nothing here lives in the open by choice, and nothing lives alone. In a basin where the air itself is mined for heat and the stone grows sharper than flint, solitude is simply a slower way of freezing. Every creature in the valley survives by becoming part of a thicker skin—wedged against a neighbor, plastered to a weeping wall, or sunk into the briny dark of the lower plazas.

At the base of it all is the black slime. It is not a plant, and it does not behave like moss; it is a cold, slick grease that drinks the raw minerals weeping out of freshly extruded basalt and feeds on the faint chemical tang of the thermal sinks. Wherever the freezing condensation sheets down a tower face, the slime thickens to the depth of a thumb, smelling faintly of sulfur and wet iron. It is the only harvest in the valley, and everything else is either eating it, or eating what eats it.

### The Street-Packers

Down in the narrow, unweathered alleys, the blind grazers move in jams so dense they resemble shifting masonry.

They are the size of calves, though built lower and broader, with shells not of smooth bone but of terraced, slate-gray plates that grow in ragged concentric rings like tree bark. Because they spend their lives pressed flank-to-flank against raw stone and one another, their carapaces are scarred with deep, straight gouges where the city’s razor-sharp corners have planed them down. Over decades, the black slime colonizes their backs as readily as the walls, so a herd in motion is indistinguishable from a collapsed gallery—a slow, breathing landslide of wet mineral crust.

They have no eyes, not even vestigial pits; the front of the head is a blunt, armored wedge split by a vertical mouth. From it extends a ribbon-like tongue studded with iron-hard, serrated teeth. They do not bite; they *rasp*. Ten thousand grazers scraping the same alley wall produce a sound like heavy canvas being torn in slow motion, a wet, rhythmic *shhhk-shhhk* that forms the middle register of the valley’s roar.

Their motion is a study in conservation. A single grazer may take three minutes to unstick one broad, cleft hoof from the slick stone, slide it forward two inches, and test the edge of a sunken grate before committing its weight. They wedge themselves shoulder-to-shoulder across the width of a street not for warmth—none of them has warmth to spare—but for purchase. Braced against each other, a column of grazers can climb a sixty-degree ramp of wet matte-ceramic without sliding into the abyss. Their breathing locks into sync by sheer physics: in a corridor only four bodies wide, one animal’s wet, explosive snort pushes a plug of freezing air down the line, triggering the next, and the next, until a mile-long alley sighs in a single, rolling wave.

### The Conduit-Broods

Above the herds, the undersides of the cantilevered slabs and the mouths of the hexagonal drainage conduits belong to the climbers.

If the grazers are stone, the climbers are pale, cold-proof gristle. Their bodies are long, segmented, and flattened like centipedes made of translucent, ammonia-scented rubber—the antifreeze in their hemolymph keeping them supple at temperatures that would shatter ordinary cartilage. Dozens of hooked limbs sprout from their flanks, each terminating in a split, glassy claw designed to straddle a knife-edged parapet or hook into the microscopic grain of the matte-ceramic alloy.

They are creatures of two completely different speeds. Physically, a climber can hang motionless beneath an overhang for sixteen hours at a time, letting the freezing condensation sheet over its pale hide while its metabolism drops to a near-halt. Yet its nervous system runs hot and fast in brief, violent bursts. Through slit-like organs along its head-plate, it fires rapid, overlapping trains of high-frequency clicks—the sound like gravel pouring onto tin—to map the impossible geometry around it.

They listen for more than just walls. The climbers are the valley’s cartographers of tension. When a new tower extrudes a millimeter further from the bedrock, or a cantilevered slab groans under the weight of ice-cold runoff, the climbers hear the shift in pitch before the human ear could register a tremor. Whole colonies will unhook in eerie, silent unison and flow into a neighboring conduit minutes before a razor-sharp shard of basalt shears off and plummets into the dark.

They feed mostly on what the condensation washes down: sloughed-off ribbons of slime, drowned mites, and the tiny, blind crustaceans that breed in the cracks of the grazers’ shells. Yet when a grazer slips from a high gallery and lands crippled on a lower ledge, the climbers descend—not rushing, never rushing, but dripping down from the ceilings in pale, multi-legged curtains, taking hours to slowly rasp through the softer seams of the fallen animal’s belly.

### The Sink-Crust

Stranger still are the things that live *on* the thermal sinks.

No sensible animal approaches the slender, needle-like spires or the recessed grates that pull heat from the air; the draft around them is bitter enough to flash-freeze wet hide, and the air tastes sharply of ozone. Yet because they are the coldest points in the valley, moisture condenses there fastest, and the constant electrostatic pull draws dissolved minerals out of the mist.

Coating the bases of the grates are brittle, colonial organisms that look like pale, inverted anemones spun from fiberglass. They have no mouths and no muscles. Instead, their hollow, mineral-plated fronds vibrate continuously in the downward draft of heat-stripped air, harvesting the faint electrical potential generated by the sinks and drinking the ozone-rich runoff. When the wind shifts, a whole spire encrusted with them will begin to hum—a thin, glassy whine that cuts cleanly through the low roar of the herds below.

Among their brittle thickets crawl tiny, flat, silver-shelled scavengers that seem entirely immune to the cold. They graze on the broken fronds and, stranger still, gather around the freshly extruded walls where piezoelectric flashes occasionally flare blue in the dark, pressing their bellies to the stone as if drinking the stray voltage.

### The Deep Boomers

At the bottom of the bowl, where the weeping walls dump their freezing curtains into the sunken plazas, the city becomes a flooded, briny labyrinth. The water here is thick with salt leached from the bedrock and sits just shy of slush.

Here live the largest things in the valley. You never see even a silhouette of them, for they rarely break the surface except to exhale. They are massive, blind, elongated creatures—something between a benthic skate and a bloated, pale-bellied salamander—whose skin hangs in deep, blubbery folds that ripple with the slow currents of the runoff. Their heads are broad and flat, studded with pressure-sensitive pits that can feel a pebble drop into the water three streets away.

Because light is meaningless and the upper city’s roar turns to a muffled rumble underwater, the deep dwellers own the sub-bass. Every few minutes, one of them will rise just enough to clear a dorsal blowhole, let out a long, wet exhalation that smells of brine and old marrow, and then sink back to emit its territorial *thrum*. The sound is felt in the teeth and the sternum long before it reaches the ears—a heavy, tectonic pulse that travels straight up the ceramic struts of the towers, making the sheets of falling water shiver.

They are the valley’s final disposers. Anything that loses its footing on the high, slick walkways—grazers, climbers, chunks of mineral crust—eventually tumbles into the flooded tiers. The cold keeps meat fresh for months, and the deep dwellers take their time, nudging carcasses into dark corners beneath sunken arches and slowly tearing them apart with toothless, crushing jaws.

### The Edge-Walkers

Where there are herds, there is always something that thins them, though predation in the freezing dark cannot afford a chase.

Out on the exposed, razor-sharp ridges of the upper galleries—places too narrow and treacherous for the herds to wedge into—stalk the edge-walkers. They are tall, gaunt, six-limbed things with bodies no thicker than a spear shaft, clad in a dull, soot-colored chitin that absorbs what little starlight filters through the clouds. Their feet are not claws, nor hooves, but deeply grooved, clamp-like pads of dense, fibrous horn that grip the knife-edges of the parapets like vises, allowing them to stand motionless for days straddling a drop of four hundred feet.

An edge-walker does not overpower a calf-sized grazer; it cannot afford the calories for a struggle. Instead, it waits above a narrow bottleneck until a grazer’s rhythmic scraping brings it close to an unguarded drop. Then, with a single, sudden, piston-like shove of its forelimbs—the only fast motion in the entire valley—it knocks the heavy animal off balance. It lets gravity and the city’s own unweathered geometry do the work, then climbs slowly down the tower face to feed on whatever the fall has opened.

### The Listeners on the Parapet

And then there is whatever stands a yard away from you in the dark.

It is not a grazer; it is alone, and it sits upright on the high parapet with its weight balanced comfortably on a broad, folded tail-plate. Nor is it a climber, though its long, heavy forelimbs end in three thick, blunt digits tipped with horn worn smooth against the stone. Its carapace is vast, segmented like overlapping roof tiles, and draped in a heavy, matted mantle of pale, fibrous bristles that shed the freezing rain in steady, rhythmic drips.

When it shifts, its shell scrapes softly against the razor-sharp basalt without taking a scratch. It is old—old enough that the black slime has stained its lower plates, and tiny, harmless barnacle-like sink-crusts have taken root along the ridge of its spine.

If there is a *whoever* in this valley, it is these. They did not build the city; the matte-ceramic slabs and the thermal sinks are as much weather to them as the freezing clouds. Yet they are the only things here that do not merely endure the metropolis, but *read* it.

Their broad, eyeless heads are dominated by twin, dish-shaped acoustic hollows where cheeks should be, lined with fine, damp membranes that twitch constantly as they parse the deafening roar of the valley. To them, the chaos of a million scraping tongues, chittering conduits, and booming plazas is not noise—it is a map in three dimensions, sharp as daylight. They know which alley is choked with grazers, which cantilever is straining toward a collapse, and where a new spire is pushing up from the mountain’s bones an inch per decade.

Sometimes, you might hear them moving along the high galleries in pairs or small, silent clusters, tapping the matte-ceramic walls with knuckles of dense bone to test the resonance of the hollow conduits, or guiding a lost column of grazers away from a sheer drop with low, clicking murmurs. They herd nothing by force; they simply nudge the slow machinery of the valley into paths that suit them, harvesting the deadfall, gathering the shed ceramic shards to wedge into their own joints, and waiting out the centuries in the freezing mist.

Beside you, the broad carapace settles back against the wet stone. Its acoustic hollows flare slightly, drinking in the sound of your breathing, the wet fabric of your coat, the unfamiliar, frantic warmth of your blood. It does not strike, and it does not flee—nothing here wastes the heat for fear. It only lets out that long, clicking sigh into the roar of the valley, and waits to hear what you will do next.
