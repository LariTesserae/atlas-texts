If you walk out across the steaming flats, you will swear at first that the plateau is entirely sterile—that nothing lives here except the architecture itself. Nothing darts away from your boots. Nothing watches you from the bleached lattices.

You have to look at the joints of the towers to see them, and even then, you would be forgiven for mistaking them for calluses of hardened sap.

Wedged into the acute angles of the flying buttresses, or bulging along the taut seams of the cistern-domes, are dark, glossy, olive-brown swellings the size of a clenched fist—sometimes the size of a melon. They have no eyes, no limbs, no mouths, and no lungs. They do not breathe; they drink directly from the feverish, pressurized pulse of the structures. If you hold a palm an inch from one, you can feel a sharper, more concentrated heat radiating from its taut rind than from the structural rib beside it, accompanied by a faint, high-pitched hum as boiled sugars and water surge through its core.

These are the tap-knots. They anchor themselves in the brief minutes when a new truss is still soft and pale, driving rings of rigid, translucent hooks into the wet cellulose just before it clicks and hardens into bone-dry reed. As the tower shoots skyward, the knot swells with it, tapping into the main vascular line like a living pressure-valve.

They do not merely feed on the plateau’s violence; they provoke it. As a tap-knot drinks, its thick, rubbery skin stretches tight as a drumhead, prying relentlessly against the rigid joint from the inside. When a tensioned vine-cable snaps like a rifle shot, or a swollen cistern-dome shudders and ruptures with a booming *whump*, it is often because a cluster of these knots has wedged the architecture past its breaking point. The tower tears itself apart and crashes into the ankle-deep water—and the knots survive the fall. Their tough husks are built like hydraulic shock-absorbers; on impact, they split cleanly along scored seams, spilling clouds of flat, glassy, hook-rimmed seekers into the scalding floodwater.

The hot sheet of water sweeps them across the hexagonal terrace and drags them down through the slatted floor, into the dark, tangled under-lattice below.

Down beneath the taut olive-ochre skin of the plateau, the scalding light never reaches. It is a dim, muffled world of submerged fibrous ribs and warm, slow-moving currents, echoing constantly with the dull *groans* and *thuds* of the towers overhead like the inside of a giant’s chest.

This is where the wind above loses its scent of rot.

Draped over the submerged ribs like wet, translucent parchment—or billowing sluggishly through the dark channels—are vast, boneless, pale-ochre sheets. They are what the glassy seekers become once they settle into the warm dark. Like the knots above, they have no organs for breathing, no gills, no darting fins. They absorb dissolved sugars and oxygen straight through their slick, delicate membranes, shifting an inch at a time with the pulse of the draining water.

Whenever a tower shatters above and shredded reed-splinters or torn leaf-sails wash down through the slats, these pale veils unfold like underwater curtains and wrap themselves around the wreckage. They dissolve the raw, sap-soaked cellulose silently, without fermentation or stink, stripping the debris down to clean, bare fiber so the creeping runners above can immediately knit it back into the floor.

The two forms are bound to the same deafening rhythm. Down in the dark, fed by the ruin of the towers, the pale veils grow broad and heavy until a fresh surge of hot runoff flushes the channels—at which point they bud off new swarms of tough, hook-rimmed seekers. Carried upward in the boiling upwelling around the jagged stumps of fallen spires, the seekers cling to the wet, uncoiling tendrils of the next burst of growth, waiting for the cellulose to click into place so they can begin swelling all over again.
