At first, you do not see it because there is nowhere for a body to stand apart from the room. In an elbow’s width of passage choked with waxy leaves and hanging willow cradles, anything that holds still simply becomes another terrace, another wet curve of mud-daub and rigging.

You notice it by the water. A fat drop beads along the underside of a clay lintel, slides sideways along a taut gut bowstring, and instead of reaching the moss-bundle, stops dead against something warm and grey-green that shifts a fraction of an inch to catch it.

It is hunched in the crooked gap beside the sunken brazier, wedged so neatly between a stack of unglazed baffles and the bulging hide wall that its spine follows the exact bow of the rafters. In the dull cherry glow from the ash, its skin has the slick, dark sheen of wet slip—not the dullness of dirt, and not the shine of sweat, though the air is wet wool. It is the sheen of clay that has just been pulled from the wheel and has not yet begun to dry.

Like the rest of the room, it has never been worn down.

There are no scars on its forearms, no calluses on its heels, no slackness at the throat or belly. Yet it does not look young in any ordinary sense; it looks *unfinished*. Along the ridge of its collarbone and the flat of its shin, the pale, damp skin bears the faint, sharp impressions of thumbprints, as if whatever shaped it had set it down before burnishing the surface smooth. Where its elbows and knees bend, the flesh folds cleanly, without a single dry crease, and around its wrists and ankles are tight, neat bindings of pale, unfrayed hemp, pinned with tiny hammered copper brads that gleam like new teeth. It wears them the way the hanging shelves wear them: to keep the pressure from splitting what is still soft.

When it moves, it does not stride. There is no room to stride. It unfolds in slow, hinged increments, one palm braced against a wet terracotta belly, one knee tucked into a vine-wrapped cradle, moving through the steaming clutter with the patient, sideways certainty of water climbing a stem. Its hair—if it is hair, and not the fine, pale fiber of the gourd-vines—is braided flat against its skull and knotted directly into a length of rigging that hangs from the ceiling-ribs, so that as it shifts from the brazier to the shelves, it must unpin itself from one knot and lash itself to another, never quite untethered from the frame of the shed.

It has no use for words in here. The upper-atmosphere wind hits the oiled hide overhead like a fist on a drumhead, filling the room with a low, relentless *thrum* that shakes the teeth; the bone-pipe vents whistle and spit. Instead of speaking, it listens with its ribs and its palms. Every few minutes it presses the flat of its hand—broad-palmed, with long, square-tipped thumbs kept clean and sharp-edged as bone scrapers—against the shuddering membrane of the ceiling. It holds its breath, reading the gusts through the hide, gauging how thin the blue is outside and how hard the void is leaning in.

If a seam complains—a tiny, sharp *crack* as the rawhide flexes—it does not hurry. It dips two fingers into the simmering water of a shallow pan, scoops a bead of raw grey mud from the weeping side of a nearby jar, and presses it into the joint with a single, practiced smear of its thumb. The mud stays wet. The seam holds.

That is its whole labor: to keep the room in the middle of arriving.

Down here at the center of the clutter, it is clear that the half-coiled jar on the wooden ledge did not spin on its own by accident. When the figure sits still, its hands do not. Even while it dozes—chin tucked into the hollow of its throat, eyes lidded like the scraped-horn panes in the rafters—its thumbs keep moving in tiny, rhythmic circles against the air, or along the rim of whatever pot is closest, coaxing another millimeter of glistening clay upward, feeding the invisible momentum that keeps the walls from drying out. If the clay were ever to dry, it would crack; if it cracked, the screaming blue outside would find the seam, and the shed would tear open like a bladder. So nothing is allowed to finish. The mortar stays slick. The sinew stays pale. The maker stays wet from the wheel.

The room knows it the way a pond knows a stone. Where the figure rests its calves against the lower tiers, white, tuberous roots creep out from the mouths of the jars and wind lightly around its ankles, mistaking its damp, mineral skin for fresh terracotta. It does not tear them away; it only pries them back gently with the edge of a bone scraper when it needs to stand, leaving behind a faint cross-hatching on its shins like the fern-fronds embossed with reed-matting.

It eats rarely, and without ceremony. It plucks a pale, steam-bloated gourd straight from the hemp rigging—the vine bleeding clear, hot water where it snaps—and drinks the pith, or lifts one of the upside-down pinched-mud cups from its bowstring without spilling a drop, tipping the warm, clay-tasting condensation into its mouth. When the palm-sized translucent salamander creeps across the back of its wrist to lap at the fresh slip under its fingernails, the figure holds its hand steady until the dark pulse in the little throat slows and the creature slides back onto the jar. When the heavy-shelled snail rasps its *scritch-scritch* deep inside the tiers, the figure tilts its head, listening—counting the sound, perhaps, as a carpenter counts the tap of a hammer, to make sure the terracotta beneath the leaves is still thick enough to bear the weight.

Only once in a long cycle does it turn toward the low, dark crevices behind the lowest baskets.

There, where the wall ought to be, the gap opens onto the dizzying sliver of the same dim, steaming room repeated downward—and below that, another, smaller and brighter with cold blue sky, stacked like nested bowls over an impossible drop. A draft smelling of ozone and ice curls upward through the crack, cold enough to make the steam vanish into nothing.

The figure crouches on its heels, its back brushing the hot, cherry-lit brick of the brazier, and peers down into the narrow well of rooms. Its pale, unweathered face shows neither fear nor wonder—only the squint of a potter checking the foot of a vessel for a wobble. When a stray tendril of gourd-vine creeps out into the gap and freezes stiff in the upper-atmosphere draft, it reaches in with two fingers, pinches the tendril off cleanly before the frost can climb back into the shelf, and wedges a freshly lashed reed basket tighter into the opening.

Then it turns back to the wet, loud, shoulder-to-shoulder dark. The hide roof booms; a drop of water hangs mid-air in the olive gloom, trembling to the bass note of the wind; and the figure settles its thumbs once more against a wet clay rim, keeping the world spinning just fast enough not to fall.
