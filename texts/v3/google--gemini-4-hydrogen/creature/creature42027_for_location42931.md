At first, standing in the deafening, amber-green haze, you might mistake it for another piece of the architecture—a slumped, pale-barked buttress rising from the ankle-deep water in the wide emptiness between the columns. It is only when the bio-electric cells at the base of the central trunk let loose a sharp, blue-white arc, and the sudden flash catches the translucent edges of its shoulders, that you realize the shape is not rooted in the peat.

It is tall, though it rarely stands at full height, keeping its long spine curved in a patient, heavy stoop that mirrors the ribs of the vault. Beneath its outer covering, its body is pale, slick, and almost boneless-looking, the same wet, fibrous cream as the inner bark, threaded with faint, sluggish veins. You never see much of that bare skin, however, because the creature wears the room’s only product like a second hide.

Over years without number, it has coated itself in thick, overlapping plates of the pale, waxy resin that drips from the reed-turbines. The wax is layered across its broad back, along its forearms, and down its long, splayed shins, molded by its own flat, spatulate fingers into smooth, candle-like ridges. In the warm, motionless air, the outer layers never quite harden; they stay supple and slightly tacky, catching the diffused afternoon light so the creature glows softly from within, a dull, waxy green.

Its entire life is paced to the rhythm of the catch-basins. Slowly, with a wading stride so deliberate it barely sends a ripple across the tea-colored water, it makes its rounds from spout to spout. It crouches beside a basin—knees submerged, long arms resting on the mineral-stained rim—and watches the resin fall, drop by measured drop. When a basin nears the brim, the creature scoops out the warm, mineral-heavy paste in broad handfuls. Some of it goes into a wide, lipless mouth set low in its smooth, featureless face, where flat grinding plates work it down; the rest it smooths carefully over its own ribs and shoulders, patching places where the warm water or the constant vibration of the floor has worn its armor thin.

Because it wears the plant’s own resin, the rest of the chamber’s fauna treats it as part of the furniture. Clouds of copper-winged midges drift lazily through the warm updrafts rising off its back. When it stands motionless for hours at a time, leaning lightly against a pulsing vascular column, the armies of pale, armored beetles march straight up its shins and across its flanks, scraping away discolored wax, stray mold, and dead flakes of skin, keeping its joints as clean and frictionless as the vine-pipes. It never brushes them off. Its small, unblinking, amber-ringed eyes—set wide apart beneath heavy ridges of wax—simply watch the steam-vents hiss and snap overhead.

It has no external ears; in a room this loud, ears would be useless. Instead, it listens with its feet and its skin. Broad, three-toed pads sink deep into the spongy peat, reading the staggered *thump-hiss* of the piston-columns and the furious, blurring whine of the turbines as a steady tremor in its marrow. Every few days, when the resin along its back has grown too thick and dull, it wades deliberately toward the central column and turns its shoulder toward the glowing, blistered root-sacs. A snapping arc of blue-white light jumps the gap, striking the creature’s waxy carapace with a sharp crack. The jolt doesn’t harm it; insulated by the dry, dense resin, it merely shudders once from crown to heel as the electricity glazes the fresh wax into a smooth, vitrified shell.

For all its glacial stillness, it is not entirely passive. Its diet of mineral-rich resin is supplemented by whatever moves within arm’s reach. If a blind loach threads too close to its submerged ankles, or a fat, slick-skinned amphibian loses its grip on a vibrating pipe, the creature’s long stillness breaks for half a second—a whip-fast, silent snatch that ends almost before the water ripples. Then it settles back into the rhythm of the pistons, chewing slowly in the amber haze.

It does not build, it does not breed, and it does not hurry. It is the silent counterweight that keeps the catch-basins from overflowing, the one loose part in the clockwork that completes the circuit. If you were to slide down the root-bound bank beneath the marsh-willow, the deafening roar of the engines would swallow the sound of your arrival, and the creature would not stir—not until the ripples of your intrusion reached its feet through the warm water, and it turned its smooth, wax-glazed head to weigh whether you belonged to the balance, or were simply something stray for the beetles to clean away.
