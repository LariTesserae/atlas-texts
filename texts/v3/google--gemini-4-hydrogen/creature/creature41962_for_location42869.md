Nothing in the chamber speaks, and nothing here has ears to listen with. Yet the city is full of conversation, carried out in touch, chemical taste, and the slow dimming and brightening of tallow-colored light.

The masons—the pale, thumb-length worms that raise the chalky spires—live by a strict, mute rhythm dictated by their own glands. Along each flank runs a row of soft, waxen lanterns that burn brightest when the creature’s gut is swollen with lime-and-mucus paste. As a mason works along a parapet, patting wet mortar into place with the flat, spade-like shield of its head, its flank-lights steadily fade. When it dims to the color of cold tallow, it does not need to be told to stop; it simply unhooks its many legs from the jelly-like scaffolding and drifts downward through the narrow street, making room for a brighter, freshly fed neighbor to take its place.

Their twelve thread-thin feelers are never still. Even when a mason hangs motionless, leaking a fresh ring of paste onto a spire’s rim, its crown of threads is tasting the water for mineral saturation, measuring the hairline tremors of settling rock, and brushing against the feelers of the mason beside it. When two builders meet on a vertical street too narrow for easy passing, they briefly braid their crowns together—a silent exchange of chemical news—before one flattens itself against the living wall to let the other glide past.

In the oldest, lowest tenements—built only weeks ago, when the basalt first buckled—live the masons that have outgrown their mortar glands. Their bodies have stiffened, their pale skin thickening into a dull, chalky crust, and their shovel-heads have worn down to serrated, bony edges. They no longer climb the towers. Instead, they patrol the dim, cramped interiors of the lower walls, chewing back the swollen, symbiotic spore-flesh that threatens to seal the doorways shut. Without these old pruners, the living city would slowly heal itself into a solid tomb, suffocating its own makers inside rooms of seamless chalk.

Out in the alleys, the flat, cloak-shaped grazers move like scraps of wet parchment caught in an invisible draft. They have no eyes and no hard parts, only a broad, undulating rim that grips the raw obsidian and unweathered calcite with gentle suction. Through their translucent backs, their stomachs pulse a vivid, bruised blue—colored by the copper in their blood and the phosphorescent mineral dust they scrape from the rock seams. They are meticulous gardeners. Where bone-white algae and threadlike fungi sprout from the sharpest cracks, the grazers lift their edges like polite skirts, gliding over the fronds without crushing them, nibbling away only the dead, grayed tips.

Beneath those shivering fronds hide the comma-shaped larvae. They are no larger than rice grains, nearly invisible save for the tiny, dark speck of their guts. They do not belong to the grazers, nor to the masons, though both tolerate them. In the shelter of the fungi, they feed on the fine, neutralized silt left behind by the grazers’ passing, growing plump and pearlescent until they are ready for their first molt. When they emerge, they are no longer commas, but tiny, six-legged spinners—light enough to ride the faint, warmish updrafts exhaled by the pulsing tenement walls, trailing threads of mineral-silk from spire to spire to weave the city’s catenary bridges.

Along the undersides of those bridges trot the scavengers. Armored in overlapping plates the color of wet bone, they move upside-down with brisk, fussy efficiency, their leaf-folded pincers snapping open and shut in absolute silence. They are the city’s collectors and recyclers. A shed cuticle, a pellet of dropped mortar, a fragment of dead fungus—all are cupped between those folded leaves, rolled into neat, tight spheres, and tucked beneath the scavenger’s thorax. When a scavenger is heavy with its load, it marches up the silk ladders to the construction sites, surrendering its spheres to the masons, who swallow them whole to re-seed their lime glands.

Watching over all of this from the upper dark are the slate-ribbon hunters.

They are thin as kelp blades and twice as patient, drifting down the vertical shafts like flakes of wet ash. Their soot-black pinprick eyes are set far forward on blunt, wedge-shaped heads, and they move without a ripple, propelled by microscopic cilia that comb the heavy water like velvet. They never hunt in the cramped lower alleys, where the pulsing walls confuse their sense of stillness and the armored scavengers offer too much shell for too little meat. Instead, they fold themselves into the sharp angles of the upper scaffolding, matching the bruised shadows of the basalt ceiling.

When a mason leans too far from a parapet, or a young silk-spinner drifts off its bridge, the hunter does not lunge with its muscles. It draws freezing water into a long dorsal sac and snaps it shut. The hydraulic burst shoots the hunter’s head forward in a single, silent coil, jaws locking onto soft, tallow-lit flesh. A faint cloud of pale blood blooms in the gloom—and then the ribbon glides upward again, vanishing into the unlit cracks above the spires.

And tucked deepest of all, inside the warmest, thickest chambers at the base of the tallest towers, sit the city’s quietest residents: the brood-mothers. They are massive by the chamber’s measure—nearly twice the length of a mason, though curled so tightly into their chalk alcoves that they look like pale, breathing tubers. They never leave their rooms, and they never eat raw stone. Brought mouthfuls of predigested algae and mineral paste by the old pruners, they spend their lives generating a fraction of a degree of metabolic heat, pulsing out new broods of comma-shaped larvae into the filtered, gently warmed water that circulates through the city’s pores.

It is for them, as much as for themselves, that the line of masons is now marching up the silk ladder toward the ceiling. The syrupy thread of super-cooled brine leaking through the fresh basalt crack brings a faint, bitter tang—and with it, the first tiny, sharp-angled brine-creepers, crystalline and clear as splintered glass, hanging motionless in the cold drip. The masons do not hesitate. Crowding around the widening seam, their twelve feelers tasting the alien chill, they open their mouths and begin to lay down a ring of warm, living paste—ready to seal the new cold out, or to build a street straight into it.
