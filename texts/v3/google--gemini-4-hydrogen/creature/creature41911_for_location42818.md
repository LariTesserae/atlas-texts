At first, even in the clear, even light of the plateau, you would not see them. A figure sitting motionless in the shade of an open-walled pavilion or stretched along the edge of a stepped plaza looks like nothing more than a pale, weathered slab of mortar-colored stone, or a natural rise in the basalt.

Only when one of them shifts—lifting a long, narrow hand, or turning a smooth head toward the sound of a falling drop—does the stillness separate into bodies.

They are tall and spare, built with the same clean, unhurried geometry as their causeways. Their skin is matte and pale grey, the color of dried silt or the pale mortar bedded between the flagstones, though along their backs and shoulders it darkens toward the slate of wet basalt. They have no hair and wear no woven cloth; the warm, heavy air holds them as comfortably as it holds the ferns. Their limbs are long and thinly muscled, ending in broad, flat-soled feet and wide, strong-fingered hands whose tips have thickened into smooth, horn-hard pads from generations of handling raw rock.

Their faces are quiet and narrow, with dark, glossy eyes set wide beneath low ridges of bone—eyes that look like small hollows of standing water and blink rarely, shielded by a slow, translucent lid. Their mouths are small, tight-lipped seams. They have no teeth for tearing or grinding, and they do not speak. Whatever passes between them happens in the placement of their hands, the angle of a seated posture, or the simple fact of sharing the same dry slab of stone.

They live on what the valley already gives, taking nothing that struggles or bleeds.

When one of them thirsts, it walks down the raised causeways—placing each broad foot squarely in the center of the flagstones, never brushing the bright cushions of moss swelling in the seams—and kneels beside one of the square settling-tanks. It does not cup its hands or plunge its face in. Instead, it lowers its mouth just to the glassy skin of the water and draws slowly, taking in the warm, clear seepage that has gathered minerals from the weeping cliffs. The surface scarcely dimples. When it rises, the tank is as still and mirror-flat as before.

The rest of their sustenance comes from the sky. In the middle hours, when the light stands brightest above the cloud line, they climb to the low, flat roofs of their dwellings or gather on the open plazas. They lie flat on the dry stone, turning their dark-backed shoulders and open palms upward, soaking in the steady warmth and the clear, unfiltered light. Without haste, without the need to hunt or harvest or tend a fire, their bodies run on a slow, quiet current—taking hours to do what a warmer-blooded creature does in breaths.

This is why the leaves are never chewed and the reed beds stand untrampled. They have no use for the plants beyond their company. If a broad-leafed fern uncurls too far over a sluiceway and threatens to break the thin curtain of falling water, one of them will not snap the stem; it will sit beside the channel and coax the frond aside, or set a small, dry-stacked curb of basalt to guide the plant in another direction.

Their city is vast not because they are many, but because they are old and nothing here is ever torn down. A single dwelling is raised over years by two or three of them, working without iron or wheels—lifting sharp-edged slabs with patient leverage, pecking shallow channels into the young basalt by tapping one hard stone gently against another, *tap... tap... tap...*, spaced so far apart the sound folds easily into the *plink* of water drops. When a roof settles or a young one grows to its full height and needs its own terrace, they simply lay another causeway farther out across the wet flats and build anew, leaving the older neighborhoods crisp and empty behind them.

In the long, windless afternoons, small groups of three or four can be found in the open-walled pavilions. They sit cross-legged or with knees drawn up, facing the same spillway, their pale hands resting on their thighs or lightly touching a neighbor’s forearm. Hours pass without a shift in posture. A single drop gathers on an overhang, falls into a full tank, and sends its small ring outward until it dies against the stone. None of them looks away. They remain as they have always remained—slow, mineral-quiet keepers of the level water, holding their places in the balance of the valley.
