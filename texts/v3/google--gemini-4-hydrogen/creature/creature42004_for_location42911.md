At first, you believe the plateau is empty of everything except the wind and the things that grow in it. There is nowhere for an animal to stand, nowhere to nest, nowhere to run. When you push through the waist-deep tangle, nothing flushes from the undergrowth. Nothing scuttles away from your boots.

It takes hours—standing wedged between the spring-loaded stalks, letting the roar of the continent numb your ears—before you begin to notice the silences.

They are small, localized absences in the noise. You might be staring into the flat gray fog, watching the dark fronds bend and the mist beads creep upward along their charged ribs, when a patch of thicket ten feet away suddenly goes wrong. The upward-creeping droplets stop. Gravity takes them all at once, and they drop straight down in a heavy, ordinary patter. Around those few stems, the oily, diffraction-grating blur sharpens into crisp focus, and beneath the wind’s roar, the dry, static-like *crackle* of cavitation inside the reeds simply cuts out.

If you force your way toward that pocket of dead air—prying the razor-edged stalks apart with gloved hands—you will find what is causing it.

At a glance, it looks like a knot of wet, salt-crusted slate wedged in the forks of the vegetation, or a swollen, dead burl wrapped in dark moss. It is heavy, roughly the width of your torso, and flattened like a river stone. Its upper surface is a dull, matte charcoal, streaked with pale, chalky crusts of verdigris and white mineral salts that look like frost.

Then you touch it, and it stiffens.

It has no head, no eyes, and no face to turn toward you. Instead, radiating from its central, shield-like mass are five or seven thick, boneless straps—asymmetrical, uneven in length, some blunted where a sharp stalk has sliced them and scarred them shut. They are wrapped tightly around the hollow reeds, bridging the gap between the wind-whipped upper fronds and the damp, grounded stems near the rock.

This is what lives here. Not an animal in any sense that requires lungs, blood, or bone, yet unmistakably alive—driven by a slow, heavy, electrochemical hunger.

Its body is a pressurized sack of tough, leathery hide, slick with a clear mucus that keeps the razor-edged flora from shredding it as it moves. It has no skeleton; it inflates itself using the very water it steals from the plants. Along the undersides of its strap-like limbs are rows of pale, muscular grooves—halfway between a sucker and a file—that lock onto the metallic striations of the reeds. Where it grips a stalk, tiny, hardened pores pierce the superconducting membrane and tap directly into the osmotic valves.

You can feel it feeding if you press your palm against its back. It does not breathe; it absorbs dissolved ozone and oxygen straight through its porous, perpetually drenched skin. Instead of a heartbeat, there is a faint, steady *thrum* vibrating through its core—the hum of current passing through flesh.

That is why the reeds go quiet around it. By draping itself across multiple stalks at once, the creature acts as a living ground. It drains the electrostatic charge built up by the wind-friction, pulls the pressurized, copper-smelling fluid out of the capillary tubes, and routes both through its own dense, mineral-rich tissues. As it feeds, a faint chemical warmth—barely a few degrees above the freezing air—bleeds from its salt-crusted back, sending up a ghostly, tattered wisp of vapor that the wind tears away in an instant.

When it has had enough, or when it senses you disturbing the local field with your own clumsy, insulated bulk, it moves.

It does not walk. It has no feet to make footsteps. Slowly, with the patient, hydraulic certainty of a closing valve, one of its limbs peels away from a stalk. There is a sharp, cold *snap*, and a spark of blue-violet light flares between its wet hide and the reed as the circuit breaks. The limb swings blindly through the dense stems, guided by rows of fine, coppery cilia along its edges that taste the voltage gradients in the fog. It finds another stalk, wraps, locks, and inflates. Then another limb lets go.

In this way, it flows sideways through a thicket too dense for a hand to pass, sliding between knives without cutting itself, leaving behind a trail of reeds that suddenly shed their water and go briefly dark before the wind charges them again.

But the upper tangle is only half of its life.

Every few minutes, that deep, unmuffled *crack* travels up from miles below—a tectonic slab shifting half a millimeter, ringing through the unweathered basalt and the taut root-network like a struck bell.

When it happens, the creatures feel it long before you do. Across the fog-choked plateau, hidden in the suffocating green-black density, dozens of them let go at once. You can see the faint, blue-violet sparks winking out in the gloom fifty yards away as circuits snap open.

They deflate slightly, purging their excess water in a sudden, heavy gush that smells sharply of hot copper, and squeeze *downward*. They force their flattened, yielding bodies into the interlocking mat of roots, slipping through crevices no wider than two fingers, down past the last of the dim gray light, until their bellies scrape against the raw, glassy basalt.

Down here, where there is no soil and no wind, the continent is still embryonic. The stone is sharp, fresh, and sterile.

The creatures press themselves flat against the newly cracked mineral shelves, spreading their straps wide into the fresh fissures before the plants’ roots can thread into them and lock them shut. Here, in the freezing dark, they discharge the potential they harvested from the canopy. Through the rasping pads on their undersides, they run a slow, steady current directly into the rock—electrochemically etching the basalt, dissolving raw silica and trace metals straight through their skin to build their chalky armor and toughen their hides.

When they finally pull away and haul themselves back up into the roar of the wind, they leave behind faint, branching scars on stone that has never known a river, a glacier, or a single century of rain.

Perhaps they evolved from whatever wild, self-replicating biology seeded the plateau—a mobile maintenance organ that forgot its purpose and learned to hunger for its own sake. Or perhaps they are simply what a world makes when it has nothing to build life out of except wet stone, wind, and excess charge. They have no voices to call out in the storm, no eyes to see the fog, and no awareness of the vast, gray ceiling pressing down on them. They only know the hum of the reeds above, the sudden *crack* of the stone below, and the endless, patient work of grounding the sky into the earth.
