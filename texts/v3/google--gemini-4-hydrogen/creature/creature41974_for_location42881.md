To survive on the plateau is to give up the eye and become an ear.

Above ground, in the blinding white glare of the steppe, vision is a liability—constantly assaulted by the reflection off bleached lignin and blinded by sudden, glittering squalls of silica dust. So the plateau’s truest inhabitants live where the light thins into a cool, ash-gray twilight: down inside the fluted canyons and the miles-deep lattice of hollow, silicified reeds.

Here, the wind’s chaotic shriek is broken up, sorted by the architecture of the dead and living wood into distinct, steady frequencies. Every fissure has a note; every canyon has a chord. And in the spaces where those notes intersect, life has arranged itself around the physics of vibration, static, and pressure.

***

High above the canyons, clinging to the spinning, copper-veined crowns of the dynamo-spires themselves, live the **filament-weavers**. They are scarcely more than weightless, pale skeletons of spun silica-glass and dried tendon, anchored to the turbine fronds by hooked, glassy claws. As a spire’s crown grinds through the gale, building a crushing static charge, the weavers’ trailing, thread-thin limbs stand straight out into the rushing air like the bristles of a brush. They do not hunt; they act as living electrostatic precipitators, snagging metallic-coated spores and golden resin-powder directly out of the turbulent eddies. When the blue-white arcs of lightning finally snap between the high branches to ignite the vented methane, the weavers do not burn. Their mineral-rich bodies are superb conductors; they channel the discharge straight through themselves into the ironwood bark, glowing for a fraction of a second like incandescent filaments before cooling back into invisibility against the pale sky.

Down in the dark, warm chambers beneath the spires—where the chitinous, brass-colored dwellers chew through oily resin-cakes—something much larger and slower wedges itself into the pressurized root-caverns. These are the **bellows-slugs**: blind, flat-sided, and vast, their hides tough as cured leather and slick with a thick, non-aqueous wax that keeps them from desiccating. They do not eat the wood; they brace their muscular, ribbed bodies across the narrow throats of the subterranean fermentation chambers. As methane and turpentine-vapor build behind them, the slugs’ bodies swell and compress, absorbing the rhythmic surges of pressure to drive their own sluggish, cold-blooded circulation. When the pressure becomes too great, they relax their grip all at once, letting the gas slam upward against the ironwood valve—acting as an unwitting biological trigger for the spire’s detonation above.

***

Yet the plateau is not ruled only by scavengers, grazers, and parasites of the engines. There are also the **listeners**—a sparse, long-lived, quiet-stepping people who belong to the plant-continent as completely as the trees do, leaving no walls, no roads, and no artifacts behind them.

They are built for the gale and the glare. Low-slung and heavy-hipped, they move on four splayed, knuckle-walking limbs when the wind rises, rising onto their hind legs only in the shelter of the canyons or during the brief, breathless lulls between storm-fronts. Their bodies are encased in overlapping, articulated plates of dense, rubbery hide—grown from their own skin and kept supple by rubbing it with the cold, oily sap of the vascular ridges. Over this dark, waxy armor lies a permanent, chalky crust of white silica dust, reflecting the high-altitude glare and insulating them against the flash-heat of the spires.

Their faces are nearly featureless. What were once eyes have shrunk to narrow, sunken slits behind thick, translucent lids of milky horn, good for little more than telling the blinding white of the steppe from the cool gray of a canyon’s mouth. Instead, their heads and ribcages are dominated by vast, pleated tympanic membranes—taut, papery drums stretched across frames of hollow bone. When they press a splayed, horn-shod palm against the woody ground, the entire plateau opens up to them.

Where a traveler from another world would hear only a deafening, undifferentiated roar, the listeners read a crisp, three-dimensional map. Every dynamo-spire within thirty miles has a signature pitch in the floor, a distinct tempo of intake, subterranean groaning, and concussive *crack*. They can feel the soft, erratic *tap-tap-scurry* of a keratin-plated grazer boring into a ridge two miles away. They can hear the rising, high-pitched hiss of a methane leak in a canyon wall long before a spark finds it.

Because they build nothing—any dead wood or woven thing left on the plateau is either torn away by the wind, eaten by the interior-dwellers, or incinerated in a pollen-storm—their entire culture is written in sound and timing.

In the deep canyons, they shape the wind itself. By gnawing precise, beveled notches into the hollow, silicified reeds of the canyon walls, or by plugging certain fissures with wads of chewed, hardening resin-wax, they *tune* the natural flutes of the rock-hard bark. A canyon tuned to a low, steady fifth marks a safe wintering hollow, sheltered from the static storms. A sharp, stuttering, discordant whistle warns of unstable pressure beneath the floor. None of these alterations are permanent; within a season or two, the silica-laden wind scours the notches smooth and crumbles the wax. The map must be constantly re-sung, re-chewed, and re-tuned by whoever passes through.

Across the wide, empty miles of the steppe, where the wind would tear a spoken word from a throat before it traveled three feet, they speak through the floor. Gathering at the edge of a resonant vascular ridge, a listener will rise up and strike the wood with a heavy, calcified heel—sending sharp, coded *thrums* racing through the compressed lignin. miles away, another listener crouching in a canyon feels the message vibrate up through their knuckles and into their chest-drums, and stamps a reply back into the living continent.

***

Their lives are governed by the rhythm of the engines.

A band of listeners will travel for days across the pale bark-steppe, hugging the grooved floor, following the deep, rhythmic *thrum* of a spire nearing the end of a long compression cycle. They arrive at the edge of its blast-zone—always staying upwind of the methane plume—and wait, flattened against the wood, their horn-lids closed against the glittering dust.

They know the exact sequence of the wood’s voice: the low, subterranean *groan* as the bellows-slugs give way; the sharp, metallic *ping* of the ironwood valve’s latch straining; the half-second of eerie, vacuum-like silence just before the *crack*.

The detonation shakes them like a physical blow, rattling the bone-frames of their chest-drums. Above them, the superheated turpentine-vapor roars into blue-white flame, and the shockwave knocks dozens of hard-shelled scavengers senseless out of the sky. Half-melted droplets of golden resin-powder rain down across the pale steppe, cooling instantly in the thin, freezing air into brittle, sweet-smelling glass.

Before the echoes have even died, the listeners are moving—scuttling out into the scorched, ozone-stinking circle beneath the spire. They carry no baskets and use no tools. They scoop the stunned, brass-colored insects and the warm resin-glass straight into wide, leathery throat-pouches, chewing the glassy droplets to extract the trapped, oily moisture within, crushing the chitinous shells between flat, grinding molars. When the next static squall begins to darken the horizon, they vanish back into the nearest fluted canyon, leaving not a footprint on the hard, dry wood.

***

And yet, for all their caution, they have an intimacy with the spires that borders on reverence—or addiction.

In the thin, freezing cold of the plateau’s high-altitude nights, when the sky turns a bruised, star-pricked indigo and the wind drops just enough for the frost to crust the vascular mats, a listener’s cold-blooded metabolism begins to slow. Their movements turn stiff; the vibrations in the floor grow dull and hard to read.

To wake themselves, they seek out a spire between its combustion cycles.

Approaching the massive, leafless trunk, a listener will reach out with a bare, mineral-crusted palm—grounded by a long, trailing tail-tendon pressed firmly into a damp, resinous fissure behind them—and touch the bristling, needle-like bark.

The spire is never truly off. Even between detonations, its spinning crown above is grinding against the gale, pouring a steady, silent river of static charge down through its fibers. As the listener’s palm meets the needles, tiny, blue-white sparks snap across the dry gap, dancing over their chalky, wax-coated hide with the smell of sharp ozone and hot pine.

Their pleated chest-membranes tighten. Their stiff limbs twitch, then straighten as the current courses through their conductive nerve-sheaths, warming their sluggish fluids, sharpening their senses until every hollow tube for ten miles around seems to hum directly inside their skull. They hold on until the deep, subterranean *groan* begins beneath their feet—a warning that the next cycle is building—and then they let go, dropping back into the dark, resonant canyons, charged and wakeful in a world made entirely of dry wood, wind, and fire.
