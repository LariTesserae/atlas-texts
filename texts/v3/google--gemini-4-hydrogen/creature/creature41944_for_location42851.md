To understand what lives in the hollow, you have to begin where the light does—down in the hair-thin fissures of the floor, too narrow for even a sand-mite to enter.

Nothing with eyes or lungs could survive there. The rock at the lip of those cracks is hot enough to blister hide, and the gas that seeps upward is raw sulfur and vaporized iron. Yet the fissures are lined with a dry, velvety fur of **cinder-braids**—mineral-skinned colonies that are neither plant nor fungus, anchored directly into the basalt. They have no moisture to lose because they are built like dry chimneys: microscopic, chalk-walled tubes that drink the thermal gradient itself, binding sulfur and iron-dust into dense, waxy pearls of fat and protein. Every twenty minutes, when the deep vents surge, the updraft shears these pearls loose and blasts them up into the iron-sand like invisible flour.

That invisible flour is the breath of the bowl.

It is what the **sand-swimmers** are after. Beneath the rippled surface of the floor, dozens of them pour through the grit in slow, intersecting spirals, never colliding, guided by the faintest tremor of another’s cilia through the sand. They are creatures of extreme patience. Their smoked-glass plates—three rows of them, seamlessly overlapping—are not just armor against predators; they are a seal. A swimmer’s body is a closed vessel of thick, amber-colored blood and stored fat. Its accordion-bellows do not draw in air the way a lung does; they pump hot sand-dust across dry, pleated membranes that strip out every molecule of oxygen and sulfur-flour, then puff the spent mineral powder back into the dark. They breed rarely, and only when the vents are quietest, pressing their tri-lateral flanks together in the deep grit to pass a single, hard-shelled egg from one pouch to another—an egg coated in a glassy lacquer that will not dry out even in a kiln.

Above them, the **wall-hangers** live a life of pure, suspended attention.

From the rim, they look like a single dead crust of slag, but up close, each flat, leathery body is a marvel of silent negotiation. They are fiercely territorial, yet they cannot afford to fight; a torn hide means fatal evaporation. Instead, they argue in heat. When one hanger crowds another too closely on the soapstone-smooth basalt, it flushes blood into the thin webbing along its rim, flaring a sudden, sharp bloom of infrared light in the dark. Its neighbor’s swiveling horn-dish catches the flare, reads the warning, and inches sideways on its suctionless pads with a dry, papery whisper. They communicate entire histories this way—the approach of a climbing swimmer, the cooling of a ledge, the subtle shift in a neighbor’s scent that means it is dying and will soon be safe to tear open for its moisture.

High above both, in the soot-thick dark beneath the overhang, the **ash-gliders** never touch the rock at all. They are born in the air, live in the air, and only fall to the sand when they are killed. Their vellum-thin wings are ribbed with hollow, gas-filled quills that keep them buoyant on the slightest rising eddy. They are so light that a breath from a swimmer’s bellows can send one banking sideways into the dark. Because they cannot afford the weight of heavy jaws or thick armor, they hunt by patience and adhesion: their trailing tendrils are beaded with a slow-setting, resinous glue that hardens on contact with warmth. When a glider drops onto a climbing swimmer, it does not bite; it wraps, seals the swimmer’s bellows shut with its own body, and waits for suffocation to do the work, drinking through a needle-fine proboscis before the body cools.

And then there is what lives along the eastern wall.

If you were to climb down into the bowl—though the heat would bake the moisture from your eyes before you reached the floor—and walk past the shifting sand to the low, stubborn rings of stone, you would notice that the cairns are not all the same temperature.

Most of the stacked black basalt and vitrified bone is warm as a hearth-stone, encrusted three-deep with wall-hangers that cling to the rough edges. But here and there, wedged into the crevices behind the cairns, or curled tightly inside the sunken hearth-ring beneath a blanket of sleeping **husk-mites**, are shapes that hold the heat differently. They lag behind the twenty-minute pulse. When the floor surges, they stay cool a breath longer; when the vents recede, they hold their warmth.

They are **the Dry-Keepers**—though they have no name for themselves, and no voice to speak one if they did.

Whether they are the distant, warped descendants of whoever stacked the cairns, or something older that crept in from the mountain’s deep cracks and learned to wear the dead’s architecture, no one could say. They still have hands: long-fingered, broad-palmed, with joints thickened like knotted wood and pads worn as smooth and glossy as the basalt ledges they traverse. They have no metal, and they carry no tools. Their skin is no longer skin in any wet sense; it is a pale, ash-gray hide as thick as saddle leather, coated in a dry, flaky bloom of mineral wax that seals every pore.

They have no eyes. Across their brows and down the flats of their collarbones run rows of deep, wrinkled pits, dark as burned sockets, that read the infrared shimmer of the bowl with the precision of a wall-hanger’s dish. Their mouths are small, lipless slits set with broad, square-crowned teeth—teeth worn flat as millstones from a thousand lifetimes of cracking vitrified bone and grinding dried chitin into paste.

Between the thermal pulses, they do not move at all.

They fold their long limbs tightly against their chests, tucking their chins into their collarbones, and wedge themselves into the hollow centers of the cairns or the shallow depression of the hearth-ring. They breathe perhaps once every three minutes, a slow, almost imperceptible expansion of the ribs that passes air through a labyrinth of dry nasal plates, reclaiming every trace of vapor before it can escape. Husk-mites cluster in the creases of their elbows and knees like gray lichen, feeding on the shed flakes of their waxy bloom and keeping their hides free of parasites. The wall-hangers crawl right over their backs, layering them three-deep like roof tiles, mistaking their thermally masked, wax-sealed bodies for nothing more than another ridge of cooling stone.

They wait. They endure. And sometimes, in the long, silent minutes while the sand-swimmers pour beneath the grit, one of them will slowly extend a single, waxy finger—or lean its jaw against the edge of a flat threshold-slab—and scrape a faint, rhythmic notch into the stone with a blunt tooth or a horn-hard nail.

It is not a calendar. They do not know what a day is, or an outside, or a sky without soot. It is only an itch in the bone, an inherited compulsion to mark the passing of the pulse, left over from whatever hands first insisted on drawing a line in the dark.

Then the vents beneath the floor begin to brighten.

The hair-thin fissures flush from black-red to a bruised, angry ember. A wave of dry, scouring heat rolls upward from the iron-sand, and the whole cathedral wakes in hushed synchronization.

The sand-swimmers erupt from the grit and surge up the lower walls. The hangers peel away from the stone in silent, leathery clouds, banking down into the cooler eddies or spiraling toward the rim. As the living shingle tears itself off the eastern cairns, the Dry-Keepers unfold.

They do not rush. To rush is to burn water. They rise from the hearth-ring and step out of the broken stone mounds with a slow, liquid certainty, their wax-sealed hides dull in the ember-glow. In the brief, chaotic minute while swimmers and gliders trade places in the dark, a Keeper’s long arm shoots out—not striking, just catching. Broad, soapstone-smooth hands close around the matte-glass collar of a climbing swimmer, or snag the trailing filaments of a low-banking glider before it can rise.

There is no struggle. A single, silent pressure of those wide palms cracks the silica plate with a soft *pop*. Before the sand-mites can swarm, before a single drop of thick, warm fluid can touch the thirsty air and vanish into steam, the Keeper presses its lipless mouth to the breach and drinks the creature dry.

When the thermal surge crests and begins to fade, and the hangers drift back to settle on the cooling walls, the Dry-Keepers are already retreating into the eastern dark. They carry the empty, paper-light husks back to the cairns and tuck them carefully into the gaps between the stones—mending the walls, thickening the mounds, endlessly repairing a boundary whose meaning the hollow swallowed long ago.

Then they fold their limbs, let the husk-mites settle into their joints, and become stone again in the dark.
