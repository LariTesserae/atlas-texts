You look for a silhouette, because that is how eyes are trained to find life in water. You scan the endless pale-blue clarity for the dart of a fin, the dark coin of an eye, the slow shadow of something large turning in the distance.

You will not find one. In a sea this clear, anything made of flesh and blood would be visible for kilometers, and anything trying to swim against a sixty-knot river would be torn into red mist before it crossed a single spire. The only visible things are the pale-green ribbons of weed snapping like tattered flags on the leeward stone, and the occasional torn streamer whipping past you into the bright void.

To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for bodies and start paying attention to the *manners* of the water.

Start at the bottom, where the black basalt and pale silica needles rise like surgical blades. If you drift down toward one of those spires—carefully, letting the back-eddy carry you—you will notice that the violence of the flood stops a hair’s breadth from the stone. Your hand, reaching out, meets a sudden, uncanny slickness. There is no wall there, no membrane you can pinch between your fingers, yet the tearing current simply slides off it without friction.

That sheath is not an accident of geology. It is a skin of held attention.

What lives in this ocean does not swim through it; it *holds* it. Its hearths lie beneath the floor, inside the freshly extruded fissures where the plateau is still groaning upward, hot with iron and raw mineral. From those cracks, it extends itself upward into the optical-glass water—not in limbs of bone or chitin, which the cavitation would shatter, but in vast, taut architectures of tension and charge.

If you hang weightless in the open void and let your eyes unfocus from the empty kilometers, you begin to catch the edges of them.

Watch the tiny bubbles knocked loose from the churning, silvered surface above. When they snag on those unseen lines of force and march sideways in crisp, parallel ranks, they are not drifting in a stray current. They are caught on filaments—hair-thin catenaries of tension stretched between one basalt spire and the next, leagues apart. Every faint *ping* as a bubble snaps out of existence is a tiny sip of charge, a measurement taken, a knot pulled tight.

Watch the towering, slow-motion maelstroms out in the open blue, and the geometric caustics that sweep across the emptiness like searchlights. Those caustics are too sharp, too deliberate to be mere waves. They are light bending through immense, invisible lenses—volumes of dense, chilled water held in place against warmer upwellings, shaped and angled by something that steers the continental currents the way a potter’s hands steady a spinning, wet wall of clay. When a shimmering refractive wall suddenly collapses into a roaring eddy, it is not a weather front failing. It is a hold being released, a breath let go, a river redirected before it can smash against a fragile spire of silica.

They are many, or they are one thing broken into countless patient stations along the plateau—it is hard to tell which, because their boundaries are made of sound and pressure rather than skin. They have no faces, no mouths, no need to hunt. In a sterile sea with nothing to eat, they feed on the sheer kinetic fury of the place: the ozone stripped from the silvered surface, the iron weeping from the rising stone, the concussive *booms* of collapsing cavitation that roll through the water like distant thunder. Every boom is a pulse in their circulation; every slow, metallic groan from the bedrock is a word in a language too deep and patient for ears.

And they are not entirely alone. They tolerate—perhaps even cultivate—the sparse, stubborn weed.

Look closely at where those pale-green ribbons meet the rock. Their glassy holdfasts do not merely cling to the outside of the basalt; they pierce straight through that frictionless skin, fused directly into the raw mineral beneath. Nothing could breach that protective sheath unless the sheath itself opened to let it in. In a world without nerves of flesh, the ribbons are the closest thing the spires have to cilia. Whipping and snapping horizontally in the sixty-knot flood, each translucent streamer translates the fine, local trembling of the current into sharp vibrations, singing down through its glassy root into the stone where the holders listen.

When a current finally tears a ribbon loose, it does not simply wash away to rot—there is nothing here to rot it. Instead, drifting miles through the empty blue, it often snags on one of those parallel lines of force out in the middle of nowhere, hanging suspended in the bright, cold water like a pale-green pennant tied to an invisible mast. Kept there, riding the self-correcting gyres, until the tension slackens and drops it onto the leeward flank of some newer, bare spire rising miles away.

Why keep an ocean like this—sterile, deafening, lifted miles into the thin air, scrubbed clean of silt and fish and decay?

Because they only exist while the world is unfinished.

Once the currents are allowed to grind the basalt into sand, once the water clouds with plankton and the first crabs scuttle into the crevices, the clarity will fail. The acoustic reach will muffle. The razor edges that anchor their tension will round off into mud, and the great, held field will slacken into an ordinary, crowded sea. So they hold the birth open. They freeze the pale mineral plumes into brittle crystalline fins before the flood can scatter them; they wrap the young stone in stillness; they keep the glass clear and the rivers singing.

Hang there long enough, weightless in the middle of the roaring nothing, and your own body—warm, opaque, soft, interrupting the clean geometry of the flood—will draw their notice.

Nothing will swim up to inspect you. No shadow will fall across your face. Instead, you will feel the roar around you subtly shift pitch. A rank of tiny bubbles will veer neatly around your ribs, snapping *ping, ping, ping* in a crisp arc a hand’s breadth from your skin. For a few heartbeats, the sixty-knot shear nearby will lose its teeth, parting around your shoulders in that same uncanny, frictionless quiet that protects the basalt needles. A bright caustic will sweep across your eyes, sharp as a searchlight, measuring your density and your heat.

And then, finding you neither stone nor ribbon nor current—just a brief, soft knot in the glass—the tension will let you go, and the vast, invisible architecture will turn back to the loud, tireless work of keeping the world unborn.
