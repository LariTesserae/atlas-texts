Nothing in the basin is still long enough to be purely one thing. Even the life here—human, animal, fungal, and the half-feral iron that scuttles between them—has been ground and pressed by the heat and the crowding into shapes that belong entirely to the loop.

Start at the waterline, where the ancient basalt piers drink the tide and the sulfur-eating biofilms crust the stone like dried tallow.

The oldest inhabitants here are not the people, nor even the fish, but the **mats**. Where a greener world would have moss or kelp, the basin has thick, rubbery pelts of chemosynthetic fungus and bacterial felt. They cling to every damp underside, pulsing faintly as they digest the warm, mineral-heavy runoff from the sluices. In the deep, unlit pockets beneath the causeways, these mats grow meters thick, hanging in pale, bruised-looking curtains that weep clear, salty brine. They are warm to the touch—warmer than the air—and if you press a palm against one, you can feel the slow, chemical fizz of it working. Without the mats, the city would choke on its own exhaust; they eat the sulfur, bind the heavy metals, and serve as the foundation of every pantry in the basin.

Down in these wet, roaring shadows, the **flake-crabs** do their work. Flat as roof-tiles and armored in overlapping plates of dull, slate-colored chitin, they wedge themselves into the vibrating joints of the piers by the thousands. They are blind, navigating by the sub-audible thrum of the mass-dampeners through their legs. Whenever a programmable wall above ripples and sheds its spent, translucent skin into the canal, the water boils with them. They snatch the drifting polymer flakes and chew them down with grinding, scissor-like mandibles. Because their diet is half biofilm and half smart-silicate, their shells grow laced with trace ceramics and metallic salts; an old flake-crab doesn't clack like bone when it walks—it chimes faintly, like dropped porcelain.

Between the piers, the brackish water is thick as broth with the **silver-darts**, the blind filter-feeders that move in tight, nervous shoals. They are ribbon-thin, almost entirely boneless, with skin so pale and reflective they look like strips of torn foil twisting in the murk. They have no eyes—only pits along their snouts tuned to pressure-waves, allowing them to weave through the standing geometric ripples of the sluices without ever brushing a piling.

And where there are shoals, there are **silt-tongues**—long, sluggish predators that anchor themselves by their tails in the pitted grooves of the basalt foundations. Most travelers never see them, mistaking their mottled, mineral-crusted hides for dead conduits or sloughed masonry. They wait in the warm turbulence with their wide, toothless mouths propped open, letting the current and the vibrations guide the silver-darts straight down their gullets.

Above the tide-line, wedged into the roaring canyons of the lower causeways, live the people who tend this soup.

You can tell a **Basin-dweller** by their skin and their hands before you ever hear them try to shout over the steam. Generations under the humid glare and the constant salt-mist have left most of them with a slick, weather-cured sheen, their skin toned in pale, mineral greys, tarnished coppers, or deep, oiled umbers that shed water rather than soaking it in. Many have a faint, pale callousing across the cheeks and forehead where salt-crusts form and are wiped away a dozen times a day. Their eyes are narrow, often shielded behind hinged, smoked-ceramic visors or fitted with secondary, translucent lids—surgical or inherited—to cut the blinding specular flash off a million wet metal surfaces.

Nobody here wears anything that rots. With no cotton, no wool, and no flax, clothing is a second skin of extruded polymer-silk, cured glider-leather, or woven basalt-fiber that pulls heat away from the ribs and dumps it into the air. Garments are slick, close-fitting, and fastened with magnetic clasps or bone-pins so nothing snags on a passing transit-pod or gets dragged into a sluice-grate.

In the deafening wash of the street-level roar, speech is a luxury reserved for tight rooms behind water-curtains. Out on the causeways, the crowds talk with **flick-cant**—a rapid, jagged language of finger-snaps, wrist-turns, and pulsing light-strips woven into their sleeves. Two dock-hands can argue a cargo weight, insult each other’s lineage, and agree on a bribe in three seconds flat without once opening their mouths. For intimate talk in a crowd—a warning, a price, a secret—they press two knuckles or a forehead against the other person’s collarbone and hum, letting the bone carry the words straight past the screaming pneumatic vents.

Even their bodies keep time with the city’s foundations. The older **pier-grafters** and **sluice-tenders**, who spend twelve-hour shifts ankle-deep in vibrating grates, lose their high-frequency hearing by thirty. They don't mind; they read the basin through the soles of their bare, thick-padded feet and the fillings in their teeth. A sluice-tender can tell you if a gravity-baffle three blocks away is slipping out of phase just by the way the puddle under their heel changes its geometric grid.

---

Because nothing green grows, the basin’s hunger is fed entirely by vats, nets, and scrapers.

At every third corner, jammed beneath a staircase or cantilevered over a canal, is a **curd-house**. Inside, the noise drops to a damp, muffled throb behind a hanging curtain of moving water. Steam rises from copper-lined troughs where **fungus-bakers** tend dense, pale loaves of cultured mycelium—sliced thin, pressed in salt, and fried on hot ceramic plates with rendered crab-fat and mineral yeast. It tastes sharply of iron, brine, and roasted nuts. Beside the curd-slabs hang strings of dried silver-darts, smoked over chemical-heaters until they are brittle and savory as salt-chips, and bowls of pickled **wall-bloom**—a crunchy, sulfur-rich blister-fungus scraped from the warm exhaust pipes.

The flake-crabs are eaten too, cracked open with brass mallets on the curb-stones, their soft, grey meat scooped out with needles. Nothing of the crab is wasted: the iridescent shells are bought up by **mortar-mixers**, ground into fine grit, and folded back into the programmable silicate paste used to patch the older, non-shifting walls.

---

Move higher, past the bulbous condensation-tanks and the humming braided conduits, and you meet the **Skin-Coaxers** and the **Rig-Herders**.

The city’s restless walls do not reconfigure themselves by accident. The Skin-Coaxers are a guild of gaunt, nimble technicians who cling to the vertical faces in spider-harnesses, carrying tuning-forks, field-wands, and pots of catalytic grease. When a thoroughfare clogs with too much foot traffic, a Coaxer walks the wall, pressing vibrating prongs against the programmable silicate. Under their touch, the wall softens like warm wax, ripples, and slowly extrudes a new buttress or swallows an old kiosk, relieving the pressure by morning. They are deeply superstitious about the walls; many refuse to speak aloud while working, convinced that shouting leaves a stutter in the stone’s memory.

Sharing the vertical surfaces with them are the **maintenance rigs**—multi-legged, low-slung machines gleaming with wet graphite and grease. Most belong to the municipal grids, scuttling endlessly up pilings to scrape mineral scale or weld a weeping seam.

But in a city this dense, things slip the ledger. There are **feral rigs**—old, patched-together machines whose tracking transponders rusted out decades ago, or whose logic-cores were rattled loose by the mass-dampeners. They have developed odd, stubborn habits. They nest in the dry pockets behind steam-manifolds, hoarding stray copper washers, shed silicate flakes, and bits of shiny ceramic. The locals treat them like half-wild alley cats. A market-vendor might toss a feral rig a dollop of spent grease to keep it from chewing the bolts off their stall, or tap its carapace with a broom handle when it tries to weld a customer’s boot to the grate. Some Coaxers swear the feral rigs do better repair work than the grid-bound ones, patching leaks out of sheer, mechanical restlessness.

---

At the very top—on the catwalks that sway on tensioned filaments above the endless flat glare—live the **Thermal-Riders** and the **gliders**.

The **leathery-winged gliders** own the narrow canyons of sky between the cargo-rails. They are pale as bleached limestone, with wide, translucent wing-membranes veined in dark purple to dump excess body heat. Their nostrils seal shut with muscular flaps when they dive through steam-plumes, and their sharp, metallic shrieks aren't cries of anger—they are sonar-pings, pitched high enough to slice cleanly over the city’s bass hum and bounce off the swaying transit-pods.

They are vicious, opportunistic scavengers. A glider will stoop straight through a hanging water-curtain to snatch a dried fish from a pedestrian’s hand, or peel open a carelessly tethered ballast-bladder just to lick the condensation inside. Yet the **High-Walkers**—the lean, wind-burned people who live on the swaying upper decks, tending the rainwater-catchers and the wind-baffles—keep them as half-tamed partners.

A High-Walker wears heavy, ceramic-plated gauntlets and carries a whistle tuned to the gliders' echolocation. They feed the birds scraps of crab-meat and let them roost under the eaves of the upper awnings; in return, the gliders keep the upper rails clear of **wire-mites**—tiny, copper-eating ticks that otherwise chew through the insulation of the tensioned filaments—and alert the catwalks whenever a sudden, scalding squall-front is building out over the silver-grey expanse.

When a High-Walker dies—or a Silt-Tapper, or a Skin-Coaxer—there is no earth to bury them in, and no wood to burn them with.

Instead, their bodies are carried down to the lowest, quietest vaults beneath the cast basalt piers, where the **Dissolvers** keep the great, warm digestion-vats. There, the sulfur-eating mats and engineered bacteria do their final work, breaking bone and muscle back down into clean mineral brine, salt, and heat. The water is pumped back up into the sluices; the minerals are fed into the curd-troughs or pressed into the silicate slurry of the walls.

In a world where nothing truly leaves, the dead do not go away. They become the steam on the glass, the salt on the lip of a stranger, and the quiet, trembling skin of the street beneath their children’s feet.
