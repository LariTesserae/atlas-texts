If you stay in the basin long enough for your own pulse to slow—long enough for the silt you kicked up at the culvert to settle back onto the brick ledges—you begin to realize that not all the heavy, moss-draped shapes slung across the overhead gantries are bundles of lagged pipe.

One of them is resting on a horizontal run of braided copper cable just above the stamping house. At first glance, it looks like a collapsed bolster of grey rubbery resin that has been lying there for thirty years, upholstered in olive-drab water-moss and dusted with the same pale calcium that coats the ferns. Only when you watch it against the dim orange glow of a nearby indicator lamp do you see the edge of it shift: a thick, pleated fold of skin opening a fraction of an inch, drawing in the warm, iron-heavy water, and closing again. A single breath takes nearly three minutes.

There are only a handful of them in the whole basin—five, perhaps six, though it is hard to be sure when an animal can lie motionless in an alley for days without stirring a grain of silt. They are roughly the length of a narrow skiff, broad and flattened through the middle, tapering into a blunt, heavy-jawed head at one end and a thick, rudderless tail at the other. Yet they have no real shape of their own, because they wear the basin on their backs.

Down at the end of the automated winch-chain, where the neat ingots of extruded mineral wax and grey resin pile up in the mud, the creatures come to dress themselves. They do not eat the ingots. Instead, they press their broad, muscular bellies against the fresh, still-pliable blocks and drag their flanks across the sharp corners, scraping off curls of wax and tacky resin with rows of hardened, keratinous ridges along their ribs. Mixed with a slow, oily mucus they secrete from their pores, the industrial waste forms a dense, waterproof carapace across their shoulders and spines. Over the years, iron-oxide settles into the wax, hardening into the same matte-black magnetite crust that seals the iron beams. Bladder-vines take root in the creases of their hides. Water-ferns sprout from the silt trapped behind their necks. When one of them settles onto the floor grates to sleep in the fever-warmth of the geothermal vents, it is indistinguishable from a mound of drowned machinery.

Beneath that borrowed armor, their true skin is pale, rubbery, and almost translucent, the color of lard or wet lime, veined with dark, sluggish blood. You only ever see it along their undersides, or when one scrapes a shoulder raw squeezing through the narrow gap between a brick chimney and a cast-iron hatch.

They do not swim. Swimming churns the water, wastes heat, and risks snagging on the dense lattice of conveyor chains and overhead busbars. Instead, they climb. Four short, immensely thick limbs jut from their sides, ending not in fins or fingers, but in split, calloused pads tipped with blunt, brass-stained hooks. The tendons in these limbs lock like ratchets. Once a creature clamps onto a two-inch conduit or the lip of a brick gutter, it can hang there for a week without spending a flicker of energy, swaying imperceptibly whenever a warm thermal plume rises from below.

When they do need to rise into the cooler dark near the vaulted ceiling—to graze on the tender, silt-free moss upholstery of the upper roofs—they use the plants to lift them. Reaching out with a heavy, prehensile upper lip, a creature will nip off dozens of the translucent, gas-filled bladders from the overhead vines, swallowing them whole without popping them. Stored in a muscular pouch behind the throat, the trapped marsh gas slowly buoys the animal’s heavy, mineral-weighted body until it lifts off the pipes like a sunken dirigible. It drifts upward through the tangle of cables, steering with lazy sweeps of its tail and occasional pulls on a hanging chain. When it is ready to return to the warm floor grates, it simply opens its throat and lets the gas escape—a single, hollow *blup* that rises through the dimness toward the bedrock ceiling.

In a place with no sun and almost no light, eyes are little more than a memory. Each creature has two small, recessed lenses set into the sides of its blunt head, clouded to the color of steeped tea. They cannot make out the bricks or the rusting valves, only the difference between dark and dim—the sudden, sickly-green flare of an electrolytic vat cycling through a porthole, or the dull orange smudge of an indicator lamp.

What they *do* have is the grid.

Running from snout to tail along their pale flanks are double rows of tiny, open pores, sensitive enough to read the sixty-hertz thrum of the submerged wiring like a map. To them, the basin is not a dark, cramped ruin; it is a glowing, humming architecture of warm copper veins, leaking voltages, and pressure differentials. They know when the massive iron flywheel in the stamping house completes its four-minute turn by the rhythmic shiver it sends through the floor grates. They know when a distant thermal valve expands with a muffled *tink*, feeling the tiny shockwave in their dense, calcified inner bones long before the water carries it.

Because of this, they have a strange, almost tender relationship with the electrical equipment. You will sometimes find one draped across the top of a humming transformer shack, its pale belly pressed flat against the warm casing, its chin resting between the porcelain insulators while the blind, chalk-white crayfish pick patiently at the algae around its nostrils. Neither bothers the other. The creature is simply soaking in the steady, tireless vibration, letting the stray current tingle through its lateral pores, keeping its sluggish blood warm.

They eat whatever the basin offers at the basin’s own pace. A mouthful of leathery fern-fronds, rasped off a chimney-crack with a broad, mineral-hardened plate in the lower jaw. A slow, methodical licking of the chalky, luminescent bacterial film that coats the brass intake valves down in the dark. Once in a great while, if a pale ribbon-eel lingers too long near an induction coil, a blunt head will lunge—not fast, never fast, just a sudden, hydraulic surge of muscle that seems explosive only because everything else is still—and the eel is gone.

For the most part, though, they are solitary, patient, and nearly silent. Once every few months, two of them may cross paths in one of the narrow brick alleys, where there is barely room for two bodies to pass abreast. When they meet, they do not fight or hurry past. They simply halt, hanging side by side from the overhead pipes, and press their wax-crusted flanks together. For an hour or more, they stay like that in the dim, amber-bruised water, exchanging warmth, sharing the faint static hum of their skins, their moss-covered backs brushing gently in the thermal drift.

Then, with a soft scrape of magnetite against brick, one of them unhooks a claw, lets out a quiet bubble of marsh gas, and sinks slowly back down toward the floor grates, leaving the other to continue its endless, unhurried circuit of the drowned town.
