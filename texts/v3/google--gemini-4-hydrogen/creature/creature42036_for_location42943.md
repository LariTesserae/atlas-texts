They are best understood not as guests in the stone, but as its slowest weather.

Up close, the pale creatures look less like animals than like things whittled from old marrow and left to cure in the glare. Their hides are smooth and tight over long, spare bones—not scaly, not furred, but dense and faintly translucent at the edges of the ears and the webbing between their toes, the color of wet bone when the morning condensation clings to them, drying by noon to the matte, chalky ivory of the walls. Around their elbows, knees, and the deep creases of their throats, fine salt gathers in pale lines like the mineral rings left behind by retreating puddles. They do not sweat; their skin is a seal against the kiln-heat, waxy to the touch and cool even when the air around them shimmers.

Their heads are flat and broad, shaped like river-smoothed spades, with wide-set nostrils that pinch shut when a dry gust of chalk dust drifts down from the rim. Behind the jaw-hinge sit shallow, lipless ear-pits, always open to the room’s small acoustics. Their eyes are their strangest feature: large, set far forward, and veiled behind a double set of lids. The outer lids are thick and leathery, kept perpetually half-drawn against the ricocheting light of the selenite; beneath them, a milky, opalescent membrane slides sideways across the eyeball, filtering the shadowless glare down to a bearable, silvered dimness. Through that milkiness, they do not seem to look at things so much as register gradients—where the stone is dampest, where a rosette is swelling, where a neighbor sits three paces off in the brightness.

Their posture is a study in conservation. With limbs too long and slender for their dog-sized torsos, they fold themselves into the natural pockets of the limestone like gaunt, patient herons, knees drawn up past their shoulders, their long, three-fingered hands resting lightly on the grit. Each hand has two long, supple fingers and a short, heavy thumb-spur, tipped not with claws but with dark, thickened horn pads—calloused from decades of pressing flint against gypsum.

They move only when the room demands it. As the sun climbs and the angle of the oculus-beam shifts across the floor, a figure on the eastern ledge will uncoil in slow, deliberate increments—a knee straightening over ten seconds, a palm sliding across the chalk with a dry *shhh*—until it settles into a slightly different hollow, keeping its ribs pressed to the stone where the rock’s deep coolness still lingers. There is no waste in the motion, no twitch of impatience, no idle grooming. Their hearts beat with a slow, reptilian economy, sometimes pausing for several seconds between strokes.

Their industry is as quiet as their rest. A creature may spend four hours hunched over a single shallow saucer in the stone, holding a dark flake of surface-flint wedged against the heel of its palm. *Tink.* A pause of three breaths. *Tink.* It is not carving art, nor digging shelter. It is coaxing the rock’s own geometry by the width of a hair—deepening a natural hairline seam so that tomorrow’s pre-dawn condensation will trickle an inch further to the left, pooling where its broad, flat chin can reach without lifting its weight from the ledge. Another sits near a floor-crevice, working a notched splinter of bird-bone under the tough, salt-bloomed outer leaf of a sage-green rosette. It peels the waxy skin back in a single curled strip, exposing the pale, damp pith beneath, which it rasps away slowly with a broad, pebble-like tongue.

When a flint edge dulls or a bone scraper snaps under too much pressure, there is no frustration. The fingers simply open. The tool drops into the white dust at the base of the wall, joining a quiet stratigraphy of discarded flakes and snapped vine-spines that the morning dampness is already cementing into the floor. Nothing is kept, nothing is carried. The room itself is the cache; whatever they need will still be lying in the grit tomorrow, or washed down through the oculus in the next season’s rain.

They are solitary without being territorial. Though dozens share the chamber, they never touch except in the brief, cool dark of late night, when the stone exhales its stored heat and the air finally drops. Even then, their gatherings are silent and unhurried. Females lay clutches of three or four leathery, chalk-white eggs no larger than walnuts, tucking them deep into the dampest pads of silver-gray moss along the shaded base of the walls. The moss swells with morning dew and holds it like a wet bandage around the shells, keeping them from baking in the afternoon glare. When the young hatch, they are no bigger than a human thumb, nearly clear-skinned, with dark, visible organs pulsing slowly beneath the pale hide. They do not leave the moss for their first year, drinking directly through their bellies from the damp felt until their skin thickens to bone-color and they are large enough to climb onto the lower ledges and pick up their first spine.

Around the ankles of the adults, and in the damp seams where the moss meets the chalk, lives a second, smaller world that depends entirely on the first.

Translucent mites, no larger than grains of salt, migrate in slow herds across the creatures’ folded knees and into the creases of their throats. They are blind, soft-bodied scavengers that graze on the tiny flakes of shed hide and the bitter mineral crusts that form where condensation dries on leathery skin. The pale creatures never brush them away; the mites keep their joints from cracking with salt. Beside them, in the silver-gray moss, live pale, wingless insects—long-horned and delicate as spun glass, with segmented bodies that seem to hold tiny bubbles of trapped air. They drink from the microscopic beads of dew that cling to the moss-hairs, and they lay their eggs in the damp grit beneath the discarded flint flakes. Now and then, a young creature will press its long tongue gently into a moss-seam and sweep up a dozen of the insects for the moisture in their bodies, but the adults rarely bother; the insects are too small to be worth the calories of pursuit.

Everything else in the chamber arrives by accident from forty feet above.

Occasionally, a swift nesting near the rim of the oculus misjudges the updraft and tumbles into the kiln-heat below, or an owl’s pellet breaks apart on the rim and rains down gray fur and dry femurs. The pale creatures do not hunt, and they do not scavenge meat—their stomachs are built for bitter pith, mineral salt, and the rare, drowned beetle washed in by a summer cloudburst. Instead, they wait until the heat and the mites have stripped the fallen bird clean. Weeks later, a long, three-fingered hand will reach out, pick up a sun-bleached wing-bone from the center of the floor, snap it neatly against a ledge, and carry the splinter back to the perimeter to scrape a rosette.

They have no names for one another, no songs, no memory of a world where stone is dark or air moves fast. Their lives are measured in the slow thickening of a lime crust over an old flint flake, the millimeter-by-millimeter lengthening of a water-groove, the daily pendulum of light across the bowl. When one of them grows too old to shift out of the midday glare, it simply stays in its pocket of the wall, its milky eyes closed, its hide drying tighter and tighter to its ribs until it is indistinguishable from the salt-flecked limestone—until the mites have finished, and the morning dampness begins, very patiently, to coat its bones in white.
