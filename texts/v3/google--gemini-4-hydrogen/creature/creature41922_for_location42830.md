They are a broad-chested, unhurried people, built like the stones that weight their catwalks. Generations of living between the silvered ceiling and the root-mat floor have shaped them to the bowl’s slowness: their ribs are deep and sprung wide from a lifetime of breath-hold work, their shoulders heavy from hauling cobble-weighted air-bladders down from the surface, and their palms calloused pale as peeled willow-bark where the brine and cold water never quite leave them.

To a stranger peering down from the limestone scree, they would look at first like seals or drifting logs—dark, bundled shapes gliding through the shafts of pale gold without a wasted kick. They wear thick, lanolin-heavy wool sheared from mountain-pasture flocks far below the snowline, greased twice a month with tallow so the water beads off the outer weave while a thin, body-warmed film stays trapped against the skin. The wealthier or older divers wear oiled sealskin, patched and re-stitched with sinew over decades, traded up from the distant coast in exchange for bundles of hammered copper pins and kegs of salt-cured trout. Before a long shift in the freezing hollows near the floor, they rub their faces, wrists, and ears with a pungent salve of rendered fat and pine-resin, which leaves a faint, waxy sheen on their skin and keeps the chill from biting straight to the bone.

They do not speak underwater, for the tarn is already full of noise, and a human throat cannot compete with the deep, chest-rattling thrum of the northern inflow. Instead, they converse with their hands and with the braided-fiber swim-lines that web the town together. A sharp double-tug on a line means *air-bell low*; a slow, rhythmic thrumming of the cord calls the weavers to the central plaza; a flat palm held edgewise in the sunlit avenues signals that a trout-kin shoal has entered the lower weirs. Because the water is clear as blown glass, a diver on the outer ledges can watch a neighbor’s hand-signs across two hundred paces of open green, reading the tilt of a wrist or the flare of fingers as easily as a dry-lander reads a shouted greeting.

Inside the inverted domes, where the air hangs heavy with the smell of wet wool, resin, and salt fish, their voices are low and deliberate. They have no use for haste; rushing burns breath, and breath is hauled down by hand. Every morning begins with the air-drawers—usually the young, whose lungs recover fastest from the cold—swimming up to the bright surface with collapsed gut-bladders lashed to their belts, filling them with the thin, slate-tasting mountain air, weighting the mouths with river-cobbles, and sinking them back down to tip fresh draughts up under the rims of the willow bells. When a dome’s air grows stale and thick with the damp heat of bodies, a weighted valve-flap at the crown is cracked open just enough to let the foulness bubble up toward the canopy while fresh cold air is poured in from below.

Life in the hamlet is arranged by depth, following the temperature and the light.

Near the surface, where the translucent fronds of the bladder-cedars spread like stained glass and the water is mildest, the elders and the small children spend the sunlit hours. Children learn to swim before they are steady on the larch-plank catwalks; for their first three summers they wear woven willow harnesses tethered by short, bright-dyed cords to the reed rafts or to their mothers’ waists. They bob among the emergent sedge and bitter-cress, learning to hold their breath while they pry thumb-sized crayfish from the upper moss-cushions or chase the silver fry that shelter under the rafts. The elders sit half-submerged on the buoyant reed-beds or crouched inside the uppermost basket-domes, their stiff joints eased by the gentler light, scraping hides with flint, twisting soaked plant-fiber into cordage, and tending the damp moss-pots where the pale-glowing marsh-fungus is bred.

The middle tier—hung in the wide, quiet spaces between the great amber trunks—belongs to the working adults. Here, in the four massive air-bells lashed to the oldest cedar, the town’s steady labor unfolds. In the workshop bell, artisans sit cross-legged on dry willow mats, shaping soft, native copper with smooth river-stones and antler punches. Because no spark may ever be struck beneath the water, the copper is worked entirely cold, hammered thin and folded and hammered again until it hardens into needles, fish-hooks, and cloak-pins. The muffled *tok-tok-tok* of their stone hammers travels outward through the pitch-caulked walls, mingling with the hollow knocking of the cedar trunks until the whole basin seems to share a single, patient pulse. Next door, in the curing-room, the air is sharp enough to sting the eyes; fish-packers split broad trout-kin with bone knives, rub the flesh with coarse mountain salt, and stack them in cedar-bark barrels weighted with cobbles.

Only the hardiest divers—often the same men and women who haul the air—spend much time on the deep floor. Down in the near-freezing dimness, where the braided root-mat gives like a wet mattress underfoot, they tend the stone-filled wicker cages that anchor the town, clear silt from the wooden sluice-baffles, and mend the mesh fish-weirs with long-handled bone gaffs. They move down there with a solemn, economical grace, never stirring the mud, sharing the root-hollows with the fat, mottled salamanders. The tarn-folk never hunt the salamanders; they know each of the oldest ones by the pattern of spots along its flanks and listen carefully to the barking pops of their throat-sacs. A sudden silence among the salamanders, or a shift in the bass-note of the northern inflow, warns them before any surface cloud appears that a thaw or a storm is building on the peaks above.

They are not prisoners of the water. Above the northern rim, tucked into a wind-scoured fold of gray scree, sits a small stone-walled camp that belongs to the town—a place of dry hearths, iron cauldrons, and iron-gray ash. Small parties climb out onto the rocks every few weeks to render mutton tallow, boil pine-pitch from deadfall brought down the slopes, or anneal copper ingots in open wood fires before carrying them back down into the cold. Yet they never linger on the dry stone longer than the work demands. To them, the upper world is harsh, glaring, and treacherously thin—a place of sudden wind-gusts, rockslides, and blistering sun. Down in the tarn, the temperature scarcely changes from winter to summer; the water holds them up, cushions their joints, and wraps them in a familiar, resonant dark.

They measure time not by the hurried turning of the peaks’ snows, which melt and freeze again too quickly to trust, but by the tarn’s own arithmetic: the finger’s-breadth of new growth on a cedar-frond, the slow thickening of mineral crust on a fish-weir’s frame, the generational shift of a catwalk’s larch planks as the wood grows too soft with age and must be replaced.

When the mountain shadow drops across the water at dusk and the golden shafts vanish in a single breath, the town’s rhythm barely falters. Along the catwalks and outside the four central bells, tenders swim out to unhood the wicker lanterns, letting the cold, greenish-white glow of the fungus-jars spill over the braided lines and the moss-velvet. The divers climb up through the hatch-holes into their family domes, shedding their dripping sealskins and greased outer wool onto wicker drain-racks so the brine drips quietly back into the basin. Inside, curled on dry heaps of sedge-straw under heavy wool blankets, they eat cold cured trout wrapped in bitter-cress and pass around skins of fermented birch-sap traded from the lower valleys.

As the night deepens into slate-blue, the voices inside the bells drop to a murmur and then to silence. Outside, the crayfish still click in the moss, the hollow cedars groan softly in the drift, and the northern inflow pours its endless, steady organ-note through the walls—rocking the suspended town gently on its lines, holding its people in the dark, clear quiet until the sun clears the eastern peak again.
