If you drifted through the amber twilight of the bladder-tree forests, you could pass within arm’s reach of one and never know it was not part of the stone.

They are low, heavy, and pale—the color of primordial granite seen through tea. Their broad, rounded backs are dappled with the same velvet-moss that curtains the canopies, and along their shoulders and hips, the skin has thickened over long ages into dull, calcified plates that look indistinguishable from the ancient holdfasts gripping the bedrock. In a sea where almost no animal has left an eye or a fin to be seen, they have neither. Where eyes might have been, two smooth, milky domes sit flush with the sides of their blunt, river-stone heads, registering nothing sharper than the slow dimming of a brine-storm overhead or the cool, silken brush of a detached shadow drifting past.

They do not swim. There is nowhere here to swim, and nothing to outrun. Instead, they haul themselves across the smooth, slumped whale-backs of the ridges on four thick, supple limbs, their broad, four-fingered hands gripping the fibrous granite or curling around the soft trunks of bladder-trees to pull themselves upward when the canopy thickens. When they cross the high crests where gravity grows indecisive, they do not fight the lightness; they simply loosen their hold, let the water lift their heavy bodies an inch or two off the stone, and drift forward with a single slow push of a palm, like boulders learning to float.

To live here, they have had to make peace with a world that no longer keeps a single pace.

Because the sea swallows every vibration before it can travel an inch, they are entirely deaf—and entirely silent. They possess no lungs, no vocal cords, no organs for catching a ripple. Instead, the pale, pleated skin of their undersides and the broad, fleshy pads of their palms are extraordinarily sensitive to pressure, heat, and the subtle, invisible grain of the water. They feel the sea as a shifting architecture of weights and temperatures: the tight, lateral crowding of a cold pocket; the rising, blood-warm column of a thermal plume; the sudden, dizzying thinness where gravity forgets which way is down.

Most crucially, they feel the edges of time.

A creature that wandered blindly across the boundary between a fast-moving thermal plume and a stagnant, cool hollow would tear itself apart—one half of its blood racing through seasons in a minute while the other half froze in a single, stretched-out beat. The dwellers of the ridges survive this by being segmented within. Along the length of their thick torsos, muscular valves can seal their circulation into separate chambers, allowing them to let one limb dangle in a warm fissure while the rest of their body rests in the slow, heavy dark. 

This is how they build—if *building* is the word for something done without tools, without plans, and without haste.

When one of them wishes to make a shelter on the high ridges, it does not cut or break the living kelp. It gathers a loose tangle of young, supple kelp-roots in its broad hands, weaves them into a low, hollow dome with nothing more than the nudge of a shoulder and the patient threading of blunt fingers, and then anchors itself beside the frame. Positioning the woven arch directly over a warm, mineral-rich fissure, the creature settles its own body into an adjacent pocket of cold, dense water. There it waits. In the slow pocket, its own heartbeats stretch into hours, then days, while inches away, inside the fast-time plume, the kelp-roots drink the rising heat and stone-milk, thickening, hardening, and calcifying over decades in what feels to the weaver like a single, unhurried afternoon.

When the dome is finally rigid enough to turn aside a silent brine-storm, the dweller crawls inside, curls its heavy tail-keel across the entrance, and sleeps while the canopies outside are bent flat against the rock without a sound.

The cairns are made differently, and for no reason as practical as shelter.

Along the highest, flattest ridges, where the water is clearest and the rules of the world are most frayed, they gather river-rounded pebbles—stones worn smooth before this sea ever rose, or loosened from the fibrous bedrock. With palms that can read the faintest upward tug of indecisive gravity, they stack them. They lean a flat stone against a current that has been rising in the same spot for a century; they wedge a round pebble into the invisible seam between two waters moving at different speeds, so that it hangs tilted at an angle that ought to be impossible, held upright by the sheer, packed weight of the sea itself.

No one knows if the cairns are markers, memories, or simply a way of touching the edges of a broken world to see what still holds. In a place without speech, where tracks in the silt vanish and light smears every distance into guesswork, an arrangement of stones is the only thing that stays put. When another dweller crawls along the ridge—perhaps an hour later, perhaps three hundred years later, for who can say which current it rode to get there—it will pause beside the cairn. It will lay its broad, sensitive palm lightly against the balanced stones, feeling the tilt, the pressure, the ghost of the current that held them when they were placed. Sometimes it will add a pebble of its own. Sometimes it will simply sit beside them, letting the velvet-moss creep a little farther up its flanks.

They rarely meet, for the plateau is vast and the forests are dense enough to hide a world. When two of them do brush against one another in the warm gloom—an unmistakable, heavy contact, wholly different from the slick give of a seed-pod or the soft yield of a bladder-tree—they do not startle. They simply stop. Slowly, they press their broad, flattened heads together, or lay their mossy flanks side by side, holding perfectly still until the heat and pace of their bodies equalize across the skin. They share no words, only the quiet, physical fact of having arrived at the same moment in the same pocket of water. Then, after a long while, they part, disappearing back into the curtains of kelp.

They eat little: the pale, sweet pith of swollen seed-pods, the velvet-moss scraped from the domes with toothless, leathery lips, and occasionally, when one drifts near enough to brush their skin, one of the blind, paper-shelled crawlers that leave faint tracks in the silt. The great, translucent ribbon-creatures that undulate between the trunks ignore them, and they ignore the ribbons in return, parting their limbs to let the miles-long—or inches-long—bodies glide past without disturbance.

Nothing here hurries them. There are no predators to fear, no tides to catch, no seasons to outrun. Above them, the smeared sun hangs forever unformed; below them, the lower world dries and cracks in the thin, cold air beyond the trembling amber wall of the sea. 

The dwellers of the ridges do not look down, and they do not look up. They only move from dome to cairn, from warm plume to cold hollow, nudging rounded stones into impossible balance, waiting with infinite, moss-grown patience for whatever comes after the last, slow age of a sea that forgot how to fall.
