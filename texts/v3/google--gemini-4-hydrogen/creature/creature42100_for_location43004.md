If you stay in one of the narrow thoroughfares long enough to see the suspended walkway rotate its first millimeter, you begin to notice that the city is not empty.

At first, they are easy to mistake for architectural details you simply overlooked: a buttress tucked into a shoulder-width slot, a tall pale stanchion leaning into the acute angle beneath a folded stair. In the flat, shadowless light, they cast no more darkness than the bollards do. They are the same colors as the basin—chalk-white, matte ceramic gray, the wet dark of newly cleaved slate—and they share the city’s habit of absolute stillness.

They are upright and narrow-shouldered, scaled precisely to the slots and stairwells around them. Where skin might be, they are sheathed in a fine-grained, bone-white composite, dry to the touch and perpetually chilled just above freezing. At the elbows, knees, and collarbones, this pale casing does not wrinkle when they bend; it is laminated in thin, tight-fitting plates that slide over one another without a sound. Over decades of standing in the damp alleys, a faint mineral rime gathers along their ridges and seams, so that the oldest among them look partially encrusted with the same frost-gray extrusion that slowly raises the curbs.

They have no eyes in the ordinary sense. In the upper plane of their narrow, canted heads sit two small, open-ended hollows—miniature versions of the pressurized cylinders embedded in the city’s walls. Nothing gleams inside them. There is only a faint, steady distortion in the air, as if the space within each socket is slightly denser than the street outside. When one of them turns its head toward you, you do not feel watched so much as weighed, as though the barometric pressure around your shoulders has risen by a fraction of an inch.

Like the bridge that passes cleanly through the hexagonal pylon, the inhabitants do not always insist on exclusive space.

You may see one standing flush against a slate foundation, and only when it shifts its weight—taking perhaps the length of a long breath to move an inch—do you realize its left shoulder was not resting *against* the stone, but occupying it. Both the shoulder and the slate remain entirely solid. If two of them meet in a passage too narrow for a human to turn sideways, neither steps back. They walk forward at their unhurried, deliberate pace, and for a moment their edges double-expose—pale composite and dark mineral film overlapping in the clear air—before separating cleanly on the other side, leaving no warmth and no disturbance behind.

They do not hurry, because hurry makes no sense where twelve paces from the outside stretch to forty within. Most of their time is spent in a patience that matches the stalagmite pace of the construction around them. One may stand for three days in the pinched corner of a stairwell, one long-fingered hand resting inside a bundle of the pale, hair-thin filaments that run like nerves along the walls, reading the slow tension of a tower three streets away as it settles into its neighbor. Another may sit motionless on the rim of a damp trough for an entire season, watching the shallow water that reflects only a blank, featureless gray, waiting for nothing in particular.

Yet they are not statues. When they move, they move with a quiet, economical precision. They drink by laying their dry palms flat against the sweating seams of the geometry, drawing the cold condensation directly through the microscopic pores of their chalky casing. Now and then, one will tap a bone-white lattice with a knuckle, producing a single, clear note like struck steel—a note that sometimes seems to reach the far end of the basin just before the knuckle meets the beam.

Near the center of the city, where three thick conduits converge around the sunken cistern and gravity tilts politely uphill, several of them can usually be found together. They do not speak; the cold air carries no gossip, and their faces have no mouths to shape it. Instead, they place small, flat discs of mirror-bright alloy on the sloped flagstones and watch them migrate steadily upward toward the lip of the tank over the course of months. It is impossible to tell whether they are measuring the tilt, calibrating the growth of the basin, or simply keeping company with the only things here that move faster than the walls.

They treat the sparse life of the margins with a distant, unbothered courtesy. When a flat, slate-colored beetle crosses a threshold and climbs onto one of their ankles to graze the mineral film, the inhabitant does not shake it off; it waits until the beetle has moved on before shifting its foot. And on the rare occasions when a solitary, pale-plumed bird drops from the upper air to drink at a trough, every figure within sight goes entirely still—not out of fear, but out of an apparent recognition that the bird belongs to the brisk, ordinary physics beyond the rim. They watch it step through the gray, unreflecting water, and long after it has lifted silently back into the flat sky, their recessed, high-pressure gazes remain tilted upward, taking the measure of the empty air it left behind.

There may be thirty of them in the basin, or three hundred. Counting is unreliable in a place where rooms fold inside one another and a single figure can stand simultaneously in two overlapping corridors. They do not build the city, and they do not appear to command it; they are simply what has learned to live inside a structure that is still precipitating out of the cold air—quiet tenants of the seams, keeping pace with a schedule measured in centuries.
