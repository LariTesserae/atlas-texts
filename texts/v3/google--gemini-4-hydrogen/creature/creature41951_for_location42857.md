At first, you would think the valley has no true masters—only the patient geometry of rock and frond, and the occasional pale grazer inching along a terrace. The water is too clear for anything large to hide in murk, and too still for anything to dart unseen.

Yet if you stay suspended in the golden-green light long enough for your eyes to stop searching for movement, the shadows on the stepped basalt begin to resolve into shapes that do not belong to the stone.

They are few—perhaps only a few dozen in the entire drowned bowl—and they live as though speed were a kind of violence.

Roughly the length of a tall human, though far more slender, their bodies are built for threading through the crowded, wrist-thick *bladder-stems* without snagging a single curtain of silver-green silk. Their skin is the matte, blue-black of wet basalt, so that when they rest in the crisp shadows cast by the upper ridges, they vanish completely against the valley floor. Over the decades of their long, unhurried lives, the same velvety chartreuse and crimson moss that wraps the rocks takes root along their shoulders and spines, softening their silhouettes until they look like fractured spurs of the mountain itself.

Only their undersides are pale, and only their hands and feet look entirely distinct from the stone: broad, heavily padded lobes of tough, supple flesh. In a valley where the freshly shattered talus rests at precarious, hair-trigger angles and every rock edge is razor-sharp, a hard claw or a careless kick would be fatal—both to the swimmer and to the fragile architecture of the slopes. So they never kick, and they rarely swim in open water. Instead, they climb and glide, testing each precarious stone with a soft pad before shifting their weight, or pulling themselves gently from one rigid bladder-stem to the next with a buoyancy so finely tuned they barely stir the water.

Their heads are narrow and smooth, with deeply recessed eyes shielded behind thick, amber-tinted lids that filter the fierce, unfiltered glare of the high-altitude sun. Along their ribs run delicate, feather-fine fringes that can sense the faintest thermal drift—the slow, silent turn of the water as the alpine day warms the surface and the night lets it cool.

They do not have gills, nor do they ever breach the distant, windless surface above. Instead, they live in quiet partnership with the flooded forest. When a slender, matte-black figure needs air—or wishes to lighten its buoyancy by a fraction of a gram—it draws close to a frond of the *bladder-stems* and gently parts the amber-and-emerald leaves. Using a fine, hollow spur tucked beneath its lower lip, it pierces the glassy skin of a swollen oxygen bladder and draws in a slow, sweet sip of trapped gas. The deflated sac is left to mend or wither.

Sometimes, though, a bladder is tapped not for breath, but for speech.

In a basin wrapped in so heavy a velvet hush, voices would carry too harshly, or require too much effort to produce. They have no vocal cords. Instead, when one wishes to reach another across three hundred meters of sunlit clarity, it selects a taut, overripe bladder, pinches the stem just below it, and snaps it free.

*Plink.*

The single, glassy note carries effortlessly through the motionless water. To an outsider, it sounds like the forest merely shedding its excess oxygen. To the dwellers of the valley, the pitch of the snap, the size of the bubble, and the long, measured minutes of silence between one *plink* and the next form a sparse, unhurried language—a way of marking a loose slope of talus, noting the drift of a pale-shelled grazer, or simply answering another’s presence across a golden-green chasm.

Their days follow the faint pulse of the sun. By morning, they climb into the upper canopy of the bladder-stems, clinging motionless to the stalks in the fierce, warm light. There, the moss growing across their backs drinks the sun and feeds sugars directly through their skin, supplemented only occasionally by a careful cropping of the crimson rock-mats—never stripped bare, always pruned so the stone remains safely wrapped—or a rolled spool of silver-green filaments gathered from between the stems.

Where the filaments hang thickest, in the quietest corners of the forest, they sometimes braid the wet silk into loose, buoyant cradles. Inside these suspended nets hang their young: tiny, translucent things, no larger than a closed fist, drifting safely above the razor-sharp floor while the bath-warm water feeds them oxygen through their thin skins.

At dusk, when the upper water cools and begins its glacial sink, the adults detach themselves from the stalks and descend into the deeper terraces. They gather—never crowding, always leaving an arm’s length of clear water between them—along the broad, lazy geothermal seepages. Resting their soft-padded limbs on the warm, velveted stone, they tuck their heads beneath their moss-grown shoulders and slip into a stillness indistinguishable from the rock around them, waiting for the highland sun to return and wake the forest with light.
