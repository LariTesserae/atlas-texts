If you stand in the chamber for five minutes, you will leave certain that you were alone. Even after ten, the room seems to contain nothing more than stone, iron, water, and the slow, stubborn geometry of the leaves.

It takes at least two cycles of the machinery—forty minutes of standing still in the baking dark, letting the pulse in your ears settle—before you begin to notice that one of the basalt ridges near the transformer housings is not quite as angular as the rest.

It lies flat against the heated floor just outside the drip-line, where the wet, bruised-violet foliage gives way to bone-dry stone. At first glance, it looks like a collapsed shelf of rock, or a mound of the oldest, thickest leaves that have dried and blackened in the fever of the earth. Its upper surface is matte soot-black, broken into broad, overlapping plates that mirror the flat planes of the fractured basalt around it. Dust and pale mineral ash have settled into the seams between those plates. Nothing about it catches the dull amber light of the junction boxes.

Then the solenoid clicks inside its sealed casing. The faint, low-frequency hum rises through the stone, vibrating for sixty seconds—and in the cover of that tremor, the ridge shifts.

It does not scuttle. It does not startle or coil. It moves with the heavy, deliberate patience of a root prying into a crack, sliding an inch or two closer to the sweating flank of the condenser tower before the hum subsides and the silence locks back into place.

What lives here is low-slung, broad, and built for the dark. It has no eyes—only a pair of sunken, crescent-shaped pits beneath a thick brow of keratinous horn, tuned to the faint cherry-red of the heating elements and the invisible gradients of warmth radiating off the floor. In a room without sunlight, sight is useless; it knows the chamber entirely by temperature, vibration, and the sudden, sharp coolness that marks a pocket of water.

Its body is a compromise between the rock and the plants. Across its back and shoulders, the heavy plates are hard and beveled, scarred by the razor edges of the young basalt, tough enough to bear the weight of an iron pipe without bruising. Along its flanks, however, the hide softens into broad, leathery flaps of bruised violet and waxy bone-white, veined with the same dull, metallic sheen found in the oldest leaves. When it presses itself against the base of a condenser tower and spreads those flanks outward, it becomes indistinguishable from the dark islands of vegetation—another thermotropic surface drinking in the ambient radiation.

It feeds on what the room offers, and nothing goes to waste. Beneath the canopy of the plant mounds, where detached leaves stew in the damp heat until they collapse into a dark, mineral-rich paste, the creature wedges its flat, blunt snout into the mulch and draws it in slowly, without sound. It grazes, too, on the oldest, heaviest leaves just before they rot, tearing them away with wide, flat teeth stained dark green at the roots. Because its diet is rich in copper runoff and dissolved stone, the seams of its hide, the rims of its heat-pits, and the blunt, gripping claws along its four splayed limbs have all taken on a faint crust of pale verdigris.

Water is too precious here to lap or splash. Instead, when the air grows too parched, the creature drags itself up to the lowest bends of the cooled copper manifolds. It presses the pale, porous skin of its throat directly against the sweating metal, absorbing the thin film of condensation through its hide, holding still for hours at a time while a single drop gathers, swells, and vanishes into its scales.

There is no malice in it, and no hurry. It has no predators to fear in this sealed pocket, and no prey to chase; there are no insects to snap at, no bats to listen for. Its entire existence is governed by the twenty-minute rhythm of the solenoids and the slow drip of the towers. During the long intervals of silence, it lies draped over the thick, braided copper cables, soaking in the residual warmth of the current, its ribs rising and falling once every few minutes—so slowly that even if you knelt beside it in the dark, you might mistake the movement for the faint expansion of cooling iron.

Yet it is not indifferent to the room. In its blind, slow way, it tends the boundaries of the dark islands. When a dead, fibrous stem threatens to choke the shallow basalt basin beneath a dripping valve, the creature hooks a copper-stained claw around it and drags it aside, clearing the path for the next drop. Sometimes it wedges its broad, cool-bellied body against the base of a pipe for days at a time, subtly redirecting the trickle of condensation toward a struggling mat of pale roots. Whether it does this out of care or simply to ensure its own pastures do not dry out, the result is the same: the plants endure, and the creature endures with them.

If you remain in the chamber, it knows you are there almost immediately. To its heat-pits, you are not a shape in the dark—you are a towering, unsteady column of roaring warmth, shedding precious moisture with every breath, noisy with the wet, frantic pulse of your blood. It will not lunge at you. Violence costs water, and water is the only currency here.

Instead, it flattens its violet flanks tighter against the stone, tucks its pale throat away from the air, and waits. It can outwait anything that breathes this fast. Long after your boots have grown heavy with the heat and you have turned back toward the surface, it will still be there in the dark—listening through its belly for the next soft click of the solenoid, and the slow, irregular *tock* of water falling onto a waxy leaf.
