If you stand on the perforated grating long enough, watching the amber bulbs sputter in the damp, it is easy to assume the settlement is running on its own—an old machine left to wind down in the dark. The tin sheds look shut. The catwalks are empty.

Then you notice the details that do not belong to a ruin. A brass pressure-valve has been freshly bound in pale, damp silk to stop a leak. A grease-tin sits wedged behind a dynamo housing, its lid propped open with a sliver of root. Hanging upside down from a warm steam-pipe, just inside the ten-foot halo of an arc-lamp, are three pairs of heavy, split-toed leather boots, slowly dripping gray water onto the moss below.

Someone is here. They simply do not occupy the light the way you do.

You usually realize one of them is nearby not by seeing them, but by feeling a sudden, sharp *tink-tink-clack* vibrate up through the soles of your shoes—the sound of a brass wrench tapped against a steel suspension cable fifty yards away. A moment later, a figure steps out of the dark between two root-pillars, moving with the unhurried, slightly crouched balance of someone who has spent their whole life on wet, vibrating iron.

They are small-boned, lean, and perpetually slick. Living in air that never dries—where spores drift in every fan-draft and the moss tries to colonize anything that sits still for an hour—has forced them to borrow the hollow’s own defenses. Their skin is pale, the color of bleached bark, and rubbed daily with a thin coat of bitter resin-oil spun from the taproot sap. It gives them a faint, waxy sheen and makes them smell sharply of turpentine, warm grease, and roasted meal. Their hair is cropped close or tucked under tight caps of boiled weaver-silk, so the ceiling-midges and dampness have nothing to tangle in.

Their eyes are the strangest part: wide, pale-irised, almost colorless, built to drink in the sickly-green pulse of the bioluminescent fungus and the dim edges of the incandescent pools. When they have to work directly under a sputtering blue-white arc-lamp, they flip down slotted visors of tarnished brass to keep from being blinded.

They wear layers of pale-gray cloth—harvested from the silk curtains under the catwalks, boiled in sap-lye until it is tough as canvas—stained at the knees and elbows with verdigris and machine oil. Around their chests and hips they wear heavy leather harnesses bristling with brass carabiners, hooked scraping-knives, and spare fuses. Most go barefoot when they are inside the sheds, or wear thin, greased leather wraps; thick boots would make them deaf.

In a cavern this loud, voices are useless. You could scream at the top of your lungs three feet from someone’s ear, and the *clank-hiss-thud* of the piston-pumps and the dry *shhhk-shhhk* of ten million beetle-legs would swallow it whole. So the crews do not speak aloud, except in the quietest corners of their burrows, and even then it is a low, chesty hum pressed close to a listener’s temple. 

Instead, they listen through the iron. By resting a palm on a tensioned cable or standing barefoot on a girder, a tender can read the health of the entire hollow: the stutter of a worn bearing three platforms away, the slackening of a rusted anchor-bolt, the subtle, rhythmic groan of the root-walls swelling before a steam release. They talk the same way—tapping out a crisp, syncopated code with wrenches and steel-shod knuckles against the trusses. The messages ride the metal skeleton of the hamlet from one side of the void to the other, completely invisible to the ear amidst the roar of the turbines. Within the ten-foot circles of amber light, they switch to a brisk, clipped sign language, their oil-slicked fingers flashing in the gloom.

They do not sleep in the corrugated tin sheds. Those are for the centrifuges, the grinding hoppers, and the dynamos—too hot, too loud, and too likely to shake your teeth loose while you rest. Instead, the crews live *behind* the walls.

Over generations, they have carved and coaxed chambers into the colossal, swollen tubers and the hollow pockets behind the braided taproots. To reach them, you have to climb down a knotted silk rope behind a pumping station and squeeze through a low arch where the living wood has been trained around an old iron doorframe. Inside, it is warm and remarkably quiet, the deafening roar of the cavern muffled by ten feet of fibrous root into a steady, comforting thrum, like a heartbeat. The walls are lined with thick mats of dried gray moss and hung with curtains of boiled silk to catch the condensation. They light these rooms not with bulbs—which draw midges and burn too hot—but with wide glass jars packed with damp root-shavings and colonized by the sickly-green fungus, casting a soft, cool glow over hammocks, clay jugs, and shelves of salvaged brass.

They are not the masters of this place, any more than the beetles are. They are its pruners, its scavengers, and its symbiotes.

Much of their work is keeping the machinery and the cavern from choking one another. They swing out on rope-slings along the vertical root-pillars to clear the brass sap-taps, scraping away the beetle-ribbons and chipping back the living wood whenever it tries to swallow a valve whole. They tend the wide iron funnels with their rotating wire brushes, clearing jams when too many millipedes pack into the chutes, and hauling the heavy sacks of roasted, ammonia-sharp chitin meal from the grinding hoppers.

With the cavern’s native life, they keep a careful, practical truce. They never scrape the translucent conduit-slugs off the junction boxes; they know better. When a transformer needs rewiring, a tender gently pries the pulsing, gelatinous mat aside with a horn spatula, works the copper, and lays the slugs carefully back in place so their slime can reseal the box. They treat the slugs like slow, blind livestock, occasionally tossing them scraps of corroded copper wire to keep them clustered where the insulation is needed most. 

They tap the plated shells of the dog-sized siphon-grazers with their wrench-butts to make the heavy creatures unseal and shuffle off a buried bolt—and when an old grazer finally loses its grip and dies, they pry its curved shell free to use as roofing shingles, buckets, or shields against the dripping condensation. They even know the flight-paths of the eyeless, sonar-clicking predators in the upper dark; before stringing a new steel cable across the void, the crews tie fluttering strips of silk along its length so the winged hunters’ clicks will read the obstacle and bank away.

Their entire lives are measured not in days or nights—there is no sun here, and no memory of one—but in twenty-minute intervals, anchored to the *blows* of the central pressure-tanks.

A few minutes before a blow, the tenders feel the pressure building in the grating beneath their feet. A rapid *tink-tink-tink* ripples down the suspension cables. Across the dim void, figures pause at their valves, clip their harnesses to the nearest iron truss, and pull their silk hoods tight over their noses and mouths.

Then the tanks shriek. The wave of waste steam rolls out, hot and blinding, and the root-walls flex and sweat. As the cavern erupts—the grazers unsealing to drink, the beetles surging, the winged predators dropping from the vault in a whistling cloud—the crews move fast. This is the brief, chaotic window when the sap runs thickest from the flexed taproots and the wire brushes scrape the richest loads of chitin. In the fog and the deafening roar, oiled figures dart along the slick catwalks, swapping out burnt arc-carbons and freeing jammed overhead buckets before the intake fans spin up to clear the mist.

Then the fans settle back into their low drone, the mist thins, and the tenders slip back into the ten-foot shadows between the lamps.

They have lived here long enough that they no longer wonder what the overhead buckets were originally meant to carry, or why the centrifuges spin the sap. The machines keep the air moving, the water sweet, and the lights lit; the cavern feeds the machines. When a tender grows too old and stiff to outrun the damp—when their joints lock up and the moss begins to take an interest in their boots—they are not buried in stone, for there is no stone here. They are laid into a hollowed pocket of a young taproot, packed in dry moss, and sealed with a slat of oiled wood. Within a season or two, the living wall quietly heals over them, just as it healed over the anchor-bolts, folding them back into the warm, humming engine beneath the world.
