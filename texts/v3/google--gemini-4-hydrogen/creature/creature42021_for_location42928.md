In the tea-colored dusk beneath the waist-high canopy, where the light is too weak for color and the air is too thick to carry a scent farther than an arm’s length, the true masters of the basin move on their knuckles and knees.

At a glance, they look like nothing more than another layer of the pelt—broad-shouldered, low-slung shapes the color of bruised plums and wet ash, no taller at the crown than the undersides of the waxy fronds. They do not stand upright; there is nowhere to stand without pressing your head into the oven-glare of the sky, and no reason to rise above the mats where every slit-eyed lizard and clinging snail can see you. Instead, they flow through the tangled labyrinth of pale aerial roots in a loose, four-limbed sprawl, their long forearms swinging them over the knee-deep seeps, their flanks brushing through curtains of slick, gelatinous fungi without a sound.

They are built for a world that has never known softness.

Their skin is hairless, smooth, and perpetually weeping—not with sweat, which could never evaporate in the saturated air, but with a thin, bitter oil that beads along their ribs and collects in the deep pleats of their flanks. The oil keeps the midges from settling, makes them too slick for the gill-frilled reptiles to grip, and turns aside the worst of the parasitic snails. Beneath their chins and along their sides, thin, vascularized membranes hang in loose folds like damp curtains, flushed dark with blood; when they pause beneath a heavy frond, they tilt their heads back to catch the slow, cool *plip-plip-plip* of condensation directly onto those membranes, shedding the fierce, trapped heat of their bodies one drop at a time.

Their hands, knees, and feet are another matter entirely. Where the rest of them is supple and slick, their undersides are armored in thick, layered plates of glassy-black horn—dense, continuously growing calluses as hard as hoof. The raw basalt underfoot is sharper than any blade, and it does not yield; as they step across the blistered slag, the stone scores pale, chalky lines into their soles, shaving away thin curls of dead horn without ever dulling its own edge. Every few days—though there are no days here to count—the outer layer of a sole peels loose in a tough, ragged sheet. They pull it free with their teeth, chew it soft, and swallow it whole, or save the largest strips to wrap around shards of broken slag. Nothing is left on the glass for the armored, many-legged scavengers to follow.

To look at their faces is to understand what the basin has taken from them, and what it has given in return. Their eyes are wide, lidless, and pale as milk-glass, shielded by clear membranes that flick sideways every few seconds to wipe away the drifting moisture. In the permanent twilight, they see only shapes, sheen, and motion. Their ears are small, tight slits sealed against the deafening, unbroken roar of the stridulating wings and booming throat-sacs that fills the air.

Instead, they listen with their jaws.

The lower jaw of each dweller is massive, broad, and bare of oil at its tip, ending in a flat, callused pad of dense bone wired straight to the inner ear. When one of them halts on a bare shelf of vitrified stone, it lowers its chin until the bone rests flush against the glass. Instantly, the maddening wall of biological noise in the air drops away, and the world beneath opens up. The newly cooled basalt plates are continuous, unbroken by soil or frost, and they ring like giant, dark bells. Through its jaw, the dweller feels the *tick-tick-tick* of a thousand scavengers swarming a dead amphibian three ridges away; it feels the heavy, wet *slump* of a tree-snake dropping into a warm seep; it feels the slow, deliberate *tap… tap-tap… tap* of another of its kind, half a mile across the steaming flats, striking a knuckle against the stone.

This is how they speak, how they hunt, and how they remember. Voice is useless here; the frogs and beetles own the air. The stone belongs to the listeners.

They live in loose, shifting knots of three or four, wedged together among the thickest tangles of aerial roots above the water, or lying half-submerged in the mirror-still seeps with only their nostrils and one pale eye breaking the surface. Without night or winter, they never truly sleep. They rest in halves—one side of the brain drifting into a dull, heat-soaked torpor while the other keeps a single hand pressed to the nearest ridge of basalt, reading the tremors of the floor. When a hairline fracture clogs with mineral crust and a seep begins to cool and sour, the knot untangles and moves on, sliding quietly beneath the mats until they find fresh heat.

Though they wear nothing—cloth or hide would rot against their skin in an hour—they are not bare-handed. Across their chests they sling cords of braided aerial root, hung with hollowed beetle-carapaces packed with bitter mineral salts, loops of shed horn, and small, sharp flakes of volcanic glass. In the tea-colored dusk, they are meticulous gardeners of the bare stone. Wherever the suffocating pelt of plants grows too thick over a broad plate of basalt, they tear the pale roots away, clearing small, jagged islands of naked glass.

These clearings are their listening-posts, and their larders. When they kill—prying a heavy, flat-headed amphibian from the shallows or cracking the shell of a giant snail—they drag the carcass onto the bare, razor-edged stone. Within moments, the clicking tide of many-legged scavengers pours out of the fissures to strip it. The dwellers crouch at the rim of the clearing, waiting until the scavengers are heaped three-deep over the meat, and then scoop them up by the double-handful, cracking the carapaces between flat, heavy molars while the glass beneath remains as clean and sterile as the day it cooled.

Yet they are not the largest things in the basin, nor the oldest.

Out in the widest, deepest seeps, where the buoyant root-mats thicken into islands that sag under their own weight, lie the things that make the occasional, guttural sighs heard across the miles. They are vast, flat-bodied creatures—broad as a collapsed lava-blister, sluggish as cooling pitch, their leathery hides so encrusted with pale fungi, clinging snails, and rooted fronds that they are nearly indistinguishable from the basin itself. They move perhaps once in a hundred sheds, dragging their immense bulk a few yards when the roots choke their nostrils, their lungs emptying in a long, sulfurous groan that vibrates through the water and rattles the teeth of the listeners half a mile away.

The dwellers do not fear them, and do not hunt them. They are too massive to kill and too slow to be a threat, unless one stumbles directly in front of a crescent mouth when it yawns open to suck in a vortex of warm water and weeds. Instead, the dwellers treat them as moving hills. They perch on the broad, mossy backs of the sleeping giants to rest above the razor-sharp glass, picking the largest beetles from the folds of their necks while the giants lie indifferent, half-buried in the endless, steaming wet.

And when one of the dwellers dies—of age, or infection from a deep glass-cut, or the sudden venom of a coiled snake—there is no burial. There is no earth to dig, and no time to linger before the scavengers arrive.

The remaining kin drag the body out onto the highest, barest shelf of basalt they can find, exposing it to the dull, coppery-grey twilight. They crouch around it in a tight circle, pressing their chins and knuckles flat against the vitrified stone, and as the clicking tide of scavengers rises from the cracks to strip the flesh before it can even cool, the living tap a slow, unbroken rhythm into the glass. They feel the work of the thousands of tiny legs through their jaws, listening until the rhythm changes, until the scraping stops and only the white, clean bones remain on the black stone.

Then they gather the longest bones—the femurs, the broad pelvic plates—and carry them back beneath the canopy. Wedged into the hairline fractures, or gripped in callused hands as hammers to strike the listening stones, the dead do not rot into dirt. They become the voice that travels through the glass, ringing out beneath the deafening roar of the basin, endlessly repeating itself in the windless, timeless heat.
