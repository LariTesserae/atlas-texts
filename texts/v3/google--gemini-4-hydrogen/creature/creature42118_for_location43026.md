Beneath the porous stone canopies, where the amber filaments hang like droplets of trapped noon, the air is cool, wet, and faintly metallic. Here, out of reach of the white glare, live the tenders of the plateau.

They are a people pared down to the essentials of altitude and heat: long-limbed and narrow-waisted, yet broad through the ribs to draw enough oxygen from the thin, high-altitude air. Generations spent in the dim, mist-fed galleries have bleached the pigment from their skin, leaving it the pale, faintly translucent gray of wet sinter. It is never quite dry; a thin, natural oil—closer to the heavy surface tension of the brine than to sweat—sheens their forearms and shoulders, keeping the ambient salt from crusting into their pores. Their hair is sparse or shaved close, and their eyes are large, set beneath heavy, protective brow-ridges, with wide, pale-rimmed irises that drink in the amber gloom.

If you meet one in a maintenance corridor, the first thing you notice is that they make no sound. Their feet are bare in the under-structures, the soles thick and calloused as horn, and they walk with a rolling, heel-to-toe deliberation that never slaps the wet basalt. In a city where machinery is mute, noise is the grammar of failure. A hiss means a split hydraulic bladder; a dry rattle means a failing magnetic cushion; a sharp crack means thermal shock in a glass span. The tenders live inside an architecture of quiet vigilance, and their own bodies have learned the same restraint.

They rarely speak above a breathy murmur, pitched low enough to stay within arm’s reach. Across the length of a damp gallery, they communicate instead through the stone itself—tapping coded rhythms with knuckles or copper rods against the water-pipes, or pressing their palms flat to the warm walls to send and receive low, chest-born hums that travel through the basalt like the vibration of the manifolds overhead. Even their garments are chosen for silence: tunics and wide trousers woven from spun mineral-fiber and cured bladder-wing membrane, supple and slick, so that limbs brush past one another without the rasp of cloth.

Their days are governed not by clocks, but by the slow thermal pulse of the plateau and the steady *drip, drip, drip* of the condensation troughs.

Fresh water is the only true wealth here. The brine that sheets the surface is oily, scalding, and thick with silica; a mouthful would sear the throat and coat the gut in stone. So the tenders worship—or at least guard with a fierce, quiet tenderness—the weeping ceilings of their under-cities. Every gallery is angled toward stone runnels that catch the cool distillate seeping down from the porous roof-slabs. They drink it straight from ceramic ladles, use it to rinse the salt-rime from their joints, and ration it to the subterranean terraces where their food grows.

In the deepest, warmest levels, fed by the waste-heat of the thermoelectric spires and lit by strings of amber glass, they cultivate vast, tiered beds of pale, fleshy shelf-fungi and bulbous, salt-tolerant tubers that taste faintly of sulfur and wet chalk. To this they add the harvest of the evaporation pans: the flat-shelled grazers, whose translucent carapaces they crack open with small bismuth hammers to scoop out the rich, briny meat within, and occasionally the long, ribbon-like predators trapped in wicker baskets beneath the causeways, smoked slow over heated copper plates until they turn into strips of dark, savory leather.

Much of their labor is a kind of patient grooming. Teams of tenders move through the sub-basements with long-handled scrapers, clearing the silica scale that forever tries to choke the intake valves and heat exchangers. They tend the vast banks of hydraulic bladders—enormous, leathery sacs of cured animal-hide and synthetic resin that swell and contract to open the surface sluices—rubbing them down with rendered fat from the pan-grazers to keep them supple. In the control alcoves, there are no screens or clattering switches, only walls of thick glass tubes filled with mercury and dyed brine. A tender can stand before such a wall for hours, watching a meniscus rise by the width of a fingernail, reading the health of a mirror array three miles away in the subtle tremor of a fluid column.

When a fault demands that someone ascend into the midday glare, the preparation is solemn, almost ritualistic. The chosen tender pulls on thick-soled boots of layered cork and ceramic to insulate against the scalding sinter—and to feel, through the heels, the low hum of the buried conduits. Over their pale skin goes a heavy, hooded mantle of porous, woven stone-fiber, soaked thoroughly in fresh water from the condensation troughs; as they step out into the oven-air, the water evaporates slowly from the fabric, wrapping them in a private, cooling shroud of steam. Last comes the mask: a broad, flat-brimmed visor of blackened bronze pierced only by two horizontal slits of smoked mica, narrowing the blinding white world into a manageable band of amber and shadow.

Up on the plateau, in the fierce noon stillness, a surfaced tender moves like one of the stilt-feeders—lifting each foot with agonizing care, leaning into the heavy air, wiping salt-crust from a parabolic mirror with a damp rag or freeing a wedged barge from a magnetic rail. The surface fauna barely registers them. A flat-shelled grazer will creep straight across the toe of a stationary boot; a bladder-wing will drift against a tender’s wet hood, discharge its tiny static prickle into the damp cloth, and float away unharmed.

It is only in the late afternoon, when the high air cools and the rising steam collapses into that sudden, blinding white mist, that the under-structures truly open.

As the glare dissolves into luminous fog, hundreds of figures emerge from the terraced stairwells onto the pale ceramic causeways and the broad dikes between the pans. Without the sun’s hammer, they shed their heavy masks and walk bare-headed into the cool whiteout, letting the mist settle on their hairless scalps and oil-sheened shoulders. They move in silent, purposeful lines—hauling woven baskets of harvested salt-cakes from the unmanned barges, resetting the wicker traps beneath the bridges, gathering the fresh mist-water from the broad collector-sheets spread along the roofs.

Through the thickening vapor, their pale silhouettes mingle with the motionless stilt-feeders wading in the mirrored brine. Neither speaks; neither splashes. For an hour, until the mist drains away into the stone and the night’s chill begins to draw the heat back out of the basalt, the builders and the beasts share the wet plateau on equal terms—quiet, unhurried, and entirely at home in the steam.
