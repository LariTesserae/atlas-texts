At first, there is nothing to distinguish it from the rest of the room’s long, slow accretion. In a chamber where vines have turned to wood inside transformer casings and rust has grown like stalactites from the pipe joints, a shape hunched along the upper curve of a cast-iron heat-exchanger looks like just another blister of mineral sinter.

It is only when the enormous pressure-bladder somewhere in the gloom gives its wet, minute-long *thump*—and the fine, dry rain of rust-dust drifts down from the vault—that you might notice one of those shapes does not collect the dust. The red iron-flour settles on the tarnished pewter fronds, on the sagging catwalks, on the crusted flanges, and on the thing’s broad, ridged back; and then, a few seconds later, the dust slides off in tiny, dry avalanches as the ridges shift by a fraction of an inch.

It is about the size of a kneeling draft horse, though built lower and flatter, pressed close to the curves of the iron it inhabits. It does not skitter. Nothing here has the nervous, quick-burning metabolism for skittering; in this oven-thick heat, speed is a way to cook yourself from the inside out. Instead, it moves on the same clock as the chamber: a patient, heavy, unhurried crawl that treats the space between one steam-throb and the next as plenty of time to lift a single foot.

When it finally unhooks itself from the heat-exchanger and begins to descend toward the lower manifolds, its body reveals itself by pieces in the bruised twilight. It has six limbs, though the front pair are shorter, thicker, and set closer to the chest, ending in blunt, spatulate claws of dark, copper-stained horn. The four walking legs are long and double-jointed, built for bridging the gaps between corroded pipes and gripping the braided copper cables overhead without putting weight on the brittle, calcified roots. Its feet have no pads of soft flesh; the soles are thick slabs of heat-cured, blackened callus, cracked like baked clay and entirely numb to the scorching iron.

Its hide is a dry, segmented armor of overlapping plates—not glossy like a beetle’s shell, but matte and pitted, the color of oiled cast iron and dark basalt. Over the decades, the chamber has written itself onto that armor. Pale streaks of mineral crust have formed along the seams between the plates, where condensation gathers and evaporates. Here and there, where the hide is oldest and most deeply fissured, stray filaments of the pale-orange tendrils have taken root, weaving a sparse, fibrous beard along its shoulders and spine. Where those tendrils brush against the faint bio-electric charge of its own nerves, they have formed two or three small, knotty galls that glow with a dim, steady amber—moving lanterns carried through the gloom, no brighter than the dying indicator-filaments on the walls.

Its head is broad, wedge-shaped, and nearly eyeless. There is no use for keen sight in a place packed too tightly to see across, lit only by feverish plant-glow and the cherry haze of overheated coils. Instead, behind heavy, mica-flecked brow ridges, it carries two recessed pits lined with dark, heat-sensitive membrane—enough to read the room as a map of warmth, distinguishing the dull red silhouette of a cooling pipe from the fierce, bright line of a live busbar. The rest of its perception comes through bone. Its lower jaw is massive and underslung, almost always pressed lightly against whatever metal or stone it is climbing, drinking in the sub-bass thrum of the buried turbine. In a room this deafening, sound is not something heard through the air; it is the texture of the floor. If a pinhole leak widens in a manifold forty feet away, or a heavy frond tears loose under the weight of its own water-bladders, the creature feels the change in the vibration of the iron beneath its chin.

It descends onto a sagging catwalk, the rusted grating groaning softly under its weight—a sound swallowed instantly by the singing steam. It is thirsty.

Reaching out with one of its shorter forelimbs, it hooks a broad, leathery pewter frond and draws it gently downward without tearing the stem. The underside of the frond is ribbed with waxy channels leading to a cluster of swollen, translucent bladders, taut with hoarded vapor. The creature’s mouth opens—not wide, just a narrow, vertical parting of dry, keratinous lips—and a long, grooved tongue, dark as bruised plum and stiff as horn, slides out to pierce the base of a bladder. It does not crush the sac; it drains it cleanly, with a slow, rhythmic expansion of its throat, taking only half the water before withdrawing and letting the frond spring back toward the ceiling. To strip a plant dry in this place would be to starve later.

When it feeds, it turns to the machinery. It climbs onto the sunken housing of a dynamo, wedging its heavy fore-claws into the louvered vents where thick, woody vines have grown around the copper windings inside. It is after the sweet, stewed sap that bleeds from the vine-joints where the wood presses against the hot metal, and after the knotty, mineral-rich galls that form where tendrils fuse to live wire. Its teeth are flat, chisel-edged plates reinforced with iron and copper drawn from its diet, capable of shearing through woody bark and scraping verdigris straight off a brass fitting without chipping. As it chews, a faint, sharp smell of ozone rises from its jaws—the stray current trapped in the plant-flesh grounding out harmlessly through its saliva and heavy, mineral-laden bones.

It has no predators. Nothing else with blood or breath lives in the basalt vault to hunt it, and it has no reason to hurry. Yet it is not merely a passenger in the chamber’s equilibrium; it is one of the hinges on which that equilibrium turns.

Once, perhaps every few days—though time here has no days, only the steady *thump* of the pressure-bladder and the slow creep of rust—it makes a circuit of the heat-exchangers. Where the pale-orange tendrils have grown too thick over a radiator fin, choking off the rising thermals and threatening to cook the surrounding fronds to ash, the creature tears the excess away with its blunt fore-claws and eats it. Where a dripping seam of condensation has begun to crust over with mineral sinter, sealing off a cool patch of rock it uses to shed excess heat, it scrapes the stone clean with the edge of its jaw. It does not do this out of any memory of what the machines were built for. It has never seen a sky, nor a blueprint, nor the hands that bolted the cast iron together. It tends the room the way a root tends a crack in a wall: by following the warmth, the moisture, and the hum of the current, keeping the passages open because an open passage is where life stays bearable.

Every few minutes, its ribcage expands in a long, deep, bellows-like draw, pulling in the thick, mineral-heavy air. Inside its chest, wet, folded baffles trap the baked dust and iron-flour before it can reach the lungs, binding the grit into heavy mucus. Once an hour or so, the creature pauses on a pipe, lowers its wedge-shaped head, and coughs up a small, dense pellet of compacted rust and mica—which rolls off the iron and lands on the floor below, indistinguishable from the rest of the baked crust.

When it has eaten and drunk, it climbs back up into the tangle of braided copper cables and calcified roots beneath the eighty-foot vault, choosing a spot directly above one of the monumental heat-exchangers where the rising thermals are steady and dry. It hooks its four long legs around a thick conduit, tucks its forelimbs against its plated chest, and lets its jaw rest against the vibrating metal.

The two small amber galls on its shoulders dim slightly as its own slow currents settle into sleep. Beneath it, the buried turbine turns at its agonizingly sluggish pace. The steam sings. The pressure-bladder swells and *thumps*, shaking loose another fine rain of dust. And the creature hangs there in the bruised twilight, warm as the iron it clings to, waiting out the decades with the rest of the room.
