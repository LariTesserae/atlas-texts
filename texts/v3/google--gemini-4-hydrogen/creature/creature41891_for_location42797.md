Nothing that lives on the table has a permanent address. To stay in one place for more than an hour is to risk being kneaded into the peat by a rising gear of turf, or boiled by a sap-vessel deciding to vent its pressure twenty yards to the left of where it vented yesterday. Everything here—from the smallest chitinous flyer to the heavy-limbed figures that move through the sleet—is built around timing, insulation, and the endless, deafening vibration of the mat.

Just beneath the wet skin of the black peat-moss, in the cold, dark water that weeps whenever the wind presses down, live the **circuit-hunters**. From above, one looks like nothing more than a broad, ragged patch of pale crustose lichen, indistinguishable from the carpet around it. Its body is flat, muscular, and nearly boneless, spread wide beneath the surface root-nerve. It has no eyes; instead, it grows fine, copper-threaded tendrils into the surrounding moss, listening to the electrical hum of the plateau. It waits for the *thump-sizzle, thump-sizzle* of the six-legged grazers. When a herd comes loping through the mist, their copper-dense hooves grounding the static charge of the bog with every stride, the hunter feels the rhythm of their approach through its tendrils. As a straggler splashes across its back, the creature does not bite. It arches its insulated spine, pulls the ambient charge from the nearest voltaic lichen-mat, and snaps a blinding arc straight up into the grazer’s wet belly. The beast drops in a cloud of sour steam, and the hunter unspools from the peat like a wet, pale rug, wrapping the carcass in muscular folds and dragging it down into the dark water before the translucent scavengers can smell the ozone.

While the hunters wait in the flat, cold dark, smaller things use the frayed rules of the altitude to travel. Along the bases of the giant fern-fronds, where water forgets which way is down and beads upward against the falling sleet, you can sometimes catch the flicker of **upward-swimmers**. They are sleek, blind, lungless ribbons of silver-grey flesh, no thicker than a thumb. Hatched in the freezing, pressurized groundwater beneath the moss, they find the undersides of the fronds and ride the inverted rivulets straight up into the churning, high-pressure vapor-heads of the botanical engines. Up there, in the scalding, sour mist, they breed in frantic, tangled knots, their skin toughening against the heat, before their eggs are blown outward on the steam-plumes to freeze and rain back down into the sleet miles away.

Inside the towering hydraulic reeds themselves, where the groundwater is slammed skyward in ear-splitting strokes, live the **valve-clots**. They are slow, armored mollusks the size of millstones, wedged deep inside the pressurized sap-vessels. Their shells are grown from leached iron and hardened bark-fiber, so dense they ring like anvils when the root-hammers strike. For months, a valve-clot sits in the dark throat of a reed, feeding on the rich, metallic slurry rushing past it and swelling until it nearly chokes the stalk. It acts as a living gasket, holding back impossible pressures, until its shell is packed with eggs. Then, all at once, it relaxes its grip and lets go. The reed ruptures in a deafening roar of supercooled steam and splintered bark, flinging the clot’s young across half a league of bog—and instantly drawing a shrill, clicking tornado of the blind, lard-pale flyers, who swarm the fresh wound to lay their own eggs in the warm, greasy exhaust before the stalk collapses.

Out on the wide, bruised-pewter flats, the **six-legged grazers** are the closest thing the plateau has to weather you can steer by. They move in herds of hundreds, their heavy, matted pelts always dripping, their brassy honks carrying for miles over the flat moss. Because they feed almost entirely on conductive root-fiber and voltaic lichen, their bodies are saturated with heavy metals; their milk is warm, thin, and tastes faintly of copper pennies, and their fat renders down into a dense, static-holding tallow that never quite freezes. When a storm of blue-white arcs begins snapping too violently around the engines, the herds gather in tight, shoulder-to-shoulder circles out on the empty moss, lowering their six broad hooves deep into the peat to act as a single, living ground. Sparks hiss and dance along the tips of their wet fur, lighting them up in the dimness like a ring of pale, humming lanterns.

And where the plateau’s rules fray thin, something else hunts the herds. Out in the spaces between the steam-plumes, where the flat horizon folds over itself like wet paper and a distant electrical arc flashes twice—half a second out of sync—live the **crease-walkers**. They are rarely seen clearly, and never for long. Lean, long-limbed, and covered in a matte-black pelt that drinks the pewter light, they do not run across the squelching moss the way the grazers do. They wait on the ridges of newly torqued turf-gears, watching the mist with pale, vertical-slit eyes. When the horizon folds, compressing three miles of flat bog into a single, shimmering seam for the duration of a double-flash, a crease-walker steps across the fold, appearing suddenly in the middle of a distant herd. They kill with long, serrated forearm-spurs, tearing out the copper-rich throat of a grazer and dragging it back toward the seam before the air flattens out again—leaving the rest of the herd honking in panic at a patch of empty moss where a shadow, detached from the killer, still sways in a wind that stopped blowing minutes ago.

Yet the strangest inhabitants of the table are the ones who have no fur, no chitin, and no copper hooves—the **resin-clad**, the quiet, heavy-boned people of the mist.

They are not builders. There are no walls here because they have never seen a reason to stack one stone—if stone even existed—upon another. To look at one standing in the sleet, you might mistake them at first for a weathered, leaning stump of hardened bark. They are squat, broad-shouldered, and immense through the chest, wrapped in thick, overlapping plates of cooled, amber-grey resin that they harvest from the torquing turf-gears. They pack the warm, greasy stuff onto their hides while it is still pliable, mixing it with peat-ash and the shed winter-hair of the six-legged grazers, until it sets in the freezing wind into a flexible, waterproof carapace that smells faintly of sap and scorched hair.

Beneath the resin, their skin is pale, rubbery, and thick as boiled leather, constantly flaking away in wet sheets wherever the static arcs catch them. They have no outer ears—those would have frozen off or been deafened epochs ago by the tireless bass thud of the root-hammers—only small, valved openings behind their jaws that they can clamp shut when a nearby hydraulic reed vents. Their eyes are deep-set beneath heavy ridges of bone and fat, fitted with a second, milky eyelid that snaps across the pupil whenever the voltaic lichen discharges, saving them from snow-blindness in the blue-white glare.

They do not speak with their mouths; the endless, vibrating roar of the plateau would swallow any word at three paces. Instead, they carry the sound in their bones. When two of them meet on the empty leagues of moss, they press their broad, resin-caked foreheads together and hum—a low, vibrating drone produced in twin throat-sacs that cuts straight through the skull, below the hiss of the steam and above the thud of the hammers. Across wider distances, they strip off their thick, plaited moss-sandals and stomp their broad, calloused heels into the saturated peat, sending sharp, coded rhythms through the wet root-nerve that another resin-clad can feel tingling up through the soles of their feet half a mile away.

Their entire lives are paced to the twenty-minute churn of the plateau. They carry no metal—only long, hooked gaffs carved from the dense, copper-sheathed leg-bones of dead grazers. When a kilometer-wide stretch of the flat bog begins to groan and torque upward into grinding gears of turf and root, the resin-clad do not run away. They trot *toward* the deafening, wet roar, gathering at the edges of the rising teeth with their bone hooks ready. As the grinding roots extrude long, steaming ribbons of warm, greasy resin, they snag the stuff mid-air, stuffing it into pouches of cured grazer-stomach before it hits the freezing wind and shatters. They eat it warm for the rendered sap-sugars, burn it in hollowed-out bark-bowls for a few minutes of greasy heat, and smear it over their young to keep the sleet from locking their joints.

When it is time to sleep, they do not look for shelter, because no shelter lasts. Instead, they track the brassy honking of a grazer herd through the mist, wait for the beasts to bed down in their static-grounding circle, and crawl straight into the warm, humming pile of wet fur and six-legged flanks. There, pressed between the beating hearts of the beasts and the endless bass thud of the moss below, they close their milky eyelids and rest.

And when they wake and stand up into the bruised pewter light, the first thing a resin-clad always does—before checking their gaff, before listening to the peat, before stepping out across the weeping lichen—is look down at the moss at their feet, to make sure their shadow is still attached, and swaying in the right wind.
