At first, even if you stood on the highest terrace for days, you would swear the plateau was empty.

Nothing moves against the bright sky. Nothing disturbs the shimmering, parallel windrows of iron, silica, and carbon out on the flats. The groves sit miles apart like dark, oiled-bronze islands, and when a frond dries out and stiffens into a brittle wafer of pure carbon, it lies where it fell, untouched.

To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for movement in the air or tracks in the dust, and start looking at the seams where the rock and the groves meet the current.

Deep inside the thickets, beneath the heavy, paddle-shaped leaves, the stillness is crowded with small, cryptic life. Clinging to the undersides of the canopies are flat, shield-backed things no larger than a fingernail, their shells built of matte-black carbon and pale, translucent quartz. They have no mouths and no legs. They slide microscopically along the oiled skin of the leaves on a thin film of silvery emulsion, seeking out the tiny voltage leaks where a leaf-vein nears the surface. They drink the stray charge, and at the peak of the afternoon heat—when the quartz ridges flex hardest and the canopies begin to hiss—they open invisible pores along their rims to bleed the excess static into the dry air, keeping the oil-rich leaves from scorching under their own current.

Deeper still, packed into the dark, razor-sharp crevices alongside the copper-tinted moss, live slender, segmented things like lengths of oiled wire. They coil tightly around the groves’ pale metallic taproots, bridging them to stray flecks of magnetite in the raw basalt. They are living fuses: when a sudden thermal shift sends a spike of high voltage down a terrace edge, their liquid-metal cores swell, absorbing the surge and bleeding it off harmlessly as dry heat into the stone.

Yet these are only the smallest inhabitants—the parasites and stewards of the thickets. There is something else on the plateau, larger and far rarer, though you could stare straight at one from thirty paces away and mistake it for an outcrop of fractured stone.

They are angular, faceted, and asymmetrical, built from the same grammar as the terraces: hollow struts of matte-black carbon, overlapping plates of oxidized verdigris, and joints swollen with heavy, non-conductive oil and silvery emulsion. They have no eyes, no mouths, no faces. Where a head might be, there is only a clean, geometric cluster of pale quartz prisms, or a fanned crest of dark bronze that tilts slowly to follow the sun—not to see the light, but to gauge its thermal weight.

They never set foot in the dust basins. To step onto the compacted alkali and graphite would leave a track, and scatter the neat, electrostatic windrows. Instead, they move exclusively along the bare, unweathered stone of the terrace edges and the exposed ribbons of native copper, stepping on three or four needle-sharp limbs tipped with raw quartz. Because their frames hold no heavy water—only honeycomb carbon, light oil, and fine threads of liquid metal—they move with an eerie, weightless precision, leaving not even a scuff on the glassy striations of the basalt.

For most of the day, they do not move at all.

They fold their angular limbs close to a terrace rim or the outer edge of a grove, and extend fine, pale metallic filaments from their undersides directly into a copper seam. Once wired in, they vanish into the geometry of the rock. In that stillness, the plateau’s steady direct current flows up through their liquid-metal veins, energizing the quartz lattices inside them, and the entire valley opens up to their senses.

They do not hear the wind or see the sky; they feel the voltage gradients. Wired into the crust, a listener on a western ridge can feel the piezoelectric strain of a quartzite shelf thirty miles away, or the faint seismic ring of an alloy rod snapping at an extrusion vent out in the middle of a graphite pan. They can feel the exact, balanced draw of every grove across the flats.

And through the copper seams, they speak. By subtly shifting the flow of liquid metal in their joints, they alter their own internal resistance, sending faint, precise ripples of changing voltage down the native metal veins. To anything standing in the thin air above, the plateau remains dead silent. Beneath the stone, the copper ribbons hum with slow, geometric exchanges—conversations of thermal pressure, of current and load, of the afternoon peak cresting and fading.

When the sun finally drops behind the distant ridges and the high-altitude air turns bitter cold, the quartz relaxes and the great current fades to a whisper. The listeners do not seek caves or build shelters. They simply draw their filaments back, lock their oiled joints, and wait on the bare stone in the dark, holding the last residual charge of the day inside their internal capacitors—listening through their quartz feet to the dry, solitary *tick* of cooling alloy rods out in the basins, until the first morning light strikes the terraces and wakes the circuit again.

And when one of them grows old—after centuries of thermal cycles, when its quartz plates cloud and its carbon struts grow too brittle to flex—it does not rot or collapse in the dust. It walks slowly along the bare rock to the far edge of a basin, where a hairline tectonic fault is already pulling the gleaming alloy rods back down into the earth. There, it folds itself neatly over the fissure, letting the slow, frictionless mineral creep draw it back into the crust to be remelted, leaving the plateau as clean, quiet, and unchanged as it found it.
