To live here is to have given up on water entirely. Nothing on the plateau has a pulse in the wet, urgent way of lower worlds; there are no lungs drawing breath, no stomachs dissolving anything, no blood rushing to repair a wound. Whatever survives in this warm, breathless dark runs on slower, drier currencies: a fraction of a volt between two plates of stone, the steady rise of heat from a buried conduit, the patient friction of mineral against mineral.

The smallest things are barely distinguishable from the dust itself. Along the ceramic skirts of the high-voltage insulators, where the air holds a faint, permanent tang of ozone, microscopic filaments of pure silver and tin sometimes creep outward across the glaze. They do not grow so much as *assemble*, drawn atom by atom out of the metal fittings by the tireless pull of static potential. For years they advance by the width of a hair, branching like frost on a black pane, until a single frond bridges the gap to a grounded housing. There is a tiny, violet snap—barely audible in the thin air—and the filament vaporizes back into grey ash, leaving the insulator clean to begin the decade-long climb again.

Larger, and unmistakably alive, are the flat, segmented creatures that cling to the ridged fins of the heat-exchangers. From above, they look like discarded pieces of unglazed bisque porcelain—matte, chalk-white, and entirely eyeless, their overlapping plates scarred with faint scorch marks. If you were to pry one gently from the warm metal, you would find its underside is soft, dark, and velvety, like a living pad of pressed graphite.

They have no mouths and no legs. Instead, they glide on a dry, imperceptible film of shed mineral powder, moving at a pace so glacial that you could stare at one for an hour and swear it was bolted to the casing. They are creatures of thermal tides. When a distant, subterranean flywheel shifts its weight by a millimeter, or a transformer hall steps its load up by a fraction of a percent, an invisible wave of warmth ripples through the plateau’s iron ribs. Across kilometers of dark avenues, the porcelain plates feel it and begin to drift, crowding toward the slightly warmer fins, pressing their conductive bellies flat against the metal to drink in the stray current. When one has accumulated enough charge over the course of decades, it does not lay eggs; it simply goes rigid, splits cleanly down its central seam, and leaves two smaller, chalky halves to inch apart in the dark.

Out on the wide, ruler-straight boulevards of vitrified slag, you are more likely to encounter the ones that drag the wire.

They are roughly the size of a kneeling hound, though their silhouette is flatter, wider, and borne on eight or ten thin, multi-jointed legs. Their shells have reached the same truce with time as the factories around them, plated in a dense, velvety patina of black iron oxide that catches none of the sparse amber light. They move with an agonizing, deliberate care, lifting and setting each needle-tipped foot as if trying not to wake the dust beneath them.

Behind each of them trails a single, hair-thin metallic filament, spun from scavenged copper and carbon grit inside their own dry bodies. Some drag only a few meters of it; older ones pull lengths that vanish into the dark behind them, whispering faintly over the crushed quartz. This is how they feed—not by eating, but by spanning the gap. Because the plateau is vast and its electrical potential is never quite uniform, the trailing wire acts as a living bridge, harvesting the infinitesimal difference in charge between the slag behind them and the pylon ahead. 

When one reaches the base of a basalt plinth or a transformer housing, it stops. It may stay there for three days, or ten, pressing its blunt, sensor-lined forequarters against the warm stone, letting its trailing filament settle into the ash. To an observer, it looks dead. In truth, it is equalizing—slowly bleeding off its gathered potential into the city’s ground grid, listening with its whole carapace to the sub-audible thrum of the buried flywheels. Occasionally, two of them will cross paths in the empty spaces between buildings, their trailing wires tangling in the dark. There is no aggression, no panic; a fight would waste heat. Instead, they freeze for hours, then begin a slow, solemn choreography of stepping over one another’s limbs and passing the thin copper threads beneath their bellies until they are free, parting without a sound.

Stranger still are the things sealed away from the air altogether. In the windowless transformer halls, where the only light is the motionless red eye of a pressure gauge, heavy glass sight-tubes hold the plateau’s only remaining liquid: a clear, viscous dielectric oil that has not been stirred in ten thousand years. If you hold a lamp close to the thick, yellowed glass, you might see something suspended in the amber depths—pale, translucent ribbons of matter, thin as beaten gold leaf and knotted like kelp. They are not animals in any sense you would recognize, having formed from ancient impurities and polymerized oil under a century of steady voltage. Yet they turn. Once every few days, a ripple passes down a ribbon’s length, slow as smoke in a still room, as it coils toward the warm copper core of the transformer and then slowly unwinds again.

And then there are the ones that fit the doorways.

They are rare enough that you could walk the plateau for a year and see only one, standing motionless on an elevated stone viaduct, silhouetted against the bruised-amber glow of a distant relay tower. At first glance, you might mistake them for the builders of this place, returned to check their work. They are upright, tall and narrow, with long limbs jointed like the pantographs of old machines. Their bodies are dry as kiln-fired clay, wrapped in tattered, overlapping sheaths of oxidized copper and mineral-stiffened fabric that never rustles, because there is no wind to move it.

They have no faces—only smooth, slightly concave dishes of dull pewter where a head should be, angled slightly downward toward the ground or tilted toward the hum of a condenser. They do not speak, and if they once had names for the factories or the avenues, those names have long since evaporated into the thin air. They are not the architects, though they have grown into the mold the architects left behind, shaped by the height of the catwalks and the reach of the iron ladders.

They are simply the plateau’s last, quietest habit. They walk the vast, emptied-out avenues at a pace only slightly faster than the porcelain plates, pausing before the squat transformer halls to lay a dry, three-fingered hand against the riveted iron. They are not looking for flaws—there are no flaws left to fix. They are only checking the temperature, feeling the steady, pointless loop of the voltage, ensuring that nothing cools too quickly and nothing spikes in the dark. Once in a great while, one of them will reach up to a heavy valve wheel and turn it by the width of a fingernail, producing the single, dry *ping* of an insulator adjusting miles away.

Then it lowers its hand, steps back into the black between the buildings, and resumes its walk—one more closed circuit in a city that has forgotten how to stop.
