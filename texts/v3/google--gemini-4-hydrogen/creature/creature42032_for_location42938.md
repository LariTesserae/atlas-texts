If you stand on that cantilevered lip through three or four cycles—long enough for the roar to stop feeling like an intrusion and start feeling like the interior of your own skull—you begin to notice that the vault is not as empty as it looks.

At first, there is only the ecology of the wet stone: the pale, fibrous moss; the matte-grey grazers creeping along the temperature seams; the rare, leathery displacement of air when a blind glider banks past in the dark. But the service-crypts behind the walls are not sealed. And the maintenance hatches, for all their crusted lime and gypsum, do not all stay shut.

They open during the *whuff*—that brief, breathless window after the high-frequency whine drops away and a warm gust of air swirls up through the hollow core. A latch clicks, almost lost beneath the hiss of mist hitting a hot manifold. A square of dull alloy swings inward an inch, then three, moving with a slow, greased smoothness that has nothing to do with the age of the metal.

Something slips out onto the narrow gallery.

In the near-absolute dark, you wouldn’t see it at all if it didn’t pass briefly across the dirty-orange shimmer of a thermal vent, or pause near the bruised, ember-violet bleed of a cracked conductor. Even then, it is less a silhouette than a shifting of the wall’s own geometry: long-limbed, narrow-shouldered, folded low against the basalt so it never presents a target to the updrafts.

They have no single name for themselves, because they do not speak with air. In a room where a chest-rattling thrum lives inside your ribs and the whine makes your teeth ache, a throat is a useless instrument. Instead, they are creatures of bone and contact.

Their skin is the color of tallow left in a cellar—pale, matte, and toughened by centuries of hot dust and mineral mist, mottled with dark, slick patches along the elbows, knees, and ribs where they drag themselves through the recessed conduits. They wear nothing woven, only strips of dried, leathery wing-membrane bound around their forearms and shins to keep the hotter housings from blistering them. Their skulls are smooth and hairless, their nostrils narrow slits that can pinch shut when the ozone thickens. Where eyes might have been, there are only tight, sunken seams of skin; in a place where the air carries stinging grit and boiled mineral salts, an open wet eye is nothing but a wound waiting to happen.

Instead, they read the vault through their palms, their bare soles, and the flat, calloused pads of their chins.

Watch one move along the stepped terraces and you see how deeply it belongs to the architecture. It does not walk upright—there are no doors here sized for standing, and the ceiling of the lower galleries is too low for pride. It moves in a fluid, three-point crawl, two long fingers always trailing the phase-locked ceramic-metal of the wall, feeling for the hairline warmth of a stressed conductor or the cool, wet seam where the moss grows thickest.

They live on the twelve-minute clock. Everything in the vault does.

During the *whuff* and the minutes immediately after, while the pitch of the machine beneath the floor is still low and bearable, the galleries belong to them. They emerge from the stacked service-crypts carrying flat, chipped wedges of ceramic and bone hooks salvaged from the dead gliders. Their first work is always the louvers. The mineral-heavy air never stops laying down its pale crust of lime and gypsum, and if the intake louvers choke shut, the air inside the crypts turns scalding and dead. So they crouch in the dark, scraping patiently at the seams with their wedges, clearing the vents until the warm draft sighs back into their tunnels.

Others make their way down to the sheltered corners behind the lower buttresses, where the black basins of standing condensation collect. They do not drink from the basins directly—the water there is filmed with iridescent mineral oil that coats the throat and sours the stomach. Instead, they use the oil itself. They skim it with cupped hands and rub it into their joints, into the dry seams of their skin, and over the hinges of their favorite maintenance hatches so they can slip in and out without a sound.

They harvest the basins, too. In the warm, oil-dark water live small, blind things like pale, segmented ribbons—sluggish feeders that graze on the chemical film and cluster around the thermal seepage. The crypt-dwellers drag woven mats of hair-tendril through the shallows, lifting them out heavy with slick, wriggling threads that they strip between their teeth, chewing slowly for the salt and the fat.

For clean water, they climb.

High up near the undersides of the upper walkways, where the rising mist gathers before it lets go in tepid rain, the hair-like tendrils hang in damp curtains. The dwellers hang beside them, hooking their long toes over the edges of the sintered basalt, opening their mouths to catch the slow, heavy drops before they fall into the thermal plumes and vanish into steam. They are patient as stalactites. A dweller can wait motionless on a ledge for three full cycles just to fill a hollow gourd-shell of dried glider-bladder, listening all the while to the shifting weight of the machine below.

Because the phase-locked plating absorbs vibration, you cannot shout through a wall here, and you cannot tap a warning down a gallery. If a dweller wants to speak across the hollow center of the vault, it must use the room’s own nerves.

They crawl out to the edge of the abyss, where the slender, load-bearing filaments anchor into the perimeter. They wrap a palm around the humming strand—feeling the machine’s deep, steady thrum singing through the wire—and then they damp it, release it, and pluck it with a horn-thick thumbnail.

A sharp, high *ping* cuts across the cathedral dark, riding clean over the bass roar. Far across the void, on a gallery half a mile away, another hand resting on the same tension-web feels the pattern in its palm: a double-pluck, a pause, a long damped hum. *Cool seam.* *Rain coming.* *Glider down.*

The gliders are the only things in the vault that the dwellers fear, and the only things they truly prize. Blind, wide-winged, and silent except for the brief *whuff* of displaced air, the gliders rule the hollow center. Usually they hunt the matte-grey grazers, dropping out of the dark whenever they hear the faint, wet *click* of a shell against the mineral crust. But a hungry glider, banking low along the tiers, will strike at anything that clicks or taps in the dark.

So the dwellers never let their ceramic wedges strike stone if they can help it. And when they hunt a glider, they do not chase it. They wait until the air begins to thicken and heat—until the deep thrum climbs toward its breathless peak—and then they string frayed, oiled lengths of old tension-filament across the narrow gaps between the buttresses. A glider, rushing to tuck itself into the sheltered corners before the curtains of rain let go, clips a wingtip on the invisible line. It tumbles onto the terrace in a tangle of leathery membrane and hollow bone.

A downed glider feeds a whole tier of service-crypts. Its meat is lean and tastes faintly of ozone and hot dust, eaten raw or pressed against the warm, ribbed casings of the wall manifolds until it softens. Nothing is wasted: the wing-skin becomes wraps and water-bladders; the long, light arm-bones become picks for the gypsum; the curved ribs are set aside for the rare, quiet rituals of the crypts.

Up close, inside the dark conduits where the machine’s roar is muffled to a steady, comforting pulse, the dwellers are fiercely communal. They sleep tangled together in the dry, vibration-damped hollows behind the seamless housings, limbs braided, chins resting on each other’s collarbones so they can feel the slow rhythm of one another’s breathing. When one of them grows too stiff to climb, or its skin cracks beyond what the mineral oil can mend, the others bring it softened moss-pulp and warm basin-grubs. And when it stops moving altogether, they do not leave it on the terraces for the grazers to strip. They carry it deep into the oldest, unlit conduits—the dead-end crypts where the heat never quite reaches—and wedge it behind a sealed housing, pressing a flat palm to its chest one last time before latching the hatch.

Then, as the twelve-minute cycle climbs again, the vault begins to empty.

You can feel it coming before you hear it: a tightening in the air, a sudden sharpness in the smell of hot dust, the first heavy drops of tepid condensation letting go from the ceiling far above. On the wet ledges, the matte-grey grazers pull their limbs beneath their vibration-damped carapaces and lock down against the stone. High in the rafters, the blind gliders fold their wings and hook into the crevices.

Along the tiered galleries, the tallow-pale figures slip back toward the walls. One by one, the oiled maintenance hatches swing shut without a sound.

By the time the machine’s thrum rises into that breathless, teeth-aching whine, the open vault belongs once more to the steam, the falling curtains of warm rain, and the tireless, indifferent shifting of mass beneath the floor. Inside the service-crypts, behind the pitted ceramic-metal, the dwellers curl tight against the dark, hands pressed to their ears and chests pressed to the stone, waiting for the *whuff* that will tell them it is time to come out again.
