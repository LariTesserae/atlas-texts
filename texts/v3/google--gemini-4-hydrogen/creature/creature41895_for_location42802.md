If you drop through the timber-framed hatch at midday, you will not see it at first. The glare is too fierce, and the room is too busy throwing light back at itself. Your eyes will track the fast nets of caustic gold whipping across the flagstones, the roaring white plume from the bronze grate, the pale grit skating toward the drain. Nothing darts. Nothing breaks the line of the walls.

You only notice it when the swell hits.

A wave rolls against the quay outside—*boom*—and a moment later the intake blasts its cloud of aerated water into the upper half of the room. As the hiss of rising bubbles fills the glare, thousands of tiny silver beads catch along something that is not stone. For three seconds, before the cloud thins and vanishes toward the ceiling beams, they trace a long, coiled outline pressed flat into the recessed channel of the floor.

It is the color of the room: chalk-white along its arched back, fading to a faint, watered dusty-rose along its flanks and underside, as if it were cast from the same limestone and crushed-brick mortar as the walls. In the blinding midday light, its skin is just translucent enough at the edges that the caustic gold nets pass straight through it, blurring its shape into the flagstones beneath.

It is not a fish, and it is too heavy and slow to be an eel. Roughly the length of a man’s arm and as thick through the middle as a cedar beam, it has a broad, flattened belly and a blunt, eyeless head—or rather, a head where the eyes have been sealed over long ago by thick, milky skin, leaving only a pair of shallow, dimpled pits along the snout. It has no use for sight in a chamber with no shadows. Instead, it reads the room through its hide.

Where other sea creatures rely on speed or shells, this one relies on grip and weight. Along its underside run six pairs of short, muscular claspers—not claws, which would slip on the dressed stone, but broad, calloused pads like wet leather. When the current drives across the floor, those pads lock onto the parallel ridges left by the mason’s toothed chisel, holding the creature fast while the pale grit skates over its back, scrubbing its skin clean of any stray spore or parasite.

It moved in through the low seaward lintel sometime during the chamber’s first weeks, perhaps drawn by the bathwater warmth or driven out of the crowded, nipping dark of the lagoon outside. Whatever brought it, it found a paradise made of noise and light.

The creature lives by the room’s mechanical pulse. Between surges, when the current slackens and the terracotta pipes begin their hollow, jar-lip drone, it uncoils from the floor channel and hauls itself slowly toward the far bulkhead, hand-over-hand along the mortar seams. It never hurries. It pauses at the cedarwood flap-valve, nudging the hemp-lashed river cobble with its blunt snout as if testing the weight, then settles just beside the three terracotta mouths.

Along its spine runs a row of short, hollow, cartilaginous flutes—pale and slightly flared at the tips, like the mouths of tiny trumpets. As the pipes drone, the creature raises those crests into the slow back-eddy. They catch the vibration and begin to shiver, answering the terracotta with a low, wet thrumming that you cannot hear with your ears so much as feel in the roots of your teeth. When the next swell booms against the quay and the flap-valve lifts with a dull *clack*, the creature flattens instantly, clamping its flutes shut against its back while the surge roars over it and spills into the dark.

It has made a quiet arrangement with the room’s few other inhabitants. Because there are no crabs to pinch it and no barnacles to crust its hide, it has no reason to hide in corners, though it visits them often. Behind the stepped ledges, where the current slows to a gentle swirl, it tends the sparse tufts of bright green hair-algae and the translucent ribbons of sea lettuce. It grazes them with a wide, rasping mouth set far under its chin, taking only the longest, streaming tips and leaving the anchored bases intact—a careful gardener in a bare stone yard, keeping its only crop alive.

At noon, when the sun drops straight through the cedar grate overhead and turns the water into a blinding, silky broth, the creature leaves the floor altogether. Pulling itself up the vertical wall by the sharp corners of the limestone blocks, it drapes its heavy, rose-flushed body across the underside of the ceiling beams. There it hangs, half-blinded by glare, wrapped in the hissing white cloud of bubbles each time the intake fires, soaking in the heat and the thunder—the first and only tenant of a loud, bright, newborn world.
