If you stand still long enough for your ears to stop fighting the noise—long enough to count three or four turns of the great flywheel—you begin to notice that not all of the thick, ribbed hosing in the hollow is bolted to iron, and not all of the bloated, gray-green stems are rooted in the mat.

What lives here does not hurry, and it does not look at you.

You see it first as a subtle wrongness in the silhouette of the copper pressure vessels. What looked like a heavy, waterlogged strangler-fig root draped across the curved metal is shifting—not slithering, but *swelling*, lengthening by fractions of an inch as warm sump-water is pumped through its internal chambers. It has no legs to snag on the braided rhizomes, no bones to rattle against the sixty-cycle hum. Its body is a long, heavy, tapering cylinder of pale, cartilaginous flesh, stained the color of weak tea and dusted along its creases with the same powdery verdigris that crusts the iron. In the greenish-gold haze, slick with the perpetual drizzle, it looks like a length of living conduit or a drowned, barkless bough.

It is too large to see all at once. Much of it stays submerged in the warm, dark sump beneath the three-foot crust of liverwort and tubers, threaded through the sunken concrete plinths like an eel through a shipwreck. Only portions of it surface at a time, hauled up by broad, muscular belly-pads that grip wet steel and slick root-fiber with the patient suction of a mussel’s foot.

It has no eyes, because eyes would be useless in the tannic dark below the mat and blindingly redundant in the mist above. Instead, it perceives the cathedral hollow as an intricate architecture of pressure and sound. Running along its flanks are recessed seams of pale, delicate tissue, fringed with microscopic cilia that hang suspended in a film of mucus. These seams are tuned to the room. They read the sixty-cycle drone of the buried power cables the way a spider reads silk; they feel the shudder of the root-mat when the piston draws back; they map the exact position of every cane-stalk by the way its hollow stem resonates on the organ-note of the pump.

When you stepped down into the hollow, your boots broke the surface tension of the mat and sent a dull, irregular thud through the sump. To the dweller, it was a brief, ugly discord—like a stone dropped into a bell. It paused, holding its breath in the dark water beneath your feet, waiting to see if the rhythm of the room would mend.

Now, forty seconds have passed. The flywheel completes its turn. The iron groans, the brass relief valve trips with a deafening *clank*, and a plume of warm, mineral-scented vapor blasts upward into the canopy.

This is when it opens.

From the underside of the rusted steel gantry, where a thick loop of its body has anchored itself beside the tar-sheathed cables, a row of pale, translucent flaps parts along its upper ridge. Beneath them lie gill-like fronds, intricate and feathery as submerged ferns, flushed bright pink with circulating fluid. They unfurl directly into the descending cloud of steam. The creature drinks the mist—not just the water, which it already has in abundance, but the dissolved iron, the copper salts, the trace sulfur, and the sheer, concentrated heat of the condenser’s breath. As the cavernous hiss fades into the steady pattering of the canopy drizzle, the fronds fold neatly back behind their cartilaginous shutters, leaving only a smooth, slick hide.

It does not merely endure the machinery; it has grown into the role of its quietest, blindest attendant.

Down by the central plinth, where the monumental piston rod slides in and out of its cast-iron housing, another length of the creature rests coiled around the base. Every forty seconds, as the slick steel rod emerges gleaming from the cylinder, a blunt, muscular lobe of flesh presses gently against the moving shaft. There is no mouth there in the way a beast has a mouth—only a wide, rasping lip of hardened, mineral-rich tissue, constantly weeping a thick, oily slime. As the iron glides past, the creature’s rasp scours away the flaking rust and mineral scale that would otherwise score the cylinder and seize the pump. It swallows the iron-rich grit; its mucus coats the rod in a frictionless film. It has no concept of drainage or extraction, no memory of whoever bolted the plinth into the earth. It only knows that this warm, rhythmic stone tastes of salt and metal, and that if the rhythm stops, the water will grow cold and still.

Higher up, near the weeping embankment walls where the pale, leafless creepers wrap the live high-voltage conduits, the dweller’s thinner, tapering tail-end often lingers. It does not eat the creepers. It simply presses its nerve-rich flank against their swollen, warm stems, soaking in the raw electromagnetic hum until its own tissues vibrate in silent sympathy, like a cat leaning against a radiator.

There is nothing else like it here, unless you count the pale, blind slugs that occasionally inch across the brass junction boxes—small, solitary neighbors that share its deafness to the roar and its indifference to the dark. Beneath the root-mat, in the warm, tea-colored sump, the dweller has carved smooth, slick tunnels through the petrified peat, hollowing out resting chambers around the submerged copper pipes where the water is warmest. When it sleeps—if a creature bound to a forty-second pulse can be said to sleep—it simply anchors itself to the concrete plinth and lets the sixty-cycle current rock it gently in the dark.

If you stay very still on the springy mat, and let your breathing slow until it matches the long, patient sigh of the condensers, it may eventually grow curious about the new, warm obstacle standing among the stems.

You will not hear it coming over the drone of the cane-stalks. You will only feel the root-mat beneath your left boot swell slightly, as if a pocket of gas were rising through the peat. Then, rising quietly from a gap between the gray-green hoses, a blind, blunt tip—no thicker than your wrist, pale as boiled tendon and glistening with tannic water—will lift into the greenish-gold haze. It will sway an inch to the left, then to the right, its lateral seams flared wide to taste the way your body interrupts the hum of the hollow, before slowly, deliberately, sinking back into the warm dark beneath your feet.
