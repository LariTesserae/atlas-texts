If you stay on the wet grating long enough for your eyes to adjust to the bright, mist-laden glare, and long enough for your mind to stop fighting the roar of the turbine, you begin to notice that the room is not quite as empty as it first appeared. 

Down in the shallow basin, near the north bulkhead where the vibration is thickest, something is sitting in the three inches of clear, trembling water. 

At first, it is indistinguishable from the room’s other mineral accidents. It has the exact pale, matte color of the calcified lime curtains that hang from the brickwork, and its back is rounded and smooth like the water-worn concrete of the formwork. You might mistake it for a collapsed pile of mortar, or a piece of the ceiling that fell a century ago and was slowly glazed over by the weeping mist.

Then you notice the symmetry of it. 

It is roughly the size of a kneeling person, though its proportions are all wrong for a human—too broad through the shoulders, the limbs too long and densely jointed, folded tightly against its flanks like the legs of a resting heron. Its skin is not really skin at all, but a thick, heavy hide, slick as wet soapstone and entirely hairless. Where the light from the overhead skylight strikes its shoulders, the water sheets off it in an unbroken, glassy film. In the deeper creases of its neck and behind its knees, where the spray settles without running, a fine velvet of emerald moss has taken root, trembling in time with the ferns on the wall.

It has no pulse. If you were to climb down the iron ladder and press your palm against its flank, you would feel no heartbeat, no rise and fall of a ribcage, no warm rush of blood. Its chest is entirely still—or rather, it moves only with the room. 

Inside that dense, pale body, there is no heart to pump and no lungs to draw breath. Instead, the creature is built around the sixty-cycle thrumming of the ancient motor behind the wall. Its ribs are wide, flat, and slightly hollow, acting as acoustic chambers that catch the industrial drone and pass it inward. The clear, mineral-heavy fluid that fills its veins does not circulate by muscular contraction; it is kept in a state of perpetual, microscopic suspension by the relentless vibration of the chamber, oscillating cleanly through its limbs sixty times a second. It does not need to breathe, because the mist condenses directly onto the pleated, gill-like ridges along its collar, feeding oxygen and dissolved lime straight into its tissues.

It is an animal that has outsourced the work of being alive to the turbine. If the power ever failed—if the sluice-gate were ever cranked shut and the room fell into cold, dead silence—its internal fluids would settle, thicken, and harden like wet plaster before morning.

Because it has no use for ears in a place where sound is a solid wall, the sides of its head are smooth and unbroken. Its face is broad and featureless save for two small, dark, lidless eyes set wide apart, as clear and unblinking as the water in the basin. They do not dart or scan. When the creature moves at all, it moves at the speed of the afternoon sun crossing the concrete—sliding one broad, flat-fingered hand a few inches across the yellow-glazed tiles to keep its shoulders in the warmest band of daylight, or shifting its weight so the silent floor-drain pulls a fresh current of silt past its ankles. 

It does not hunt. The midges that hover above the fern fronds are safe from it, unless one blunders into the sweet, mineral-rich condensation on its back and is slowly absorbed by the moss. It feeds mostly on what the water brings down from the walls: dissolved salts, microscopic algae, and the steady, unearned heat of the motor.

Yet it is not unaware of you. 

You cannot hear your own footsteps on the steel catwalk, and neither can it—not with ears. But the moment you stepped down through the service hatch, the weight of your boots sent a tiny, sharp dissonance through the iron perimeter, a brief stutter in the flat, unvarying vibration of the room. 

Down in the basin, the pale hump shifts. It is a heavy, deliberate motion, like a rusted valve turning after decades of disuse. Its long neck extends just an inch, lifting its smooth head out of the mist, and those two dark, water-clear eyes angle upward toward the catwalk where you stand.

It does not bristle or flee. Outside the hatch is a world of shifting winds, freezing nights, and terrifying, irregular silence—a world where it would have to work to stay warm, and where nothing hums. Here, it is entirely at rest. It simply watches you through the bright, drifting spray, taking the measure of the strange, frantic thing that has entered its equilibrium—a creature full of ragged, temporary rhythms, standing on the grating with a loud, uneven heart hammering inside its ribs, waiting for you to climb back up into the quiet and leave the room precisely as it was.
