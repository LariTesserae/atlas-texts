If you only stand in the courtyard for a minute, you will leave believing the plants are the only living things here. Your eyes will sweep the razor-edged terraces of basalt and quartz, register the neat, unguided rows of hexagonal bismuth wafers, and dismiss every low, stepped shape on the stone floor as just another outcrop of unfinished geology.

You have to stay longer than a minute. You have to sit on a flat shelf of rock—careful of the glassy edges—and wait until the shadows shift by a finger’s width, matching the glacial turn of the magnetite slab in the center of the hollow.

Only then do you notice that one of the low, pale mounds near the base of a copper-veined thicket is no longer where it was.

At first glance, it looks like a discarded casting or a cluster of fused mineral plates, roughly the size of a heavy ledger. It has no head, no eyes, no legs, and nothing about it suggests breath or appetite. Its back is a stepped, overlapping carapace of cloudy quartz and dull, iridescent bismuth, arranged like the fins of a cooling tower. Between each rigid plate sits a thick, dark gasket of waxy tissue—the same tough, non-aqueous flesh as the plants, glistening faintly with dielectric oil and smelling of hot resin and ozone. In the narrow, shadowed slots between the plates, you can sometimes catch the faintest ember-orange glow, not of fire, but of quiet, resistive heat.

It does not scurry. It does not even walk. If you crouch beside it, holding your hand just above its blistering surface, you can feel it humming in exact phase with the floor. Beneath its stepped shell, instead of feet, it rests on a dense, velvety mat of fine copper bristles, slick with oil so they do not snag on the raw basalt. By pulsing tiny, rhythmic currents through its own waxy joints, it sets those bristles vibrating at sixty cycles a second. It glides across the stone on a cushion of imperceptible tremor, advancing perhaps a foot in an hour—a motion so smooth and patient it looks less like travel and more like a shadow lengthening.

Its leading edge is marked only by a slight thickening of the dark, waxy rim, studded with a row of polished, pinhead beads of pure tin. It is completely blind to the bright, ordinary sky above. Instead, those tin beads read the invisible architecture of the pocket: the steep gradients of radiative heat, the stray arcs of dry voltage bleeding from the magnetite rotor, the subtle pull of magnetic lines across the floor. To it, the courtyard is not empty stone; it is a dense, shifting topography of current and charge.

It feeds the way the pocket itself operates: slowly, dryly, and without violence.

When it crosses one of the dark drifts of carbonized spore-pellets spilled by the plants, it does not chew or swallow. It simply settles over the drift for half an afternoon, vibrating gently, drawing the uniform pellets up into the oiled, shadowy gaps of its underside. Inside, warm, oil-filled chambers break the carbon down—not for moisture, which does not exist here, but for the raw material to mend its dark gaskets and regulate its own internal resistance. When its quartz and bismuth plates wear thin from the abrasive dust, it pauses beside the neat, unguided rows of hexagonal wafers at the edge of the ore beds, presses its soft underside down, and draws a fresh, loose wafer into its rim, fusing it into place over days with a slow, localized surge of heat.

And when its internal insulation runs low, it glides into the sparse shade of a botanical thicket. It does not tear the pleated, metallic-green fronds. It nudges its blunt, oil-slicked rim against the base of a thick stem, parting the waxy skin just enough to siphon a few drops of hot dielectric oil, leaving the plant unharmed—sometimes even discharging a small, clean static arc into the roots before it slides away.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the courtyard, though you will never see them gather. Nothing here herds or hunts. Yet now and then, two of them will cross paths on the bare basalt, their tin-beaded rims aligning along the same magnetic contour. They halt—if a stillness can be said to halt something already so slow—and press their leading edges together.

For an hour or more, they sit bridged in the scorching, thin sunlight. A tiny, blue-white spark, no larger than a grain of salt, may snap once or twice across the gap between them, balancing their potentials. While they are joined, a slow exchange of thick oil and stray carbon pellets passes between their undersides. Then, without a sound, they angle apart and resume their separate, patient arcs across the stone.

Every few seasons, one of them will seek out a deep, sunless crack near the rotating magnetite shelf, where the dry current runs thickest. It folds its quartz plates tight, anchors its copper bristles into the raw mica, and goes entirely still. Over the course of weeks, it extrudes a single, fist-sized nodule of milky quartz and dark resin, cementing it to the rock before gliding off to leave it behind. Inside that hard, ceramic shell, a tiny core of bismuth begins to warm and hum. When the dry heat finally splits the casing with a sharp, solitary *tick*, nothing wet or helpless emerges—only a miniature, geometric hinge of quartz and copper, already blind to the sky, already tuned to the sixty-cycle pulse of the mountain, beginning its first, imperceptible slide toward the nearest drift of ash.

They do not know they are strange. They have no awareness of the world beyond the sharp, unweathered lips of the ridge, where water falls from the sky and things run, breathe, and rot. To them, this dry, blistering crack is simply what existence is: a bright, quiet engine that asks nothing of them except to hold their charge, keep their plates clean, and drift forever along the invisible lines of a stone that has forgotten how to sleep.
