You would not see them first, even if you brought a lamp—and a lamp would be a violence here, blinding the milky eyes of the upper roosts and sending a panic of wings through the thin air. You would feel them first as a change in the vibration of the stone beneath your boots, a slow, rhythmic *thrum* that sits below the high, needle-sharp rain of the bats and the papery clatter of the oil-birds. It is the sound of lungs like leather bellows, pulling hard at the oxygen-starved warmth.

They call themselves nothing, because naming requires breath to spare, and in the belly of the peak, breath is the only currency that cannot be scavenged.

To the touch—which is how they know one another, and how they know the world—they are broad, dense, and surprisingly hot. Their shoulders are massive, sloped like river-worn boulders, built to house ribcages deep enough to wring life from air that would leave a valley-dweller gasping on the mud. Their skin is the color of tallow and slick with a thin, musky grease that smells faintly of ammonia and wet limestone, a natural coat that keeps the caustic damp from rotting their hides. Hair grows only in coarse, pale tufts along the ridges of their collarbones, the backs of their wrists, and the crowns of their skulls—thick, bristly stuff, harvested carefully with a sharp edge of bone when it grows long enough to twist into cordage.

Their faces are flat and quiet. Their eyes are small, sunken beneath heavy brow-ridges, and clouded over with a pearl-gray film; they do not flinch at the sickly green glow of the shelf-fungus, though they know where the brightest patches grow by the slight, dry heat the fungus gives off, or by the taste of the spores on the air. Instead of sight, their faces are all nostril and ear—wide, flared nostrils that flare and flatten with each deliberate pull of air, sorting the room’s thick soup of scents into a map of who is healthy, who is sleeping, and what has recently died.

It is their hands, though, that have become the true organs of their minds.

The fingers are long, pale, and startlingly slender compared to the thick, knotted wrists that drive them. The pads are calloused into smooth, horn-like plates from centuries of gripping wet basalt, yet they remain sensitive enough to read the slightest tremor in the rock. When two of them meet on a narrow ledge—where the stone has been dished out by ten thousand years of resting hips and elbows—they do not speak over the roar of the chamber. Shouting wastes air and startles the crickets. Instead, they press their palms flat against the stone between them, or lay their foreheads together, and hum.

The sound begins deep in the chest, a wet, gravelly bass note that barely leaves the throat, vibrating straight through the skull and the collarbone into the limestone. Through the rock, they pass the simple, vital news of the dark: *This hollow is warm. A bird is heavy and weak on the third shelf. The water in the east basin is low.* A mother holding an infant—a rare, wrinkled thing that clings to her chest-tufts with prehensile toes—will hum against the small of its back for hours at a time, teaching its bones the pitch of the chamber before it ever learns to reach for a cricket.

Their lives are governed by an almost geological patience.

Because the food web inside the mountain is tight and the air is thin, haste is a kind of suicide. A sudden lunge might catch a single roosting beetle, but it would cost three minutes of panting and send a ripple of alarm through the wall-carpets of translucent crickets, driving them up into the unreachable crevices of the chimney. So they move like the drip of the stalactites. One of them might spend half a morning easing three feet along a mineral-glossed shelf, testing each handhold with a faint tap of the knuckles, waiting for a sleeping oil-bird to shift its weight before sliding a foot past its tail-feathers. The birds barely wake; to them, these pale, slow-moving shapes are just another warm, shifting fold of the mountain itself.

When they hunt, it is scarcely hunting at all. They harvest the chamber’s overflow.

A hand drifts out through the velvet dark, fingers splayed, moving slower than the eye could follow even in daylight. It parts a living curtain of fist-sized crickets, feeling the *tick-tick-tick* of their antennae brushing against the calloused palm, until the sensitive tips locate a sluggish, fat-bellied beetle gorging on guano-mold, or a cricket whose carapace has grown brittle with age. The fingers close—not snapping, just tightening with a steady, inexorable pressure—and lift the prize away without disturbing its neighbors.

Farther down, near the floor where the mist gathers in shallow, calcite-rimmed basins, they come to drink. Here, the pale, blind salamanders hang suspended in the lukewarm water, their feathery external gills barely stirring. The primates treat the basins with a reverence born of absolute necessity. They kneel on the slick, compost-soft floor—careful not to kneel too deeply, lest they sink into the warm, rotting mire of feathers and shells—and lower their faces until their chins nearly kiss the surface. They drink by dipping a cupped palm and letting the water trickle between their lips, never stirring the silt, never startling the salamanders whose presence keeps the tiny pools clear of insect larvae.

Every individual carries the weight of the chamber’s history in its crevices.

They build nothing, for there is nothing to build with and nowhere to expand, yet their ledges are cluttered with an inheritance so old it has lost its beginning. Smooth, heavy river cobbles—granite and quartzite from valleys miles below, where the rivers run loud under an open sky—sit wedged into natural pockets in the basalt, each one tethered by a knotted loop of twisted sinew and coarse, pale hair. No living resident has ever seen a river, or knows what a valley is. To them, these stones are simply *the hard ones*—objects denser and more permanent than the soft, acid-eaten limestone of their world, handed down from hand to hand across generations that blur into one another like the layers of the floor.

When an oil-bird finally loses its grip in the upper dark—its heart giving out in its sleep, or its wings tangled in the starved, dangling roots of the alpine scrub—its heavy, grease-rich body tumbles through the roar of the bats and hits the lower ledges with a dull, wet thud.

The sound is an invitation. From the surrounding dark, shapes detach themselves from the stone, moving with a sudden, oiled efficiency that never quite crosses into hurry. Bone chisels—splinters of bird-femur or the dense, yellowed rib of an ancestor, ground to a flat edge against the basalt—are lifted from their hiding cracks. A tethered river cobble is untied and cradled in a heavy palm.

Then comes the *tock-tock-tock*—a dry, rhythmic tapping that cuts cleanly through the ambient roar of wings and insects. They crack the hollow bones for the warm marrow, strip the oily fat from the skin with their teeth, and leave the plucked feathers and crushed cartilage to settle into the rising floor, where the crickets and the rust-tinted mold wait to finish the work.

Nothing is wasted, not even their own dead. When one of the heavy-shouldered elders finally stops humming, its breath thinning until the great bellows of its chest lie still, the others do not mourn with noise. They gather close in the dark, pressing their palms and foreheads against the cooling skin until the last of its body-heat has bled into the stone. Then, with the same patient, unsentimental thrift that governs the fall of a bird, they take the longest tendons from the legs, the coarsest tufts of hair from the crown, and the thickest bones from the forearms, before lowering what remains into the deep, warm compost of the floor’s farthest recess, where the shelf-fungus glows greenest.

Far above them, through the crooked, frost-shattered cleft at the summit, the alpine wind still skips like breath across a bottle. Sometimes, in the dead of winter, a single flake of snow survives the twenty-pace drop and lands on a roosting bat, melting instantly into a bead of lukewarm mist. The primates below do not look up. They do not wonder what lies beyond the pewter-colored sliver of light that dies before it reaches their shelves. Outside is only the cold, the glare, and the terrible, empty thinness where nothing hums back when you press your hand to the stone.

Here, the dark is warm, the stone is worn to the exact curve of their spines, and the roar of the crowd is loud enough to keep the mountain alive.
