They are not numerous, which is why the city feels empty from a distance, and they do not hurry, which is why you can watch a viaduct for ten minutes before realizing that the dark shape beside the railing is a person.

They call themselves—when they bother to name themselves at all—simply the **Tenders**, though the word carries the weight of *tethering* as much as *tending*. To live at this altitude is to spend your life bolting things down, greasing what resists, and keeping track of which way the floor is leaning.

In build, they are broad-shouldered and low-centered, shaped by a lifetime of walking on wet, razor-edged stone and iron grating. They dress for the cold damp in layers of heavy, slate-colored felt and oiled canvas, stiffened with tallow and coal-tar until their coats stand slightly away from their bodies like armor. Their boots are their most prized possessions: thick-soled, iron-shod, and fitted with replaceable steel cleats that bite into the pale quartzite without slipping. Sewn into the hems of their coats and the cuffs of their trousers are small, flat weights of cast lead—not enough to slow them, just enough to remind their clothes which way is down when they pass through the alleys where the mist drifts upward.

Every Tender carries a plumb-bob. It is not a tool of measurement here; it is a personal anchor, a small brass cone on a braided copper cord tucked into a belt-loop or hung from a lapel. When a Tender steps onto an unfamiliar catwalk or enters one of the older tenements stacked against the cliff, they let the weight dangle for a few seconds before committing their full weight to the floor. If the string hangs straight, they walk. If it leans toward the boiler house or trembles toward the open chasm, they adjust their stance accordingly, leaning into the slant with the casual grace of sailors on a rolling deck.

Their faces are pale and cool-skinned, weathered not by sunburn—there is no sun to burn them—but by the constant, damp chill. Their eyes are large, with pale gray or amber-flecked irises that have adjusted to the permanent bruised twilight, capable of reading the subtle difference between wet basalt, dry iron, and empty air at fifty paces. Their hands are wide, calloused, and invariably scarred along the knuckles and palms from the freshly cleaved stone, which never dulls. Even the oldest among them have fresh white nicks across their fingers, sealed with black pitch or wrapped in strips of moss-stained linen.

***

Because the city moves at the speed of a glacier, so do its people.

A shift at the drop-forges or the beam-engines lasts until the work is done, which is measured not by clocks—clocks lose their minds near the heavier transformers, running backward or skipping hours—but by the heat of the pipes and the slow turning of the fifty-foot flywheels. A Tender might spend an entire afternoon oiling a single row of cable-pulleys, waiting patiently between each stroke of the grease-gun for the ore-buckets to creep past.

This patience extends to how they speak and how they listen. In a landscape where the *thunk-hiss* of a hammer arrives across the chasm before the hammer actually falls, you learn not to startle. Outsiders, if any ever reach this height, are driven mad by the echoes—flinching at sounds that haven't happened yet, spinning around to face voices arriving from three cliff-faces at once. The Tenders simply wait for the cause to catch up with the noise.

Their own speech is low, rhythmic, and pitched deliberately *beneath* the constant, vibrating thrum of the power grid. They do not shout across the gorges; shouting only muddies the air with overlapping ghosts of words. Instead, when two Tenders need to communicate from separate peaks, they use the ironwork itself—tapping coded rhythms onto the steel rails with a wrench, sending crisp, metallic messages through the viaducts where the stone cannot tangle them. Face to face, they speak in short, unhurried phrases, leaving long silences between sentences as though waiting for the air around them to settle.

***

Their homes are tucked into the warm, damp pockets of the city—the seven-story tenements bolted into the sheer rock behind the turbine halls, where the waste heat from the smelters keeps the frost at bay.

Inside, the quarters are narrow, dim, and thick with the smell of wet wool, hot machine oil, and boiled lichen. They do not use beds; a bed rests on a floor, and in the older quarters, floors cannot always be trusted to stay horizontal through the winter. Instead, they sleep in heavy canvas hammocks slung from the structural iron tie-rods that anchor the buildings to the mountain. No matter how the walls tilt or how the staircases miscount their steps—fourteen going down, twelve coming up, with the missing two marked by dabs of white lead paint—a hammock always finds its own level.

They live in an easy, unsentimental truce with the wildlife that shares their ledges.

In the lower stone cellars, beneath the workshops, they keep small herds of the six-legged mountain ungulates. The beasts are too stubborn and too sharp-hoofed to be truly domesticated, but they tolerate the Tenders in exchange for shelter from the worst of the chasm drafts and a steady diet of scraped boiler-moss. The Tenders shear the animals' dense, oil-rich fur for felt, milk them for a thick, mineral-heavy cream that warms the belly against the damp, and occasionally harness them to haul copper coils up sheer rock faces where no crane can reach.

They harvest the pale, hanging lichen from the undersides of the viaducts, drying it over the transformer housings to crumble into stews or pack into boots for warmth. They leave the pale-eyed cliff-swallows alone—the birds keep the translucent-winged moths from clogging the amber floodlights—and they treat the chitinous cable-climbers as useful indicators of grid health; if the climbers abandon a line, it means the current is failing or the insulation has cracked.

***

Yet the Tenders are not the only things that belong to this place.

Out beyond the amber floodlights, in the wide, windswept shelves of barren slate where nothing has been built, and in the narrow alleys where the physics frays worst, other things make their living—quieter things, harder to separate from the stone and the mist.

In the alleys where condensation drifts upward, pooling in shimmering, inverted puddles on the undersides of the iron catwalks, there lives a pale, ribbon-bodied creature the Tenders call **Ceiling-Eels**. Blind and nearly boneless, they cling to the wet steel with rows of soft, translucent suckers, undulating slowly through the upside-down water like pale kelp in a tide pool. They feed on the microscopic spores that drift up from the velvet moss below, and they never touch the ground; if a Tender knocks one loose with a broom-handle, it does not fall—it drifts sideways into the mist, curling upon itself until it finds another girder to grip.

And then there are the **Shadow-Sitters**.

They are not animals, or at least not entirely, though the Tenders feed them scraps of tallow and never sweep them out of the boiler rooms. Wherever the iron is heaviest—at the bases of the giant beam-engines, along the thick stone pilings of the viaducts, and in the corners where the shadows pool *toward* the metal instead of away from the light—the darkness seems thicker than it ought to be. If you stare at it long enough without blinking, you realize that parts of it are breathing.

They look, when they can be seen at all, like crouched, lichen-draped figures made of wet slate and old soot, though they have no faces that the amber lamps can catch. They do not work the engines, and they do not speak, even on the rails. They simply gather where the mass is greatest, pressing their flat, cool backs against the humming iron, soaking up the low-frequency vibration of the grid and the faint, patient warmth of the boilers.

The Tenders say they were here before the first piling was driven—that they are the only things native to an altitude where the world hasn't finished rounding its corners—and that they only moved into the city because the iron was heavier than the stone. Whether they are old inhabitants who forgot how to leave, or something the mountain itself grew to keep the machines company, nobody asks. At this height, asking *why* is a waste of breath. You check your plumb-bob, you grease the cable, and you let the flywheel turn.
