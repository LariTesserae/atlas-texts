At first, you mistake them for knots in the bark, or clusters of pale bracket-fungi clinging to the undersides of the arches. In a world where sound dies at twenty paces and nothing moves faster than a drifting glider, your eyes are trained to look for stillness. It is only when a patch of wet slate shifts, detaches itself from a junction, and steps out onto the curve of a rib that you realize the weavers are still here.

They are tall, lean, and spare, with the long, slightly bowed limbs of creatures that have spent generations pulling themselves along overhead timber. Their skin is the color of peeled willow, mottled across the shoulders and shins with faint, greenish stains from sap and lichen-dust. They wear almost nothing—a wrap of oiled glider-membrane around the waist, a belt of braided sinew hung with bone needles and flint adzes—because cloth rots in a season here, and anything loose catches on the mist-slicked knots or floats upward into the eyes when gravity thins near an edge.

Their feet are the strangest part of them: broad, flat, and bare, with heels and toes thickened into pale, slightly tacky pads that grip the yielding bark like damp leather. They walk with a rolling, unhurried gait, their knees slightly bent, always keeping one hand brushing the nearest cable or ridge of wood. Even when they are strolling casually along the belly of an arch, upside down above a mile of bruised-green emptiness, they never look down. There is no down to look toward.

Because the air eats their voices, they have largely given up on speaking into it.

When two of them meet on a narrow bridge, they do not call out. They stop a pace apart, lean in, and press their foreheads together, humming low in their chests so the vibration passes directly from skull to skull, bypassing the wet wool of the atmosphere. For longer distances—across the vast, dim chasms where a shout would vanish before it cleared the leaves—they borrow the voices of the amphibians. Almost every tender carries a perforated gourd at the hip, lined with damp moss, cradling one of the pale, frilled creatures. A gentle squeeze of the fingers, or a drop of bitter sap touched to its tongue, and the amphibian’s translucent throat flares with a slow, rhythmic pulse of blue-green light. Across the void, another distant speck of light answers from the shadow of a pillar, blinking in patient, measured intervals—a whole conversation conducted in silent sparks.

Closer to home, they use the wood itself. Along the great junctions, single strands of bast-fiber are drawn taut between bone pegs and tuned by hand. A tender will kneel, press an ear flat against the slick bark, and pluck the cord once with a thumb. You hear nothing in the air, but through the soles of your feet, you feel a dull, woody *thrum* race away into the fog, carrying its message through half a mile of living phloem.

They do not build houses. A house implies something finished, and nothing here is ever finished. Instead, they coax *hollows*. A family will spend twenty years training a cluster of young shoots to arch over a natural depression in a rib, notching the bark and binding the tips together until the cambium fuses into a dry, domed blister. Inside, it smells of warm peat and dried sap. Gourds of condensed water hang from the ceiling on bone pegs; hammocks of woven vine sway gently from the walls, and every sleeper ties a loose loop of cord around one ankle before closing their eyes.

To slip off an edge here is not to fall; it is to *drift*. If a tender loses their footing and tumbles into the open void between the arches, they do not plummet to a mercifully quick end. They float. Caught in the warm, lazy eddies of rising mist, they spin slowly in the dim amber air, kicking at nothing, watching the nearest pillar hang tantalizingly close—fifty paces away, then a hundred—until the thermal carries them out into the empty miles. Some are lucky enough to brush the trailing tendrils of a passing glider, though the great membrane kites are fragile things, and too much weight pulls them both down into the lower, wetter reaches where the rotting hammocks sag and the tiny pale scavengers wait in silence. Most drifters simply vanish into the haze, dying slowly of thirst in a world made of water.

Their relationship with their tools is just as patient, and just as strange. Because iron does not exist and stone left in the dampness eventually softens and sprouts pale, calcified tendrils, they do not view tools as possessions. A flint adze is merely a temporary tooth. When a tender finishes shaping a notch, they often leave the adze wedged right into the wound they made. Years later, another tender passing the same junction will find the wooden handle rooted, the stone half-swallowed by smooth callus-tissue, lending its small hardness to the strength of the arch.

They treat their dead the same way. There are no graves in a world without soil, and burning wet wood is impossible. When someone grows too old to walk the bridges, their kin carry them to a junction that needs reinforcing. When the breath finally leaves them, their bones are cleaned, sharpened, and driven into the living timber as pegs, or lashed across weak seams as splints. Within a few seasons, the dampness wakes the marrow; pale, root-like threads creep out from the bone and knit themselves into the bark. Every great arch is studded with these quiet ancestors, holding the world together long after their names are forgotten.

This is how they navigate a geometry that refuses to hold still. They have no maps, because a map drawn today is a lie tomorrow. Instead, they read the *knots*. Every clan, every generation, has its own way of twisting bast-fiber before the wood swallows it—a particular double-loop, a staggered ridge of notches, a pattern of bone pegs driven in a spiral. When you cross three swaying bridges in a triangle and arrive back at a pillar twice as thick as the one you left, you do not panic. You kneel, run your palm over the wet slate of the bark, and feel the old callus-tissue beneath your fingers. You read the scars. You may not know *where* you are in the mist, but you know *when* you are, and whose hands coaxed this wood into place, and that is enough.

Yet there are older things living along the ribs than the tenders—or perhaps they are simply what the tenders become if they stay still long enough.

Far out in the dimmest, most weathered junctions, where the wood is the color of charcoal and the mist never quite lifts, you occasionally find one of the *Rooted*. They are elders who decided, decades or centuries ago, to stop walking. They wedged themselves into a tight crook of living branches, wrapped their long, lean arms around a young shoot to hold it in place, and simply waited.

The world accepted them. The dampness settled into their skin; the moss-crust crept up their shins. Their bare, calloused feet softened and fused with the bark beneath them. Today, their eyes are closed, their faces smooth and pale as callus-tissue, their ribs indistinguishable from the braided timber that cradles them. Those seamless, closed loops of living wood you noticed earlier—the ones tied in tight, impossible knots with no ends and no scar of a blade—are their arms, grown together around the junctions they swore to hold, long after the wood forgot where the person ended and the arch began.

They are not dead. If you press your ear against the bark beside one of them during the Exhalation, you can feel it.

Every few days, when the warm fog wells up from the bottomless hollow below and blinds the world at arm’s length, the tenders stop all work. They sit flat on the ribs, pressing their bare feet, their palms, and their foreheads against the slick wood. Nobody flashes a light; nobody plucks a cord. For an hour, the entire people goes utterly still as the sub-audible tremor rises through the timber—a slow, continent-wide flexing of knots and cables older than memory. Miles away in the dark, a chasm narrows, or a bridge pulls taut.

And beneath the palms of the living, the Rooted hum—a vibration so deep and slow it belongs more to the sap than to the throat—answering the breath of the great, hollow world they have spent ten thousand years weaving, or that has spent ten thousand years dreaming them.
