To live here is to learn the pace of wet stone and low voltage.

The people of the basin are built for thin air and long waiting. They are broad-shouldered, deep-chested, and unhurried, with a slow, flat-footed walk that keeps their weight centered on the moss-slick cobbles. Nobody hurries at this altitude; the air refuses to reward haste, punishing a sprint with a ragged, metallic ache in the lungs. Even their speech is economical—short, low-pitched phrases that carry just far enough across a workbench or a viaduct before the fog swallows them. When they need to hail someone twenty paces away, they don’t shout. They rap a spanner twice against an iron steam-pipe, or flick the brass shutter of a belt-lamp, sending a brief, amber wink through the pewter gloom.

Their skin is pale and close-grained, permanently damp at the temples and flushed a dull, wind-cured red across the cheekbones and knuckles. They dress in layers of heavy, felted wool the colour of wet slate, topped with short oilskin aprons and waxed canvas coats that never fully dry, only settle into a cold, heavy equilibrium with the air. Every coat has deep pockets lined with flannel, and every belt carries the small, practical hardware of a town run on mist: a brass pressure-key, a horn-handled knife, a rag soaked in tallow for wiping down iron, and a *telltale*—a thumb-sized glass bulb wired to two copper prongs. Before a worker touches a damp junction box or leans against a brick retaining wall, they press the prongs to the moss to see if the stray current is running hot enough to light the filament.

The basin does not keep time by the sun, which is only a pale, wandering bruise behind the stratus. It keeps time by the two daily tides of fog.

In the slack hours between tides, the town settles into a damp, humming stillness. This is when the *pelt-cutters* walk the terraced alleys and overhead catwalks. They move singly or in pairs, carrying insulated shears and canvas satchels, tending the emerald wire-pelt that creeps along the power conduits. They do not try to eradicate the moss—a tight, even sleeve of wire-pelt keeps the freezing mist out of the porcelain insulators and quiets the rattle of the junction boxes. Instead, they groom it like a hedge, shaving it back only where its bulbous velvet growth threatens to bridge a switch or choke a transformer fin. When they peel a thick strip away, it comes off with a wet *rip*, smelling sharply of hot copper and crushed fern, and a scattering of tiny, dull-green mites—no bigger than sesame seeds, their backs dusted with verdigris—tumble sluggishly from the warm underside into the gutter. The cutters stuff the trimmings into their satchels; dried over the boiler-house flues and tossed into the smelting crucibles, the moss yields back the trace metals it drank from the grid.

Up on the whale-backed ridges, where the cloud-trees are twisted too low to offer shelter, the *sail-minders* wait for the western bank. They are the leanest, most weather-bitten of the basin’s folk, wrapped to the eyes in greased wool scarves and brass-rimmed goggles that they wipe constantly with scraps of chamois. Twice a day, the western lip of the plateau darkens to a deeper, bruised grey, and the air sharpens with ozone and wet slate. As the mist-tide rolls over the rim, the towering galvanized-mesh sails begin to sing—a vast, steady *shhh* that drowns out even the groan of the powerhouses. The minders walk the narrow planks behind the frames with long wooden scrapers, clearing dead heather and rime-ice from the mesh so the copper troughs below can drink freely.

Sometimes, while they work the high rigging, the glider-birds come up from the abyss. They never land on the frames or the stone. Pale, long-winged, and silent as drifting ash, they bank out of the grey void beyond the plateau’s edge, riding the invisible thermals that rise from the valleys miles below. A sail-minder will occasionally leave a strip of dried mutton or suet wedged into the top crossbeam of a sail; without a single beat of its wings, a bird will slip through the mist, pluck the scrap from the wood with needle-sharp toes, and vanish back over the rim.

Down in the bowl, the arrival of the tide wakes the town’s machinery. As water pours down the gravity-driven sluices and fills the leaching vats, the great stone-housed waterwheels groan into motion. Along the narrow streets, the tungsten work-lamps brighten from a dying-coal orange to a steady, comforting gold. Inside the soot-darkened brick workshops, the *vatsmen*—their fingernails permanently stained a faint, greenish-black from handling mineral-heavy brine—lean over the steaming tanks with long wooden skimmers, watching the pale salts precipitate out of the cold mist-water.

Their homes sit directly above the workshops or wedged into the dry, sheltered hollows behind the boiler houses, where insulated steam-pipes run along the low ceilings and keep the plaster warm to the touch. Inside, the air is thick and intimate, smelling of coal-tar soap, boiled roots, wet wool, and strong tea brewed in heavy iron kettles. The windows are small, thick-paned, and perpetually weeping condensation down onto the slate sills.

Here, the people keep small jars of lukewarm runoff water, each housing one or two of the translucent salamanders from the boiler troughs. They are not pets so much as living barometers. Motionless for hours, their pale gills barely stirring, the salamanders only grow restless—pressing their blunt snouts against the glass—when the barometric pressure drops and a heavy tide is climbing the mountain flank. In the evenings, when the sluices are shut and the town’s voltage drops to a low, steady hum, families gather around the iron stoves. They play quiet, resonant instruments—concertinas and small wooden dulcimers strung with salvaged copper wire—whose notes hang warmly in the steamy rooms, never needing to compete with the muffled silence outside.

At the very bottom of the basin, where the terraced tiers end and the basalt slopes down into the mirror-still sumps, almost no one goes. The water there is black, heavy with leached minerals, and cold enough to stop a heart in minutes. Only the *sump-gaugers* descend the slick stone steps once a week, lanterns held low, to lower weighted plumb-lines and check the sediment levels.

Even there, something lives. When the gaugers haul up the submerged iron chains to inspect the links, they often find them crusted with *sump-glass*—slow, colonial things related to nothing above the waterline. Pale, brittle, and hollow-boned, they filter the dark, chemically strange runoff in total stillness, building delicate, coral-like lattices of silica and copper-salt along the sluice-gates. The gaugers scrape them off with iron chisels and drop them into buckets, leaving the chains clean, and by the time the next tide turns the wheels overhead, the pale crusts are already beginning to grow back in the dark.
