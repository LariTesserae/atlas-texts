If you stand still on one of the warm, weeping ledges long enough for the shrieking gliders in the canopy to lose interest in you, you begin to notice that not all of the pale, fluted boulders in the travertine pans are stone.

One of them—half-submerged in a shallow pool of steaming runoff, its rounded back striped with iron-orange and mossy green—shifts by a fraction of an inch. A crease opens near its base, and a thick, milky membrane slides sideways off an eye the color of boiled tea.

They are the basin’s oldest residents, and they have spent so many centuries in this high-altitude boiler that the mountain has begun to grow over them.

At first glance, one looks like a low, broad bridge of living rock—roughly the bulk of a heavy bear, though slung closer to the mud on four splayed, pillar-thick limbs. Its wide, webbed feet end in blunt, shovel-like claws that spread its weight across the spongy root-mats so it doesn’t sink into the marsh. Where the mineral crust hasn’t taken hold—along its soft belly, its throat, and the undersides of its legs—its skin is the slick, dark gray of wet basalt, deeply grooved to channel the constant rain of condensation away from its eyes and nostrils.

In air this thick and hot, anything that hurries cooks itself from the inside out. So they do not hurry. They do not even pant. Tucked beneath the overhanging shelves of their shoulders are deep, pleated folds of dark, blood-rich tissue that expand and collapse once every minute or so, drawing in the heavy, oxygen-poor steam with a quiet, wet *shhh*.

Their backs are another matter entirely. Because they spend hours at a time motionless beneath the dripping tree-ferns and weeping cliff-walls, the basin’s mineral-heavy water leaves its mark on them, layer by microscopic layer. Over decades, dissolved lime and iron calcify along their rough dorsal ridges into smooth, terraced plates of living sinter—pale, porous, and fluted by the runoff, indistinguishable from the rock ledges around them. 

It is a practical kind of armor. The stone shields them from the fierce, unfiltered UV light that hammers the high valley, and its porous grain wicks up the warm seepage, keeping their underlying hide constantly damp. In the damp crevices between the mineral plates, coppery moss takes root. Miniature tree-ferns sprout from the hollows behind their shoulders. Even the armored, iridescent beetles treat them like walking bark, nesting in the warm seams of their backs and clicking in metallic waves as they graze on the algae growing along the sinter. When one of these creatures rises from a pool and lumbers past you in the fern-shadow, it brings a piece of the valley’s deafening static with it.

They are not mindless beasts, though their thoughts move at the same glacial tempo as the water. They are the basin’s quiet tenders. With slow, deliberate sweeps of their blunt claws, they clear dead, waterlogged fern-fronds out of the travertine spillways before the pools can stagnate, and they nudge fallen sinter loose to keep the warm seepage flowing evenly across the marshes. They graze mostly on the swollen, water-logged root-mats and the broad-leafed succulents, peeling away the waxy skins with horny, flat beaks. Only rarely—when one of the fat, translucent amphibians booms a little too close to a submerged snout—does a creature strike. Its thick neck shoots forward in a sudden, hydraulic lunge, snapping the frog under the water. Afterward, it will sit in absolute stillness for an hour, paying off the heat of that single burst of speed.

In a stone amphitheater loud with drumhead leaves, shrieking gliders, and echoing frogs, an airborne voice would be useless. So they speak through the mountain. 

When one wants to reach another across the steaming shelves, it settles its heavy belly into the mud, presses its broad, flat lower jaw directly against the wet basalt bedrock, and hums. It is a sound too low to hear clearly over the canopy’s roar, but if you are standing barefoot on the stone, you feel it in the soles of your feet and the hollow of your chest: a slow, tectonic shudder that travels cleanly through the solid rock. Half a mile away, another elder resting in a travertine pan feels the vibration in its jawbone and its mineral-plated ribs, and answers with a patient, resonant thrum of its own.

Only the young ones have any restlessness in them. Smaller, sleeker, and still entirely dark-skinned before the mineral crusts begin to lock them down, they scramble through the tangled understory in loose, silent groups, chasing the iridescent beetles and wrestling in the warm mud. Sometimes, driven by a curiosity that hasn’t yet been baked out of them, a few of the young will climb the highest, barest ledges at the very rim of the basin—the place where the world simply drops away. They will sit right at the lip, where the boiler-heat of the valley spills over the cliff-edge and curdles into freezing fog against the outer sky, letting the thin, bitter wind slice across their hot, wet faces for a few minutes before turning back down into the comforting steam.

As they grow old, however, the sinter thickens until even a short walk across the marsh becomes a labor. Their joints stiffen; their plates fuse into heavy, smooth domes. That is when they make their final, slow pilgrimage—an hour’s walk for you, a day’s journey for them—toward the scattered artifacts.

They gather around the pitted, matte-gray cylinders and the half-buried rings of dark glass, settling into the concentric, pulsing rings of pale moss that grow there. Within that dozen-pace circle where the basin’s rules go soft, the air seems to take a fraction of the weight off their stone-crusted backs. Fat droplets of condensation roll off the ferns, drift sideways through the thickened light, and hang trembling around their snouts like glass beads before flattening silently against their dark skin.

The oldest ones press their chins directly against the base of the matte-gray metal or the seamless glass, closing their milky eyes. They are no longer humming to each other; they are listening to the unimaginably slow, thousand-year pulse turning deep beneath the mountain. Year after year, the mineral crust thickens by fractions of a millimeter, binding their lower plates to the mud and the roots, until it is no longer possible to tell where the creature ends and the ancient anchor begins—just one more smooth, iron-stained swell of stone, humming faintly with beetles, wrapped around the fever at the heart of the world.
