If you stay on the parapet long enough—longer than the cold seems to allow, until the faint *shh* of your own sleeve stops sounding like an intrusion and becomes part of the city’s dry hush—you begin to realize that you were only looking at the surfaces where the grey light falls.

The city is hollow, yes. Its towers have no rooms, its staircases no doors. Yet emptiness, here, is not the same as absence. It is merely space that has not yet been claimed by the stone.

Step inside one of the windowless shafts, out of the sandblasting wind, and the air changes. Outside, the dry thermal currents rise ceaselessly from the chasms; inside, the air is dead, cellar-cold, and so still that the chalky dust hanging in it never settles. If you tilt your head back and stare up into the dim, vertical well of a tower—three hundred feet of unchiseled mineral wall rising into slate-grey gloom—you will eventually see the things that have given up horizontal life altogether.

They hang like the dried husks of seed-pods, or like chandeliers made of salt-glass and parchment, suspended from the unseen ceiling by single, hair-thin filaments of spun silica. Each one is as tall as a man, segmented into hollow, papery chambers that taper to a needle point at the bottom. They have no eyes, no mouths, no limbs to reach with. When a district’s slow unspooling sends a sub-audible tremor through the walls, the filaments transmit it, and the hanging bodies begin to turn—not fast enough to watch, only enough that if you look away and look back an hour later, their sharp edges are facing a different corner of the shaft.

They are feeding on the city’s restlessness. As the great masses of accreted rock shift and grind against their own invisible seams, they build a faint, prickling static charge in the dead air. The hanging things drink it through their dry, porous ribs, storing the charge in their hollow chambers. If you press your palm to the outer wall of a tower that holds a colony of them, you will not hear a sound, but the stone beneath your hand will feel faintly, inexplicably warm—and when you pull your fingers away, the fine hairs on your wrist will stand on end.

Out in the open squares, where the light is flat and the distances lie, other things wait for a different kind of weather.

When a squall of detached shadows drifts down through the dimness like burnt paper, pooling in the sunken amphitheaters, the blind, fingernail-sized grazers pull back into their crevices. They know what comes next. From the leeward undersides of the broken arches, where the rusted-orange mineral crust grows thickest, the shadow-gatherers emerge.

They look, at first, like low, broad flagstones that have come unstuck from the pavement—flat, wide-bodied things plated in overlapping scales of pale, chalky horn, with dozens of short, dry, articulated legs tucked beneath their rims. They move with a faint, rasping whisper, like grit sliding down a slate roof. When they reach a shadow-pool, they do not drink it or tear at it; they simply crawl into the middle of the matte-black shape and press their broad, porous bellies flat against it, holding completely still until the shadow adheres to their undersides and wraps up over the edges of their shells like soot.

They need the dark. The flat, slate-grey luminescence of the high haze is patient and corrosive; anything left exposed to it for too long begins to lose its suppleness, its dry joints stiffening, its outer layers chalking over until it joins the geological habit of the streets. Coated in a fresh, stolen shadow, the gatherers can slip back across the open plazas unseen by the scaffolding-predators, and untouched by the light’s slow calcification. When the shadow finally thins and vanishes into their shells, a day or a week later, they crawl back beneath the arches and wait for the next squall.

And then, if you stay long enough that your own sense of time begins to fray like the avenues—if you learn to stop flinching when a bridge ends in mid-air or when the ground beneath your boots tilts gently toward the abyss—you will notice the ones who do not hide from the city at all.

You will not hear them coming. The first sign is usually a trick of the eye: a figure standing at the far end of a mile-long, foreshortened avenue, looking ten paces away, who lifts a foot, takes a single, unhurried stride, and *is* ten paces away—having stepped cleanly over the folded mile of pavement you would have had to walk.

Up close, they are tall, spare, and built with the same austere economy as the viaducts. Their outer skin—or shell, it is hard to tell which—has the texture and color of weathered travertine: pale ash, streaked with charcoal, deeply seamed at the joints so it can flex without cracking. They have no wet eyes to blink against the dry wind, no nostrils to flare. Across the upper half of each face lies a broad, slightly recessed band of dark, glassy mineral, smooth as river-worn slate, that catches the flat grey light without reflecting it. With that band they read what you cannot: the shimmer where space has bunched together, the faint warp of an inverted gravity pocket, the exact edge where a shadow-pool thins into nothing.

They wear no cloth, for nothing grows here to weave. Instead, they drape their narrow shoulders and long limbs in the shed, translucent membranes of the great silica gliders, layered three or four sheets deep and bound at the wrists and ankles with dried mineral-sinew. When they walk, those membranes rub together with a faint, dry *shh*—the exact sound of your own sleeve, amplified into a quiet company.

They have no tools, no fire, no language that travels through the thin air. If two of them meet on one of the razor-thin walkways that sag across the chasms, they do not speak or gesture. They simply stop, step close, and press one heel—or one long, dry knuckle—against the same curbstone. Through the mineral crystal of the bridge, they pass a slow, rhythmic tremor: a pulse too low for ears, a conversation made entirely of weight and timing.

Because they have no use for the old rules of up and down, the city’s breakdown is not a ruin to them; it is a landscape. You may see three or four of them sitting calmly on the underside of a floating, inverted bridge-span, their knees drawn up to their chests, watching the dimness of the chasm below—which to them is the sky—while a dust-devil of detached shadows spirals past their ankles. In the tilted districts, where avenues have become forty-degree cliffs, they walk the sheer faces with unhurried, deliberate balance, their jointed toes finding purchase in the tiny, sandblasted pits of the stone.

Once, in a courtyard where a lone obelisk stood half a mile from the nearest wall, I watched one of them kneel beside a patch of rusted-orange crust. With a single, blunt fingernail, it scraped a measure of the brittle growth into its palm, mixed it with a pinch of the silvery powder left behind by the tiny grazers, and carefully pressed the dry paste into the seams behind its own knees. It was greasing its joints against the city’s habit—keeping the stone from claiming the bend of its limbs.

They do not build, and they do not mend. When a district finally unhooks itself completely and begins its centuries-long drift out over the bottomless gulfs, they do not try to anchor it. They simply gather their layered membranes around their shoulders, step to the frayed edge of the plaza, and walk across the visual distance to the next ridge before the gap grows too wide.

If they see you standing on the parapet—heavy, loud with the wet thump of a heart, your breath steaming faintly in the cellar-cold air—they will not approach, and they will not flee. They will simply stop on the slanted terrace across the chasm, their dark mineral visors turned in your direction, watching with the immense, dry patience of things that have outlasted gravity. Waiting to see whether you will turn back down toward the world of sound and water, or whether you will stay until the dust settles on your shoulders, and the city begins, very slowly, to grow around your boots.
