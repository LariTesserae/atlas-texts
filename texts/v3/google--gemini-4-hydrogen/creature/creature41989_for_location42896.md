They are narrow people, built like wedges and hinges.

To live in the Hollows is to spend your life sideways, threading the vertical slots between hexagonal pillars where a broad shoulder or a careless hip would leave you pinned. Their frames are slight and long-limbed, with flat, supple ribcages that can compress slightly when they exhale, allowing them to slip through fissures no wider than a spread hand. They do not walk so much as *brace*—palms and bare soles pressed against opposing walls of basalt, moving up or down a sheer chimney with the unhurried, three-point certainty of a spider.

Their skin is the color of dry slate, matte and bloodless, stretched tight over corded muscle with scarcely a gram of spare fat. There is no moisture to waste on sweat. Instead, their hides have the cured, waxy texture of old vellum, kept supple by a slow-secreting tallow that gathers in the creases of their joints. Over the places that meet the rock—the heels, the knees, the outer edges of the forearms, the knuckles—the skin thickens into dark, layered plates of horn. You can hear them coming before you see them, if you have the ears for it: not a footstep, but the faint, dry *scritch* of keratin against volcanic glass.

They have no hair, save for sparse, copper-tinted bristles along the crown and the backs of the wrists. In the stagnant, iron-scented air, those bristles stand upright whenever a stressed quartz seam builds a static charge, prickling against the skin like a quiet warning before a shelf shears loose.

Their faces are mostly eyes and bone. The eyes are large, slightly protruding, and guarded by a second, translucent lid that flickers sideways to wipe away glass-dust without spending a tear. The irises are pale, almost milky, tuned to the lowest possible light: they can read the faint, bruised-violet warning of a glider’s flank from fifty yards away, or navigate by the greasy halo of a tallow basin. Down in the lowest tiers, where the fissures glow ember-red, they keep their outer lids half-shut, squinting against what feels to them like a blinding noon.

They do not speak with their throats. In a world where a single *tick* of chitin carries half a mile through the still air, a voice is an extravagance—and a waste of breath, which carries precious vapor out into the dry dark. When two of them meet at a slate cairn where twelve corridors cross, they converse in *taps*. A knuckle against a basalt pillar, a fingernail clicked against a bone awl, a rhythmic drumming of the heel against a resonant floor-slab. To an outsider, it sounds like the territorial arguing of the stone-borers; to them, it is syntax, carried straight through the soles of the feet. When they must be private, they press their foreheads together and tap against each other’s collarbones, feeling the words through bone rather than air.

They eat slowly, and they never drink.

All their water comes sealed inside the creatures that share the dark with them. A hunter—though *harvester* is closer to the truth, for nothing here moves fast enough to chase—will wedge above a cluster of blind, pale-shelled climbers, wait until the colony settles into its warm-pocket stupor, and pry one loose with a flint wedge. They crack the shell at camp, drink the thick, salty hemolymph to the last drop, and scrape the waxy fat from the carapace with a bone spatula. The stone-borers are harder won; their tungsten-hard mandibles can sever a finger cleanly, so they are taken by patience—trapped in narrow dead-ends with lashed-rib barricades, or flipped onto their backs with long poles of joined bone until they exhaust themselves in silent, thrashing fury.

From the gliders—those flat, leathery tapestries that hang from the basalt flutes—they take the resinous paste that coats the creatures’ bellies, scraping it gently while the glider sleeps, careful not to tear the wing-membrane. A live glider is worth more than a dead one; it will renew its coat in a season, though here there are no seasons, only the slow, unmeasured intervals between harvests.

Nothing rots in the Hollows, and nothing is thrown away. A camp is never truly abandoned; it is simply *waiting*. The shallow stone basin smeared with glowing borer-grease will still hold a dim, greasy light a hundred years after the last hand dipped a wick into it. The bone needles left beside the cracked shells were likely shaped from the ribs of someone’s grandmother, cured dry by the blood-heat air until they were harder than flint. Even the bridges—three-foot spans of calcified ribs lashed tight with sinew—are monuments of necessity rather than grief. When one of them grows too stiff to wedge through the slots, or their horn-plates crack too deep to mend, they do not ask for fire or burial. They crawl into a warm, quiet pocket, fold their long limbs tight to their chests, and dry out into a light, papery husk. Later, when someone needs a bridge, or an awl, or a ladder-rung, they will come and take what is useful. To cross a drop on your ancestor’s ribs is simply to be carried by someone who already knew the way.

Because there is no weather, no day, and no decay, time in the Hollows has no natural edges. A thousand years ago looks identical to yesterday.

Perhaps that is why they make the tally marks.

At the wider pockets, where the pillars part enough for two or three to sit without touching walls, the smooth basalt faces are scored with thousands of neat, parallel lines, all the exact same depth, cut with a flint chip held in a steady hand. They are not calendars—there is nothing in the sky above three miles of rock to measure. Some say each mark is a molt of the great borers, or a tremor from the deep mantle, or a traveler who paused at the basin and left a proof that they had passed.

More likely, it is simpler than that. When you sit in the blood-warm dark, your heart beating thirty times a minute to save heat, surrounded by stone that cooled before your kind had hands, it is easy to forget whether you are moving forward or standing still. So you take a flint chip, press it to the slate, and draw a line. Then you wait—for a breath, for a hunger, for the faint *tick* of a distant borer—and you draw another.

You count, because if you did not count, you would become part of the rock.
