At first, in the brown-gray gloom and the teeth-rattling roar of the impellers, it is easy to mistake them for the Sump itself—just another lump of terraced travertine clinging to a valve-house, or a shadow wedged behind a bundle of flanged pipe.

Then the sluice-gate shifts. The current stalls for its three breathless seconds. And in that sudden, suspended hush, the ironwork moves.

They are low-slung, heavy-shouldered things, roughly the size of a crouched hound, though wider and flatter, built to hug the curvature of the vertical town. Six thick, splayed limbs grip the diamond-mesh of the grated walkways with notched, iron-stained claws. Where a surface creature would have eyes, they have only a broad, blunt dome of wet, tallow-pale skin, scarred by old silt-abrasions and pitted with hundreds of tiny sensory pores. They do not see the dull amber cones of the pressure lamps. Instead, they *taste* the Sump—reading the sulfur, the dissolved grease, the faint metallic tang of a leaking flange, and the stinging, invisible hum of the sagging power conduits.

Over years in the mineral-heavy dark, the Sump has dressed them in its own livery. Across their backs and shoulders, the water has deposited overlapping plates of pale travertine and velvety black manganese, thick as roof tiles. Where the current blasts hardest against them, their edges are scoured smooth and bright as river pebbles; in the sheltered creases between the plates, pale, feathery gill-fronds flutter in the hot flow, tangled with the same colorless threads of heat-loving bacteria that stream from the floor-seams. They carry their own pastures on their backs.

No one knows whether their ancestors crawled up through the geothermal cracks from the deep aquifers beneath the bedrock, or whether something small and forgotten was sealed behind the bulkhead when the city above died, and simply learned to live in the churn. Whatever they were, they belong to the Sump now.

They live in the five stacked tiers the way a reef-thing lives in coral, turning the dead machinery into a neighborhood.

The cramped maintenance shacks with their cracked porthole windows are their dens. The heavy steel doors rusted off their hinges decades ago, and inside, out of the worst of the cavitation, the older ones wedge themselves beneath dead control consoles or drape their mineral-heavy bellies over rusted workbenches. If a stray flake of boiler scale drifts past a porthole in the amber dark, you might catch the faintest shift behind the glass—a blind, pale snout pressed close to the pane, its gill-fronds flaring as it reads the pressure outside.

Down on the floor-grates, where the rising thermal plumes are hottest and the water smells sharpest of scorched iron, the younger ones—lighter, their backs not yet too thick with crust—graze on the ghostly hair of the bacteria mats. They hunt the squat, chalk-white crabs out of the crevices with blunt, calloused forelimbs, cracking the thickened shells against the iron plating with a sharp, percussive *tock* that disappears instantly into the roar.

But it is the quarter-hour reversal that sets the rhythm of their lives.

They feel the shift before it happens—not in their ears, which are useless here, but in the hollow, fluid-filled chambers along their ribs that vibrate in tune with the impellers. When the pitch of the chamber drops, they crawl out onto the snapped ends of the rusted I-beam bridges jutting into the empty central void. They lock their rear claws into the pitted steel and wait.

The sluice-gate turns. For three seconds, the storm in the bottle goes slack. Silt hangs motionless in the warm, oily dark.

In that brief, weightless pause, they kick off into the open core. They glide blind and heavy through the murky void, forelimbs spread, snatching at the pale, leathery loaches that dare the crossing—before the three seconds expire, the current rips violently the other way, and the blizzard of rust and grit hurls them back toward the opposite tier. They hit the grated walkways with a dull, metallic *clank*, claws catching and locking as the water tears past them, already turning their snouts into the new flow.

In a world where the impellers never stop and the pressure-relief valves *BOOM* without warning, they do not speak into the water. They speak into the iron.

Pressed flat against the pipework, they strike the flanges and manifolds with the hardened edges of their forelimbs. *Tap—tap.* A slow, double chime that races through the bolted skeleton of the Sump, bypassing the roar of the cavitation, vibrating straight into the claws of whatever is clinging three tiers away. It is a language of structural tremors—a warning when a valve-house eddy is crowded, a call when a loach has been cornered in a dead-end alley, a steady, rhythmic drumming when the young ones gather near the third-tier junction box.

That corroded junction box is their hearth. Whenever the drowned busbar inside it arcs—casting its brief, silent, blue-white flash across the pipe-streets—they crowd as close as they dare, hanging from the overhead conduits. To their electro-receptive snouts, the failing wire is a bright, tingling warmth in the dark, a sharp pulse that makes the water taste like copper. They press close, drunk on the hum, until the next arc snaps through the water and sends them scuttling backward into the alleys in a scatter of mineral plates.

Above them, on the fourth tier, the muffled bronze warning bell still rings its slow, underwater *dong—dong—dong*, tripped by a float-switch that nobody will ever reset. They do not know what a warning is. To them, the bell is simply a landmark—a steady, reliable pulse in the ironwork, no different from the tectonic thrum of the lower grates. Sometimes one will climb up to rest its chin against the vibrating bronze, letting the *dong* rattle softly through its teeth while the warm, tireless dark swirls around it.

Only time slows them down. The Sump never stops depositing its minerals, and unlike the crabs, they cannot molt their shells. Decade by decade, the travertine and black manganese grow thicker across their backs, fusing the overlapping plates into a single, rigid dome. They grow too heavy to launch off the I-beams during the three-second stall; too wide to squeeze behind the leaking flanges.

In the end, an old one crawls into a leeward eddy behind a pump-house, locks its claws into the grating one last time, and stops moving. The heat and the sulfur keep it from rotting. Year by year, the Sump simply plates it over—terracing its back with pale travertine, dusting its flanks with velvety black manganese—until there is nothing left to distinguish it from the walls of the vertical town it lived in.
