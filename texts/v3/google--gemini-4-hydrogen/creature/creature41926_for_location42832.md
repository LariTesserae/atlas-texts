At first, the basin seems entirely empty because whatever lives here has learned not to look like life. In a world where the air is too thin to carry a cry and the ground itself is constantly shifting, anything that moves with haste or stands upright against the updrafts does not last long. 

They are called—if anyone were here to name them—nothing at all, for they do not name themselves. They are solitary, long-limbed, and roughly the size of a crouched human, though folded so tightly into themselves that at twenty paces they are indistinguishable from a slump of cooling slag.

Their skin is dry as pumice and dark as freshly fractured basalt, matte and soot-stained, with fine gray mineral dust settled permanently into its deep creases. There is no softness to them, no sheen of sweat or oil; moisture is a luxury this crust cannot afford. Over their palms, knees, shins, and shoulders, the hide thickens into overlapping, horn-like plates—hardened by generations of gripping volcanic glass and kneeling on razor-edged scoria. When they drag a hand across a sheet of obsidian, it makes only a dry, chalky scrape.

They do not walk on legs across the knife-edge ridges, nor do they take to the thin, turbulent air. Instead, they move low and unhurried, four-limbed when the terrain steepens, testing every brittle crust with the tip of a finger before trusting their weight to it. Their long, narrow fingers end in blunt, mineral-hardened pads, strong enough to pry up a slab of basalt and delicate enough to feel the faintest tremor humming inside it.

In the near-total darkness beneath the soot-choked sky, their eyes are small, deep-set behind heavy bony ridges that shield them from the flying grit. They see little more than the sullen cherry-glow of the magma seams and the sudden, pale-violet snap of static in the dust. Instead, they know the world through pressure, heat, and bone. When one pauses on a shelf of rock, it will often press its flat, earless jaw or the heel of its palm directly against the stone. Through the bedrock, the silent violence of the basin becomes loud: the deep, subterranean *thump* of a splitting fault, the glassy *tick* of a cooling spire about to give way, the subtle, electric prickle that warns of a thermal funnel forming upslope.

They are creatures of borrowed warmth. Without the heat rising from the crust, they grow sluggish, their joints stiffening until they lock in place like cold iron. Most of their lives are spent following the newest cracks, creeping from one warm seam to the next before the rock cools or the ground swallows it.

Along the lips of the warmest vents, where the brittle, charcoal-colored filaments cling to the stone, they find both shelter and sustenance. They do not hunt; they graze. Squatting beside a fissure, one will pluck a mat of the spun-iron growth, feeling it rattle dryly between its fingers before grinding it down between wide, flat molars. It is a meager, sulfur-heavy diet, yielding just enough chemical energy and bound trace moisture to keep their slow, dry metabolisms turning over. 

Because the vents are precious and easily stripped bare by the erratic updrafts, they tend them. Those low, semicircular windbreaks of raw, unmortared stone are not built merely for the creatures themselves; they are built to windward of a promising crack, creating a small, still pocket of warm gas where the gray spores can settle and the charcoal filaments can thicken untouched by the howling dust.

And when the thermal winds grow too fierce, or when the ground beneath them settles into a temporary lull, they build the rings. 

A ring is never wider than a single body curled tight—knees drawn to the chest, long arms wrapped around the shins, forehead resting against the warm basalt floor. There is no roof, for there is no rain to keep out, and a ceiling of loose stone is a death sentence when the next tremor strikes. Inside the low, jagged wall, shielded from the horizontal sting of the ash, the creature drops into a motionless torpor. Its nostrils pinch shut against the grit. Its breathing slows until the thin air scarcely moves in its chest. From above, it looks like nothing more than a mound of dark scree gathered in a hollow.

Yet they are not entirely unaware of one another across the empty miles. 

The tall, leaning cairns—stacked patiently by hand on the high ridges, balanced without mortar or tool—serve as both landmarks and slow-motion gauges. In a starless dark where the horizon is swallowed whole, a cairn is something a hand can find by touch, a fixed point in a landscape that refuses to stay still. When the ground tilts, the cairn leans, telling the next traveler which way the fault is sliding. 

Sometimes, when the silence of the basin stretches too thin, one of them will stand beside a tall cairn and rock it gently at its base—not enough to topple it, only enough to make the heavy bottom slab *thump* rhythmically against the bedrock. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* It is a sound the thin air cannot carry, but the stone carries it for miles: a dry, steady percussion beneath the random ticking of the slag, saying simply that someone is still crouching in the dark, and that here, for a little while longer, the rock is holding.
