If you stood on the damp peat long enough for the heat to stop feeling like a blow, you would realize that the boulders along the lower ledges are breathing.

Not visibly—nothing here wastes effort on a heave of the ribs—but with a slow, tidal widening of the flanks that matches the *tock* of condensation hitting stone. When one of them finally decides to move, it does not stand. It unhooks itself from the flowstone shelf in a low, broad pour of weight, four-limbed and heavy-hipped, sliding down the mossy incline with the same unhurried certainty as a snake.

They are built for the sideways world. Low-slung and wide through the chest, with long, heavy forearms and short, splayed thighs, they never rise to their full height—if they even have one—because the ceiling is a world of bats and root-braids and nothing good grows up there. Their skin is bare, thick, and faintly puckered like wet hide, dark olive and bruised slate across the shoulders where the faint light falls, paling to a chalky, almost luminous grey along the belly and the undersides of their arms. Across the broadest, stillest planes of their backs, the damp air has encouraged a fine, velvety furring of moss-spores and algae, so that when one crouches motionless in a tufa niche, it vanishes into the upholstery of the wall.

They have no hair, no claws, and no voice. Their throats are smooth and thick, built for swallowing whole fern-hearts and soft-bodied grubs, or for holding a mouthful of tepid, mineral-heavy water. When they communicate, it is by pressing the flat of a broad, spatulate hand against another’s shoulder, or by leaning their temples together until the slow pulse in their necks syncs. They also leave faint, oily smears from the glands at their wrists along the travertine ridges—chemical notes that linger for days in the stagnant air, telling whoever passes next which alcoves are occupied, which pools have gone foul, and where the blind ants are swarming.

Their eyes are the strangest part of them: large, round, and set wide on a blunt, heavy-browed face, with no visible whites—only deep, wet wells of dark amber that drink in the olive twilight. A pale, milky third lid slides sideways across the lens whenever a drop of water falls too close, or when they poke their faces into the peat. Because they see so little color, they are obsessed with whatever catches the light.

That is what the baskets are for.

Watch one of the older ones—heavier around the jowls, its back crusted with a pale rime of lime where it has slept too long against a sweating wall—as it works at a hanging curtain of aerial roots. It does not snap or tear the roots. Instead, it takes a thick, pale braid into its wide mouth, rolling it between blunt, flat teeth until the outer bark softens and splits, then strips the fibers away with thick, pad-tipped fingers. The weaving is done by touch, mostly in the dark of the lower recesses, the basket taking shape over weeks of idle, patient bending. When a basket grows too stiff with mineral crust to hold its shape, they do not repair it; they simply leave it in its alcove for the moss to swallow and begin another.

Inside, they keep the bright things. A river-smooth white pebble, carried down through the high fissures generations ago by some ancestor who ventured up into the blinding scrub at night, shines like a coal in the dimness when a bruised shaft of noon hits it just right. The iridescent wing-cases of the giant beetles—pried carefully from dead shells found under the fern-fronds—are arranged beside the pebbles in shallow, deliberate heaps. Sometimes an adult will sit inside one of the waist-high travertine rings for half a day, turning a single beetle-wing between thumb and forefinger, tilting it a fraction of an inch to watch the green-violet sheen flare and die in the gloom. It is not worship, and it is not play. It is simply the quietest way to hold onto a piece of the sky.

The travertine rings are where they rest when the floor gets too busy. Down in the damp peat, the centipedes are always hunting, and the blind ants never sleep; even a creature with hide as thick as wet leather grows tired of having its toes tested in the dark. So they stack the loose slabs without mortar, wedging them tight with their heels and palms until they have a dryish, waist-high basin. Two or three will curl together inside a ring, limbs tangled, bellies pressed to the warm stone floor to soak up the geothermal heat, letting their breath slow until a snake might glide straight over their shoulders without noticing they are meat.

The shallow niches scraped into the soft tufa walls serve a different purpose. Every few months, the mineral film of the chamber begins to stiffen their joints, caking in the creases of their elbows and behind their knees. When the itch of it grows unbearable, they back into the soft rock and grind their shoulders and flanks against the tufa, scraping away the crusted dead skin along with a fine, chalky dust. They eat the dust afterward, licking it off their palms—a habit that keeps their bones dense in a place with no sun.

They are not the masters of the chamber. They do not hunt the bats, which are too quick, and they leave the heavy-bodied constrictors alone, stepping carefully over their coiled green weight when they cross a leaf-plate. Instead, they graze the edges of the silence. They pluck the pale, slow-moving crickets off the wet walls with a flick of a thick, wet tongue; they dig the fat larvae out of the peat with blunt fingers; they wait beneath the fern-fronds for a hand-sized moth to drift too low, catching it between flat palms without making a sound and eating it wings and all, leaving only a smudge of pale dust on their chins.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the pocket—never more than the moss and the warm pools can bear. When one grows too old to climb the flowstone shelves, it settles into the lowest, warmest basin, where the water is cloudy with chalk and rotting leaves, and simply stops moving. The ants and the centipedes and the creeping liverwort take care of the rest in total quiet. And when a new one is born—tiny, translucent, clinging to its mother’s moss-furred back with broad, sticky pads—it does not cry out. It only opens its wet amber eyes to the olive dimness, reaches out a blunt hand toward the nearest white pebble, and learns to wait.
