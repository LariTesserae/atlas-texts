You do not see them until a relay clicks somewhere across the dark, and the nearest lamppost swells with its brief, bruised-violet light.

Then you notice that the figure standing at the end of the unfinished street is not a pillar, and not a crane-post.

They stand right at the edge where the bark-plate narrows into a soft, wet meristematic tip, toes curling slightly over the drop. In the cold violet wash, their skin has the same unweathered, newly extruded sheen as the catwalk beneath them—pale as tallow, faintly translucent along the ribs and forearms, with no scars, no calluses, no sign that wind or sun has ever touched them. When the lamp dims back to near-blackness, they do not move. You can still tell where they are only by the faint, dry *tick* of their jaw, like a clockwork escapement answering the hum in the vascular cables below.

They are the tenants of the gall-houses, though it is hard to say whether they moved into the town or budded out of it alongside the copper flanges.

Up close, they are tall and spare, built for narrow ledges and long, vertical drops. Their limbs have an extra stillness to them, the patience of things that wait for roads to grow at millimeters an hour. Most of them have eyes—small, milky, set deep under brow-ridges of fibrous, chestnut-colored tissue—but they do not use them the way you do. They tilt their faces *away* from the violet arcs and toward the pipes, toward the humming floorboards, reading the hollow by the pressure of the current in their teeth and the drift of damp air against the fine, pale hairs along their knuckles. When one of them turns to look at you, it is their jaw that angles toward you first, listening to the iron in your blood or the static on your coat.

They wear very little, and what they do wear is half-borrowed from the walls. Heavy aprons of cured cambium, still damp at the hem; long, sleeveless coats that look like oiled canvas until you see the faint venation running through the shoulders, connecting seamlessly to the skin beneath. Where a cuff tears or a collar hangs loose, they do not sew it. They simply stand beneath a dynamo until the ash-pale moths lift off in a noiseless cloud, and hold the torn edge still. Sooner or later, a moth settles on the gap. Its velvet wings stiffen, flatten, and darken into a neat, riveted copper clasp, cold to the touch, holding the cloth fast.

In the same way, they carry no grease-cans. When their knees or elbows begin to stiffen in the chill, they reach into the exposed gear-trains of the gantry cranes, pluck one of the wet-chalk segmented creatures from between the wooden teeth, and draw it gently across their joints. The creature leaves a thin, clear trail of mucus and crawls back into the mechanism, unharmed.

There is no hurry in anything they do, because there is nowhere finished to arrive at.

All through the hushed, windless hours—measured not by sun or bell, but by the slow cycling of the central boiler-house—they move along the suspended skeleton of the town. They walk with a flat-footed, unhurried glide, barefoot or shod in thick plates of crystallized salt harvested from the base of the perimeter walls. On the catwalks where the gravity goes slack, or where a staircase twists through a pocket of half-rendered brick and deposits them upside down on the underside of a landing, they do not stumble. They simply pause, let the water in the gutter run upward past their ankles, and shift their weight until the ceiling accepts them as a floor.

If you follow one of them long enough, you will see what their work is.

They are tenders of nothing in particular. A pair of them will spend half a cycle hunched beside a riveted copper flange, listening with one ear pressed to the warm, sweating bark while the other hand rests on a brass pressure-valve. They adjust it by a hair’s breadth, wait for the *plink* of a falling droplet to miss its cistern and drift sideways into a flywheel, and nod to each other, satisfied. Another will ride the bone-white gantry crane out over the hundred-foot drop—perched calmly on the swinging hook while the greased wooden bearings turn without a sound—just to wipe a smear of condensation from one of the three taut tendons that hold the boiler-house aloft.

Inside the boiler-house itself, suspended in the exact center of the dark void, three or four of them are always at work. It is warmer there, smelling sharply of scorched sap and hot iron. They shovel nothing into the firebox, yet the firebox glows with a dull, violet heat whenever the vascular cables surge. They read gauges whose needles spin lazily through unmarked glass, and tap on pipes that end twenty feet out in empty air, waiting for an echo that never quite comes back.

They do not speak—at least, not with their mouths. When two of them meet on a narrow bridge of braided cambium, they lean their foreheads together for a moment, or press the backs of their wrists side by side, sharing whatever tiny, sub-audible vibration is passing through the timber. Sometimes a cold spark jumps between their collarbones, smelling briefly of ozone and wet wood. Then they step past each other, Careful not to brush the blind, translucent amphibians clinging to the railings.

Only the wrong-jointed predators make them wary. You can tell when one of those clicking hunters is near: the tenants stop, flatten themselves against the nearest pillar, and go so still that their skin seems to dull and coarsen, taking on the matte, fibrous texture of bracket fungus. The eyeless predator trots past at its brisk, inverted gait, its joints snapping softly in the dark, and never seems to realize that the bump on the pillar is breathing.

At rest, they retreat into the hollow, gall-like tenements clustered along the outer walls.

The doorways are low, oval apertures edged in soft, pale bark that yields slightly when they squeeze through. Inside, a tenement is a single curved chamber, dry as a walnut shell on the floor and weeping slow beads of mineral sweat along the ceiling. There are no beds, no tables—only shallow ledges grown directly out of the inner wall, shaped to the curve of a spine or a hip. They sleep curled in these hollows, limbs tucked tight, while around them the walls tick softly as new salt crystals form.

Sometimes, in the deeper chambers, you find a tenant who has not woken up for a very long time.

They are not dead—nothing here rots, in the cool, sterile damp—but they have stayed still long enough for the town to forget the boundary between them and the wall. A shoulder has widened and fused into the fibrous curve of the alcove; fine, pale rootlets have threaded out from their fingertips to knit into the floorboards; along their collarbone, a small, wet membrane has swelled, glowing a faint, bruised violet whenever the current surges beneath the street.

The others do not mourn them, and do not cut them free. They hang their cambium coats on the hardened crook of an elbow, or lean against the ribs to listen to the hum of the relays.

And down on the lowest walkways, where the raw streets still end in wet, blind tips inching across the dark, a few of the younger tenants—smaller, smoother, their skin still sticky with the sap of the galls that bore them—stand quietly at the edge. They hold lengths of copper wire in their hands, waiting for the opposite ledge to come close enough to splice, while the moths drift past their faces like ash, and the town keeps growing around them in the dark.
