At first, you might think the pocket is empty—that the boilers and presses run on some leftover momentum, tended only by the vines that brace the ironwork. Nothing here breaks the rhythm of the *tick* and the *drip*. Nothing hurries across the catwalks.

It takes a minute, standing in the thick, motionless heat, for the shadows between the brick arches to resolve into figures. A shape near a caged filament bulb shifts by half an inch, and you realize it is a shoulder, slick with moisture and dusted with verdigris. A ceramic catch-basin is lifted from beneath a weeping pipe before it can overflow, set aside without a clink, and replaced with an empty one.

They are human, or were once, though the basalt pocket has pressed them into its own narrow, sweltering mold.

They are spare and sinewy, built without an ounce of spare flesh to trap the heat. Their skin is the pale, translucent color of the succulents that cling to the railings, flushed permanently pink near the surface where the blood runs close to shed warmth. Because sweat cannot evaporate in air already saturated with steam, their bodies are perpetually beaded with water. To keep the damp from softening their skin into sores, they rub their arms, chests, and necks with the crushed, resinous sap of the plum-colored ferns—so they carry the exact scent of the place itself: wet mortar, warm copper, and bruised leaves.

Their posture is a permanent, gentle stoop, shaped by decades of ducking beneath lagged steam-mains and squeezing past rows of pressure gauges. They go barefoot, their soles broad and leathery, gripping the slick iron grates while reading the faint, low-frequency hum of the electrolytic vats through their heels. Hair is a liability near grease-darkened flywheels and low-hanging vines, so they shave their heads or bind short tufts tightly with scraps of copper wire. They wear almost nothing: short, oil-stained aprons of heavy linen or woven vine-fiber, slung with brass wrenches, grafting knives, and strips of dry rag for wiping the condensation off gauge-glass. Spectacles are useless here; any lens clouds over in three breaths. Instead, their eyes have grown wide and dark-pupiled, accustomed to the dim pools of amber light and the dull cherry-red glow behind the boiler mica.

To survive here is to match the speed of the machinery. In this breathless humidity, rushing raises the core temperature until a person cooks from the inside out. So they move at the pace of rising sap and clock-hand flywheels. If you watch one of them tighten a flange-bolt, it looks as though they are moving through deep water—a slow, deliberate turn of the wrist, followed by a long pause to let the pulse settle. When two of them meet on a five-inch gantry, there is no jostling and no haste; one simply flattens against the warm brick or hooks an elbow around a cool pipe, and the other glides past in a silent, practiced choreography.

They barely speak. Loud voices would be both exhausting and unnatural in a chamber where the sharpest sound is the sigh of a relief valve. Communication is mostly tactile or resonant: a light tap of a knuckle against a copper pipe to signal a pressure change three tiers below, a hand resting briefly on a neighbor’s damp shoulder before stepping past, or a low, breathy murmur that scarcely stirs the hanging ferns.

Most of their language is spent listening to the settlement itself. They do not need to look at a thermometer to know how a transformer is faring; they look for the small, heat-tolerant beetles that sit motionless on the warm casings. As long as the beetles stay put, the load is safe. If the casings are bare, a tender will slowly trace the conduits back to the electrolytic vats to see where the current has thickened. In the same way, they watch the blind, chalk-white millipedes threading the rims of the condensation pans—knowing by the creatures' sluggishness when the mineral content of the rising steam has shifted.

Their work is a quiet, continuous stewardship of the loop. On the lowest, hottest tiers, where the air shimmers above the thermal fissures, they work in short, silent shifts—dredging mineral sludge from the cracks and packing it into the hydraulic presses. Higher up, where the air is marginally cooler, others scrape the dark, pressed mineral-cakes from the plates, crumble them into the electrolytic baths, or mix the briny runoff with the pulped mash of thick-stemmed tubers. They guide the fibrous vines away from the moving teeth of the gears and onto the ceramic insulators, where the plants seem happiest. They eat the pale, starchy liquor squeezed from the tubers, seasoned with a pinch of mineral salt scraped from the boiler seams, and they drink the cooled condensate filtered through beds of crushed basalt and charcoal.

There is no day here, and no night. The filament bulbs never darken; the heat-exchangers never cool. Time is measured by the slow curling of the broad fern-fronds toward the amber light, and by the fractional advance of the hydraulic rams. Sleep is not a long, shared darkness, but something taken in turns—curled inside the brick-arched alcoves or slung in woven vine-hammocks between the gentler pipes of the middle tiers. Even asleep, they remain half-tethered to the room; a slight change in the pitch of a humming vat, or the sudden absence of a familiar *drip* into a basin, is enough to bring their eyes open without a start.

Because the cavity is young—opened by some recent shift in the deep rock—they still treat the raw, razor-edged basalt with a wary respect. They are settlers who have packed their old ironwork and their strange, light-starved gardens into a fresh wound in the earth, and they know how fragile the seal is. 

Children born into the pocket do not learn to run; a running child would scald against an unlagged main or tear themselves on the unsoftened stone. They are carried in linen slings against damp, steady chests until their heartbeats learn the slow cadence of the flywheels. Their first lessons are lessons in stillness: sitting on a warm brick ledge, watching a single bead of water swell on the underside of an overhead pipe until it finally drops.

And when the tenders grow too old to climb the riveted gantries, they do not leave—there is nowhere beyond the basalt to go. They settle onto the middle tiers, where the heat is mildest, tending the nurseries of pale succulents or turning the small hand-screws on the tuber-presses. When they die, nothing is carried out. In a pocket packed until there is scarcely an inch of empty air left, even the dead are returned to the equilibrium—laid deep in the brick planter-troughs beneath the plum-colored ferns, so their moisture rises again toward the ceiling, and their bones brace the roots that brace the iron.
