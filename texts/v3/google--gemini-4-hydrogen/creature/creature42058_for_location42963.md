If you stand long enough on one of the terraced parapets—long enough for the sun’s shadow to creep a hand’s-breadth across the hexagonal basalt slabs—you begin to see that the cities are not empty of movement. They are only moving at the speed of the stone.

The people who built the terraced enclaves and string the braided cables across the chasms are shaped by the same dry, patient physics as the *Velamen* overhead. They are tall, broad-ribbed, and deliberate, with a low, steady way of walking that never breaks into haste. To run across a sunlit plaza here is not merely bad manners; it is a kind of structural carelessness, like heating cold dolomite too quickly until it cracks.

### The People of the Steps

Up close, their skin has the matte, warm finish of oiled slate or sun-cured parchment, always lightly dusted with fine white gypsum from the air. They have no sweat glands and no tears; moisture is a rumor from another kind of universe. Beneath the dry, flexible hide—thickened over the shoulders and forearms into faint, overlapping plates—their bodies run on the same closed-loop chemistry as the rest of the shelves: dense, pale lipid-oils circulated by slow, rhythmic contractions along the torso, and silver-salted nerve tracts that carry thought as a steady, low-voltage current.

They do not have beating hearts. If you press your ear to a builder’s chest, you hear only the faint, rhythmic rustle of air being drawn through slotted vents along the ribs, passing across dry, mineral-lattice lungs that strip out oxygen and exhale a warm, dust-free puff.

Their faces are quiet and heavily browed, with deep-set eyes shielded against the bleached ceramic glare by smoky, translucent inner lids that slide shut whenever the wind lifts grit from the ledges. Their hands are long, dry-knuckled, and tipped with hardened, conductive calluses. An engineer does not need a gauge to know if an electrostatic mast is drawing well; she simply lays the pad of her thumb against the copper grounding-rod and feels the charge prickle up her wrist like warmth.

Because the dry air turns every movement into static, they dress in loose tunics of woven basalt-fiber and heavy shawls threaded with fine copper wire, grounded through graphite-soled sandals that have polished the city streets to a dull mirror over centuries. When two neighbors meet in the shade of an overhanging rock, they do not clasp hands or embrace. They touch the backs of their copper-wrapped wrists together to equalize their charge. A tiny, blue-white spark snaps softly between them—or, between old friends, a faint, warm hum passes from skin to skin. Only then do they speak.

Their voices are not wet or loud. Sound carries for miles across the empty chasms, and shouting is considered clumsy, a waste of breath and stillness. They speak through taut, reed-like membranes in the throat that produce a dry, resonant cadence—something like wind moving across tuned ceramic, or the low thrum of a tensioned steel cable. Conversations are unhurried. Two stone-cutters might stand at a railing for ten minutes between sentences, watching a freight gondola clear the midpoint of a three-mile canyon, neither finding the silence anything other than companionable.

### Slow Work and Quiet Comforts

Sustenance here is as dry and dense as the landscape. In the shaded markets tucked beneath the cliff overhangs, vendors weigh out pressed bricks of refined lipid-paste, cakes of ground sulfur-crust scraped from the windward ledges, and the rich, waxy cores of harvested *Tessellates*, seasoned with powdered barite and bitter salt. 

They do not drink, but they *charge*. Along the edges of the quiet plazas stand low, copper-inlaid benches wired into the city’s trickle-lines. In the late afternoon, when the sun begins to drop behind the western mesas and the cliffs cool with deep, audible groans, the city-dwellers sit together on the warm metal, letting a gentle, steady current soak into their soles and spines to replenish their ion-channels. To them, it feels the way a warm hearth feels to creatures of water and blood.

At night, they retreat into thick-walled chambers of sintered stone that hold the day’s heat, reclining on slabs of soapstone and entering a deep, still torpor. Their lipid-oils thicken slightly in the cool dark; their static hum drops to near zero.

Because nothing rots and nothing burns fast, they live for centuries. A master mason lays the foundation of a terraced tower knowing she will likely still be alive to set its capstone eighty years later. Quarried blocks of salt-stone sit in curing yards for decades, tended by apprentices who learn first how to wait, and second how to read the grain of rock with their fingertips. Their art is built for the same scale: wind-harps of braided steel strung across narrow alleys that sing only when a week-long dust storm rises, and vast, geometric reliefs carved directly into the three-kilometer cliff faces—readable only from the gondolas crawling across the abyss.

### Companions of the Ledges

The city-dwellers do not stand apart from the dry ecology; they are braided into it.

Along the outer parapets, *Velamen*-tenders stand with long, non-conductive poles of fused silica, gently snagging the trailing filaments of passing giants to comb out the rare-earth dusts and mineral spores trapped in the charged fibers. After the annual dry-molt, when the great kite-winged creatures shed their outer membranes onto the ledges, weavers gather the papery, silica-reinforced sheets to stretch across window frames—translucent, airtight, and warm as amber.

In the curing yards, herds of *Tessellates* are kept inside low basalt curbs. Left alone with a rough-hewn block of dolomite for two or three years, their chisel-tipped legs and grinding gizzards will polish the stone smoother than any iron tool could manage.

And inside the cities themselves—in the mortarless joints of the walls and beneath the woven-basalt awnings—live the **Seam-Listeners**. No larger than a palm, flat-bodied and armored in speckled barite, they are distant, six-legged cousins to the *Wire-Clingers*. They feed on the microscopic crusts that try to etch the city stone, and in return, they are exquisitely sensitive to thermal stress. When a load-bearing beam or a cliff-anchor begins to micro-fracture from the daily breathing of the rock, the Seam-Listeners gather over the invisible flaw and begin a faint, rhythmic clicking—a dry, ticking alarm that brings the engineers with their graphite-greased jacks long before the stone can fail.

### The Unseen Strata

Yet the shelves and the sunlit cities are only the middle skin of the world. Above them is the bleached sky; below them, three kilometers down, lies the white haze.

No sun reaches the floors of the deep basins as anything more than a milky, diffuse glow. Down there, the air is thick, still, and warm, choked with suspended gypsum and the endless, slow snowfall of the upper world: shed *Velamen* wings, fractured *Tessellate* shells, flakes of dried rust-crust, and the dust of weathered cliffs.

Through this knee-deep powder glide the **Haze-Gleaners**. They are vast, blind, carpet-like things—twenty or thirty feet across, pale as chalk, with no clear top or bottom and no need for eyes. They creep almost imperceptibly over the basin floor on waves of stiff, mineral bristles, drawing in the dead flakes and shed lipids that rain down from the light. Inside their flat, felt-like bodies, a slow, dry digestive warmth breaks down the silica chitin and waxes, leaving behind neat, compacted trails of pure mineral ash. Nothing from the cities ever goes down to the basins intentionally, save when a cable snaps—and if a heavy freight gondola plunges into the white mist, the Haze-Gleaners are already drifting toward the impact, patient as gravity.

And deeper still—not in the basins, but miles beneath the cities, inside the dark, breathless shafts where the geothermal turbines turn once every few minutes on bearings of powdered graphite—live the **Soot-Spinners**. Tiny, pale, and blind, they never feel the wind or see the ceramic sky. They cluster around the warm housings of the dynamos, sipping stray static from the copper coils and weaving ultra-fine, conductive webs of carbon-rich silk from the stray dust of the graphite lubricant. Most of the time, the shaft-keepers leave them be, listening to the quiet rustle of their webs in the dark; only when a curtain of soot-silk grows thick enough to bridge a gap and short a coil do the engineers descend with long, insulated brushes to sweep the warm iron clean.

### The End of the Cycle

When the sky begins to thicken into the dull, luminous amber of a continental dust storm—three days arriving, a week of amber stillness, three days clearing—the whole vertical world draws inward.

The cities close their heavy ceramic shutters. The *Velamen* reel in their filaments and anchor themselves to the iron rings driven into the cliff faces, rattling softly in the charged wind like moored sails. The Seam-Listeners tuck themselves deep into the basalt joints. Inside the warm, shuttered halls, lit by the steady, ozone-scented glow of low-voltage arc lamps, the people of the steps sit together on their copper benches, sharing quiet plates of pressed lipid-cake and listening to the wind-harps groan in the alleys outside.

And when one of them finally reaches the end of their long, slow century-span, there is no grave dug into the unyielding rock, and no fire wasted on wood that does not exist.

They are carried up to the leeward wind-niches carved high above the city, dressed in a simple, ungrounded shroud of woven basalt, and laid facing the empty chasm. They do not rot. They do not bleed. They simply stiffen and lighten in the dry, steady heat until, over decades, the patient wind fractures their outer shell—flake by papery flake—sending them drifting out past the anchored *Velamen*, down into the milky haze of the basins below, where the world begins again.
