They are not guests in the cleft; they are its second skin, laid over the raw basalt in shifting, breathing strata. To understand anything that lives here, you have to begin with the cold and the thinness of the air, which together act as a kind of heavy, invisible oil, slowing every impulse to the pace of dripping water.

The foundation of the hollow’s life is the great, anchored multitude. From a few meters above the clinking scree all the way to the upper pinch of the ceiling, the walls are shingled with the hound-sized sleepers. Up close, they look less like beasts than like weathered geological plates that have learned to sigh. Their hides are slate-grey and deeply pleated, folded like heavy leather bellows along their flanks. When they draw breath—a long, dragging *shhh* that takes half a minute to complete—the pleats part, revealing brief, pale seams of damp, almost gelatinous skin underneath, laced with dark veins that drink whatever stray oxygen the thin air offers. When they exhale—a wet, heavy *ahhh*—the plates settle back into armor, and a faint plume of mist, warmer by a fraction of a degree than the stone, curls out into the dark.

Their six limbs are long, stiff-jointed, and oddly angular, ending in blackened chitinous spikes that do not merely grip the rock but are driven into its hairline fractures like pitons. Once wedged, a sleeper may not unhook a limb for days. They move across the sheer face by inches, sliding their broad, flat bellies over the glass-sharp facets with a slow, muscular ripple. Those bellies are their mouths and their anchors at once: soft, ridged, and perpetually slick with a mildly acidic mucus that dissolves the salt-crusts, moss-hairs, and ash-colored liverworts directly off the stone. Where a sleeper has passed, the basalt is left gleaming and stripped clean, a wet track no wider than a man’s torso.

They have no eyes, and if they possess any awareness of one another, it is only as warmth and pressure—a neighbor’s flank pressing against their own, the shared, overlapping surf of ten thousand lungs keeping the cleft’s air just damp enough to keep their hides from cracking. When one of them finally dies, its chitinous tips remain locked in the stone. Its soft underside dries out, its pleated hide stiffens into a brittle shell, and it hangs there for months like a forgotten husk until a shift in the mountain or the pry-bar of a scavenger snaps its brittle joints and sends it clattering down into the shards below.

Threading through this living, sighing wall—and using the pleated backs of the sleepers as highways to avoid the razor edges of the bare stone—are the pale ones.

They are smaller, scarcely the size of a curled fox, with long, gaunt limbs and skin the color of wet chalk, thin enough that the faint indigo of their veins shows at the wrists and throat. They have no eyes at all; their faces are smooth, sloped planes dominated entirely by two enormous, dish-shaped ears that stand out from the sides of the skull like satellite cups. Because the hollow is never quiet—filled always with the wet roar of bellows-lungs and the *whoom* of wind shearing across the roof—their ears are in constant, twitching motion. Each ear can fold, flatten, or rotate independently, filtering out the low, continuous wash of breath so they can isolate the sharp, high frequencies that matter: the dry clink of a shifting shard on the floor, the scrape of a sleeper’s chitinous foot, or the distant *tock... tock... tock* of another pale one’s stone.

Their forelimbs are disproportionately long, ending in three-fingered hands with thick, calloused pads—the only part of their bodies tough enough to press safely against unweathered basalt. Almost every adult carries a tool, though they never shape the stone themselves. Instead, they search the scree at the bottom of the cleft for naturally cleaved splinters of basalt or the curved, hollow ribs of fallen sleepers, binding one end in tight, damp coils of twisted bladder-frond fiber so the grip will not slip in the wet dark.

With these picks, they work the seams. They wedge a splinter beneath a leathery bladder-frond to drink the swollen, bitter fluid inside; they scrape mineral salts from cracks too narrow for the sleepers’ broad bellies; they crack open the brittle carapaces of whatever falls from above. When they meet one another in the dark—crawling belly-down across a sleeper’s heaving back—they do not touch immediately. They tap their picks against the stone or against the hollow rib-bones they carry, sending out measured, rhythmic clicks. A sharp double-tap might warn of a loose, razor-edged flake of slate; a slow, rolling cadence calls others to a newly fallen sleeper whose soft belly-meat has not yet soured. They have no nests, no dens, no language of permanence. When they need to rest, they simply wedge themselves into the warm, damp V-shaped troughs between two sleeping bellows-beasts, folding their great ears flat against their skulls and letting the slow expansion and contraction of their hosts’ ribs rock them into torpor.

Forty meters overhead, where the cleft pinches toward the bruised-indigo slit of the sky, lives a third kind of creature, lighter than the rest and strangely luminous in the scarce light.

These are the mica-wings—translucent, thumb-sized things with segmented, glass-clear bodies and four stiff, veined wings that look as though they were flaked from mineral crystal. They hang in shivering, near-touching sheets from the overhanging lip of stone, tens of thousands of them packed so tightly that they form a living, trembling frost across the ceiling. Their constant shivering is not fear, nor cold, though the wind that shears across the summit slit is bitter; it is the only way they can keep their flight muscles warm enough to function in the thin air.

They feed on what the mountain’s breath brings them: microscopic spores, ice-bound pollen, and wind-torn dust carried up from valleys miles below and trapped in the swirling eddies of the cloud-banks. When a sudden gust forces its way down through the roof, hundreds of them are torn loose at once. They do not dart or swoop; in air this thin, their stiff wings can barely buy them lift. Instead, they descend in slow, papery spirals, their wings rattling softly against one another like dry leaves. Most catch the updrafts halfway down and claw their way back toward the indigo slit. The unlucky ones—those whose wings are too heavy with condensation, or whose strength gives out—drift all the way down into the dark. Some snag on the pleated hides of the sleepers; others strike the floor, where the pale ones hear the faint *tick* of their landing from twenty meters away and begin their slow, deliberate crawl toward the sound.

Yet the flyers’ journey does not end on the scree. At the very base of the cleft, where the silver threads of condensed mist gather in shallow, angular basins among the flat-sided shards, a fourth, nearly invisible layer of life waits out the dark.

Here, in water scarcely deeper than a finger’s width and cold enough to ache, tiny, thread-like larvae—the young of the mica-wings—drift between the sunken fragments of basalt. They are blind and pale, feeding on the dissolved salt, the fallen scraps of liverwort, and the microscopic rot washed down from the walls above. They grow with agonizing slowness, taking years to lengthen by a fingernail’s breadth. When at last one is ready, it crawls out of the angular pool onto a damp shard, sheds its soft skin, and waits—motionless, translucent, its new mica wings drying in the wet draft—until it is light enough to catch a stray thermal rising off the breath of the sleepers, and begin the long, spiraling climb toward the slit of sky.

Nothing in the hollow hurries. Nothing wastes a motion. In the pitch dark between the cutting walls, the sleepers breathe, the pale ones tap and pry, the flyers shiver against the wind, and the larvae turn in the cold pools—each tier feeding the next, bound together by mist and gravity, waiting out the slow centuries of the peak.
