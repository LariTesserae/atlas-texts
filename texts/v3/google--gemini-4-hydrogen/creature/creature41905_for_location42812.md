If you stand still long enough for the shock of the noise to settle into your bones, you begin to notice that the crowd is not uniform. There are seams in it—pockets where the deafening static thins, where the *tick-tick-tick* of the fliers bends around an unseen curve of stone, leaving a hollow of muffled, heavy air.

In those seams, and in the wet margins between the terraces, lives everything that cannot afford to be loud.

Down in the travertine pans, beneath the thrashing tangles of ribbon-bodied filterers, the pale terraced rims are not merely rock. They are built, grain by microscopic grain, by things too small to feel against your ankle: blind, thread-thin worms that anchor themselves in the mineral-saturated water and weave loose nets of mucus to catch the falling chalk. As the lime precipitates out of the cloudy water, it hardens around their threads, cementing them into tiny, porous chambers. Over centuries, their discarded houses become the pale, scalloped lips of the basins. When a grazer’s heavy, armored edge scrapes too deep into a rim, the worms retreat into the stone’s pores and wait for the slow drips from the ceiling to heal the wound.

Higher up, clinging to the damp underside of the limestone overhangs, live the parasites of the air—flat, pale ticks no bigger than a fingernail, with hooked legs and no mouths to speak of until they find a host. They wait near the roosts, dropping blindly whenever the wind of a passing wing stirs the air. A single flier might carry a dozen of them tucked into the fluted acoustic ridges along its ribs. They do not drink blood; they feed on the oily secretions that keep the flier’s leathery skin supple, and in return they stay perfectly still, because a flier that cannot hear its own echoes cleanly will soon dash itself against a pillar.

And moving among all of it—among the grinding herds, the rattling bone-chimes, and the stalking hunters—are the ones who belong to the valley the way a hand belongs to a glove.

You would not hear them coming, even if the air were quiet. You would only know one was near when the *tick-tick* of the fliers overhead suddenly parted around a stillness, or when a cold, wet hand—broad-palmed, with long, splayed fingers—brushed your elbow in the dark to test what kind of stone or shell you were.

They are not tall, or rather, they rarely choose to be. To stand at full height in the open terraces is to invite the blind, leathery river of fliers to batter against your chest and face. So they move low, knuckles or palms grazing the wet limestone, their long-waisted bodies folding easily into the crouch of a climber. Their skin is cool, thick, and the color of wet chalk, mottled with grey-white patches where they have rubbed themselves with mineral paste from the pans to keep the stinging sulfur-slimes from taking root.

To a visitor’s eyes—if a visitor brought a lamp—they would look almost ghostly, traced in faint, cold blue-green light. They have no eyes themselves, only smooth, broad faces hollowed slightly like shallow dishes of bone beneath taut skin, and they do not know that the crawler-mucus they smear across their shoulders and joints glows in the dark. They use it because it softens their skin and masks their scent from the scavengers, turning them, by pure accident, into drifting constellations of pale fire whenever they cross the gloom.

They do not speak with their throats. In a valley where the air rings like a struck bell, a voice would be swallowed within a foot of the mouth. Instead, they carry language in their bones. When two of them meet, they press their foreheads or collarbones together and hum—a low, vibrating drone that passes straight from skull to skull, bypassing the roar of the cavern entirely. Across longer distances, they tap the limestone floor with short, weighted mallets carved from old grazer-shells, sending sharp, deliberate patterns through the rock that their broad feet can read twenty yards away.

They live in the acoustic shadows.

Over generations, they have learned to shape the valley’s noise the way people elsewhere shape light. They find natural alcoves beneath the overhangs and wall the mouths of them with curved baffles of porous travertine, guiding the ceiling drips with grooved stone channels so the mineral water slowly cements the blocks into place. Across the entrances, they hang curtains of transplanted calcareous pods—harvested while still soft and waxy—binding them together with dried sinew from the fliers. These curtains drink the high-frequency shriek of the open air. Step behind one, and the deafening wall of sound drops to a dull, distant surf. You can hear yourself breathe. You can hear the slow *plink* of water falling into a pan.

And you can hear the warning, if one of the eyeless hunters pads too close. The hunters’ padded toes make no sound on the wet rock, but they displace the damp air as they move, and a curtain of hung pods will answer a hunter’s passing with a sudden, heavy clatter—different in rhythm from the flutter of a flier’s wing.

They are neither masters of the valley nor merely its prey. They tend it, quietly and without sentiment.

In the shallow pans near their hollows, they farm the rust-colored biofilm, building low, circular dams of chalk-paste to trap the richest mineral drips. When the slime grows thick and greasy, they do not harvest it themselves; they take their shell-mallets and drum a slow, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* into the rock—a vibration that mimics the heavy scrape of a rival herd—and the broad, flat grazers come inching out of the dark in overlapping waves, rasping the pans clean. The dwellers follow behind them, prying loose the old, cracked plates of chitin the grazers shed as they grow, and gathering the fat, blind scavengers that swarm to the feast.

To cross the open terraces safely, where the hunters listen for any irregularity in the roar, the dwellers become part of the static. They carry hollow pods filled with dried, broken shell, shaking them in an unbroken, mechanical hiss that matches the grinding of ten thousand grazers. Or they wait for the Breath.

Every dozen hours, when the distant weather shifts miles above the earth and the cavern begins to inhale or exhale, the dwellers are already awake. They feel it before the hanging pods do—a faint, itching pressure in the tiny fluid-filled pits along their jaws and collarbones. They gather in the mouths of their alcoves, pressing their palms to the cooling rock as the slow draft begins to move.

When the upper roosts detach and millions of fliers pour into the open space in a roaring, leathery avalanche, the dwellers step out into the storm of wings. Under that total, deafening cover, even the hunters’ dish-shaped ears are overwhelmed, drowned in too many echoes at once. It is the time for trading between distant alcoves, for hauling heavy slabs of travertine, for finding mates from other hollows by the familiar, bone-deep hum of a stranger’s chest.

And when one of them grows too old to keep pace with the herds, or is caught in the open by a hunter’s concussive *crack*—a sound so violent it can rupture the delicate hollows of their inner ears and leave them forever deaf to the rock—they are not buried. There is nowhere to dig in solid limestone.

Instead, their kin carry them to the quietest, deepest mineral pans, far back under the low arches where the ceiling drips fastest. They lay the body in the ankle-deep, cloudy water, propped gently against the travertine rim, and leave it to the blind, thread-thin worms and the patient, mineral-laden drops. Within a season, the body is dusted in pale lime. Within a decade, it is ribbed and scalloped like the rest of the basin. Over centuries, it becomes simply another shelf of stone, holding the water, catching the drips, and turning the roar of the valley back into the dark.
