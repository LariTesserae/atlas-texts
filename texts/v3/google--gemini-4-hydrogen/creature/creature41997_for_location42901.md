They are built like the cliff-figs that share their walls: compact, deep-chested, and slow to change.

If you were to climb the sheer, baking granite from the unseen plains below and peer over the lip of the bowl, you might not notice the people at first. In the perpetual smoked-amber twilight, their skin—weathered by generations of dry wind and lightly oiled with goat-tallow against the desiccating heat—matches the ochre lichen and the sun-baked clay plaster of their homes. They wear loose, sleeveless tunics of unbleached hemp and woven goat-hair, dyed only with the muted slate of crushed stone or the rust of caper-root. Their feet are bare more often than not, or shod in simple hemp soles; either way, the skin of their heels has thickened into pale, cracked horn, gripping the soapstone-smooth stairways with an effortless, unthinking certainty.

They are a people shaped by the conservation of two things: breath and water.

In the thin, warm air of the summit, nobody hurries. To run is to pant, and to pant is to spill precious moisture out into the dry sky. Even the children learn early to keep their mouths closed, breathing slowly through flared nostrils, their broad ribcages rising and falling in a deep, unhurried cadence. Gestures are kept close to the body. When two people meet on a stairway barely wide enough for a single pair of shoulders, neither stops and neither speaks; they execute a practiced, fluid turn, sliding past one another like water over pebbles, barely brushing tunics.

Their eyes are striking—wide, dark, and adapted to the dimness. Because direct sunlight never touches the floor of the bowl, their irises have grown large to drink the tallow-lamp glow and the pewter light filtering through the high dust. When someone must climb out onto the exposed lip of the domes to tend the mist-nets or gather lichen, they tie narrow visors of carved goat-bone over their faces, peering through razor-thin horizontal slits to save their sight from the blinding white glare of the plains miles below.

This boundary—the lip of the chasm—is where their day truly begins. In the hour before dawn, when the thermal updrafts rising from the distant valleys cool just enough to ghost into invisible vapor, the mist-tenders crawl out onto the lashed-timber catwalks that overhang the void. They move with an agonizing, reverent gentleness. Unrolling the fine, dark webs of woven goat-hair, they stretch them across the throat of the updraft and wait in absolute stillness. As the nets darken and grow heavy with microscopic beads of dew, the tenders draw them in hand over hand, wringing the hair over wide, shallow stone troughs. The yield is measured in thimbles, funneled into narrow-necked clay amphorae and immediately stoppered with stone plugs sealed in fig-gum. 

Water is never gulped here. It is sipped from tiny bone cups, held against the palate to wet the tongue before it is swallowed. Most of the bowl’s thirst is quenched in other ways: by chewing the tart, fleshy leaves of the silver-jade succulents, or by drinking the thick, salty milk of the nimble goats that clatter across the stone roofs. 

The goats are the town’s shadow-kin, treated with a familiarity bordering on reverence. Their copper bells provide the constant, bright punctuation to the bowl’s roar, and their hair is more valuable than any metal. They browse on the thorny caper-bushes and the tough, waxy leaves of the figs, turning dry scrub into cheese that can be cured on the warm lintels until it is hard as sandstone and keeps for years. Beside them, the rock-hyraxes are tolerated as noisy neighbors, though a fat one snared in a noose of twisted air-plant fiber means a rare, celebratory stew, thickened with the ground flour of roasted roots and bitter, leached fig-nuts.

To live in the bowl is to live inside a drum. The curved granite walls catch the whirring of ten thousand cliff-swallows, the dry rasp of cicadas, the clatter of hooves, and the *thock-thock* of the wooden looms, bouncing the sound until the air itself seems to vibrate. A shouted word here becomes an unreadable smear of echoes. 

Because of this, the people speak in two distinct registers. Indoors, in the dim, dry-stone rooms lit by tallow wicks, they use a low, chesty murmur pitched beneath the chatter of the birds—a soft, gravelly hum meant to be heard from a hand’s-breadth away, or felt as a vibration against the ribs when embracing. Across the open chasms, however, from catwalk to tiered roof, they do not shout. Instead, they use a sharp, percussive language of tongue-clicks, finger-snaps, and short, high whistles borrowed from the swallows—sounds narrow and bright enough to pierce the ambient din without straining a single lung.

Their social life is as tightly wedged and interlocked as their mortarless walls. There are no grand plazas, no solitary estates; privacy is found not in distance, which does not exist, but in the sheer wall of noise that wraps every doorway, and in a strict, unspoken etiquette of looking away. Authority belongs not to warriors—there is no iron to forge weapons, and nowhere to march—but to the *Masons*, who read the subtle settling of the dry stone, and the *Tally-Keepers*, who track the clay amphorae and the weight of the seed-stores with notched bone rods. 

They measure wealth in patience and repair. A cracked lintel wedged tight with a sliver of slate, a hemp bridge re-lashed before a strand parts, a fig-root coaxed around a doorway without snapping—these are the acts that earn quiet respect. Nothing is thrown away, because nothing can be replaced from outside.

Even the dead remain part of the town’s dense crust. With no soil to dig and no wood to spare for pyres, the departed are wrapped in mats of dry air-plant fiber and carried up the highest switchbacks to the ancient honeycomb hollows in the overhanging rock. There, out of reach of the mist and the birds, the baking warmth of the granite turns them to light, odorless parchment within a single season. After a generation, when space in the hollows is needed for the newly dead, the bones are brought back down. With sandstone files and endless, unhurried care, the long bones are shaped into loom-shuttles, hairpins, and the fine needles used to mend the mist-nets. To weave a tunic with a grandmother’s femur, or to draw a goat-hair net taut with an uncle’s rib, is neither morbid nor sacred to them; it is simply how the bowl holds itself together.

At dusk, when the smoked-amber light deepens toward dark pewter and the tallow lamps flare in their recessed niches, the town settles. The looms fall quiet. The *grind-grind* of the bedrock mortars ceases. People sit on the warm, lichen-crusted roofs with their backs against the living rock, chewing pickled caper-buds and listening to the swallows settle into the clay eaves. Beneath them, the evening thermal begins its slow rise from the unseen world below, carrying the faint, phantom scent of distant rain that will never fall here. They breathe it in—slowly, economically—as much a part of the mountain’s oldest scar as the stone itself.
