In the relentless, echoing roar of the hollow, there are holes. 

Not holes in the ancient stone, and not pauses in the chorus of croaks and ceiling-drips, but dead spots in the sound itself. If you could map the cathedral by the frantic clicks of the blind fliers, you would trace the bulbous curves of the walls, the hanging tapestries of wet tendrils, the thickets of hollow reeds—and here and there, clinging to a high shelf or standing ankle-deep in a warm marsh-pool, you would find a blank space. A heavy, crouching shape where the sound waves go in and simply do not come back.

That is where the dwellers sit.

There are never more than a dozen of them in the hollow at any time, a slow, long-lived lineage that has inhabited the chamber for so many generations that their bodies have forgotten the concept of an outside, of a sky, or of an eye. 

To the touch, one of them is vast and strangely soft—roughly the size of a kneeling ox, with four heavy limbs built for gripping slick, mineral-crusted rock. Their forelimbs are long and thick-wristed, ending in broad, flat pads; their hind legs are short, pillar-like anchors meant for bracing in the squelching moss. Their heads are neckless and broad, dominated by a wide, scalloped dish of pale, slate-colored cartilage and pleated flesh that cups forward like an inverted bell. There are no sockets, no vestigial ridges where eyes might once have been—only smooth, unbroken skin, veined with thousands of fine, trembling nerves. Beneath that great listening dish, tucked close to the heavy chest, sits a wide, lipless mouth.

If a flier’s sonar strikes one of them, no echo returns, because they wear the room itself. Their broad backs, shoulders, and heavy forearms are carpeted in a living, cultivated pelt of the pale, rubbery moss that blankets the floor, tangled with short, damp, fibrous tendrils. They tend these coats carefully, scooping up mineral-rich silt from the warm puddles and patting it into their own flanks so the moss stays lush, waterlogged, and thick. 

In a world where everything shouts to see, an object that swallows sound is invisible. To the fliers sweeping through the central void, a dweller looks like nothing at all—or worse, like a patch of distant, empty air. 

They hunt with the patience of dripping water. A dweller will crouch motionless on a high stone shelf for days, its mossy back indistinguishable from the ledge, its broad sensory dish tilted toward the void. When a blind flier, fooled by the acoustic blankness, swoops straight toward what it believes is open air, the dweller’s long forelimbs rise—soft as wet wool, silent as mist—and close around it. There is no shriek, no snap of bone; the thick, waterlogged moss muffles the struggle until it stops. 

Yet they are not merely ambushers. They are the quiet gardeners of the dark, moving at the glacial pace of the chamber itself. They wade through the warm marshes, peeling the waxy, hollow reed-stalks with blunt claws to eat the sweet, mineral-heavy pith at the core. They gather the hard-shelled grazers from the ledges, holding them deep inside their padded chests to crush the shells so the crack will not disturb the room’s acoustics. They prune the hanging curtains of tendrils when they grow too thick and begin to block the air currents, tearing them away slowly, fiber by fiber.

They never make a sound in the air. They have no need to click or whistle; instead, they drink the noise that is already there. Every *plok* of a falling drop, every croak from a throat-sac, every frantic salvo from the fliers washes into their broad, scalloped dishes. By reading the tiny delays and warps in those borrowed echoes, they build the entire cathedral inside their minds—not in color or light, but in pressure, texture, and distance. They know when a reed-stalk three hundred paces away is heavy enough with condensation to snap. They know when a marsh-pool has risen by the width of a finger.

When they speak to one another, they do not use the air at all. They use the mountain.

A dweller wishing to greet another will press its broad, flat sternum against a wet stone ledge, or sink a splayed foot into the mud of a marsh-rim, and flex a set of deep, internal muscles. It sends a slow, sub-audible thrum into the rock—a pulse too low for the fliers or the amphibians to hear. Fifty paces away, across the echoing dark, another dweller feels the tremor rise through its ankles and settle into its jawbone. Their conversations are unhurried, a single exchange of weight and rhythm stretched out over an hour, punctuated by the steady fall of water from the ceiling.

Because they live in this world of bone-conducted vibration, the dwellers know the chamber’s five or six ancient artifacts better than anything else in the hollow. To them, those incomprehensible structures are not silent relics; they are the loudest presences in the world.

Near the eastern wall, where the three slender, curved ribs of dark matter stand half-embedded in the weeping stone, the dwellers gather to rest. They lie with their heavy bellies pressed directly against the rock, letting the ribs’ deep, sub-audible hum align their own slow pulses. Sometimes, one of them will sit beside a nearby puddle and trail its blunt fingers across the surface, feeling the crisp, geometric ripples that the machine’s vibration carves into the water—tracing the standing waves over and over, as if reading a story whose meaning has long since worn smooth.

And they know the seamless polyhedron suspended thirty feet above the center of the void. They cannot touch it, and they do not try; they know the air around its hull thickens into something like warm syrup. Instead, they sit on the opposite ledges and *listen* to it. Because the air around the object is strange, the fliers’ clicks bend lazily as they pass it, arriving at the dwellers’ sensory dishes a fraction of a second late, softened and warped. To the listeners in the dark, the polyhedron is a warm, singing lens hanging in the middle of the room—the one place where the world’s noise curves like water around a boulder.

They live for decades, perhaps centuries, measuring time by nothing at all—until the room tells them it is time to wait.

Days before it happens, the dwellers feel a subtle tightening in the hum of the eastern ribs, a microscopic shift in the geometry of the puddles. One by one, they pull themselves out of the warm marshes, leaving the croaking frogs and rasping grazers behind, and climb slowly onto the highest, broadest shelves of the curved walls. They tuck their heavy limbs beneath them, tilt their great, pale dishes toward the center of the void, and become entirely still.

When the suspended polyhedron finally shifts its position by the width of a finger, the deep chime strikes the air. 

The fliers scatter in blind panic, clicking wildly as a sudden shower of trapped droplets shakes loose from the ceiling. The dwellers do not flinch. They open their wide, lipless mouths just a fraction, letting the great, ringing note pass into their chests, their skulls, their mossy backs. For a few seconds, the whole cathedral is unified by a single, pure tone—the only event in a world without seasons, the closest thing they will ever know to a sunrise.

And when the chime fades back into the steady *plok* of water and the endless, blind roar of the dark, the dwellers slowly uncurl, slide back down into the warm mud, and return to their quiet tending.
