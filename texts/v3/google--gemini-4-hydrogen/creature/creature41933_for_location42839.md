At first, the valley feels empty of anything larger than an amphibian. You can walk a kilometer along a sunken transit trench and hear nothing but the steady *plip* of condensation falling from the ferns into mirror-still pools, and feel nothing but the faint, static prickle of the cantilevered plazas overhead.

Then you notice the moss.

Across the wide, unbuilt terraces of raw basalt, the pale, spongy mats do not grow entirely unchecked. Here and there, they are pressed flat in long, narrow ovals—spaced too far apart for a human stride, too deliberate for a falling stone. Along the razor-edged shelves, the sharp glassy ridges have been worn smooth in a few precise places, not by centuries of weather, but by the repeated, patient passage of something heavy and sure-footed.

They are easy to miss in the bruised twilight, because they carry the color of the valley itself.

When one steps out from behind a bismuth-toned monolith, it looks at first like a tall, narrow shadow detached from the wall. They stand a head or two taller than a human, built with an unhurried, articulated grace—long-limbed and lean, with double-hinged knees and ankles that fold neatly when they crouch to navigate the fractured rock. Their skin is bare, matte-dark as wet slate and bruised plum, though the palms of their hands, the ridges of their shoulders, and the broad, cushioned pads of their feet are pale and mineral-hard, like the bismuth plates of the towers.

Like the towers, their bodies run slightly colder than the warm, heavy mist. Wherever they stand still for more than a few moments, moisture beads along their collarbones and forearms, gathering in fine, natural channels along their skin until it drips silently from their elbows and long fingers. They smell faintly of crushed fern and metallic ozone.

They have no use for airborne sound. Their throats are smooth, unbroken columns; they possess no ears in the way a creature of the open air would recognize. Instead, the sides of their narrow, crestless skulls are lined with pale, recessed membranes that ripple faintly when the air pressure shifts, and their wide, milky eyes—set deep beneath heavy brow ridges—catch the pearlescent glow of buried conduits the way a night-animal’s eyes catch firelight.

What you feel in the soles of your feet and the fillings of your teeth is them speaking.

A single individual will pause at the edge of a viaduct, plant one broad, pale heel firmly against the carbon lattice, and go completely still. Deep inside its torso, a dense, resonant organ—half-bone, half-mineral—strikes against itself. No sound escapes into the mist. Instead, a clean, sub-audible pulse travels down through the leg, into the viaduct, and out across kilometers of freshly split bedrock.

This is why the city is laid out in such vast, sparse strokes. To crowd together would be to live inside a deafening, muddy roar of overlapping tremors. Each architectural cluster belongs to one, or at most a pair, of them—separated by kilometers of open, unbuilt stone so that when a neighbor sends a pulse across the basin, it arrives attenuated and distinct, a single clear sentence vibrating up through the soles of the feet.

They do not live *inside* the towers in the way humans inhabit rooms. They move through the city as if it were a landscape of caves and ridges rather than furniture. Their wide, pale eyes seem untroubled by the walls that are out of phase with the light; where a stranger sees only an oily shimmer and the dim hills behind it, they see the true geometry of the barrier, resting their cool palms against the solid air as they climb. When a high facade folds inward like dark origami to vent a plume of warm, dry vapor, two or three of them will sometimes gather on the nearest cantilevered plaza—not to speak, for the venting is too loud in the stone, but simply to stand in the dry, warm draft and let the perpetual dampness evaporate from their skin.

Between those rare gatherings, their days—measured not by a sun, which never breaks the geometric veil overhead, but by the slow, irregular rhythms of the conduits—are spent in quiet maintenance of themselves and their margins.

They are not farmers, and they do not weed the jungle back. They seem to regard the surging, shallow-rooted greenery with the same patient indifference they give the mist. Yet they are woven into it. They kneel beside the razor-lipped drainage channels to drink the mineral-heavy water from the basalt crevices, ignoring the pale, translucent amphibians that cling inches from their fingers. They gather the thick, spore-swollen caps of the pale moss and strip the velvety, bruised-purple leaves for their bitter, salty sap. Sometimes, when a tower’s buried conduits brighten and the canopy releases a lazy cloud of silver spores, you can see one of them standing in the middle of an empty plaza with its long arms spread, letting the spores settle on its wet skin—absorbing something from the dust, or simply letting the drifting silver map the thermal currents around it.

Smaller things orbit their quietness.

In the shadowed undersides of the floating plazas, where the static pressure of the braided gravity is thick enough to make the hair on a human arm stand on end, flat, disc-shaped creatures no larger than a dinner plate cling to the bismuth plates. They have no eyes and no visible mouths, only a fringe of pale, feathery cilia that comb the upward-drifting mist for silver spores and tiny, blind midges. They never fall, anchored to the underside of the stone by the same invisible tension that holds the plaza aloft.

Down in the shallow, mirror-still pools trapped in the fresh crevices, hair-thin glass-worms weave through the water, feeding on the dead moss that washes down from the viaducts. High above them, the slender, six-limbed gliders slip from vine-curtain to vine-curtain, occasionally pausing on the shoulder of a motionless inhabitant as if mistaking the cold, dripping figure for another outcrop of basalt.

The inhabitants rarely brush them away. They stand on the high terraces in the bruised twilight, weeping condensation into the moss, sending their slow, silent tremors through the young stone—patient as the city itself, waiting for the distant pulse of an answer from three kilometers away.
