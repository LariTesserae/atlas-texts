At first, you would think the city is abandoned, or that it was built solely as a monument to the absence of things. You could drift through the wide, black volumes between the towers for hours and see nothing move except the occasional translucent ribbon, pale as a sliver of onion skin, undulating in slow motion along a plaza’s edge.

It takes time for the eye to separate the inhabitants from the architecture, because they are made of the same restraint.

What you took for a moored polyhedron—one of those hollow, skeletal frames hanging in the dark beside a cantilevered plane—is not anchored to the basalt. There is no filament holding it. If you watch it for ten minutes, or twenty, you realize it has rotated by the width of a fingernail. Its edges are razor-clean and matte, drinking what little bruised-violet light reaches it from the joints of the nearest tower. Inside its open frame, the black water passes straight through without a ripple.

They do not have faces, or limbs in the way a swimmer has limbs. They are assemblies of thin, light-drinking planes and long, articulated rods, no thicker than slate tiles and fine as calipers, held together by tensions you cannot see. Some are tall and narrow, like folded screens drifting vertically through the shafts; others are wide, geometric constellations of dark shards arranged around a single, hollow center. Where a joint meets—where two matte planes hinge at an impossible, paper-thin angle—there sits a single pinprick of pale slate-blue or violet, cold and unblinking, identical to the lights tucked into the masonry.

They do not swim. To kick a fin or sweep an arm here would be a vulgarity, a tear in the quiet obedience of the water. Instead, they *reconfigure*. A creature hanging above a terraced plaza will subtly shift the angle of its outer planes, altering its own displacement by a fraction of a millimeter, and the damped, heavy water simply yields to it. It glides across thirty meters of open dark over the course of an hour, leaving no wake, disturbing no stillness, arriving at a causeway with the silent finality of a needle settling on a scale.

They are the reason the water obeys. The city’s geometry is not a machine running in the dark; it is an extension of their own stillness, a latticework built to hold the abyssal currents at bay. Each inhabitant carries a small sphere of quiet around itself, and where their spheres overlap, the water locks into that steady, cool equilibrium.

If you stay long enough, you begin to understand the sounds.

That single, impossibly crisp *tick* that seemed to come from deep within a structural joint was not the stone settling. It was a greeting, or a correction. When two of them hang in the empty water on opposite sides of a plaza—never coming too close, always preserving a wide buffer of untouched dark between them—one will lock a joint or snap a hair-fine internal tendon. The sound does not echo; it travels straight and clean through the dustless water, strikes the other’s matte planes, and vanishes. 

Minutes later, or perhaps the next day, the other replies: a low, sub-audible vibration that you feel in the hollow of your chest rather than your ears. It is a heavy, deliberate thrum, tuned to the resonant frequency of the spire’s young basalt. Together, across the vast, empty spaces of the precinct, they carry on conversations made almost entirely of silence, where a single *tick* is a syllable and an hour of motionless suspension is the grammar that gives it weight.

On the lower terraces, where the basalt is sharpest and most sterile, they sometimes descend to dock. They do not rest on their sides; they lower their razor-thin edges onto the unweathered stone, fitting themselves into the conchoidal fractures with a precision that leaves no gap. There, they remain for weeks, completely indistinguishable from the cantilevered walls, their violet nodes dimmed to near-extinction.

It is during these long pauses that the blind, quartz-shelled things creep out from the overhangs. They cluster around the docked forms just as they cluster around the buildings, pressing their frosted shells against the matte planes, drinking whatever faint, cold leak of energy seeps from the joints. The inhabitants do not shoo them away, nor do they seem to notice them. To beings that measure time by the cooling of volcanic glass, a creature that takes three days to cross a stone is simply part of the texture of the dark.

What they are doing here—why they chose this freshly cracked spire, kilometers below the world, before the first film of bacteria could even settle on the rock—is impossible to tell from watching them. Perhaps they are listening to the immense weight of the ocean pressing down from above, reading the microscopic shifts of the planet’s crust through the soles of their docked planes. Perhaps they arrived the very moment the basalt split open, drawn by the rarity of a clean, unbothered wound in the deep, and built this sparse city simply to keep the dark around it neat.

Sometimes, one will detach from a high shaft and drift out to the very rim of the precinct, where the manicured stillness ends and the raw, chaotic currents of the open abyss begin to tug at the rock. It will hang there at the threshold, half in the quiet, half in the cold, unruly dark, its matte planes vibrating almost imperceptibly against the flow—measuring the outside world, testing its weight, before gliding smoothly back into the amber pause of the city.
