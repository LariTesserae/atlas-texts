In the long spaces between the bruised-violet pools of the mercury-vapor lamps, where the mist hangs thick enough to swallow the catwalks, something moves that knows the basin better than the pumps do. Not fast, and not often.

If you caught one in the brief, sizzling blue flash of a snapping relay, you would mistake it for a heap of wet slag or a particularly thick crust of lime-scale sloughed off a pipe joint. They are low, heavy-shouldered creatures, roughly the size of a coiled cable-drum, built to hug the floor and resist the current. Their hides are the exact slick, featureless gray of the poured-basalt tiles, kept perpetually wet by the dripping ceiling. Along their spines and broad shoulders, they wear thick, jagged plates—not quite shell, not quite bone, but a porous, calcified armor built up over seasons of pressing themselves against the leaking pipe joints, letting the salt and lime crystallize directly onto their backs until their silhouettes have the same sharp, unweathered edges as the mineral crusts on the walls.

They have no use for eyes in an eight-mile dark. Where a face might be, there is only a blunt, flattened wedge of pale tissue, pitted with shallow hollows that drink in vibration and temperature. They see the basin by its *thrum*. Through the broad, calloused pads of their feet, they read the deep turbine-hum rising out of the floor, feeling the subtle drop in pitch when a distant pump kicks on, or the tiny, frantic tremor of a sump-crab clattering across an iron grate fifty yards away. Beneath their jaw-plates, sensitive, translucent barbels twitch at the electrical weather—the heavy, ozone-sharp prickle radiating from the three-story transformer tower, and the faint, steady warmth of the amber pilot lights on the control cabinets.

In a place hovering just above freezing, heat is the only true currency. Their bodies are too slow and dense to generate enough of their own to stay active for long in the open flats, so they live by a strict thermal rhythm, orbiting the basin’s few warm organs. Between the floods, you can find them wedged into the sunken trench around the six massive heat-exchangers, their wet gray bellies pressed flat against the warm steel of the silos, soaking up the radiated heat while condensation patters onto their salted backs. Others climb the transformer tower—ignoring the small, leathery-winged things roosting in silence behind the dark baffles above—and drape themselves across the porcelain insulators and warm oil-casings, carefully keeping their damp limbs clear of the live relays.

They know when the tide is coming long before the two-tone klaxons groan. Minutes before the eastern sluice-gates grind upward, the pressure in the upper headers shifts, and a faint, high-frequency chatter runs down the vertical concrete chutes. The dwellers peel themselves off the warm steel. 

Some climb the concrete pilings just above the waterline, settling into the velvety mats of blue-white lichen, where they look like nothing more than extra buttresses cast into the pillars. Others—the larger, heavier ones—wade out into the ankle-deep runoff of the broad causeways and lock the dark, horn-like hooks of their feet into the iron floor-grating, turning their blunt heads into the east. When the cold gray brine surges out across the terraces in a wide, foaming sheet, it washes right over their calcified backs. They hold fast against the twenty-minute river, opening slatted gill-rakers along their flanks to sift the churning water, or snatching the stunned, blind amphipods and torn ribbons of translucent kelp swept out of the upper pipes.

After the gates slam shut and the exhaust fans pull the mist clear, they go to work on the margins. They pry the flat-shelled sump-crabs off the grates with a sharp *crack* that rings off the tiled walls. They haul themselves onto the undersides of the condensation trays to crop the rubbery kelp-ribbons, always leaving the bases intact to grow back in the draft of the cooling fans.

Most strangely, they tend the motor housings. Tucked beneath their heavy climbing-pads are smaller, jointed inner digits—pale and surprisingly nimble. With these, they reach inside the warm, grease-slicked casings of the electric motors and harvest the brittle, orange-brown clusters of iron-eating bacterial coral. They eat the softest, freshest buds and carefully scrape the dead, crusty scale away from the vents and intake screens, keeping the metal bright. Whether they do this because unobstructed motors run hotter, or simply because they cannot stand the off-beat rattle of a choked fan vibrating through the floor, is impossible to say.

They are solitary by habit, spaced across the vast, empty flats much like the lamp stanchions themselves, each holding a loose territory centered on a heat source and a stretch of drainage channel. Yet they are not wholly out of touch. Over the foundational roar of the blowers and the rhythmic *clack-hiss* of the pneumatic valves, a sharp, deliberate *tink-tink... tink* will sometimes ring out—the sound of a calcified knuckle striking an iron catwalk or an overhead pipe-bundle. Because the concrete is crisp and bare, the sound does not soften; it bounces off the retaining walls and travels for half a mile before dissolving back into the industrial weather. Another, far off in the wet shadow, will answer with a double tap on a yellow safety bollard.

It is a sparse, unhurried conversation, conducted in the only language the basin understands: a checking of positions, a warning of a flooded sump, or simply an acknowledgment that the tide has passed and the warm silos are still humming.
