At first, you take them for part of the masonry.

In the brief, pale-green flicker of a disturbed grazer, a shape near the base of a root-arch looks like nothing more than a thicker knot of kelp-timber, or a boulder of compacted turf that the current has rounded over centuries. Even if you brush against one in the dark, your hand meets something cold, heavy, and yielding-yet-dense—like wet, cured hide stretched over thick bone. It does not flinch. It does not dart away.

Only if you keep your palm there for a long moment do you feel it: a slow, profound thrum, slower than a human heartbeat, timed not to blood but to the long, rolling bass notes of the bellows-swimmers passing overhead.

They are still here, the ones who braided this valley. They never left; they simply finished, and then they settled into the same cold, unhurried stasis as the walls they grew.

To see one clearly, you have to wait for a swimmer to drift close, trailing its string of dim blue sparks. In that faint, passing wash of light, the shape separates itself from the bruised-plum turf. They are heavy-bodied and long-limbed, built with the dense, mineral-weighted bone of creatures meant to walk and kneel on the bottom rather than fight the water’s lift. Their skin is the color of slate and old driftwood, slick and cold, mottled with pale scars where ancient kelp-spines once bit into them, and dusted here and there with the same microscopic film that coats the viaducts.

They have no eyes. Where eyes would be, the skin of the brow is smooth, unbroken, and slightly concave, curving down into broad, flat cheekbones. In a place where the sky is only a bruised rumor miles overhead, sight was the first thing the deep water took from them—or the first thing they gave up. Instead, their heads and necks are scored with deep, fluted grooves, fringed with fine, fleshy filaments that tremble invisibly in the current. Along their ribs and down the undersides of their long forearms run twin rows of pale, recessed pits, sensitive enough to read the slightest shift in water pressure.

They do not need to make sound to find their way. The valley is already deafening. The ceaseless *shhhk-shhhk* of the grazers, the dry *clack-clack* of the snapping arches, the organ-pipe booming of the swimmers—all of it floods the basin like daylight. The builders move through a world lit entirely by noise. Every spire, every hollow causeway, every drifting herd casts an acoustic shadow, and they read those shadows across their skin as effortlessly as a surface creature reads sunlight on stone.

You notice, after a while, how the grazers treat them.

A fist-sized, pale-shelled grazer will crawl straight off a root-pillar onto a builder’s shoulder, rasping methodically across the slate-grey hide, cropping the thin film of algae and bacteria that settles there during years of stillness. The builder does not brush it away. That gentle, wet scraping against their collarbone is company, and hygiene, and one more small voice keeping the valley’s chord intact. Only when a grazer wanders too close to the sensitive acoustic grooves along the jaw does a long, spatulate hand rise—slow as a drifting frond—to lift the creature by its shell and set it carefully back onto the kelp-wall.

Their hands are extraordinary: broad-palmed, double-jointed at the wrist, with long, calloused fingers flattened at the tips like bone spatulas. There are no weapons in them, and no tools. These are the limbs that coaxed living holdfasts into mountain rock, that held young, springy kelp-timbers bent against the current for months at a time until the wood lignified and remembered the curve. When they move now, their fingers still curl and press against the arches as they pass, testing the tension of a thousand-year-old graft, feeling for the hairline vibration that would mean a hollow beam is beginning to split.

They are few—far fewer than the structures would suggest. Perhaps they were always few, and the vast, town-sized expanse of plazas and colonnades was never meant to be crowded. A single builder can claim a whole terraced ridge, spending decades in a kind of waking torpor half-sunk in the springy turf, listening to the valley turn.

When they do move, it is with a heavy, deliberate grace. You see one rise from a meadow of dark-olive moss, stirring no silt—there is no silt here—and stride slowly toward a sweeping viaduct. It does not swim; it walks the bottom in long, buoyant, leaning steps, leaning forward against the slow current, its long arms trailing to brush the turf for balance. Every few paces, it pauses to tilt its eyeless head toward the open water as a bellows-swimmer pulses past, letting the low bass note wash through the hollow resonance chambers of its own chest.

They still tend the valley, though their work is no longer creation. It is subtraction.

In a place locked in finished peace, the only threat is clutter—anything that might choke the open water or dull the valley’s voice. If a great bellows-swimmer finally dies of cold and age and sinks onto a plaza, two or three builders will gather out of the gloom, converging from distant ridges without a single spoken word. Together, with slow, immense patience, they will heave the pale, deflated bulk across the velvety turf to the edge of the cordillera, and tip it silently over the sheer ledge into the mile-deep blackness below, where the scavengers of the true floor wait. If a rogue shoot of eelgrass tries to sprout across a tuned archway, they will pinch it off with thumb and forefinger and feed it to the grazers.

Otherwise, they eat little—cracking an occasional grazer against a buttress, adding one sharp *snap* to the clatter of the arches, or opening their wide, toothless mouths to strain the thin, cold drift of larvae that rises from the abyssal drop-offs. Their blood runs nearly as cold as the mountain water itself; a single meal can last a season that does not exist.

And sometimes, rarely, they gather on the monumental open-air plazas—five or six of them, spaced widely apart in the dark, facing different directions. They do not speak, and they do not touch. Instead, they raise their broad forearms or lean their flanks against the porous, chattering walls of the surrounding arches, subtly shifting their weight, blocking a cavity here, opening a gap there.

For an hour, or a day, they alter the shape of the roar. They damp the dry clacking of the segmented things so the low booming of the swimmers swells to fill the basin; then they step back, letting the high, wet scraping of the walls rush back in. It is not music in any sense a surface ear would recognize—there is no melody, no beginning, no end. It is more like a blind man running his hands over the faces of his family, just to be sure they are all still there, and all still breathing.

Then, one by one, they lower themselves back onto the thick, leather-like turf, tuck their long limbs close to their ribs, and let the grazers climb back onto their shoulders. The valley settles. The thunderous, unchanging hum closes over them once more, and they wait in the cold dark, indistinguishable from the bones of the world they grew.
