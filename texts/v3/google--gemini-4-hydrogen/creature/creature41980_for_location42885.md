If you stay crouched at the rim long enough for your eyes to adjust to the glare off the silica plates, you realize that the pocket is not merely full—it is *negotiated*. Nothing here has room to waste on territory or display. Every creature lives at the exact tolerance of its neighbor’s shell.

Take the *galvanic chitons*. From above, they look like a static mosaic, a tiled bathroom floor laid by a madman over stone and iron. Watch them through two or three cycles of the thermal relay, though, and you see they are in constant, glacial motion. The oldest ones—thick as roof slates, their milky plates scarred by years of warm, mineral-heavy drips—are anchored directly to the copper busbars where the current is sweetest. Younger chitons, no larger than thumbnails and nearly as clear as mica, are forced out to the margins, clinging to the sharp obsidian-slate fins where the voltage is barely a tingle. When the relay inside the rusted junction box gives its polite *click*, the brief drop in current makes the whole wall of chitons exhale. For a quarter of a second, their muscular feet relax; the entire mosaic slumps downward by the width of a hair, and in that tiny slackening, a younger chiton at the edge wedges its front plate a fraction of a millimeter closer to the copper. Then the current steadies, the feet grip, and the *shhhk* of settling silica dies away.

In the dark, greasy alleys behind the chitons, the *oil-skimmers* live by touch and tension. They have no use for the daylight pouring down from above; their world is a three-dimensional web of amber silk strung across the terminals and around the slow-turning worm-gear of the peristaltic pump. Each skimmer carries a memory of the pocket in the vibration of its brass-wire legs. Through the silk, they feel the sixty-cycle hum of the transformer, the sluggish pulse of the pump, and the sudden, sharp *tink* of a water drop hitting a glass sight-gauge—a signal that a fresh bead of oil-slicked condensation is about to roll down the brass fittings. They are meticulous, anxious housekeepers. If a thread of silk snaps under the weight of a falling drop, two or three skimmers converge within seconds to spin a replacement across the wet copper, grooming the live metal with their mouthparts as if soothing an irritable beast.

Even the spaces that look sealed are inhabited. Inside the three glass sight-gauges—streaked green with verdigris and warmed by the weeping brass—live swarms of thread-thin, translucent *gauge-worms*, no thicker than horsehair. They never leave the glass tubes. Suspended in the warm, copper-tinted water, they graze on the microscopic film of rust and algae coating the inside of the glass, riding the tiny bubbles that rise whenever the manifold’s pressure shifts. From the outside, you only notice them when the noon sun strikes the gauges at a steep angle and turns the water briefly silver with their thrashing.

And beneath the chitons, in the flat, airless seams between their feet and the rock, live the cleaners. When an old chiton finally loses its grip on the current—its foot drying to leather, its plates turning chalky and opaque—the pocket cannot afford a corpse. Within hours, paper-flat, grease-pale scavengers slip under the dead shell. They work in silence, dissolving the organic glue that binds the articulated silica plates together. Once the plates loosen, they drop into the narrow trough at the bottom of the cleft, where the three iridescent puddles lie.

Down there, the *bladder-slugs* do not mind the falling plates. They lie packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the mineral broth, pulsing in slow, out-of-phase rhythms as they siphon water through their translucent skins, concentrating dissolved salts and heavy metals into cloudy pearls inside their bellies. Above them, the pair of *caliper-crabs* hang upside down from the manifold’s iron ribs, motionless for hours at a stretch. They are patient, surgical things. Their stalkless pinprick eyes are fixed on the sheen of the slugs below; they are waiting for the telltale stretching of a membrane, the moment a slug’s skin thins enough to show the pale, metallic pearl shifting inside. When one crab drops, snips its neat crescent of tissue, and scrambles back up the pipe, the wounded slug simply contracts, seals the cut with a bead of iridescent mucus, and resumes pulsing. It will heal before the crab is hungry again.

Yet all of these—the chitons, the skimmers, the crabs, the slugs—are only the visible layer, the day-shift of the cleft.

Wedged into the tightest, darkest void of all—the pinched triangle of space behind the step-down transformer, where the stone ribs press so close to the oil-stained casing that no daylight ever reaches—lives something that belongs to neither the machinery nor the arthropods.

It is a creature built for crevices: a long, flattened, rubber-skinned amphibian, mottled the exact dull grey-green of oxidized copper and wet basalt. Its ribs are soft, almost cartilaginous, allowing it to fold its body to the thickness of a leather belt and slide through gaps a beetle would hesitate to enter. For most of the bright, hot day, it does not move at all. It lies coiled against the back of the transformer’s ribbed housing, soaking the steady sixty-cycle warmth straight into its belly, its wide, lidless eyes milky and half-shut against the glare bouncing off the slate. Its skin never dries; the perpetual sweat of the pocket beads along its spine, and the rust-colored moss that clings to the brass valves has taken root in the shallow creases behind its shoulders, giving it a faint, copper-furred ruff.

It only uncurls when the sun drops below the rim of the salt pan and the dry plain above begins to freeze.

In the sudden, sharp chill of dusk, the warm cleft breathes out a thin plume of vapor, and the creature slides forward along the underside of the manifold. It moves like thick oil poured over stone, silent and unhurried, its splayed, suction-padded toes finding purchase on the wet silica backs of the chitons without disturbing them. The oil-skimmers scatter up their silk lines at its approach, recognizing the heavy, non-electrical tremor of its weight.

It does not hunt the chitons—their armor is too thick—and it leaves the stringy skimmers alone. Instead, it hangs its broad, flat head down over the three puddles, just beside the motionless caliper-crabs. It waits for the crabs to molt. When one of the crabs finally splits its old shell and backs out soft, pale, and helpless into the damp air, the creature’s throat pulses once, twice, and it takes the crab cleanly from the iron pipe, leaving the empty, paper-light husk to drift down into the broth.

Then, before the first cold draft can stiffen its limbs, it folds itself back into the pinched shadow behind the transformer, wraps its tail once around the wrist-thick rubber cable where it enters the borehole, and settles in to listen to the hum—waiting out the night, and the forty-second click, in the only room in the world that fits it.
