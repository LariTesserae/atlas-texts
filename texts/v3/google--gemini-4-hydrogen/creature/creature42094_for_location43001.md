Those who live in the basin are shaped by two things above all else: the sharpness of the stone and the narrowness of the air.

You notice them first by the way they move. In lanes no wider than a pair of shoulders, two people meeting do not jostle or hurry past. They turn sideways with an unthinking, practiced grace, tucking elbows tight to the ribs and drawing in their breath so that wool brushes wool without catching. Walking here is never a flat stride; it is a continuous, quiet negotiation of steep stone steps, lashed rungs, and uneven lintels. Even on the flat roofs, their posture keeps a slight forward pitch, knees never quite locked, weight always balanced over the balls of their feet.

Their bodies are dressed against a world that has not yet smoothed its edges. Because the basalt and chert are freshly cleaved, no one goes barefoot, and no skin is left bare where it might meet a wall. They wear thick, three-ply sandals of boiled hide, scored pale on the soles where razor-edged gravel has bitten into the leather, and wrap their shins and forearms in strips of oiled linen or cured skin. Their clothes are layered for the basin’s abrupt climate: coarse, undyed hemp and heavy wool, greyed at the hems from damp alleys and chalked white across the knees and shoulders with limestone dust. Up on the sunlit upper terraces, where the glare bounces hard off pale rock, they wear flat, wide-brimmed hats woven from sedge. Descending into the cellar-cold slots at the bottom, they sling those hats down their backs and pull their sleeves over their knuckles.

Their hands tell the story of the town’s work before a word is spoken. Lime-and-ash mortar is hungry stuff; it pulls the moisture from skin until knuckles split and fingernails grow brittle. The builders, mortar-mixers, and stone-pryers rub their hands and wrists every evening with herb-steeped tallow, leaving a faint, sharp scent of thyme and rendered fat lingering in the damp alleys. Their palms are thick with horn-hard calluses from wooden mauls and braided hemp slings, yet their fingertips remain curiously sensitive—trained to run along a freshly split rock face in the blue-grey shadow and feel the hair-thin seam of a water-bearing crack before it is even visible.

If the basin is quiet, it is not out of timidity, nor merely out of courtesy in a town packed wall to wall. Silence here is a discipline of survival. A freshly opened fault is not dead stone; it is still settling under its own weight, still adjusting to the sudden absence of the earth that once pressed against it. The crews down at the bottom, driving wet wooden wedges into the lower terraces, need their ears unobstructed. They listen for the tiny, high *tick* of a basalt slab beginning to part, the muffled groan of a lintel taking on new load, or the faint hiss of a trapped pocket of groundwater finding air for the first time. A loud voice, a dropped tool, a heavy-heeled step—these are treated as clumsiness, the mark of someone who does not pay attention to where their weight falls. Children learn early to speak in the same low, dry murmur as their elders, a pitch that dies against the nearest hung reed mat without waking an echo.

They are not a people of deep sleep or idle mornings. At first light—while the basin floor is still pooled in cold shadow and only the highest swift-nests catch the sun—the *seep-watchers* are already moving through the lower tiers. Usually the oldest residents, too stiff-jointed for the ladders and rubble-slings, they carry small bone scrapers and clay lamps, walking the chipped rock channels to clear away overnight mats of liverwort or fallen grit so that every bead of condensation and seep-water reaches the cisterns. Water is never poured carelessly. Washing is done with dampened rags and dry ash; cooking water is saved, strained, and used again to wet the wooden wedges in the stone-cuts.

Because there is no soil to till, they eat what can be coaxed from the margins or carried down from beyond the rim. Grain and dried meat come in hemp sacks, traded or hauled from afar and stored in cool, dry-stacked cubbies high enough to stay clear of the bottom damp. On the sunlit roofs, the herb boxes are tended like small treasures—thyme, sorrel, bitter-cress, and hardy greens watered drop by drop from clay jars. Down in the darkest, wettest corners under the stairs and behind the mortar-mixing pits, they turn their spent, water-split wedge timbers into beds for pale, rubbery shelf-fungi, harvesting them by lamplight. They do not trouble the swifts nesting in the unreachable fissures overhead, nor the pale rock-lizards that sun on the fresh slabs; the lizards, in fact, are watched closely, since they always find the warmest, driest stones hours before a human hand can feel the shift.

Though the town is open to the sky, its people are reluctant visitors to the world above the lip. Crews haul baskets of damp rubble up the timber scaffolding to dump onto the wind-scoured plain, and small parties go up to cut brush or trade, yet they rarely linger there. On the rim, the wind is loud and steals moisture from the mouth; the sky has no edges, and there are no walls to lean a shoulder against. They empty their wicker loads with eyes narrowed against the dust and descend again into the cool, close geometry of the rift, where every surface is held and accounted for.

They have no ancient songs about this place, and no old graves. A town without dirt cannot bury its dead. When someone dies, they are carried up to the rim at dusk and burned on the barren stone where the wind takes the smoke—or left to the open plain, far from the lip. Sometimes, when a builder who spent forty years prying out the lower tiers is burned, the family brings a single clay jar of the clean, grey wood-ash back down into the basin, to be slaked with lime and pressed into the mortar of a new wall.

In that way, the people of the basin are much like the lichens just beginning to speckle the fresh basalt, or the wooden wedges swelling slowly in the cracks: quiet, crowded, and patient, pressing themselves into raw fracture until the wound in the rock learns how to hold them.
