If you stand still long enough for your ears to stop treating the valley as a single wall of noise, you realize the *tink-tink-tink* is not alone.

There is another, higher tapping three terraces down, and a slower, duller *thock* of a wooden mallet somewhere off to your left, muffled by a stack of ash-brick. Now and then a hemp cord goes taut beside your knee without any water touching the trough it is tied to, and somewhere out in the grey dark, a shale hammer strikes twice, out of time.

They are not machines running unattended. They are being tuned.

Wait long enough beside a tipping-trough and the mason who tends it will eventually find you—not by sight, for there is nothing here to see with, but because your woollen coat is soaking up the draft and changing the note of the nearest clay pipe.

You hear them first as a dry, leathery *shush* against the stone, like a sack of grain dragged along a wall. Then a hand—broad, square-fingered, and cold as wet slate—brushes your sleeve, pauses, and withdraws without alarm. They are used to things shifting in the dark.

If the greyness is thick enough to give you an outline, you see that they are compact and permanently stooped, built for the low lintels and waist-high walls that punish your own back. They move with a fluid, three-point gait when the alleys pinch, one palm always skimming the raw shale, a knuckle rapping softly against a cairn to count the courses. Where you bruise your shins and snag your clothes, they slide. Their forearms and knees are wrapped in stiff, mud-caked hide and plates of pale horn—not bone, you realize later, but the shed belly-scales of the blind wall-crawlers, lashed on with hemp.

Their skin is the color of wet salt and tallow, faintly translucent at the temples where the cold has driven the blood deep. Their eyes are small, milky, and kept half-shut beneath heavy, scarred brow-ridges—less for seeing than for keeping out flying splinters of shale. They do not look at you. They tilt their heads toward you, angling one ear, then the other, reading the shape of your breathing and the way the damp air breaks around your shoulders.

They do not speak—not with words, which would be swallowed whole by the clatter of the troughs and the moaning of the embankments. Instead, they live inside the valley’s noise the way a spider lives in the tension of its web.

To them, the valley is not a ruin, nor a maze, nor a folly. It is a lung, and a dew-trap.

There is no rain here and no stream, so every drop of water must be coaxed out of the cold air itself. Every wall they raise, every crooked terrace and narrow, scaffolding-choked alley, is another thousand square feet of cold stone and porous ash-brick for the dampness to sweat against. They build tight and jagged because flat ground lets the air slip past untouched; they build *raw* because weathered stone goes slick and sheds its moisture too fast, whereas fresh-split shale and tacky mortar drink the chill and weep endlessly.

And because they cannot see whether a distant tier is sweating or drying out, they make the water tell them.

The tipping-troughs and resonant shale slabs are their eyes. A mason crouched in a scaffolding-alley three hundred paces away can listen to the overlapping *clack-tip-splash-boom* of a dozen troughs and know, to the finger-width, which runnels are flowing briskly, which are choked with crumbled mortar, and which terraces have gone dry. When a clay pipe jutting from an embankment drops its moan by half a tone, it means a dry-stone wall has settled and pinched off a cold draft; within minutes, a mason is crawling through the dark with a chisel and a wedge of cedar, prying a cobble loose until the pipe’s sluggish groan rises back to the exact, wet pitch that makes the walls sweat.

That is why the valley has not decided what it is going to be. The air shifts, the green timber warps, the unmortared cairns slump under their own weight, and the masons follow the changes, tearing down a dry wall here, raising a damp screen there, forever adjusting the instrument.

They are not the only things that belong to the tiers.

As you follow—or are guided, gently, by a hand at your elbow—down toward the lower sumps, you begin to notice how crowded the dark really is, in its quiet, pale way.

The hound-sized crawlers with the leathery backs and chitinous bellies are everywhere on the vertical faces, moving in slow, sideways constellations. The masons call them nothing, because they have no words for them, but they treat them the way a shepherd treats a dog that cannot be petted. The crawlers lick the chalk-white mold and cold-loving slime from the mortar joints; without them, the slime would seal the pores of the ash-brick and turn the plank causeways into greased chutes. When a crawler wanders onto a freshly laid course of wet mud, a mason simply lifts it by its cold, leathery scruff—ignoring the dry *scrape* of its belly-plates—and sets it three stones over.

In the tiny, cramped pockets beneath the plank causeways, where the masons crouch to rest or eat, there are smaller things: pale, blind mites living in the hanging bundles of reeds; thread-thin worms thriving in the damp sawdust of the cedar troughs; thick, velvety mats of edible grey fungus that the masons cultivate on beds of rotting hemp and tallow-dregs. That, along with the mineral-heavy water skimmed from the top of the sumps, is what keeps them moving.

And down in the sumps themselves, where the water sits black and still between hand-lined cobble walls, live the heavy ones.

They are blind, broad-headed, and almost boneless-looking, the color of wet river-silt, with stubby, webbed limbs and thick, muscular tails. They spend most of their time wedged inside the narrow stone drain-culverts that link one sump to the next, eating whatever washes down the runnels—drowned mites, mold-crusts, the occasional fallen crawler. In return, their constant, sluggish thrashing keeps the culverts clear of ash-brick grit. When a sump begins to brim too high and threatens to drown a lower tier, a mason kneels at the cobble lip and taps a slow, three-beat rhythm on the stone with the butt of a chisel. After a minute, the dark water bulges. Something heavy surfaces with a wet *slurp*, nudges the mason’s grease-stained fingers with a blunt, whiskerless snout, and then dives back down into the culvert to clear the blockage.

Sometimes, a wall gives way.

You hear it happen while you are standing near the middle tiers: a sharp, rifle-crack *pop* of a green timber strut splitting under too much weight, followed by a sudden, deafening avalanche of sliding slate and crumbling ash-brick. Dust—cold, wet, and smelling sharply of iron—billows through the alleys. A clay pipe snaps off and goes silent. Two tipping-troughs are buried mid-swing, cutting their *clack-boom* out of the valley’s rhythm.

Instantly, every chisel in the dark stops.

For ten heartbeats, the valley holds its breath, listening to the last pebbles skitter down into a sump. Then, from half a dozen directions at once, you hear the dry *shush* of hide on stone. Masons converge on the collapse without lanterns, without shouts, stepping lightly over the fresh rubble. One hums a low, vibrating note—the exact pitch that settles in your teeth—and another answers it from the far side of the slide, mapping the size of the gap by the way their voices meet across the broken masonry.

They do not mourn the lost wall. Before the dust has even settled into the runnels, they are already sorting the unbroken shale from the rubble by touch, stacking the cobbles into fresh cairns, and lashing new cedar struts into place.

By the time you turn to climb back toward the rim, the broken pipe has been plugged with a wad of wet reed, a new tipping-trough is being pinned to a greased cedar post, and out in the cold, bruised greyness behind you, the steady *tink-tink-tink* has already begun again.
