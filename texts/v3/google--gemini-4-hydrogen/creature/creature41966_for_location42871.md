At first, you would take them for part of the valley’s slow reclamation—just another collapsed boulder or rusted junction-box that the sphagnum has finished swallowing.

If you stood near one of the isolated transformer sheds, waiting for your eyes to adjust to the dim, bluish twilight, you might notice the anomaly before you saw the thing itself. Everywhere else, the wet moss creeps upward over the concrete footings by millimeters a year, burying bolts and warning signs under thick olive-and-russet velvet. But along the leeward flank of the transformer casing, where the painted steel gives off a faint, dry warmth and a steady sixty-cycle hum, the moss is worn back. The concrete at the base is rubbed smooth, faintly dark with peat-oil and wax, and the steel above it is bare of frost.

Then the hump of moss beside the casing shifts.

It does not startle or bolt. Nothing here hurries. Slowly, with the deliberate, heavy grace of something whose joints are stiff with cold, it peels its flank away from the warm metal and settles onto four splayed, broad-padded limbs.

They are roughly the size of a large harbor seal, though longer in the torso and built closer to the ground, with wide, flat shoulders and disproportionately long forelimbs. Their skin is thick, pleated, and the color of wet basalt—slate-gray on the undersides where they press against steel and stone, but across the spine, the crown of the head, and the deep folds of the haunches, the valley has taken root. Patches of damp sphagnum, dark liverworts, and short tufts of beard-lichen grow directly out of the deep creases of their hide, fed by the constant condensation and the sheer stillness of their lives. When they curl against a concrete footing to sleep, they vanish into the floor of the basin.

Only after they have spent three or four days pressed against a transformer housing does their core warm enough for a thin, pale wisp of breath to curl from their slit-like nostrils when they step back into the freezing air. Most of the time, their body temperature hovers just above the freezing point of the groundwater, and they leave no vapor at all—no sign of heat in the mist that drifts through the depressions.

They have no use for ears. In a basin where the turbine-thunder of the pumps is a physical wall against the sternum, air-borne sound is useless; the sides of their broad, wedge-shaped heads are smooth and unbroken, with only a pair of sunken, scarred membranes beneath the skin. Instead, they read the valley through bone and iron.

When one of them leaves the shelter of a transformer shed, it rarely trusts the open expanse of the peat. It knows what lies beneath those yielding, treacherous mats of moss: razor-sharp fault-scarps and fresh collapse-rubble that would slice open a careless belly. Instead, it climbs the rusted steel stanchions and travels along the heavy-gauge, insulated pipelines that snake across the mile-wide floor. Its broad, leathery pads hook securely around the curve of the pipe-cladding, moving with a slow, hand-over-hand cadence above the jagged stone.

Every few yards, it pauses and presses its flat chin or the bare palm of a forelimb directly against an exposed steel flange. Through the metal, it feels the valley’s entire nervous system—the distant, steady thrum of the rotary ventilation housings, the shudder of an intake grille half a mile away, even the tiny, sharp *tink* of a raven’s claws landing on a pipe-clamp down the line. To a creature deaf to the air-borne roar, the pipelines are not just roads; they are a map of vibration.

Their eyes are small, milky-blue, and set deep beneath heavy brow-ridges. They dislike the harsh, cold-white pools cast by the work-lamps on the pump-houses, skirting the edges of the glare and keeping to the miles of gray twilight between the stations. In that dimness, they forage without haste. They scrape the mineral-rich, iron-tasting bacterial mats from the wet concrete around the dewatering sumps, using blunt, stained teeth. They crush cold-sluggish beetles gathered from the underside of hanging ferns. And now and then, when the hunger is sharp enough, one will settle motionless beside a vole’s tunnel in the warm peat near a transformer box, waiting for hours—an indistinguishable mound of wet moss—until a small, warm body brushes too close to the entrance.

They are few and far between, much like the stunted willows or the machines themselves. Perhaps four or five inhabit the entire sunken valley, each keeping to its own stretch of pipeline and its own favored dewatering station. They rarely meet, and they have no need to crowd together.

Yet they are not entirely solitary. In a roar that has no rhythm and no pause, rhythm itself is a private language. Now and then, one of them will pause atop a rusted stanchion, raise a heavy, calloused knuckle or a piece of loose basalt, and strike the steel pipe-clamp. Three slow beats, a long pause, and two beats more.

To anyone standing on the moss below, the sound would be completely swallowed by the deafening thunder of the pumps; you might only feel a faint, unexplained shiver in the soles of your boots. But half a mile away, another moss-backed figure, pressed flat against the warm casing of a transformer shed, feels the cadence travel up through its ribs and jawbone. It waits a long moment in the dim, bluish light, then lifts a heavy palm and taps twice against the steel housing in reply—before settling back into the patient, cold equilibrium of rock, wet plant, and iron.
