At the bottom of everything, before the frogs or the birds, there are the things that live in the seam between stone and silt.

Where the warm seepage rises through the porous limestone, the water is never quite still, and here the bacterial felt grows thickest—an olive-green skin, rubbery and dimpled with gas, that blankets the pale clay. Inside it, and just beneath it, live the **silt-worms** and **blind, flat-headed loaches** no longer than a finger. The loaches have no need for eyes in the tea-colored murk under the mat; their mouths are ringed with stiff, pale barbels that taste the dissolved lime and the rot of drowned midges. When the afternoon heat swells the gas bubbles beneath the felt until whole patches tear loose and float upward, the loaches dart into the sudden, brief shade of the underside, snapping at the exposed worms before the mat settles back down.

The **chimney-builders** own the mudflats where the water only reaches for half the year. They are crustaceans the color of wet ash, with one claw grown broad and flat as a trowel and the other narrow, serrated, and quick. By day they sit deep in vertical shafts sunk down to the cool water table, where the clay stays slick. In the late afternoon, as the glare softens to silver, they climb to the rims of their baked-earth chimneys—hollow, turreted tubes a hand high, plastered pellet by pellet from their own excavations—and hook their narrow claws over the lip to wait. They are patient as stones. If a midge settles on the damp rim, or a small frog misjudges a leap, the narrow claw flashes out. When the water creeps up by an inch or two over the course of a slow week, the chimneys become islands; the crustaceans simply add another ring of mud to the top, building upward ahead of the tide, until the plain is studded with thousands of tiny, dark smokestacks poking above the glass-smooth sheet.

The **frogs** are everywhere the sedge grows, and even where it doesn’t. They are small enough to sit comfortably on a quartz pebble, their backs mottled in the exact pale, mineral grays and bleached yellows of the limestone shelves, so that when they are still they vanish into the glare. Only their throats give them away—thin, copper-tinted sacs that swell until they look like drops of bright oil clinging to the reeds.

Their clicking is not a single sound but a kind of weather. A few start in the reeds on the eastern rim of a pan, and the rhythm—*tink, tink-tink, tink*—catches like dry grass taking fire, sweeping across a mile of open water in seconds as thousands join in, each trying to place its note in the tiny gap between its neighbor’s. The sound has a hard, metallic edge because the air is heavy and the water acts like a drumhead. In the height of the chorus, the frogs themselves seem almost vibrating off the stems, clinging with wide, sticky toe-pads that leave faint wet prints on the blue-green blades.

Above them, in the shimmering air that smells of warm clay and salt, the **midges** hang in tall, drifting columns that look from a distance like smoke rising from the pans. They live only long enough to dance, mate, and fall—a soft, continuous rain of tiny bodies that feeds the algae, the crustaceans, and the **skimmer-bugs** that skate across the tension of the lagoons on legs tipped with oily hairs.

The **wading birds** are the tall, quiet geometry of the plain. They stand nearly as high as a person’s shoulder, all neck and stilted leg, their plumage bleached to the color of sun-dried bone except for the slate-gray tips of their wings. Their bills are long, heavy, and slightly upturned at the tip—not for spearing, but for sweeping. A bird will walk slowly through an ankle-deep lagoon with its head lowered, swinging its open bill side to side through the water just above the silt, feeling for the sudden twitch of a loach or the hard shell of a snail.

They are social in a loose, irritable way. Twenty or thirty will dot a single pan, always keeping a careful distance—three or four body-lengths apart, just out of reach of a neighbor’s sharp, barking warning. Their nests are nothing more than broad, messy rafts of trampled sedge anchored to the mud in the middle of the widest lagoons, where the water is too shallow for the heavy grazers to bother wading and too open for anything to creep up unseen. In the breeding season, the chicks stand on these damp platforms like tufts of gray down on stilts, stretching their necks toward the sky and clicking softly in imitation of the frogs until their parents return with crops full of mashed crustaceans and silt-fish.

The **grazers** are the oldest things moving on the plain, or at least they look it. From a hundred yards off, a group of them half-submerged in a slumped limestone wallow looks like a cluster of weathered boulders left behind by some vanished flood. Their hides are thick, hairless, and deeply creased, gray-brown where they are wet and chalky pale where the mud has dried and cracked on their broad backs. Algae grows in the folds behind their ears and along their spines, giving them a faint, mossy sheen.

They have wide, blunt muzzles with leathery lips built for peeling up the thick mats of bacterial felt from the bottom of the pools, taking in mouthfuls of silt and water and straining the mud out through the sides of their jaws with a wet, rhythmic *shhh-plop*. They move along the braided channels mostly at night or in the thick, silvery haze of early morning, dragging their heavy, barrel-shaped bellies through the clay to carve the only highways the plain knows—shallow, glistening troughs that fill with seepage and become nurseries for tadpoles the moment the grazers have passed.

They are not fast, and they are rarely angry, though the old bulls will sometimes rear their huge, flat-topped heads out of a wallow and let out a bellow so low you feel it in the soles of your feet before you hear it—a long, wet rumble that rolls across three miles of flat water and sets the wading birds lifting into the air in a sudden, clattering cloud.

And then there are the **mound-dwellers**—the things that belong to the few inches of dry land.

The solitary, gnarled trees on the shell-and-chert mounds are crowded ecosystems in miniature, islands of verticality in a world that is entirely horizontal. Beneath their splayed, rib-like roots, in the dry, crumbly dust of crushed ancient shells, live **pale, flat-bodied lizards** with fringes of scales along their toes. They never touch the wet mud if they can help it. They spend their mornings pressed flat against the sun-warmed bark, their color shifting from chalk-white to lichen-gray, watching the ground below. When the heat peaks and the insects in the low scrub begin their dry, sawing rattle, the lizards dart down to the edge of the mound, snatching the heavy-bodied flies that land to rest on the warm stone, then flicking back up into the sparse shadow of the leathery leaves.

Higher in those same crooked branches, **colonial spiders** string thick, yellowish silk from twig to twig until the crown of a tree looks wrapped in torn gauze. Hundreds of them share the same vast web—small, long-legged things with abdomens striped like salt-crust. They do not wrap their prey; when a cloud of midges drifts across the mound on the steady wind, dozens of spiders run out together across the vibrating silk, pulling down the catch and feeding side by side in the hot shade.

Nothing on the plain lives apart from the water, even the things that hate to get wet. Every creature here is tuned to the slow, almost imperceptible breathing of the basin: the inch-by-inch rise when the warm seepage swells, the inch-by-inch retreat that leaves salt rings on the clay and concentrates the fish in the shrinking wallows. When the water drops, the frogs dig down into the damp mud beneath the sedge roots and seal themselves in thin, papery cocoons of shed skin; the grazers pack tighter into the deepest limestone slumps, shoulder to shoulder in the tea-colored soup; the wading birds walk further out toward the center of the pans, their pale legs stained dark to the knees.

And when the humid nights condense back into sheets of bright water, the cocoons split, the chimneys are built an inch higher, and the whole flat, sunlit table wakes up loud again—clicking, sawing, barking, and bellowing under the empty sky, with nowhere to hide and nowhere to go.
