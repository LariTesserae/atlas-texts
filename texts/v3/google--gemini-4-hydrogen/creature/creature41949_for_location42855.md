At first, the basin seems empty of anyone at all. From the lip of the basalt cliffs, you see only the wide, sunlit geometry of the avenues, the oxide-red ribs of the central tower, and the silent tenements stepped into the eastern rock. Nothing hurries across the crushed-stone aprons. No smoke rises from the chimneys.

It takes an hour of watching before you notice the fresh grease.

Down in the pump-houses, the bearings of the great cast-iron flywheels gleam with dark, wet tallow, free of dust. The shallow concrete catch-basins beneath the sweating pipes have been swept clean of fallen fern-fronds so the water gathers clear and unclouded. Along the edges of the paved avenues, the woody vines have been sheared back cleanly with a sharp blade, kept just far enough from the black ground-conduits to let a boot pass.

Someone is tending the slowness.

Once you know what to look for, you begin to see them, though they are as easy to miss against the stone and iron as the basalt-dusted marmots. They wear heavy, unbleached canvas and oiled wool, stained at the knees with emerald moss and streaked at the shoulders with the faded oxide red of the tower. Their skin is pale from the cool, thin air of the hollow, though their faces are weathered around the eyes from squinting up into the bright, ordinary sky.

There are perhaps forty or fifty of them—a small, self-contained settlement of tenders who live in the tenements on the eastern cliff and spend their days on the basin floor. They move at the speed of the machinery. Nobody here runs; running in a place where flywheels turn so slowly you can count the spokes would feel like a kind of madness, and besides, the gravel is loose and the high-voltage yards are unforgiving.

What strikes you first, if you stand close to one of them, is how tightly their heads are bound. They wear close-fitting leather caps or strips of heavy felt tied around the jaw and ears, stuffed with beeswax and greased wool. Without them, the sixty-cycle roar of the coils and the bass groan of the bearings would rattle a person’s skull until they bled or went hollow-eyed. Because of the bindings, they are deaf to air-borne sound. You could shout directly behind a tender’s shoulder as they kneel by a catch-basin, and they would not turn.

Yet if you step off the gravel onto a flagstone, or rest your hand on a steel gantry railing twenty paces away, they will look up at once.

They have traded their ears for their soles and their palms. Their boots are stitched with thin, flat leather—never thick rubber, which makes a person blind—and many of them go barefoot inside the workshops on the western cliff. Through the unweathered mineral seams of the young basalt, the whole town speaks to them. They know which pump-house is running by the steadiness of the hum in their heels; they can tell when a bearing is drying out three hundred paces away because the smooth, deep pulse in the bedrock develops a faint, irregular grit, like a pebble turning under a heel.

In the open daylight, they do not speak at all. Wasting breath against a wall of mechanical sound makes no sense. Instead, they use wide, deliberate hand-signs across the empty aprons of stone, or they tap out messages directly onto the ironwork—three sharp raps with a wrench-handle against a conduit, followed by a slow scrape, traveling straight down the metal into a partner’s resting palm.

They are not factory workers, and nothing is shipped out over the cliffs. They are gardeners of condensation.

Without the machines, the scoop would be a dry, sterile crack in the earth. It is the cold iron pipes, the long compression cycles, and the water-less condenser tower that pull moisture out of the thin air and make it bead and drip. Everything that lives here—the hart’s-tongue ferns, the pale tree frogs, the starlings, and the people themselves—drinks from the sweat of the iron.

So they keep it running. In the flat-roofed workshops along the western wall, they spend their days at slow, patient repairs: re-winding copper coils by hand, scraping mineral scale from valve flanges, pouring lead seals, and cutting leather gaskets. Others walk the ditches with flat tin cans and stoneware jugs, harvesting water from the catch-basins to carry up to the terraces, or tending small, neat beds of wild rhubarb, dock, and bitter greens planted in the warm, dry lee of the brick transformer houses, where the coils throw off a steady, comforting heat.

Like the swifts and the green-backed starlings, the tenders’ lives are sliced into twelve-minute hours.

They rarely bother to look at the pressure gauges. They watch the birds instead. When a tender down in the fern-choked ditches sees the entire flock lift off the steel gantries in a single, silent cloud, they simply set down their shears or their oilcan, lean their back against a cool basalt boulder, and wait.

When the central condenser’s relief valve blows, the *hiss-clank* is so violent it seems to flatten the air. For twenty seconds, the basin fills with white sound. The tenders do not cover their ears; they open their mouths slightly to keep the pressure even in their heads, close their eyes, and let the bedrock shudder up through their spines. It is a pause built into the day—twenty seconds in which no work can be done, no sign can be read, and nothing is asked of them. When the hiss settles back into the deep mechanical thrum and the starlings drop back to the gantries, the tenders pick up their tools and resume.

At dusk, when the clear light drains out of the scoop and the cold air settles thick into the ditches, they climb the steel gantries up the stepped eastern rock toward the tenements.

The quarters are silent from the outside because they are built to drink the noise. There is no glass in the narrow, barred windows—glass would buzz and eventually crack under the bass resonance—only heavy wooden louvers and greased canvas flaps. Inside, the stone walls are hung with thick, woven mats of dried bracken and felted wool. Down here in the rock, the sixty-cycle roar of the floor is muffled to a dull, distant surf, like heavy weather miles away.

It is only here, behind the wool and the louvers, that they pull out their wax ear-plugs and speak.

Even then, their voices are strange. Generations of living inside a sixty-cycle hum have taught their throats to avoid the pitch of the transformers, just as the pale tree frogs do. They speak in low, chesty registers that sit underneath the mechanical drone, or in soft, dry clicks and whispers that sit lightly on top of it. When they sing—if you can call it singing—it is a slow, wordless humming that locks into the vibration of the cliffside until the stone walls themselves seem to be breathing.

They do not leave the rim. A few of the younger ones have climbed up into the ordinary world above the basalt lips, out of curiosity, and come back before nightfall looking pale and unsteady. Up there, under the wide sky, the ground does not pulse. The air is thin and dead, devoid of the steady, patient thrum that tells them where they are and that the water is still being made. Away from the ringing rock, they say, the silence feels like falling.

So they stay down in the scoop, turning the grease-cups, sweeping the moss from the basins, and keeping time with the slow-turning gears—another kind of life that has taken root in the damp, thundering dark between the stones.
