At first, even if you stayed down there for an hour, you would swear the room held nothing more animal than a wet sponge. Everything is muffled in moss and liverwort; everything drips at the same steady, indifferent tempo; everything shakes with the same four-second *thump-hiss* of the sump pumps.

It is only if you stop walking—if you brace a hand against one of the waist-high manifolds to steady yourself on the springy, waterlogged grating—that you might notice the pipe beneath your palm is not entirely rigid.

What looks at first like a sagging length of rotting canvas lagging, or a swollen, moss-furred knot of fern roots wedged between a stanchion and a galvanized trough, gives slightly under your fingers. Not like wet peat, which crumbles, and not like rubber, which snaps back. It yields with a dense, turgid resistance, like a leather wineskin packed tight with silt and water. And if you keep your hand there, counting under the roar of the culverts, you feel it: every four seconds, just a fraction of a beat after the pistons hit the bottom of their stroke in the sump below, the swelling beneath your palm stiffens, surges a millimeter to the left, and relaxes again.

It has no heart. It does not need one. The pumps are its heart.

They live throughout all four tiers of the vault, though you could pass within inches of a dozen of them and take them for plumbing or rot. They have no bones, no eyes, and no proper limbs—only thick-walled, fibrous bodies built from layered cellulose, mineral-stiffened mucus, and trapped threads of hemp and aerial root. Their outer hides are the exact color of the room: mottled iron-grey and verdigris green, crusted along the undersides with white boiler-treatment salt and furred on top with a velvety nap of algae that blends seamlessly into the creeping fig.

The smallest ones, no larger than a clenched fist, cluster in the upper pipe-rack near the leaking steam-trap, where the air is hottest and the copper wire binding the lagging has corroded green. They wedge themselves into the porcelain hollows of ceramic insulators and around the sweating throats of junction boxes, looking like forgotten wads of oakum. Down on the lower mezzanines, where the air is heavier and the spray-bars rain continuously, the older ones grow as long and thick as rolled firehoses, draped in heavy, limbless loops over the cast-iron manifolds or tucked into the dark hollows beneath the sword-fern root-balls.

Because they have no muscles in the animal sense, they cannot hurry. To cross ten inches of wet iron takes one of them half a day. It moves by inflating and anchoring: extruding a broad, pale mat of microscopic filaments from its underside to taste and grip the lime-crusted metal, then letting the four-second shockwave of the pumps do the work. *Thump-hiss*: a fraction of dark, metallic fluid is forced into its leading chamber, swelling the front of its body a hair’s breadth forward. *Thump-hiss*: the trailing chamber empties, peeling its rear filaments off the moss with a faint, wet *shuck* that is swallowed instantly by the noise of the water.

They are blind to the bruised greenish twilight of the skylights and the weak amber cones of the sodium lamps, though you will often find them crowded around the wire-mesh cages of the few bulbs that still burn. They do not care for the light, which means nothing to them, but they are drawn to the dryish warmth radiating off the glass globes and, more than that, to the sharp, localized sixty-cycle buzz of the lamp ballasts. Vibration is how they know the room is alive. Where a loose pipe rattles against a steel column, or where a transformer hums behind its mesh cage, they gather in thick, overlapping heaps, pressing their bodies into the trembling metal so the current and the shudder can drive their internal fluids along.

They eat what the room gives them, which is rust, salt, and stray voltage. Inside their tough, peat-colored hides are stacked plates of precipitated iron sulfide and copper carbonate—crude, wet-cell batteries that drink the dissolved metals out of the condensate and feed on the faint electrical gradients leaking from the old conduits.

And they graze on the scale. Wherever white mineral crust builds up around the stem of a brass gate-valve or chokes the nozzles of an overhead spray-bar, one of them will eventually arrive, sliding its soft, puckered collar over the joint like a sleeve. Over the course of weeks, it secretes a weak, sour-smelling acid that softens the lime and calcium carbonate, drinking the dissolved minerals and leaving the brass slick and bright beneath a coat of clear slime. They do not know they are tending the machinery. They are only following the salt and the pressure. Yet by keeping the valves clear and packing their heavy, water-filled bodies into the vibrating gaps between pipes, they keep the room’s arteries from choking shut or shaking themselves apart.

Nothing hunts them, and they hunt nothing. Their only end is weight. As the years pass, the older ones grow sluggish even by the standards of the vault, their internal chambers silting up with precipitated iron and lime until the four-second stroke of the pumps can no longer flex them. When that happens, they simply stop shifting. Their hides stiffen into a porous, grey mineral crust; the creeping fig and philodendron roots, always searching for purchase, thread straight into their peat-rich flanks. Within a season or two, a dead one is indistinguishable from the root-balls that hold the shape of the rotted wooden troughs—just another damp, moss-upholstered lump supporting the weight of the catwalks.

Before it calcifies completely, though, the violent tremor of a surging pump or the slow creep of a fern root will often shake loose a cluster of small, rubbery buds from its underside, no bigger than marbles and crusted with salt. They drop through the diamond holes of the iron grating, splash into the concrete sluice-channels below, and roll along the rushing culverts in the dark until they lodge against the intake screens of the sump. There, pressed tight against the humming iron by the pull of the pumps, they anchor their first pale filaments, feel the four-second beat take hold inside them, and begin to swell.
