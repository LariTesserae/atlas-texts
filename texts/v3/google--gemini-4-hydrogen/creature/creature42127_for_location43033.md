At first, you might think the place is unmanned.

The alleys are too narrow for crowds, and the air is too thin to carry the usual clutter of a working town—no shouting over machinery, no footsteps clattering on iron stairs, no doors banging shut. A wrench rests on a ceramic ledge beside a half-drained catch-pan. A coil of braided copper wire hangs from a hook, still warm. The sharp, crystalline ash along the stepped paths is worn down into two smooth, glassy ruts, as if something passes this way often, though never in a hurry.

Then you notice the figure standing beside the cooling-tower louvers, and realize you have been looking at them for a full minute without seeing them move.

They are tall and spare, built less like a creature of muscle than a frame of dry cane and taut hide. At this altitude, where the air holds barely enough weight to fill a lung, nothing can afford to rush; to sprint across a terrace would be to burn out and drop in twenty paces. So the tenders move with the same patient, measured economy as the cranes. Their chests are deep and narrow, the ribs arched wide beneath loose tunics of woven basalt-fiber, rising and falling on a rhythm so slow it seems disconnected from the rest of their bodies—one long, deliberate draw of thin air every half-minute, held until the last trace of oxygen is wrung out, then released in a faint, dry sigh.

Their skin is the color of unglazed ceramic, matte and almost entirely hairless, toughened against the unblinking midday glare and the salt-sharp ash. In the open sun of the upper terraces, it looks dry as pumice, dusted with fine gray powder around the knuckles and ankles. Down in the damp condensation-alleys, where the metallic mist hangs waist-high, that same skin darkens slightly and takes on a dull, oiled sheen, drinking in the moisture it cannot afford to lose. Their eyes are set deep beneath heavy brow-ridges, shielded by a pair of thin, translucent inner lids that flick sideways whenever a gust lifts crystalline grit off the roofs.

They do not speak much. A voice at this height drops dead ten feet from the mouth, stripped of warmth and carried away by the updrafts. Instead, the tenders carry short rods of fired clay tipped with brass, and they talk to the city—and to one another—through the iron.

Watch one pause at a junction box. They press the brass tip of their rod against a riveted pipe, then rest the flat of their palm—or sometimes the ridge of their jaw—against the warm metal. Through the bolted frame of the refinery, the whole shelf hums. A rhythmic *tick-tick, pause, tick* travels half a mile through the busbar stanchions and boiler housings, arriving cleanly in the soles of a partner’s copper-nailed sandals three blocks away. It is a language of intervals and dampened rings: a valve sticking in the third sump, a transformer running hot on the east terrace, the slow, steady progress of a cathode vat nearing harvest.

When they do work, their hands are extraordinarily steady. They wear heavy, insulated gauntlets of cured glider-membrane—thin, pale, and flexible as parchment—and trail a braided copper grounding-strap from one wrist, clipping it to the handrails whenever they step near the humming overhead lines. In the dry air up here, a static charge builds on anything that moves; you can see the faint blue corona snapping off a careless tender’s fingertips if they forget to ground themselves before touching a sight-glass.

They do not live in houses so much as pockets. Behind the heavy, riveted doors set into the flanks of the factory blocks, the walls are thick with sintered slag to keep out the midday heat, and lined inside with porous, pale clay tiles. Thin threads of warm condensation are piped in from the outer manifolds and bled straight into the tiles, keeping the air within cool, dim, and faintly damp—a quiet refuge that smells of wet earth where no earth exists, and sulfur, and warm iron. Here, in the windowless dark, they sleep curled tight to conserve moisture, waking when the vibration through the floor shifts from the daytime thermal load to the deeper, slower pulse of the night-crust cooling beneath the shelf.

They are not alone in the seams of the rock, though the other things that live here are even harder to spot.

Down in the sunken cisterns and the dark, mineral-encrusted sumps, where the hot runoff collects before being pumped back into the exchangers, the water is too foul and too metallic for anything ordinary. Yet if you shine a lamp into the blackness beneath the grate, the submerged iron is furred with pale, waxy clusters—neither plant nor fungus, but slow, colonial growths that feed directly on the sulfur and the trickle-current bleeding from the vats. They grow in brittle, branching crusts like salt-coral, precipitating heavy metals out of the broth into their own chalky skeletons. The tenders do not poison them; every few months, a tender wades knee-deep into the warm sump with a flat chisel, prying off the thickest plates of living ore to be smelted down, leaving the younger roots to grow back in the dark.

Out on the sun-bleached edges of the shelf, where the basalt is still glassy and knife-sharp, smaller things wait out the noon. Flat, six-legged scuttlers no wider than a thumbnail cling to the shaded undersides of the tilted rock plates, their backs plated in dull, blistered silica that matches the pumice so closely you can stare straight at a cluster of them and see only stone. They never venture into the open glare. Only when a relief-valve sighs steam across the terrace, or when a tender scrapes the salt-crust from a heat-exchanger fin and lets the flakes drift down into the cracks, do the stones suddenly unstick themselves and skitter sideways to feed, vanishing back into the fissures before the mist clears.

Even the pale, leathery filter-feeders clinging to the sweating condenser-pipes have their place in the city’s quiet economy. The tenders step around them carefully, never scraping them from the copper; their broad, segmented bodies keep the runoff channels free of sulfur-sludge, and when they die, their dried, papery husks peel away cleanly—just the right thickness, once oiled, to cut into gaskets for the low-pressure steam lines.

Nothing up here is wasted, and nothing hurries. At the end of a long cycle—measured not by the unblinking sun, which seems pinned to the same high, pale point in the sky, but by the millimeter-thick accretion of copper on the vat plates—a pair of tenders will stand together on the rim of the terrace. They lean on their clay rods, grounding-chains dangling motionless against their shins, looking out over the flat lid of cloud that hides the lower world. A long-winged glider drifts past at eye level, riding the thermal off the black rock without a flicker of its cartilage-framed wings. The tenders watch it until it dwindles into the glare, draw one slow, deep breath of the thin, kiln-dry air, and turn back into the shadow of the pipes, where the city is waiting to be tuned.
