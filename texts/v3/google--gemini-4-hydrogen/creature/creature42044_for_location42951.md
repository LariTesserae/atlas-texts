You do not see them at first because you are looking for something that breathes, or scuttles, or puts down roots. You are looking for the kind of life that leaves droppings on the slate or stains the sluice-channels green, and there is none of that here.

Instead, there are the basins.

If you kneel beside one of those mirror-still depressions in the floor—the ones the size of a palm, or a knee, or a wooden float—and lay your hand flat against the stone beside it, you will feel that the slate is not uniformly hot. Here and there, in wide, irregular patches, the floor holds a sharper, denser warmth, like a hearth-stone an hour after the ashes have been swept away. And if you look closely at the thumbprints pressed into the damp plaster of the causeways, you will notice that the whorls are too large, too deeply incised, and that they never quite match the proportions of a human hand. They are the marks of something that has no fingers at all, only a heavy, padded underside ribbed with fine, concentric ridges for gripping slick stone.

They live in the seams of the world, and they are made of the same patience as the world itself: wet lime, iron-rich slip, trapped steam, and the restless, exothermic heat of things that have not yet finished cooling.

When one of them is still, you could walk past it at three paces and take it for a slump of raw-tempered clay left behind by some absent mason, or a low, unremarkable plinth of pale brick. They have no eyes to catch the dim, shuttered light, and no mouths to open. Their outer skin is a thick, mineral-grey rind that sweats condensation, patterned along the flanks with parallel, comb-like channels to shed the tepid water that gathers on them. Inside, they are neither flesh nor stone, but a dense, slaking paste—warm as fresh mortar, threaded through with fragments of fired terracotta, ceramic pegs, and the occasional copper pin scavenged from a collapsing arcade.

They do not eat, as living things on the surface of the earth eat. Nothing here grows, and nothing rots. Instead, they feed on the *setting* of things—on the quiet, chemical tension released when wet clay stiffens, when lime drinks the damp air and hardens, when a freshly laid terrace settles into the void beneath it. They are drawn to instability. Wherever the flat plain is about to blister and heave, they are already gathering beneath the flagstones, thin and wide as a layer of bedding mortar, swelling with trapped heat until the slate slabs neatly un-key themselves and let them rise.

To watch one move is to watch the landscape itself grow indecisive.

It does not walk. It lays a broad, flat lobe of itself forward onto the sweating stone, pressing down with the full, heavy weight of its mass until the moisture is driven out and the floor takes a crisp, shallow impression. Then the trailing half of it softens—slumping into a sluggish sheet of grey slip that slides forward with a faint, wet *shhh*—while the leading edge firms up, its corners sharpening, its surface briefly taking on the powdery, chiseled look of dressed stone. As it shifts its weight, the ceramic pegs and copper pins suspended inside it shift too, locking together for leverage and then dropping free with a tiny, distant *tink* or *clack* that carries for miles across the empty plazas.

Sometimes, when a front of steam peels off the hotter sectors of the plain and rolls over them, they lose their coherence altogether—or perhaps they simply stop insisting on it. A creature crossing a causeway will flatten into a puddle of wet slip, indistinguishable from the condensation, only to pull itself together twenty paces further on as a damp, corbeled column, already weeping water from its seams. Others do the opposite: if they linger too long on a cool stretch of slate, away from the warm voids, their outer rind dries and cures. They lock into place, sharp-edged and brittle as kiln-fired lintels, lashed tight by their own mineral fibers. Many of the rare, lonely structures scattered across the miles of bare floor—the waist-high walls, the hollow plinths—are not ruins at all. They are simply inhabitants that have taken a set, waiting in the dim quiet for the next heave of the plain, or the next hot tide of vapor, to slake their joints and wake them back into paste.

Because they have no eyes, they do not see you walking across the flagstones. They know you only as a strange, persistent anomaly in the level—a small, mobile weight that radiates a steady, unfamiliar warmth and refuses to cure, refuses to key into the floor, refuses to slump when the arches behind it dissolve.

If you stand still for too long in one place, listening to the slow *drip-drip* of water from a lintel, you may notice a slight heaviness in the steam behind you. Not a footstep—only the quiet *shhh* of something broad and warm sliding over the wet slate, followed by the faint, mineral smell of slaking lime. It does not mean you harm. It has no concept of harm, any more than a trowel has malice for the plaster it smooths. It only wants to measure you against the floor. It wants to press its whorled, steaming flank against your boot, your knee, your open palm, and hold you there in the dimness until the heat of its body drives the dampness out—until it has taken a clean, sharp, unweathered impression of you, and left you behind in the grey slip, like a thumbprint left by no hand at all.
