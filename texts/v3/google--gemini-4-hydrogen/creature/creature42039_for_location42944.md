At first—and even after hours of watching—it seems that nothing larger than the newts and the mountain birds could possibly live here. The empty air between the aqueducts remains unbroken; the vacant terraces gather only mist and sunlight. To see what truly inhabits the amphitheater, you have to slow your eyes down to the pace of the moss.

Wait until the afternoon sun clears the rim and strikes the dark hornfels. Where the light hits a cantilevered gallery, a section of what looked like wet, dry-laid masonry will uncoil.

They are long, narrow creatures, proportioned to the architecture itself—slender enough to thread the shoulder-width aqueducts and drop cleanly down the sixty-foot cistern shafts, yet heavy-boned, measuring twice the length of a tall man from blunt snout to tapering tail. Their hide is the matte, lightless charcoal of fresh hornfels, ridged along the spine and shoulders with pale, mineral-hardened plates that catch the sun with the exact cold glitter of the quartz seams. Because they move so seldom, and because the cloud-mist never fully dries, the older ones carry gardens on their backs: cushions of emerald moss wedged between their shoulder plates, and pale threads of beard lichen trailing from their flanks. When they press themselves flat against a retaining wall, they do not merely hide in the stone; they become an extension of the wall.

There are never many of them—perhaps four or five across the entire three-hundred-pace bowl, each holding a different tier of the cliffside. Like the greenery and the newts, they are cold-blooded, ruled by the slow swells of the weather. When the mist thickens and the basin turns chill, they retreat into the hollow piers of the stacked arches or sink into the dark catch-basins at the bottom of the drop-shafts, letting the cold, heavy water hammer steadily against their armored backs. There they wait in a near-torpid stillness, their heartbeats dropping to a few strokes a minute, indistinguishable from submerged boulders.

Only when the sun warms the black rock do they climb out onto the vacant terraces and high bridges to drink the dry heat. They move with an unhurried, deliberate grace over terrain that would shred softer flesh. Their broad, split-toed feet are shod in thick, fibrous pads that grip the razor-sharp scree and the damp chisel marks without slipping or bleeding, testing each dry-laid stone before committing their weight.

In a place this overwhelmingly loud, they have no use for ears like ours. The sides of their heads are smooth and sealed, unmarked by openings. Instead, they listen with their bones. When one pauses on a bridge, it lowers its broad, flat jaw and the wide, resonant plates of its chest directly against the hornfels, drinking the vibrations of the basin. Through the stone, the deafening roar resolves into something intricate and legible: the steady, sternum-shaking *thrum* of the cisterns, braided with the sharp *clacks* and *chimes* of a hundred smaller runnels. A snail inching across a parapet, a bird darting beneath an arch, a shift in the weight of the mist condensing on the upper rim—every disturbance alters the standing echoes by a hair’s breadth, and they feel it all through the soles of their feet and the length of their ribs.

They eat rarely, sustained by a metabolism as patient as the rock. You may see one grazing slowly along a wet retaining wall, using broad, flat teeth to shear away curtains of saxifrage and lichen, or snapping up a thick-shelled snail—crushing the shell against a slate slab with a single, sharp *tock* that vanishes instantly into the roar. Now and then, one will pluck a pale-bellied newt from the ringing dark water of a catch-basin, though mostly they leave the small things in peace.

More than anything else, they tend the sound.

They did not build the stonework—their blunt claws could never have shaped the copper-chiseled joints or knotted the hemp ropes—yet they keep it running with quiet, constant attention. When a bolster of moss swells too far over a lip and muffles a runnel, a broad, dark forelimb reaches out to scrape the channel clear, restoring the crisp metallic *clack*. When a shard of frost-loosened hornfels wedges into a sluice-gate, they lever it free and drop it into the void.

And it is through the plumbing that they speak to one another. A voice would be swallowed whole by the sixty-foot shafts, so they converse in altered water. From high on the eastern wall, one will dip a heavy leg into an aqueduct, breaking the flow for three slow breaths, or nudge a loose slab of slate an inch closer to a drop-shaft’s lip. Across the empty air, the rhythm of the basin stutters—a *thrum-chime-silence* that ripples through every wall and bridge—and moments later, from a shaded cistern on the far side of the amphitheater, another shifts a stone in answer.

Whether the generations of builders raised this hearthless, roofless city of bones and plumbing to house them, to call them up from the fault-fissures, or simply abandoned it to them when the last copper chisel wore down, the creatures do not seem to care. To them, the basin is not a ruin, and it is not empty. It is a single, vast instrument, and they live inside its chord.
