If you were to drift down through the slate-grey dimness until the cobble-field came into view, you would not see them at first. Even if a current parted the silt-cloud for three heartbeats and left the floor clear, you would only see what the valley wants you to see: egg-smooth boulders, iron-dark nodules, and the pale, crusty patches of coralline algae clinging to their leeward sides.

Then one of the nodules would cough.

It is a small, slow sound, barely louder than the *shhh* of sliding grit—a heavy contraction of muscle beneath a curved back, followed by a tight puff of compacted mud jetting out from a seam near the stone’s base. The silt settles back into the crevices. The nodule does not move again for ten minutes.

They are shaped by the same law that shapes the basin: anything sharp breaks, anything light blows away, and anything delicate is ground to paste. What lives here permanently—what belongs to the troughs rather than merely passing through them—is low, heavy, and built like the bedrock’s own stubborn kin.

When one finally unhooks itself from the cobble-lock, it looks less like a creature than a section of the floor deciding to travel. Its body is broad and flattened, three or four feet from blunt snout to stubby tail, armored across the back in thick, overlapping plates of cartilage and mineral-dense hide. Millennia of living in a tumbling mill have stripped away every ridge and spine; the plates are worn as smooth and dull as river-basalt, mottled in iron-black and rust-brown, scarred with pale, chalky scratches where rolling boulders have glanced off them in the dark. On the oldest individuals, stunted fronds of dark-red seaweed and crusts of coralline algae actually root in the seams between their back-plates, treating them as just another stone that rarely moves.

They have no use for eyes. Where eyes might have been, two shallow pits lie sealed beneath thick, translucent skin that can tell the bruised grey of noon from the pitch-black of midnight, and nothing more. Instead, their whole blunt face and lower jaw are stitched with rows of sensitive, slit-like pores. Through those slits, they read the valley the way a hawk reads air: they taste the faint salt-sweetness of a shredded kelp frond half a trench away, feel the sudden drop in pressure that heralds a cold current spilling off the shelf, and sense the exact weight and direction of a boulder rolling three yards behind them.

They do not swim. To lift off the floor here is to surrender to the eddies. Instead, they haul themselves along the troughs on four short, pillar-thick limbs tipped with blunt, wedge-shaped claws. Each step is a deliberate act of anchoring—jamming a claw into the narrow gap between two cobbles, pulling the heavy belly forward an inch, wedging the next limb before the current can pry them loose. Their bellies are pale, calloused, and surprisingly supple, able to press flush against the curve of a stone to seal out the grit.

Even their breathing is a battle against the broth. Feathery, exposed gills would clog with glacial flour in minutes. Their gills are tucked deep inside muscular collars behind the jaw; they drink the thick water in slow, rhythmic gulps, straining it through dense combs of stiff bristles before it ever reaches the delicate membranes. Every so often, when the combs pack tight with shell-dust and silt, the collar flexes and purges the plug in that quiet, muddy cough.

To stay heavy enough to hold the floor during the worst benthic squalls, they carry the valley inside themselves. Like the stones outside, their stomachs are packed with a fistful of swallowed cobbles—egg-smooth basalt and iron-nodules they have gulped down over the years. The stones give them ballast when the eddies twist, and they serve a second purpose: as the creature’s thick-walled gut churns, those stones grind together, pulverizing the hard-shelled things they pry from beneath the rocks—pale, blind clams, armored sea-slaters, and the brittle, mineral-rich carcasses of whatever drifts down from the shelves.

Sometimes, mixed in among the basalt in their bellies, there is a river-stone notched around its middle, or a snapped bone hook, still turning slowly in the dark, being polished even smoother than the current ever managed.

They do not understand the world above the ceiling of murk. To them, the valley has no surface—only an endless, cold weight overhead from which things occasionally fall. When a weighted wicker basket tumbles down through the gloom, scraping lightly across the cobbles in an eddy, it draws them out of their troughs. A hollow, fibrous rattle is unnatural down here; it tastes of rot and old wood and trapped bait. One will haul itself out of the gravel, pin the basket down with a heavy forelimb, and tear the softened lashings apart with blunt, stone-crushing teeth. It eats whatever drowned fish or sour meat remains inside, spits out the splintered willow, and leaves the rest to the valley’s slow grinding. 

Once in a great while, a pale, scarred octopus will have already claimed a fallen net-weight or a tangle of sinew, dragging it over the mouth of its crevice. The heavy-backed grazers rarely bother to contest it. They simply nose past the flint shield, tasting the octopus’s sharp, ink-bitter scent, and move on to the next gap.

When a true squall hits—when the hyper-dense currents pour down from the unseen edges and the walls of black silt rise to blind the water—the creatures do not flee. There is nowhere to flee to. They seek the deepest dishes in the bedrock, wedge their blunt claws into the locks between the largest boulders, tuck their throats into their collars, and go still. The silt buries them halfway. The cobble-fields shudder and creep around them. A rolling stone strikes a curved back-plate with a dull *tock* and bounces away into the dark. They wait it out, motionless as iron, letting the water press them harder into the floor.

Yet they are not entirely solitary, nor entirely silent.

In the quiet intervals between squalls, when the silt-clouds thin to a bruised grey and only the faint *shhh* of sliding gravel fills the trough, a sound sometimes carries across the basin that is not the current’s doing.

*Tock.* A pause. *Tock-tock.*

It is the same muted, flat pitch as two boulders knocking together—because that is what it is. A grazer, anchored in its trough, will lift a loose cobble in the crook of its forelimb—or simply rear its heavy, mineral-plated brow—and strike the bedrock beneath it in a slow, deliberate cadence. 

In a world where sight dies at arm’s length and scent is torn to ribbons by chaotic eddies, that *tock* is the only thing that travels straight. It is how they warn a rival off a rich seam of clams, how they keep track of one another across the miles-wide floor, and how, in the coldest stretch of the year, a male and a female find each other through a blinding wall of silt—following a rhythm that belongs to stone, yet refuses to be random.

When they breed, they do not trust the open floor. Together, they pry and shove at the base of the heaviest, most immovable boulders, clearing a shallow pocket in the grit beneath. There, sealed away from the sandblasting currents, the female lays a clutch of leathery, grey-shelled eggs, each one coated in mucus that immediately gathers a crust of mineral silt until it looks like nothing more than a cluster of small pebbles. Then the parents drag smaller cobbles across the opening, wedging them tight—not a monument, not a wall built to last, just a temporary lock that will hold until the next great squall tears it apart.

By then, the young have already hatched.

They are nothing like their parents at first. Pale as peeled willow, soft-skinned, no longer than a finger, they could not survive a single breath of the open cobble-field; the grit would flay them and the eddies would dash them to pulp. Instead, they drop straight down into the pitch-black labyrinth *beneath* the stones—into the narrow, silt-choked seams between bedrock and boulder where the currents never quite reach.

There they stay for years, threading through gaps no wider than a coin, blind and nimble, grazing on bacterial slime, pulverized shell-dust, and the thread-thin worms that live in the mud. Slowly, their hides thicken. Mineral salts from the water settle into their skin and harden into plates. They begin to swallow tiny grains of gravel, then pebbles, growing heavier season by season, until one day the crevices beneath the floor are too tight to hold them.

Then, in the quiet between two squalls, a young grazer wedges its blunt snout up between two egg-smooth boulders, pushes its way into the cold, bruised grey of the valley, and takes its place among the stones.
