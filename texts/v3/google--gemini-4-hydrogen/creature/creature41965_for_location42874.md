Nothing on the plateau hunts with speed, because nothing here can afford to waste water on a chase. Instead, everything that lives on this worn-down height is organized around a single, quiet economy: the catching, holding, and sharing of the night-mist.

The foundation of it all is a thin, nearly invisible bloom. Wherever the dampness lingers—on the pitted backs of the flat-backed pavement creatures, in the crevices of the limestone, even on the dry stalks of the grazers—a microscopic film of salt-lichen and pale algae wakes for a few hours each morning. It is this film that feeds the plateau. The tiny, gritty rasping you hear at midday is the sound of millions of mouths harvesting the backs of their neighbors, who are, at the exact same moment, being grazed by someone else. Everyone here is both pasture and grazer.

The flat-backed creatures that make up the living floor are the oldest of the small things. A single one may live for eighty or ninety years, wedged into the same hollow of chalky stone. They grow so slowly that their carapaces become indistinguishable from the bedrock, scarred by windblown grit and stained with ochre. When one finally dies, its soft, pleated underside is quietly dismantled in the dark by the blind, many-legged knots that unspool from the crevices. Its empty shell remains behind, locked into the mosaic, until the slow scraping of a thousand passing mouths wears it down to dust.

Above them, the papery, winged things hanging in curtains along the leeward ridges are not flyers. Their wings are too brittle for the open sky, veined like dried leaves and hooked permanently to the rock by tiny, calcified claws. They are mist-nets. Just before dawn, when the warm fog pools in the pans, the curtains unhook their lower edges and billow outward by an inch or two, trembling as the damp air passes through their membranes. Water beads along the fine veins and trickles down toward their small, tucked-away mouths. By mid-morning they have folded themselves flat again, rustling faintly as they dry into parchment.

Even the stalked grazers, standing in dense, motionless thickets along the basin rims, are shaped by this patience. Their calcified legs are hollow and porous, acting like wicks that draw up the brackish film-water from the pavement below. They have no eyes—only sensitive, pale tips on their long necks that can smell a damp crevice from ten feet away, and spend an entire afternoon leaning toward it.

And then there are the builders.

To see one from a distance is to mistake it for a slumped boulder that has somehow detached itself from a ridge. Up close, they are broad, low-slung animals the size of small oxen, built with the heavy, squared-off geometry of stone. Their hides are thick, dry, and deeply fissured like sun-baked clay; dust and salt pack into the creases of their shoulders, and the same faint green-gold film grows across their backs. They carry miniature gardens of the plateau wherever they go.

They move on four massive, pillar-like legs ending in wide, cushioned pads. When a builder steps across the living crust, its weight is spread so evenly that it does not crush the flat-backed creatures beneath—it merely presses them down into the dampness with a dull, hollow *clack*, and they shift back into place after it has passed. Tucked beneath the heavy overhang of its jaw are two shorter, jointed limbs ending in blunt, spatulate claws, and a pair of thick, stony mandibles that fold shut like cellar doors when not in use.

A builder’s face is mostly forehead: a broad, sloping shield of plated horn, scarred and polished from decades of pushing rock. Its eyes are tiny, milky-lidded, and set far back behind ridges of bone, useful for little more than telling the pale glare of the sky from the dark line of a terrace. A builder does not need to see far. Its real senses live in the soles of its wide feet, which feel the faintest tilt of the bedrock, and in the wet, cavernous nostrils tucked beneath its brow, which can smell a damp pan half a mile away.

They build because a flat plateau lets the morning sun and the dry air take the mist too quickly. 

A builder will spend a season—sometimes several—clearing loose cobbles from a shallow depression, nudging them with its horn-plated brow or hauling them short distances in its mandibles. It arranges them in a low, unmortared ring. The wall does not need to be tall; even a foot of piled limestone is enough to break the thin thermal drafts that rise at mid-morning, trapping the cool, wet air inside the circle for an extra hour or two. Within a well-built ring, the brackish pool lingers past noon. The algae grows thicker there. The pale, shell-less creepers gather in glistening ribbons along the inner stones, and the builder can lie with its belly pressed to the wet chalk, drinking through the porous skin of its underside while its broad tongue laps slowly at the thickened film.

The chest-high cairns scattered across the open miles are different. Those are not for trapping mist, but for making shade. A builder will stack flat stones one atop another over the course of years, wedging smaller pebbles into the gaps to keep them from toppling, until the pile casts a narrow, reliable wedge of shadow across the afternoon stone. In that moving stripe of cool air, a builder can rest without sealing its spiracles, or leave its young—blind, soft-shelled things no larger than a fist, which spend their first five years tucked into the damp gaps at the base of a cairn before their hides thicken enough for the open flats.

There is no hurry in their work, and no possessiveness. If a builder wanders away from its ring—drawn off by the smell of a fresher hollow, or simply following a gentle slope—another builder may eventually arrive and adopt it. Sometimes two builders meet at the same wall. There is no fighting; anger requires too much heat and breath. Instead, they stand forehead-to-forehead for hours, or even days, their cracked hides creaking softly as they shift their weight, breathing in each other’s mineral, damp scent. One might reach out with a blunt claw to wedge a loose stone tighter; the other might lean its heavy flank against its neighbor’s shoulder, sharing the moisture trapped in the deep creases of their skin.

They have no language of sound, only of pressure and permanence. A finished ring is a kind of message left for whoever comes next: *Here, the water stays.*

When a builder grows very old—so old that the fissures in its hide have widened into deep, dry canyons and its plated brow is worn smooth as river-glass—it stops looking for new stones. It finds a hollow, often the center of a ring it helped build decades ago, and tucks its heavy limbs beneath its belly. It pulls its mandibles shut. Slowly, over months, its breathing slows until it is indistinguishable from the breathing of the pavement around it.

The plateau reclaims it without ceremony. First come the blind knots from the crevices, then the pale creepers, then the flat-backed creatures, inching up the gentle slope of its flanks to lock themselves into the creases of its hide. The winged curtains hook their tiny claws into the ridges of its brow. By the time the limestone of its bones has begun to soften and settle, it is no longer a creature at all—just one more low, slumped swell on the horizon, holding a little shadow on its leeward side, waiting for the night-mist.
