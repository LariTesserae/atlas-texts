The people who live above the cloud-floor do not walk so much as they *ride* the world, knees bent, weight low, their centers of gravity kept close to the wood so a sudden gust cannot peel them off into the dark.

They are small-framed and long-armed, shaped by generations of thin air and verticality. Their skin—rarely touched by anything brighter than bruised indigo—is the color of wet slate or tallow, though you seldom see much of it. Against the persistent, numbing cold, they wrap themselves in layers of matted fiber harvested from the sagging water-pockets, felted dense and rubbed with the rendered fat of the cable-browsers until it sheds condensation like oiled leather. Over that go cloaks stitched from the wide, cartilage-stiffened membranes of gliders, sewn tight with bone needles and braided bast-cord. They smell strongly of tannin, wood-smoke, and the sour, sweetish tang of fermenting pith.

Their hands and feet are their true tools. Their boots are not stiff-soled; stiff leather slips on wet, waxy bark and hides the subtle trembling of the wood. Instead, they bind their feet in double layers of supple, cured glider-skin, leaving the soles thin enough that the deep bass thrum of a miles-long cable travels straight up through the arch, into the shin, and into the spine. Many of them have toes that have spread and toughened into wide, gripping pads, and their palms are crosshatched with pale, horn-hard calluses from hauling on fibrous struts and handling raw basalt.

Because the world is so loud—screaming stone, thrumming cables, booming bladders—the air is a poor medium for anything subtle. Inside the tiny, lashed shelters wedged into the lee of cable-junctions, they speak in a low, breathy murmur, pressing their foreheads or shoulders together when the gale rises so the sound passes bone-to-bone. Out on the spans, they do not speak at all. They listen with their jaws. A traveler pausing at a crossroads will kneel on the slick wood, bite down gently on a notched wooden toggle wedged into a bark-crevice, and close their eyes. Through their teeth comes the news of the quarter-mile ahead: the steady, heavy *scritch-thump* of a browser, the sharp, irregular chatter of a wind-loosed ice-crust, or the terrible, patient stillness of something sleek and blind waiting down the line.

They have a second language made entirely of strikes and plucks. Using knuckles, stone pommels, or bone strikers, they tap rhythmic codes into the taut secondary webs. A sharp triple-rap on a wrist-thick strut can carry for half a mile before the wind tears the vibration apart—enough to warn a neighboring shelter that a sleet-squall is building in the pitch of the lower spans, or to ask for an ember when a brazier has drowned in a sudden shift of mist.

Fire is the rarest, most jealously guarded thing they own. Everything around them is living tissue, damp on the surface yet rich with oils and dry, papery inner fibers; an uncontrolled flame would not merely burn a house, it would sever a bridge and drop an entire neighborhood into the abyss. So fire lives only inside hollowed-out blocks of basalt or thick, crude clay pots, fed with thumb-sized cakes of pressed, dried pith-meal. These cakes do not blaze; they smolder with a dense, oily, orange glow, giving off just enough heat to thaw stiffened fingers and boil the cold, tannin-dark water collected from the undersides of the cables. When a person travels between junctions, they carry a single coal inside a perforated bone cylinder tucked against their chest, feeding it tiny shreds of dry fungus-tinder to keep it breathing through the freezing dark.

***

The builders do not own the spans; they merely share them with older, better-adapted tenants.

Hanging beneath the great river-thick cables, where the sheltered wood weeps its steady trickle of dark water, live the **under-creepers**. Heavy, slow, and long-limbed, they look at first like swollen burls or tangled knots of dead vine. Their four limbs end in wide, calloused pads split around thick, curved keratin hooks that lock effortlessly into the leathery bark, allowing them to hang upside down in a gale without spending a muscle. Their pelts are coarse, greasy, and permanently damp, colonized by the same pale, faintly luminescent fungi they spend their lives scraping from the cable-joints. In the near-total dark, a herd of them looks like a constellation of sickly, greenish-white smudges creeping along the underside of the sky.

The people treat the under-creepers with a careful, unsentimental stewardship. They do not wipe out a cluster, because the creatures keep the heavy fungal mats from rotting the structural braids of the cables. Instead, a hunter will lower themselves beneath a span on a bast-rope, wait until a creeper is deeply absorbed in rasping a damp joint with its horn-rimmed tongue, and bleed it—taking a bladder of warm, fatty blood without killing the beast—or harvest an older, slower individual whose glowing pelt has grown thick enough to slow it down. Nothing is wasted: the fat greases cloaks and softens wood-stiffened skin; the hooked pads are boiled down into a glue for binding stone blades to bone hafts; the pale fungal scrapings from their backs are smeared onto the sharp edges of basalt spires to mark safe handholds in the dark.

Above the cables, in the roaring emptiness between the spires, belong the **gliders**. They are scarcely more than stretched leather, hollow bone, and fierce, territorial muscle. When they roost in the dry, razor-edged clefts of the young basalt, they fold their wide, membrane-winged arms tight against their ribs and hang by hooked thumbs, looking like bundles of wet charcoal rags. When they launch into the void, they drop like stones until the rising thermal catches them, snapping their membranes taut with a sound like a cracked whip.

They are completely indifferent to the dim indigo rim of the sky. A glider sees with its tongue, snapping out sharp, flint-hard clicks that ricochet off the flat faces of the slate and the taut catenary arcs of the flora. To stand on a bridge while a flock of them hunts the updrafts is to be pelted by invisible percussive needles—a dry, frantic *tick-tick-tick-clack* that seems to come from inside your own skull. They hunt the swarms of damp-winged insects that pour out of the pith-nodes at shift-of-wind, scooping them from the air with wide, whiskered mouths. The builders catch gliders by stringing weighted nets of fine bast-cord across the narrow wind-gaps between spires, waiting until a squall drives the flyers low. A single netted glider means a new waterproof roof-patch, a dozen needles from its wing-struts, and strips of dark, stringy meat that can be smoked over a pith-cake brazier until they are as hard and lasting as horn.

And then there are the **jaw-listeners**—the reason no one walks alone if they can help it, and the reason every shelter has a heavy slate windbreak that can be wedged shut from the inside.

Sleek, low-slung, and entirely blind, they have abandoned eyes altogether; where sockets might have been, there is only smooth, scarred hide stretched tight over bone. Their skulls are long, flat, and curiously wide at the hinge, turning their lower jaws into living tuning forks. A jaw-listener moves along the tops and sides of the cables in swift, silent, flowing bursts, its belly nearly brushing the wet wood, its long, splayed toes cushioned with thick, spongy pads that make almost no sound. Every few yards it freezes, lays its chin flat against the bark, and drinks in the world’s vibrations. It can tell the heavy, four-beat drag of an under-creeper from the light, erratic patter of falling sleet, and it knows the rhythmic two-step of a builder a quarter-mile away.

To outwit a jaw-listener, the people use the world’s own noise against it. When a hunter must cross an exposed span where a predator has been felt, they wait for the weather. As a gust strikes the colossal, papery bladders hanging beneath the main cables, the organs swell and collapse with a deep, chest-rattling *boom* that shakes the entire bridge. In that brief wash of thunder—while the wood is saturated with low-frequency violence—the traveler sprints three or four paces, then freezes flat against the bark before the drumbeat fades. Others carry small, weighted wooden clackers tied to long cords, tossing them onto a parallel strut to send false footsteps echoing away into the mist while they slip past in the opposite direction.

***

Even inside the flora itself, life crowds into the dark.

The hollow cores of the great spans are a world apart from the freezing wind outside—a cramped, pitch-black labyrinth of spongy pith, weeping sap-channels, and slow, yeasty warmth. Here live the **pith-borers**: fat, pale, blind grubs and damp-winged, chittering beetles that chew endless galleries through the softer tissue, seeding the walls with the very microbes that keep the plant’s metabolism fermenting and warm. Where a colony grows thick, the wood becomes soft and fever-hot, steaming faintly where it meets the freezing outside air. The people seek out these warm knots like oases, cutting small, plugged hatches into the outer bark to warm their hands, or scooping out handfuls of the oily, fermented pith-mash—grubs and all—to press into fuel cakes and bitter, nourishing porridge.

Even the colossal **barometric bladders** that hang beneath the bridges are not empty. These vast, thin-walled chambers, big as small houses, expand and contract with every shift in the high-altitude pressure, groaning and booming in the dark. Clinging to their damp, pleated ceilings are colonies of pale, papery parasites—flat, disc-shaped things no wider than a palm, with dozens of tiny suction-feet—that feed on the mist-water condensing inside the organs. When a terrible sleet-squall hits, and a lashed shelter threatens to tear loose from its basalt cleft, a desperate family will sometimes slit a narrow flap into the side of a resting bladder, crawl inside its warm, humid belly, and lace the flap shut behind them with bone toggles. There they wait out the storm in the pitch dark, curled together on the swaying, papery floor while the chamber breathes in and out around them like the lung of a sleeping whale, booming deafeningly every time the gale strikes its outer wall.

***

Life here has no center, no capital, no memory older than a few generations of knotted cords. A junction-village may hold six people, or twelve, tucked into the lee of a braided spire, tending their tiny orange embers and scraping their fungal gardens. They know the neighboring junctions by the pitch of the cables that connect them—*the high, tight bridge to the north*, *the slack, groaning span toward the wind-wall*—and they measure distance not in miles, but in how many times a traveler must stop to warm their coal.

When someone dies, there is no earth to bury them in, and no spare fuel to burn them. The survivors strip the body of everything that cannot be grown or knapped: the bone needles, the flint blade, the oily glider-skin cloak, the wooden toggle worn smooth by a lifetime of biting cables. Then they carry the body out to the windward edge of a span, tie a sharp shard of basalt to its ankles so it will fall clean past the lower webs without snagging, and let it go into the black air.

Yet nothing is truly gone for long in a place so sparse. Sometimes a body slips its stone and catches in a forgotten, lower tangle of fibrous struts, miles below the main bridges. Within weeks, the damp-winged insects find it; the pale, glowing fungi creep over the skin; and the under-creepers gather in the dark to graze, turning the lost traveler into a faint, cold lantern hanging over the abyss, visible from the high spans on clear nights like a fallen star that never quite hit the ground.
