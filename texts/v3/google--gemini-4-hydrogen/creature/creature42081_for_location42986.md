If you stand on the rim long enough, your eyes adjust to the constant, quiet motion below, and you begin to notice the things that do not move quite like the rest.

At first, they are indistinguishable from the clutter. A length of thick, slate-colored rubber wedged between two brick cistern-rims turns out, a minute later, to have shifted against the pull of a reversing channel. What looks like a pale mineral collar crusted around the base of an iron piling detaches itself without a splash and slips beneath the surface of the brine, leaving the pipe bare.

Nothing here announces itself. Whatever lives in the basin has learned the same rule as the mist and the greased sluice gates: to make no sound, and to hold no shape for long.

When a bank of dark silt suddenly blisters and slides away, you might catch one fully exposed in the cool, even daylight—not startled, not fleeing, simply adjusting to the loss of its cover. It is low-slung and heavy, roughly the size of a large seal, though built with none of a seal’s sleekness. Like the ironwork and the eaten brick around it, it looks as though it has been worn smooth by decades of grit and water. Its hide is thick, pliable, and entirely hairless, the texture of weathered inner-tube rubber—dark slate along its broad back, bruised pale gray on its underside where it drags across porcelain and mud. Pale mineral salts gather in the creases of its shoulders when it rests, only to be sheared away the next time it squeezes between a copper float and a leaning pole.

It has four heavy, splayed limbs, the front pair disproportionately long and muscular. They end not in claws, which would catch and tear in the dense tangle of cables, but in broad, three-lobed pads of tough, grippy tissue. When the ground beneath it liquefies, it presses these pads flat against the nearest curving pipe or tarred timber stilt, bracing effortlessly until the surge passes. Behind it, a thick, strap-like tail coils loosely around a rusted piling, anchoring it against the sudden, silent tug of the brine.

In a world where the water is clouded with silt every few minutes and the air drinks up sound, eyes are of little use. Its face is blunt and broad, the eyes reduced to small, sunken pits behind thick, grit-scratched membranes that catch the occasional amber or green blink of an indicator lamp without seeming to care. Instead, its awareness sits in the pleated, fleshy hood along its jaw and the rows of tiny pores running down its flanks. With these, it tastes the salt and the leaked machine-oil in the water; it reads the faint electrical hum bleeding from the sagging black cables overhead; and it feels, through its bones, the sub-audible vibration of the brushless motors.

They do not tend the machines—they have no use for the work the machines are trying to do—but they live inside their rhythms as other animals live inside the tides.

One will drape itself over the counterweight of a tall steel tripod, riding the slow, greased glide down the guide-wire to reach a newly scoured pool without wasting its strength against the mud. Another will wedge itself beneath the tarred timber pump-house in the center of the basin, pressing its pale belly against the oil-filled housing of a motor to soak up the faint, steady warmth in the cold wet. When an automated dredger-arm dips into a channel, lifts nothing, and swings away, it sometimes carries a heavy, motionless passenger curled around its hydraulic elbow, dropping quietly back into the brine at the far end of its arc.

Most of all, they listen to the gauges and the rams.

They cannot read the twitching needles behind the glass domes, of course, but they feel the sudden tension in the steel tripods and the rapid, frantic adjustments of the hydraulic pistons a heartbeat before the slate below gives way. The machines react to the subterranean pressure first; the creatures simply follow the machines. When a motor’s sub-audible hum deepens and a nearby ram twitches tight, two or three of them will already be converging silently through the murky channels, threading between the rubberized conduits like heavy, patient eels.

When the fissure finally blows and a wall of silt collapses, they are there in the clearing water, probing the newly exposed brass valves and porcelain insulators with their blunt, sensitive snouts. They feed on what the pressurized upwelling forces out from beneath the slate—blind, pale shrimp, sulfur-worms, and pockets of mineral-rich sludge—as well as the solitary crabs and thin eels caught off-guard by the shift. They eat without thrashing, pulling their prey into the quiet gaps beneath the cisterns.

There are perhaps a half-dozen of them in the hollow, maybe a few more, though you rarely see more than one at a time. There is no room here for herds, and no need for noise. Yet they are not wholly solitary. When two cross paths in a narrow, clear-running sluice—one bracing against the brickwork, the other sliding over a submerged cable—they will pause for a fraction of a second and press their broad, pore-lined flanks together. A low, wordless thrum passes between them, vibrating through the wet iron and slate at the exact pitch of the motors, indistinguishable from the basin’s own pulse. Then the brine clouds over again, and they separate, slipping back into the machinery.

Standing on the rim, watching the ceaseless, quiet churn below, it is easy to believe the basin is empty—just a forgotten engine tearing itself apart and rebuilding in the daylight. Yet these creatures are as native to it as the weeping stone: neither its masters nor its caretakers, just the slow, grit-worn life that has learned to breathe in the silence between the gears.
