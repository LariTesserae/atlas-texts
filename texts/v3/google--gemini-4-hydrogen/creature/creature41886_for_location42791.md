If you dropped into the cleft and held your breath long enough for the cold to settle into your joints, you would not notice them at first. Against the tallow-colored stems and the dripping curtains of root-hair, they look like nothing more than knots of living bark, or swellings where two gourds have fused.

Then a knot detaches itself from a doorway, reaches for a wedged flint scraper, and begins to pare back the moss.

They are small—no taller than a forearm from elbow to wrist—and built low and dense, with heavy shoulders and long, narrow ribs that taper to a short, thick waist. Their skin is neither furred nor scaled, but matte, pale, and faintly waxy, the color of wet ash or peeled tuber. It exudes a thin, bitter oil that keeps the mites from boring into their joints and makes the grazing flatworms turn aside when they brush past in the dark. Around their wrists and ankles, where they grip the braided stems, the skin thickens into dark, peat-stained calluses.

They have no use for the thin grey thread of light far above, except as a clock that barely ticks. Their eyes are small, sunken beneath heavy brow-ridges, and filmed over with a milky membrane that registers only the faintest shift between the dimness of the upper cleft and the true black below. Instead, they read the pocket through the broad, slit-like nostrils set low on their flat faces, through the sensitive, pore-lined ridges along their jaws and forearms, and most of all through their hands.

Their hands are long, five-fingered, and astonishingly deliberate. Every movement they make has the unhurried precision of things that run cold. Because they carry no fire—there is nothing dry enough to burn here, and smoke in a sealed pocket would kill everything in it—their bodies take the temperature of the weeping basalt. They do not shiver, and they do not rush. To hurry in this cold is to waste food you cannot easily replace.

They have no voices. Their throats are smooth and un-notched, built only for slow, shallow breathing. When they need to speak across the empty spaces between districts, they tap. A knuckle against a hollow gourd-wall, the butt of a flint scraper struck twice against a taut root-cable—the living city carries the sound. A district answers itself in a quiet, woody Morse of clicks and dampened thuds, traveling along the stems without ever rising loud enough to stir the flat-headed newts in the cisterns below. Up close, in the dark of a woven chamber, they press the damp flats of their wrists together, or trace slow, deliberate patterns across one another’s palms.

Everything they do is a negotiation with growth and rot.

A household is not a permanent thing; it is a space kept open by stubbornness. You will find them crouched on the thresholds of their gourd-chambers, knees drawn up to their chins, methodically shaving away the pale new shoots that try each week to seal the doorway shut. When a terrace tilts because the tuber beneath it has drunk too much mineral water, three or four of them will gather with notched bone levers and sinew cord, wedging splints of reed beneath the weave to level the floor for another season. They do not fight the plants, and they do not fight the swarming fauna. They treat the city’s creatures the way coastal farmers treat the weather.

When the nightly tide of grazing slugs rises from the lower rot—triggered by the faint chill seeping down from the outside dark—the builders do not try to slaughter them. They simply climb to the upper tiers, draw up the lower root-ladders, and wait, listening to the soft, wet rasp of a thousand radulae scraping the lower mats clean of old fungus. They leave the peppercorn-sized beetles alone to patrol the stem-joints for wood-mites. They sweep the pale amphipods off the walkways with brushes of split sedge, tipping them into woven baskets to be mashed with salt-grit into a cold, grey paste. Even the blind newts in the stepped cisterns are tolerated, nudged aside with a bone prod only when one wedges its flat head into a reed sluice and threatens to flood a lower garden.

Yet they are not entirely sealed off from the world above.

At long intervals, when the air tasting of salt grows sharp with frost and the grey thread above goes completely black—a sign of heavy fog or a moonless winter night—the lightest and quickest among them climb the upper root-curtains toward the hairline cleft. To them, the coastal shelf outside is a terrifying place: vast, dry enough to crack their waxy skin, roaring with wind and surf, and blinding even under starlight. They move along the frost-rimed sedge like pale shadows, gathering only what the wet pocket cannot grow: wave-tumbled cobbles of beach flint to knap into scrapers, dry thorns from coastal scrub to pin their bryophyte roofs, the sun-bleached bones of dead gulls and voles, and scraps of dried sinew. Before the sky begins to pale, they drop back through the seam, sealing themselves once more into the quiet, dripping dark.

Their young begin life in the water, like the larvae in the cisterns, though kept apart in the highest, cleanest basins where the aqueducts first catch the wall-seepage. For their first season, the young are small, translucent things with feathery external gills, swimming silently in the mineral-cold dark while older kin drop crushed amphipods into the water for them. Only when their gills shrink and their waxy hides thicken do they haul themselves over the woven basin-rims, shivering slightly in the open air for the first time, to be guided along the braided-stem bridges by the hand.

And when one of them grows too stiff with age to pare back their own doorway, or falls from a mucus-slick bridge into the void below, there is no grave to dig in bare basalt. Their flint tools and bone pins are taken by their kin—nothing worked by hand is ever thrown away—and their body is carried down to the lowest, untouched layer of the pocket. There, they are tucked deep beneath the living root-mat, into the slow, cold-fermenting peat of dead plant generations.

Within a season, the tallow-colored stems above them thicken and swell, drinking what is left, and another chamber is hollowed from the living wall.
