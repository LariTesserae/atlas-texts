Because nothing here can afford to waste energy on eyes, everything that lives in the basin is built to listen, to taste the static on its skin, and to endure the slow, crushing weight of the dark.

Start at the level of the cables and the stone, where life is packed so tightly into the damp air that you cannot wave a hand without brushing against it.

The sagging catenaries that string the machines together are never bare rubber for long. Wrapped around the thick insulation are long, ribbon-bodied colonists—pale, eyeless, and the texture of wet chamois—that wind themselves in tight spirals along the copper runs. They do not eat flesh or vegetation; they feed on the leak. Wherever high voltage hisses silently into the saturated air, these sleeves thicken to the width of a human thigh, growing warm and faintly turgid as they drink the stray current. If you press your palm against a stanchion in the dark, you feel them pulsing in a slow, peristaltic rhythm, milking the wire. Every few minutes, a bead of warm, waxy tallow sweats out of their pores and drips—*plip*—into the ankle-deep water below, leaving a faint, greasy sheen that smells like scorched lard.

Down in the brine vats, where the long-stroke beam pumps dip with geological indifference, other things cling to the submerged iron. They are half-mineral, half-cartilage: blind, encrusted colonies that precipitate salt straight out of the black water to build jagged, articulated shells around the pump rods. When the beam lifts them out of the vat into the body-temperature air, they hiss and crackle as their dry salt-crusts drink the moisture, shifting their weight with dry, grinding clicks before the next slow stroke plunges them back under.

Between the machines and the razor-flint, life has had to make peace with two things: the frayed edges of cause and effect, and the fact that the valley is stacked like wet pages.

Consider the pale, rubbery ambushers—fat as a forearm, boneless, and ringed around the mouth with copper-tasting palps—that coil around the crooked iron stanchions just above the waterline. They can hang motionless for hours, indistinguishable from a bundle of spare cable. Then, without warning, one will uncoil into empty air and snap its jaws shut on nothing at all. Three seconds later, a velvet-winged flyer, streaming down the static corridor of the power line, swerves straight into the volume of space where those teeth already are. The ambusher is not fast; it has no need for speed. In a basin where a splash arrives before the stone leaves your hand, it simply strikes at the thermal echo of a collision that has not happened yet.

The tall, stilt-legged waders use the same frayed seam of time to survive the stone. Beneath the tepid water, the basalt and black flint are sharp enough to open a boot to the bone, yet the waders step through the marsh at a steady, deliberate *plip… plip…* without ever cutting their whisker-fringed pads. If you stand close enough to feel the warm displacement of air around their knees, you can sometimes feel them hesitate—a sudden, sharp twitch of muscle, a sideways shift of weight—several seconds *before* their foot even descends toward a submerged blade. They feel the sting of the cut ahead of the cut itself, and step around the pain before it arrives.

And then there are the ones who tend the tallow.

You do not meet them all at once, because they do not keep all of themselves in the same layer of the valley.

You might be wading across a flat stretch of marsh, listening to the distant *tock* of a relay, when you walk into a pocket of air that is slightly too warm—not the ambient, tepid dampness of the basin, nor the sharp, dry heat of a transformer, but a heavy, animal warmth, smelling faintly of boiled salt, old leather, and hot grease. If you reach out, your fingers meet a column of thick, pleated hide, slick with mineral oil and planted firmly in the ankle-deep water. Its base splays out over the razor-edged basalt like vulcanized tire-tread, indifferent to the stone, anchored in the bacterial slime.

Follow that limb upward with your hands, and it goes wrong. Above the knee, the solid flesh thins out—not tapering, but losing density, becoming spongy and half-present, like wet smoke or a reflection in oil—until your hand passes straight through it into empty air, or bumps instead against the wet, weeping face of a slate ridge that shouldn’t be standing in the middle of the marsh.

Their torsos, their broad, eyeless heads, and their slow-beating hearts are tucked sideways into the adjacent folds of the valley—quieter, drier pages of the wet vellum where there are no submerged busbars and no cruel stone. They live across the grain, keeping their vitals in the empty rooms next door and pushing only their working parts into this layer to do what needs doing.

You hear them before they arrive. There is a heavy, wet *sluck*—like a boot pulled from deep mud—as a shoulder or a long, multi-jointed arm slides into phase beside a sunken concrete pit. A bellows-like *shhhk* of breath leaks through the overlap, carrying the smell of damp fur and scorched copper. Then huge, blunt-fingered hands—without nails, tipped instead with dense, calloused pads—reach into the dark to scoop pale tallow from an iron bucket and smear it patiently along the teeth of a barn-sized flywheel as it inches past.

If your hand were ever to slip across the phase-line into the layer where they keep their faces, you would feel no eyes, no nose, no mouth—only a wide, unbroken plane of tough, rubbery skin, flanked along the jaw by two rhythmic gill-slits that draw in the heavy air, and crowned with a tangle of fine, wet filaments that sway continuously toward the nearest high-voltage line, drinking in the static hum.

They do not speak. Sound dies too quickly in the saturated dark, and they are often miles apart. Instead, they talk through load and arc. When one of them needs to summon another across the flat water, it leans its weight against a sagging catenary cable, or drops an iron bar across a pair of copper busbars. Miles away, another watcher—resting half-in, half-out of a quiet slate ridge—feels the sudden dip in the sub-audible hum through its jawbones, or smells the sharp bloom of ozone twenty minutes before the bar is even dropped. It pulls its arms out of the dry dark and begins wading toward the signal before the sender has even decided to make it.

To them, the machines are not machines; they are simply the slowest, heaviest animals in the basin. A flywheel turning once every twenty minutes is a tide. A beam pump dipping into a brine vat is a lung. They tend the tallow and clear the salt-crusts from the rods with the same unhurried, cyclical patience that the filter-feeders use to fan the oily water. There is no rush, because in a place where effects arrive ahead of their causes, hurrying makes no sense. They are already standing beside a leaking pipe with a wad of greasy oakum three seconds before the iron splits; they wipe the tallow from their palms twenty minutes after the gear teeth have turned away.

They do not die so much as they settle.

When one of them grows too old or too heavy with absorbed salt to pull its mass back across the overlapping shorelines, it simply stops shifting. Its pillar-like legs remain planted on the razor-flint in this layer, slowly calcifying as the brackish water weeps over its ankles, while pale, rootless mats of fungal felt climb up its shins to soak up the last of its stray heat. Its hidden half cools down over months in the quiet layer next door, until the stilt-legged waders step carefully around it in the dark, treating it as just another crooked iron stanchion holding up the heavy air.

And if you stand still long enough in the dark—listening to the water drip from steel onto water, feeling the faint hum in your molars—you realize the valley is never empty, not even for a yard. Between the clicking silica-weed, the silent ribbons of flyers brushing your forehead, and the slow, unseen bulk of a tender resting half a phase away from your chest, the dark is crowded to bursting with things that have forgotten the sun, all of them breathing in the exact same space, waiting together for the next slow cycle to turn.
