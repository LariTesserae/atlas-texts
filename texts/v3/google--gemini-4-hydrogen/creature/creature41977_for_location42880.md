If you stand on one of the cantilevered walkways long enough for your eyes to adjust to the bruised amber-violet light, you begin to realize that the city is not empty. You were simply looking for things that walk, or fly, or leave tracks in the wet silt.

Look instead into the recessed balconies of the modular chambers, tucked back out of the direct sheet of the rain. Look along the undersides of the weightless terraces, where the moss hangs thickest, and down into the dark stone basins at the bottom of the void.

At first, they look like part of the infrastructure—heavy, rounded manifolds of wet basalt, or thick coils of dark-gray conduit draped over the railings to bleed off heat. They are roughly the size of a curled human, though some on the lower tiers are broader, slumped across the porous stone like great, folded mantles of glistening hide. Their outer skins are the exact matte-charcoal of freshly cleaved rock, slick with condensation and veined with faint, branching threads of dull, oxidized copper. They have no eyes, and no faces to turn toward you. In a place where the light never rises above a dim, pulsing bruise, sight is a waste of energy.

They do not move on their own because movement, here, would be a distraction. They are creatures of stillness and pressure, built to be *acted upon* by the chamber.

Along their flanks run deep, fluted folds—halfway between gills and acoustic membranes—that remain tight and nearly invisible in the quiet minutes between the rain-cycles. When a warm draft rises from the lower grates, carrying the sharp scent of crushed fern and ozone, those flutes part by a fraction of an inch, drinking the saturated air directly into copper-rich tissues. That is where the metallic tang in the chamber comes from; the air isn’t just carrying the taste of the machinery. It is carrying their breath.

Wait for the water-lens above you to grow too heavy.

A half-second before the invisible containment field gives way, the things on the balconies *tighten*. You can see it if you are watching closely: a subtle, synchronized hunching of their dense, cartilaginous frames against the stone. Then the field releases, and a ton of warm water drops thirty meters through the empty core, exploding against the basins below.

The booming crash rolls up the tiers, shaking the mist out of the air—and *that* is when they are most alive. The shockwave passes straight through the cantilevered stone and into their bodies, rattling their internal fluids, driving dissolved minerals and oxygen deep into their membranes. In the basins at the bottom, where the deluge strikes hardest, smaller, flatter forms—pale and translucent, almost indistinguishable from the swirling slate-water—press themselves against the dark stone floor, letting the violent churn scour their backs clean before settling back into invisibility as the water calms.

Up on the tiers, in the ringing silence after the crash, they speak to one another.

Those hair-thin, matte-black filaments spanning the gulf between the walkways are not part of the city’s power grid. Each one is anchored into the recessed chambers, pressed directly against the sensitive, copper-veined ridges along the occupants’ backs. When one of them shifts its internal pressure or warms a patch of its skin by half a degree, a faint thermal ripple travels across the misty void, vibrating the filament. They are carrying on a slow, dense conversation in heat and micro-tremors—a chorus of tiny, feverish pulses passing back and forth over the roaring emptiness, completely undetectable to anyone listening for voices.

The city was not built for no one. It was carved recently, and switched on in haste, because they needed a place where the rock was still sharp and unweathered, where no insects would foul the filaments, and where the rain would never stop falling. Whatever dry, sunlit, or airless place they came from, they came down here to soak—to soften their brittle, dormant frames in the warm, thunderous dark, and to wait out a cycle that has only just begun.

To an outsider, the walkways are immaculate and deserted. To the things curled in the wet corners of the balconies, the chamber is crowded, loud with conversation, and humming with the exact, violent weather they need to wake up.
