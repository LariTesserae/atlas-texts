If you stand on one of the middle catwalks long enough, you begin to realize that the valley is not empty—it is simply *inward*.

The figures you occasionally glimpse in oiled canvas coats are not lone watchmen patrolling a ruin. They are the exterior limbs of a population that lives almost entirely inside the thickness of the place, tucked into the dry pockets between the wet stone and the humming iron.

When one of them steps out onto a gantry to grease a bearing or clear a fern-choked sluice, they look, at first, like part of the weather. Their coats are stiff with linseed oil and dark wax, colored like wet slate or bog-moss, buttoned high to the chin with brass clasps gone green from the mist. They wear wide-brimmed oilskin hats or deep hoods that shed the constant drip from the viaducts above, and heavy boots studded with brass or grooved rubber to grip the slick iron grates. You rarely see their faces clearly; in the flat gray fog or the amber halo of a sodium lamp, they are mostly a silhouette, a pair of chapped, oil-stained hands working quickly with a spanner, and then the soft *clack* of a heavy door latching behind them.

Follow one through that misty doorway, though, and the valley turns inside out.

Behind the swollen timber and gasketed iron hatches, there are no streets and no ground floors. There are only *pockets*. A family’s kitchen might be wedged into the dry spandrel beneath a stone viaduct arch, its ceiling curving upward with the masonry. A sleeping-loft sits directly above a brick boiler-house, where the floorboards stay faintly warm all year round and smell of dry mortar and hot wool. Drying-rooms—essential here, where nothing outside ever truly dries—are strung with iron racks of stiff coats dripping steadily into stone-lined floor gutters that feed straight back into the valley’s drainage.

The people who live in these pockets are lean, quiet, and built for stairs. Generations of climbing steep service flights and balancing on narrow conveyor bridges have given them thick calves, a low center of gravity, and an unconscious habit of keeping one hand near a railing even when standing in a flat room. Their skin is pale—the color of peeled willow or damp chalk—because the sun here is only a slate-gray thinning of the clouds, and their eyes are accustomed to low contrast: reading the difference between wet iron and black algae by the glow of a caged twenty-watt bulb.

They do not shout. In a gorge where saturated air and thick mats of moss swallow a human voice before it travels twenty paces, shouting is both useless and exhausting. Instead, they speak in a low, flat register pitched just beneath the hum of the step-down transformers and the *shhh* of the spillways. Out on the open catwalks, they often don’t speak at all. They communicate by tapping rhythms onto the galvanized handrails with a knuckle or a wrench—*tink-tink, pause, tink*—sending messages cleanly through the steel trusses from one tier to the next.

Because the fog can slam across the cleft in a single breath, cutting off one slope from the other, they also use the valley’s own machinery to stay close. A grandmother tending the upper glasshouses will tuck a jar of pickled fern-shoots, a bundle of dry willow-bark, or a pair of mended woollen socks into the corner of an empty return-skip on the aerial cableway, clip a grease-penciled slate tag to the rim, and let the copper wire carry it through the mist to her grandson working the lower sluice-gates.

The glass-paned cultivation halls are the warmest, brightest places in their world, and the closest thing they have to town squares. Inside, the ten-degree chill gives way to a thick, lukewarm humidity smelling of wet loam, crushed leaf, and warm glass. Here, the heavy canvas coats come off, hanging in rows by the airlock doors, revealing layers of felted wool in lichen-green and undyed gray. Because there are no bees in the high fog, the elders and the children spend quiet hours moving along the raised beds with small squirrel-hair brushes, pollinating the blossoms by hand or shaking the willow catkins, while the broad-leaved greens and pale roots grow under the milky sky.

Their days are not measured by sunlight, which barely changes from morning to afternoon, but by the rhythm of the apparatus. Everyone in the valley knows the hour by what is moving past their walls: crushed slate grinding down the covered conveyors in the early shift, damp timber at midday, crates of harvested greens gliding overhead in the late afternoon before the sodium lamps brighten. Every twelve minutes, the main sluice on the fourth tier shifts, sending a deeper *shhh* through the retaining walls; every hour, the east viaduct’s counterweight drops with a muffled *thud* you feel in the soles of your boots.

Over lifetimes, this constant mechanical texture becomes a sixth sense. To an outsider, the valley is just a soft, wet hum. To someone born above a transformer bay, that hum has a hundred distinct voices. A tender can wake from a dead sleep because a bearing three trusses away has begun to dry out—not loud enough to hear as a screech, just a slight tightening in the singing of a steel cable, a tiny grit in the vibration of the bedframe.

And they are not entirely alone in the mist, though the non-human life here is as quiet and specialized as they are.

Around the caged sodium lamps cling pale, heavy-bodied moths with thick, furry thoraxes built for the cold. They do not flutter frantically against the glass; they sit on the warm wire cages like scraps of wet ash, conserving their strength until the rare bright noon. In the dry spaces tucked beneath the corrugated steel walkways, a handful of solitary blackbirds have forgotten that trees exist, weaving their nests out of roof-moss, frayed hemp rope, and snippets of scrap copper wire. Even their songs have adapted: short, low whistles that sound almost indistinguishable from a hydraulic valve bleeding pressure.

Deeper down, in the bottom-most drainage tiers where the runoff from the boiler-houses keeps the water tepid and ice-free, live slow, slate-colored eels and pale, near-blind amphipods. They inhabit the stone-lined gutters and the dark settling-tanks, grazing on the mineral algae and the grubs washed down from the cultivation halls. The tenders never trap or kill them; they call them *pipe-clearers*, and consider it bad luck to scoop one out of a sump, knowing that without them the intake screens would choke in a week.

And then there are the plants—the moss, the giant ferns, the creeping ivy—which the people do not treat as scenery, nor quite as weeds, but as a slow, green neighbor with whom they keep an endless truce. They scrape it away from the rail-switches, the weep-holes, and the moving chains, and everywhere else they let it grow, grateful for the way its sodden cushions drink up the rain and quiet the iron.

In the highest, driest vaults of the retaining walls sit the relay-rooms, where the oldest tenders take their turns at the indicator boards. There is no grand command here—no one drives the valley. It mostly drives itself through float-valves, gravity, and clockwork timers. The watchers simply sit in the warm amber light, mending old schematic books, drinking boiled-leaf tea, and listening to the click of relays and the pulse of water behind the stone, ready to step back out into the fog whenever the mountain pushes a little too hard against the iron.
