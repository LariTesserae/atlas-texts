At first, you might mistake them for another quirk of the architecture—a cluster of pale, jointed struts shifting along a tower’s flank, or a wet knot of dark vine pausing on a balcony before the next cable snaps into place.

They are built for the same physics as the towers, and they move at the same restless, intermittent tempo. When one steps out from behind a rib of calcified cellulose onto a swaying terrace, you see a tall, remarkably narrow figure, all long levers and wet sheen. Their skin has the same softened, water-smoothed pallor as the basin’s soapstone rim, mottled faintly across the shoulders and shins with copper-green freckles where moss-spores have taken hold in their pores. They wear no cloth—anything woven would rot in a week in this muggy, steaming air—only a sparse harness of live root-tendon that the towers occasionally tighten or re-braid as they pass. From the crown of the head down between the shoulder blades grows a thick mantle of dark, rubbery filaments, slick with condensation, which bristle slightly whenever a gravity-lens hums nearby.

Watch their feet as they cross the open marsh between towers. Their toes are long, wide-set, and webbed halfway to the knuckle, spreading their weight across the springy root-mat so that only a shallow ring of tea-colored water weeps up around their heels. Their knees and wrists are subtly double-hinged, allowing them to lock into stillness on a trembling pad of moss, or fold their limbs flat against their ribs when they hitch a ride up one of the vertical water-columns—slipping an arm straight into the shimmering, gravity-defying pillar and letting the upward current haul them, dripping and weightless, toward the upper tiers.

They do not speak into the air. In a bowl this small and this loud—with the bass thrum of the lenses, the heavy patter of condensation, and the ceaseless *clack* of cables—airborne voices would be drowned before they crossed a single terrace. Instead, the flat, glossy membranes on either side of their skulls are sealed tight against the din, tuned only to low vibrations carried through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. Out in the glare of the white sky, they converse in sharp, economical gestures and percussive taps: two quick raps of a hardened fingernail against a lignin railing, a flat palm slapped against a tensioned cable to send a coded shudder down the bridge.

Their real speech waits for the dark.

Whenever a bank of broad, translucent leaves along a tower’s flank snaps shut like louvers, plunging a lower gallery into sudden, green-tinted shadow, the figures nearby converge on it at once. Inside that brief pocket of dimness, the pale, flat sacs along their throats and collarbones begin to pulse—not with the sharp geometric flashes of the ribbon-finned fish below, but with slow, overlapping washes of amber and faint violet light. They stand close, faces inches apart, reading one another’s chests in silence while the workshop noise roars outside the closed leaves.

It is in these shaded galleries, and inside the newly hardened bladder-chambers during their few minutes of existence, that the real business of the basin happens. Nothing here can be hoarded, and nothing dry can last, so their wealth and their memory are kept liquid. You will see a pair of them reach into a passing water-column, pluck out a glowing packet of nutrient and data, and cradle it between their four palms. As the sphere’s light flickers against their wet faces, they are reading—not words, exactly, but the accumulated ledger of the basin: which tower is growing too heavy on its southern roots, where a cable-knot needs to be cut before it strangles a water-column, or the recorded final thoughts of someone who stepped into a submerged intake three seasons ago. When they are done, they press a thumb to the sphere to add a fresh pulse of light, and push it back into the upward stream just as the chamber’s ribbed walls begin to soften and sag into dripping pulp.

They are neither masters of the city nor its pets; they are its editors. Without them, the basin’s frantic, automated engine would eventually choke on its own enthusiasm. They wade shin-deep into the warm pools, ribbon-finned schools swirling around their ankles, to clear tangled moss from the submerged intakes. They intercept the dog-sized, velvet-shelled climbers on the vertical water-columns, taking a single silver-skinned tuber for food and, in return, scraping the pale salt-crust from the climber’s joints so it doesn’t seize up. They watch the featherless marsh-birds closely, because the birds always rise in a screaming, iridescent cloud three seconds before an invisible clearing-pulse sweeps across the open avenues—giving the walkers just enough warning to leap onto a tower’s lower ribs before the reeds bend flat and the water-skin is combed clean.

Even their rest is shaped by the rule that nothing is allowed to finish. They never lie down for long in one place. When exhaustion takes them, they climb into the high, vaulted ribs beneath a cantilevered terrace—right in the pale-violet haze that smells of ozone and crushed mint—and hook their double-jointed arms around a swaying cable. There they hang like wet, pale fruit, sleeping half a brain at a time while the other half listens through their fingertips for the tension-change that means the cable is about to unravel.

And when one of them grows too stiff to make the jump onto a re-braiding bridge, or their rubbery mantle turns brittle and gray, there is no funeral and no marker. Four hundred paces of living mat has no room for monuments. On a bright, muggy afternoon, while the birds pipe and the water-columns hum, they simply wade out into the deepest part of a clear pool, press their last memories into a glowing packet, and let the root-tendons and the warm, tea-colored soup take their calcium and lignin back into the floor—leaving the avenues open, the noise unbroken, and the city free to keep rearranging its bones in the sun.
