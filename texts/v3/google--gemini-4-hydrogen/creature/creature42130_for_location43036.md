At first, you would swear the blister is empty—that the only things moving here are rising heat, falling water, and the slow, mechanical drift of the balconies.

The clue that you are wrong is a sound you might easily mistake for the glass walls cooling: a dry, rhythmic *click-tink*, like a fingernail tapping an obsidian pane, coming from high up where the city’s outer skin meets the sweating rock.

Watch that seam long enough, and a piece of the dark velvety alloy seems to detach itself from the wall.

It is not alloy. It is a living thing, roughly the length of a human arm, though it rarely shows its full span at once. Its upper mantle is matte charcoal—lightless and soft-looking, evolved to drink in the dull ember-orange glow rising from the shaft below—while its underside is a series of pale, pleated bladders, translucent as oiled vellum and faintly warm to the touch. It has no face, no eyes, and no clear head; instead, its leading edge is rimmed with a fringe of tiny, pearlescent nodules, chemically kin to the city’s pale ceramic anchors.

Because of those nodules, it does not fall.

When it leaves the wall, it simply lets go and hangs in the thick, shimmering air, buoyed by pockets of heated vapor inside its pleats and caught by the same unseen field-lines that hold the balconies aloft. It drifts across the dizzying gap between tiers without a sound, trailing a bundle of hair-thin, salt-crusted tendrils behind it like the rigging of a ship. Where those tendrils brush a gossamer filament, they hook and glide, using the taut threads as rails to pull itself through the stagnant pockets where the updraft fails.

There are dozens of them in the shaft—perhaps hundreds—though you will almost never see two in the same place. They are creatures of the thermal boundary, living in the narrow, shifting margin between the furnace-heat of the core and the freezing damp of the outer shell.

When the thermal cycle below turns over and the sluggish updraft begins to rise, they emerge from the windowless chambers. They are the only things in the blister that seem to understand the wrongness of those rooms. Where a corridor angles deeper than the building’s thin shell should allow, folding extra volume into the pocket, these things tuck themselves into the impossible corners to sleep. Folded tight, their dark mantles pressed flat against the walls, they look like nothing more than architectural baffles or stacked shutters.

Awake, they graze.

They crawl—slowly, deliberately—along the damp seams where sulfur and salt bloom in delicate, branching crusts. With hard, silica-rimmed rasps tucked beneath their pleated bellies, they scrape the mineral growth from the stone, *click-tink, click-tink*, keeping the seams clear without ever meaning to. They hang upside down beside the pale ceramic nodes, waiting for beads of condensation to detach from the dome above. When a droplet pauses mid-air and begins to slide along an invisible curve toward a slotted intake, a tendril flicks out—so fast it blurs the dim light—and draws the water straight into a gill-slit along the creature’s flank.

They do not know what the city is, or what it is calculating. They have no use for the answers humming in the walls.

Yet their bodies have grown around the machine’s habits so completely that they have become a kind of echo of it. Along their pale undersides, thin slits of tissue pulse with a faint, cold-white luminescence—not quite bright enough to read by, just enough to cast a ghost of light onto the obsidian beneath them. When a nearby membrane brightens to signal some shift in the hidden equation, the nearest drifters answer it, their own flanks flickering in a slow, rippling cadence as they pass the light from tier to tier.

It is not language, and it is not worship. It is only what happens when something lives long enough inside a clockwork organ, breathing its dampness and drinking its stray light, until the rhythm of the mechanism becomes the rhythm of its blood.

When one of them grows old, its pleated bladders lose the heat that keeps them buoyant. It does not crash; it simply stops catching the updraft, sinking with terrible, unhurried grace through the layers of shimmering air, trailing salt-dust as it goes, until the amber haze below swallows it whole. Days later, the updraft will carry a faint, dry smell of scorched slate back up through the shaft, and along the sweating walls, new blooms of sulfur will begin to crust the seams.
