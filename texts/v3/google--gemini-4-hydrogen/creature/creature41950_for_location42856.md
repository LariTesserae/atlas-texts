If you stood on the plateau, you would know its inhabitants first by the way the air changes around you—a sudden thickening in the mist, a shift in the rhythm of the ground beneath your boots, or a faint, oily drop in the sixty-cycle hum that makes your molars ache. Nothing here announces itself to the eye. Everything announces itself to the skin, the eardrum, and the bone.

High above the bulbous canopy, where the sagging, thigh-thick power cables sweep between the iron pylons, live the quietest things on the shelf. They are flat, wide-ribbed creatures, the color of wet tallow, with six jointed limbs tipped in thick, non-conductive keratin hooks. They never touch the iron towers; to bridge the gap between the humming copper-clad line and the grounded frame is to vanish in a sharp, ozone-heavy pop. Instead, they spend their entire lives suspended along the catenary curves of single cables, moving with a slow, deliberate hand-over-hand crawl through the warm deluge. 

Their hides are coated in a thick, waxy secretion that sheds the mineral rain and insulates them from the static crackle of the lines. They feed on the cables themselves—or rather, on the slick, oily crust of drifting spores and dead midges that the electrostatic charge pulls out of the air and plasters onto the vibrating metal. They scrape it off with broad, rasp-toothed tongues, inching miles across the chasms in the dark. When a continent-wide weather spiral begins to turn and the barometric pressure drops, the static along the lines rises to a frantic, hair-lifting hiss. That is when they breed, pressing their dry, chitinous belly-plates against the cable and stridulating in rapid, rhythmic bursts. The tensioned wire carries their songs for miles from pylon to pylon, turning the entire power grid into a vast, thrumming instrument of courtship.

Down below, in the drowned dark where the aerial roots drag through the black water, life is heavier and far less patient.

The flooded karst-tubes and the buried slurry pipelines share the same subterranean maze, and in the warm, mineral-thick runoff between them live the *silt-bellies*—vast, blind, boneless things that look less like animals than like lengths of pale, pulsing hose. They wedge themselves into the horizontal seams of the weeping limestone, anchoring their tails in the rock and letting their broad, pleated mouths drift open in the current. They do not hunt; they simply breathe the iron-heavy soup, filtering out the bacterial mats and the crushed, sulfur-rich detritus that washes down from the automated ore-mills. Over decades, the travertine crust grows right over their anchored flanks, cementing them into the cliff-face until they are indistinguishable from the stone, save for the slow, rhythmic *glug-hiss* of their gill-slits and the faint, metallic tang of the copper-veined moss that roots itself along their backs.

Between the high wires and the drowned stone lies the great, roaring middle of the jungle, where every sound is a lie or a warning.

The canopy predators—those long, silent things with resonant throat-sacs—are not the only ones that have learned to speak the language of the machines. The soft-padded browsers, heavy as oxen and blind from birth, move in tight, rumbling herds along the terraced valleys, their long, rubbery snouts constantly sweeping the air for the smell of fresh fern and hot grease. Their calves learn the world by ear before they ever take a step. A mother will press her broad, vibrating throat against her calf’s skull, teaching it the difference between the true *thrum-clack* of a distant pumping station—which never wavers, never hurries, and means warm, cleared stone—and the slightly breathy, uneven *clank-hiss* of a canopy-mimic waiting in the aerial roots above. A machine never pauses to swallow. A machine’s rhythm does not quicken when you step beneath it.

And then there are the ones who do not merely hide from the machines, or mimic them, or cling to their wires, but who *keep* them—not out of duty to whoever built them, for the builders are as forgotten as the sun, but because in a world of endless, drowning rain, warmth and rhythm are the only things worth having.

You would not recognize them as people at first, if you could see them. They are tall, stooped, and dense-boned, with long, heavy arms that often brush the ground to steady them on the slick, rounded basalt. Their skin is hairless, slate-gray, and perpetually slick with the same oily spore-film that coats the leaves; they do not fight the wetness of the plateau, they wear it. Where a face might be, there is only a broad, cartilaginous cowl—like a folded acoustic horn—that flares from the collarbones and cheeks, framing a recessed vertical slit of taut, fluid-filled membranes. They angle these cowls constantly as they walk, catching the whistle of the gorge-reeds, the drip of the travertine curtains, and the low, chest-rattling thrum of the buried pipes. Below the acoustic cowl sits a wide, heavy-jawed mouth, and along their forearms run rows of fine, whisker-like filaments that read the slightest tremor in the air or the water.

They live in small, tight-knit bands in the acoustic dead-zones—tucked behind the flared concrete buttresses of the substations, or in hollow chambers carved into the limestone directly behind roaring cataracts, where the thunder of the falling water masks their movements from the armored steam-ambushers. 

To them, the plateau is not a wilderness dotted with factories. The geothermal turbine-towers and the automated ore-mills are simply the eldest, loudest, and most generous features of the landscape—iron mountains that never sleep, never flood, and bleed a constant, comforting heat into the blood-warm dark. They know the schedule of every steam-valve for thirty miles in any direction, not by clocks, but by counting the slow, steady cycles of the subterranean pistons in the soles of their broad, splayed feet. 

Just before a valve is due to blow, a band will gather at the edge of the scalded clearing, moving with the quiet, unhurried grace of things that cannot afford a careless splash. They carry bundles of tough sulfur-fern tubers and armored river-crawlers wrapped in leathery fronds, wedging them into the dry, cracked stone right beneath the iron nozzle. Then they retreat behind the concrete buttresses, folding their acoustic cowls flat against their skulls and pressing their palms over their ribs. When the valve shrieks—a violent, deafening blast of superheated steam that scours the clearing bare and drives the jungle back—they feel the shockwave in their teeth. Moments later, as the hiss dies back to a low simmer, they step out into the blinding, scalding mist to gather their steamed meal, moving quickly before the armored ambushers crawl in from the dark to see what the noise has left behind.

They have no fire, for fire has no place in a world of drowning air and swamp-gas bladders. Instead, they work with grease, copper, and bone. They harvest the copper-veined moss that grows thickest along the buried power conduits, braiding it into heavy, conductive nets that they cast into the flooded karst-basins; when a stray current bleeds through the soil, the nets hum and stiffen, stunning the segmented electro-skitterers and the fat, blind eels that hide in the mud. They cure the ruptured skins of pneumatic spore-bladders in hot lubricant skimmed from the turbine weep-holes, stretching them over hollow sections of gorge-reed to make resonant beacons. Wedged into the cliff-seams along the terraced valleys, these drum-reeds catch the constant updrafts from the abyssal drop-offs and hum in distinct, recognizable pitches, marking safe crossings over the roaring black water.

And when they need to speak across the miles of suffocating jungle, they do not shout into the rain. They walk to the nearest iron pylon, or dig down through the spongy moss until their knuckles find the warm, sweating skin of a buried slurry pipeline. Pressing their jawbones against the iron—letting the sixty-cycle buzz or the heavy *thrum-clack* rattle through their skulls—they tap against the pipe with heavy, iron-capped sticks. 

*Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.* 

The iron carries the message for leagues beneath the mud and the roots, riding the pulse of the machinery: *A pylon has fallen in the lower gorge. The browsers are moving north. A great spiral of low pressure is turning above the cloud-ceiling.*

High above them, the wind shifts, carrying the sharp, mingled scent of crushed spore-pods and hot machine oil. The hollow reeds along the gorge-rims begin their rising, discordant whistle. Down in the wet dark, the tallow-colored crawlers on the sagging cables begin to chitter; the silt-bellies pull deeper into their limestone throats; the blind folk behind the concrete buttresses angle their cowls toward the sky, reading the sudden weight of the air. And as the turbines adjust their pitch to meet the coming storm, the whole vast, lightless plateau settles deeper into its own rhythm, turning in the warm, roaring dark.
