It is easy to miss it at first, because nothing in the chamber is shaped to draw the eye. In the dim, sodium-orange haze of the pilot-lights, any silhouette between the squat turbine-housings looks like another bundle of sweating foam insulation, or a spare length of black rubber hosing coiled over a manifold. You only notice it when the cold blue-green glow of the dangling eels parts around a pair of shoulders, and something moves through the slim aisle with a slowness that has nothing to do with the draft.

Whatever lives here is roughly the height of a person, though it never stands quite straight; its neck and upper back hold the permanent, practiced stoop of something accustomed to clearing the low pipe-cradles by half an inch. It is pale—the exact, damp, chalky gray-white of the uncured concrete walls—and entirely hairless. Like the silver weld-beads overhead and the crisp-faceted bolt heads along the flanges, its skin shows no sign of age, no scars, no calluses, no weathering. It has the smooth, slightly swollen, unfinished look of something cast in a mold and pulled out just before the surface hardened.

Where it wears anything at all, it wears a heavy, seamless apron and high, cuffed boots of supple black rubber that still carry the faint, sharp smell of sulfur and fresh resin. It is impossible to tell where the rubber ends and its own slick, pale hide begins; both are equally wet, equally unblemished, beaded with the same shivering film of condensate.

It has adapted to the thin, needle-sharp air not with urgency, but with volume. Beneath its pale skin, its ribcage is broad and barrel-deep, and along the flanks—tucked just below the arms where the air from the intake vents hits coldest—run rows of soft, pleated membranes, pale and faintly translucent. They open and close in long, unhurried bellows-strokes, drawing the oxygen from the freezing mist with a wet, faint wheeze that disappears instantly into the roar of the room. Its face is smooth and blunt-featured, with no external ears—only two flat, taut discs of chitin-tight skin set behind the jaw, slick with water, vibrating continuously in sympathy with the chamber’s immense drone. Its eyes are large, lidless, and milky-amber, catching the blurred halos of the pilot-lights; in air this saturated, they never need to blink.

It lives inside the sound the way a fish lives inside a current. The *thrum-clack* of the heavy piston-pumps does not batter it; its long, flat-footed stride along the galvanized grating falls cleanly on the beat, heel striking steel on the *thrum*, toe lifting on the *clack*. When it pauses beside a humming transformer or a cast-iron manifold, it rests both broad, flat-padded palms against the vibrating metal, letting the sustained, metallic choir of the bellows-limpets travel up through its wrists and settle into its chest. At times, its pleated throat-membranes swell and release a low, wet, resonant hum—not a word, not a distress call, just a single steady pitch that locks into the chord between the pumps and the shrieking bleed-valves, as if it were tuning itself to keep from drifting out of phase with the room.

The swarm treats it not as a predator or an intruder, but as another piece of the furniture. As it walks the narrow catwalks, the curtains of slate-blue, eyeless eels brush smoothly across its rubbered shoulders and pale cheeks, leaving faint smears of cold blue-green luminescence on its skin that fade within seconds. The millions of pale, six-legged mites crawl unhurriedly over the toes of its boots and up the seams of its apron, grazing on the thin film of machine oil it picks up from the valves, never biting, never scattering. When it lays a hand on a pump-housing, the ribbed bellows-limpets clustered around its knuckles scarcely shift, flexing their chitinous shells in unbroken time with the pistons.

It does not hunt, and it does not hoard. To sustain itself, it moves in a slow, unvarying circuit through the labyrinthine aisles, pausing at the sharpest, coldest edges of the machinery where the draft from the intake vents turns the weeping water into translucent, slushy rime. With a broad, flat thumbnail, it scrapes the rime clean before it can thicken over the valve stems, and lifts the icy, mineral-rich slush to its mouth. It drinks the condensate directly from the curved undersides of the copper busbars, or cups its palms beneath the steady curtains of water dripping from the cable trays.

And always, it tends the room—though tending here means nothing more than touching what is already fixed. It tests the crisp bolts with its bare fingers; they are tight. It runs a pale palm down a weeping concrete wall, clearing a glassy sheet of water that immediately begins to weep again behind its wrist. It peers into the caged amber pilot-lights, checking a steady, un-flickering glow that has never dimmed. It carries no tools, no rag, no bucket of paint. There is no impatience in its stoop, no sense of waiting for the heavy, unmarked bulkheads to swing open and let the rest of the structure in.

It is simply the quiet, breathing hinge of the chamber’s equilibrium—an unfinished creature in an unfinished room, keeping the same wet, freezing, deafening watch, forever five minutes away from starting its work.
