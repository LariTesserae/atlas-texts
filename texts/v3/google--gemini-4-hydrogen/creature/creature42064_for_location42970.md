If you kneel beside one of the fault-line sumps—close enough to smell the thick, sweetish mineral oil weeping from the fracture—you begin to see how little of the plateau’s life is built for speed, and how much of it is built for endurance.

The sump looks lifeless at first, a still mirror of black grease catching no sky. Then the surface dimples. Something flat and pleated, the width of a shovel blade and the color of tarnished pewter, glides just beneath the oil-film. It has no eyes, no mouth in the usual sense; its underside is a dense, velvety comb of fine metallic bristles that sweep the bottom of the pool, straining out iron filings, shed carbon, and microscopic flakes of oxidized scale. Around the rim of the sump, half-submerged in the warm sludge, rest three or four heavy, segmented limbs—the severed or shed appendages of larger things, or sometimes a living creature itself, parked motionless in the dark. 

A refinery-grazer has come to soak its forward roller. The massive beast sits silent, its glowing filaments retracted, while the flat, pleated sump-dwellers swarm over its submerged axle. They work like living gaskets and scouring pads, sliding into the tight clearances between bronze housing and biological steel, stripping away the crusty slag-grit that accumulates over miles of basalt. The grazer does not flinch. In an hour, when its joints turn without a catch, it will heave itself out of the oil, dripping black lubricant onto the stone, and roll back into the dark, leaving the sump-dwellers fat on metal dust and old grease.

Nothing here wastes a surface. Even the stillness around the sumps is crowded.

Step away from the oil, toward the damp condensation-basins where the wire-sedge grows, and the silence reveals itself to be full of tiny, precise arguments. At first you think the soft *tick-tick-tock* is only static jumping between mineral spires. Look closer at the copper-tinted stalks of the sedge and you see them: thumb-sized creatures with bodies like glazed ceramic beads and six jointed legs tipped in dark graphite. They climb the stalks until they find two blades close enough to bridge. Stretching across the gap, they grip one stalk with their forelegs and the other with their hind, holding the plateau’s slow current inside their own bodies like a held breath.

When the charge in a bead reaches its threshold, it snaps shut—releasing the voltage in a single, sharp *tock* that jumps down the root-system into the stone. Another answers three feet away. Then another. Across an entire basin, hundreds of them turn a patch of sedge into a chattering, rhythmic circuit, signaling territory, readiness to mate, or the approach of something heavy enough to shake the roots. Beneath them, the soot-black lichen swells slowly around the base of the stalks, acting as both insulation and a blind, patient battery. When a mat of lichen grows too charged to hold its own shape, it blisters and splits with a faint *puff* of dry carbon dust, seeding the downwind stone.

Death on the plateau is just as methodical.

An hour after a siphon-hound drains an ore-borer, the borer’s carcass still stands in the dark, propped on its locked piston-legs like a stalled engine. Its internal furnace has cooled from cherry-red to a dull, fading ember, then to nothing. No flies come; there are no flies to come. Instead, the dark around the carcass begins to click.

First come the unfasteners—wide, low-slung things with shells like flattened cast-iron skillets and four pairs of specialized forelimbs ending in cold-chisels, pry-bars, and fine, acid-weeping needles. They climb the dead borer’s flanks without haste. One team dissolves the calcium-flux welds along the dorsal plates; another pries the heavy iron ribs apart, rolling the long, insulated copper nerves onto bony spindles along their backs to carry away. They do not eat the meat of the machine—there is very little soft tissue to eat. They strip it for parts, sorting the alloys by weight and conductivity, leaving behind a clean, hollow cage of pitted iron ribs.

By the time the unfasteners have moved on, the cage is already claimed.

Deep inside the borer’s empty furnace-cavity, where the stone beneath is still faintly warm, a pale, soft-skinned creature has dragged itself in through the vents. It looks nothing like the armored things outside—no bronze carapace, no ceramic joints, only a bloated, translucent sac veined with faint, pulsing threads of silver. It roots itself into the dead borer’s main grounding-cable, tapping whatever trickle of current still leaks from the nearby graphite seam, and begins to hum. A low, steady warmth builds inside the iron ribs—not the fierce red of the living borer, just enough heat to keep the dew from condensing. Inside that borrowed shell, shielded from the static and the siphon-hounds, the creature extrudes dozens of soft, unplated larvae. They will spend months curled inside the dead furnace, drinking the trickle of current and slowly precipitating their first coats of iron-sulfide armor from the mineral-heavy air, before they ever step out onto the basalt.

The plateau’s life does not stop at the surface. That sub-audible thrumming you feel through the soles of your boots—the vibration you took for geology—is only half-geological.

Down in the narrow fractures where the piezoelectric quartz grinds against the magnetite, things live that never see even the faint violet glow of a capacitor-ray. They are blind, blunt-headed worms the thickness of a tree trunk and made almost entirely of dense, pressure-hardened muscle and diamond-grit teeth. They do not tunnel through the rock so much as they *wedge* themselves between the grinding plates, letting the continent’s own tectonic weight press the quartz against their rasping jaws. They feed on the raw, fractured crystal and the surge of voltage released each time a quartz face shears. Every time one of them shifts its bulk in the deep dark, a faint tremor travels upward through three hundred feet of basalt, and the static on the surface skips a beat.

And if you tilt your head back—staring up into the heavy, starless ceiling of carbon haze where the air thins to nothing—you realize the sky is inhabited, too.

Miles above the plateau, hanging just beneath the dry static fronts, drift things that have never touched stone. They are vast, translucent bladders of toughened membrane, swollen with warm hydrogen cracked from deep-earth steam, glowing so faintly with trapped ionization that they look like bruises in the dark haze. From their undersides hang threads of conductive silk—so fine they are invisible, yet miles long, trailing all the way down until their weighted tips brush the tops of the mineral spires. They sip the plateau’s ground-charge through those impossibly thin lines, rising when the current swells, sinking when the air cools, drifting silently on the slow barometric tides like deep-sea jellyfish tethered to the ocean floor.

Yet for all the grazing, the stripping, the drifting, and the hunting, there is one kind of presence on the plateau that does not fit neatly into predator, prey, or scavenger.

You might walk for three days across the warm flats before you see one, and even then you might mistake it for a natural outcrop of slate—until it shifts its weight.

They stand twelve feet tall on three broad, heavily insulated legs that end in wide, copper-soled pads. Their bodies are narrow, vertical frames of dark, vitrified bone wrapped in coils of matte-black sheathing, with no chest to heave and no mouth to open. Where a head should be sits a broad, flat, dish-like crest of layered mica, angled slightly downward, edged with a fringe of fine, trembling whiskers that read every shift in the magnetic field.

They do not hurry. They do not hunt the grazers, and the siphon-hounds give them a wide, nervous berth, sensing perhaps that there is nothing soft inside them to puncture, and far too much voltage to ground safely.

They are the plateau’s listeners—or its balancers. Walking alone along the ancient graphite seams, one will pause beside a mineral spire where the static has been building too sharply, threatening to arc violently across a basin of wire-sedge. Slowly, with the deliberate grace of a crane swinging into position, it extends two long, jointed arms—one tipped in raw iron, the other in a dense brush of carbon fibers—and touches the spire with one while planting the other against a damp fracture in the bedrock.

For ten seconds, a thick, silent current pours through its frame. Its mica crest shudders, the whiskers standing straight out as the excess charge bleeds harmlessly into the deep crust. Then it withdraws its arms, lifts one broad copper foot, and steps onward into the dark.

It does not do this to save the sedge, or to protect the spark-mites. It feeds on equilibrium—on the smooth, unbroken hum of a continent running without a hitch. Where a sump has clogged with slag, it may kneel and clear the vent with a single, heavy finger; where a dead grazer has fallen across a major vein and begun to short the local field, it will patiently drag the bronze hulk ten yards to the side, out of the circuit.

Watching one recede into the warm haze—just a tall, three-legged silhouette moving silently between the glowing filaments of the grazers and the distant, soundless sheets of dry lightning—you understand why the plateau feels like a factory floor, even without a single wall or road. Nothing built it. Nothing owns it. It is simply a world that woke up as a machine, and these are the things that grew to keep it running.
