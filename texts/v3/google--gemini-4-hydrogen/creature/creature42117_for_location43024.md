You notice the people last, because they do not stand apart from the stone.

In the dim, unchanging twilight of the basin, their skin has taken on the matte, pewter-gray tone of the wet shale around them. It is rarely bare skin, though they wear very little—a wrap of cured hide at the waist, a sling of woven sinew across the chest, thick-soled sandals of layered leather strapped high around the ankle to keep the freshly split basalt from laying open their feet. Every exposed inch of shoulder, back, and forearm is kept slick with a paste of rendered fish-oil and fine gray mineral silt. When the paste dries—or rather, settles into the heavy humidity without ever quite drying—it forms a dull, greasy rind that sheds the condensing mist and turns away the clouds of biting midges. A newborn is rubbed with oil and silt before its cord is cut with a basalt flake; an elder’s skin is permanently stained a deep, mottled slate, the pores packed with clay and oil until it looks almost like the wet biofilm on the rocks.

Because there is no breeze to cool them and the air is already saturated with water, they do not sweat the way dry-land people do. Sweat cannot evaporate here; it merely beads and runs down into the mineral grease. Instead, they move with a deliberate, unhurried economy, squatting low on the stone plinths, conserving heat and effort. Their bodies are compact, heavy-shouldered, and thick-fingered from generations of stone-breaking and hide-scraping, with calluses on their knees and heels as hard as horn.

And they are loud—or else completely silent.

In a valley where a million frogs roar from the drainage channels and thousands of shorebirds shriek from the eaves, a normal human voice is swallowed before it crosses an alley. To speak at all, the basin-dwellers press in close, standing toe-to-toe or leaning forehead-to-temple, pitching their voices into a sharp, nasal, clicking register that cuts *between* the low drone of the amphibians and the high rattle of the birds. They do not speak in long sentences. Words are clipped, percussive, backed by bursts of breath that smell of roasted shellfish and salt.

Most of the time, though, they do not use their voices. Their hands do the talking—broad, gray-stained palms flashing in the dim light, fingers snapping into sharp, angular shapes that echo the fractured rock. It is a language built for narrow, echoing canyons: a flick of the knuckles against the jaw for *wet*, a flat palm slicing downward for *stone*, a rapid vibration of the fingertips near the ear to mean *someone is coming*. Children learn the hand-signs before they can walk, and the oldest among them—many of them wholly deafened by eighty years of knapping basalt and listening to the stone walls bounce the roar back into their skulls—are often the sharpest conversationalists in the city, reading a cousin’s intent from the twitch of a shoulder across a crowded terrace.

Without sun, moon, or weather, their lives have no natural hinge. There is no morning to wake to and no night to drive them indoors. Instead, the city runs on a quiet, staggering relay.

At any given hour, half the population is outside in the warm gray mist, squatting under the stretched-hide awnings, striking blades from basalt cores or hammering cold copper pins on flat rock anvils. The other half is inside the thick-walled, windowless stone dwellings. Those interiors are the only quiet places in the world. Chinked with gray clay and roofed with heavy hides over curved bone ribs, the dark rooms muffle the exterior clamor down to a low, steady thrumming, like water rushing through a pipe. Inside, in the damp dark, people sleep in tight, tangled heaps on woven sinew mats, draped in cured skins to keep the condensation off their faces. When a sleeper wakes—drawn out by hunger, or a stiff hip, or the simple internal tide of exhaustion having run its course—they crawl out through the low doorway, blink into the perpetual dusk, and take the stone mallet or the bone awl directly from the hands of a kin-member who is ready to crawl inside and take their place on the warm mat.

Nothing in the city ever truly stops. The slow guano fires never go out; they are simply fed. The drying racks never empty; they are simply rotated.

Because the world around them is raw, unweathered, and sharp enough to draw blood at a careless touch, the people of the basin are obsessed with smoothness. To them, a sharp edge is cheap—you can strike a razor-sharp flake off a basalt cobble in three seconds. Smoothness is wealth. Smoothness is proof of time, patience, and human care. A mother’s gift to a child is a piece of shale rubbed for years against wet hide and silt until its corners are as round and soft as a frog’s belly. The copper fishhooks and pins they hammer cold on their stone anvils are polished with fine mineral mud until they catch the dim, pewter light like drops of oil. Even their dead are not buried—there is no soil to bury them in—nor burned, for fuel is too precious. Instead, the bodies are laid out on the high stone platforms above the city for the shorebirds to strip, and the clean bones are rubbed with fish-oil and stacked in the foundation chinks of the family house, smooth and pale against the jagged dark rock.

They are not the masters of this place. They are simply the largest and most dextrous strand in a thick, wet mat of life.

Everywhere they step, the animals give way and close back in behind them. The millions of slate-backed frogs and pale-bellied toads packed into the drainage channels are more than noise; they are the city’s living skin. They eat the larvae that breed in the rust-orange biofilm; in turn, the people scoop them up by the basketful with woven sinew nets, skinning them with tiny copper blades for waterproof pouches and rendering their pale fat into lamp-oil. The frogs breed endlessly in the warm, stagnant balance of the shallows, indifferent to the baskets, replacing themselves faster than the city can eat them.

Above them, along the corbeled roofs and sagging drying racks, the squat, dark-plumed divers and long-legged shorebirds live in a state of loud, bad-tempered truce with the builders. The birds nest wing-to-wing on every ledge, fiercely territorial, jabbing at passing heads with needle-sharp beaks if anyone reaches too carelessly for an egg. Yet they rely on the city’s offal—the crushed shellfish husks, the fish heads, the guts tossed into the alleys—and the city relies on them. The thick, chalky crust of dried guano scraped from the roof ledges is what feeds the slow, smokeless cooking fires. Without the birds, the people would eat everything raw.

Lower still, in the slick dark canyons between the houses, live the crabs. Pale-shelled, flat-bodied, and quick as spilled water, they swarm out of the chinks in the dry-stacked foundations whenever the alleys clear for a moment, scuttling sideways across the wet stone to strip every dropped scrap of flesh, every dead frog, every bit of spoiled hide. They are the reason the dense, steaming city does not rot into pestilence. A child learns early not to leave a hand resting flat on a foundation stone, lest a pale claw test a finger for deadness.

And then there is whatever lives beyond the terraces, out where the causeways end and the pale mineral mud deepens into the warm, mist-choked flats.

You do not see them clearly from the city. The light is too dim, and the hanging mist never lifts. But the roofs of the houses are held up by curved ribs thick as a man’s thigh, and the awnings are stretched from hides so heavy it takes four workers to drag one across a plinth. Out in the outer sumps, where the rust-orange biofilm grows in thick, quivering carpets over the stagnant pools, the mud-wallows are home to sluggish, broad-backed giants—blind or nearly blind creatures with skins like wrinkled wet slate, encrusted with pale mineral salt and colonies of clinging snails. They move through the warm mire like submerged boulders, vacuuming up mats of algae, worms, and slow frogs with wide, whisker-ringed mouths.

Once every long, unmeasured stretch of effort, a crew of hunters—their bodies smeared thick with mud to mask their scent, carrying long basalt-tipped lances and coils of cured-gut rope—wades out along the submerged stone ridges into the mist. They do not shout out there; they signal with quick ripples of the hand and the tap of stone against stone beneath the water. When they bring one down and haul its massive, oily bulk back up the causeways, the whole tier of the city pauses for a rare, collective feast, the *clack-clack* of the knappers giving way for an hour to the wet *thwack* of heavy stone cleavers and the high, triumphant clicking of a hundred voices pressed close in the dim, roaring heat.

Then the bones are dragged up onto the roofs, the hides are pegged out over the slow guano fires, the crabs clear the slick red stones of the alleyways, and the basin settles back into its exact, wet, deafening balance—flesh and stone and frog and bird, locked together under the pewter sky, humming in the dark.
