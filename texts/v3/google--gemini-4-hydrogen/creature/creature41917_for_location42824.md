Nothing in this room has color, because color is a luxury for places that have finished arriving. Whatever lives here is made of the same palette as the chamber itself: wet chalk, unglazed porcelain, frosted glass, and the thin, watery silver of a reflection that arrives too early.

At first, you only notice the small, frantic traffic of the air and the seams.

The ribbon-swimmers are the simplest of them—creatures no thicker than shaved mica, drifting on no current you can feel. They have no eyes, no discernible mouths, only a pale, fluted edge that ripples continuously from head to tail, keeping them suspended in the refrigerated glare. When they feed, they simply pass through the dampness after a weather cycle, absorbing the chalky mist straight through their skins. Most of the time they are scarcely visible—just a faint warping of the white air, like heat-shimmer turned cold. Only when one drifts through a fractured corner does it betray itself: for a fraction of a second, the single ribbon fractures into three or four overlapping echoes, each undulating slightly out of phase, before snapping back into one body as it clears the seam.

Beneath them, along the damp crevices where the bleached moss clings like eyelashes, move the herds. They are palm-sized, pale, and busy, built low to the greasy floor on dozens of short, stubby legs. Along their flanks, feathery external gills flare open whenever the light sharpens and the walls begin to sweat. They crop the moss with a tiny, rapid rasping—soundless except for the faintest dry *shhh* when their backs brush together. They never stop moving, and for good reason: the room is impatient to turn everything into bedrock. When a grazer outgrows its shell and slips free of it, the discarded skin does not soften or rot. Within hours it stiffens, drinks up the mineral damp, and fuses to the floor, becoming one more sharp-edged ridge in the pale plaster.

And watching the herds from the far walls are the waiters.

If you stare long enough at a stretch of limestone, you begin to see them: long, flat-bodied predators with jointed, porcelain-white limbs pressed so tightly against the stone that they look like accidental reliefs in the rock. They do not stalk. Distance means nothing in a room whose corners fold too far inward. Instead, a waiter anchors itself beside a spatial crease—one of those places where the air bends slightly and your eye slides off—and becomes entirely still. Its joints lock. Its pale carapace dulls to the exact matte finish of wet bone. When a grazer wanders near the crease, the waiter does not cross the room; it simply extends a forelimb *through* the bent air. The porcelain claw emerges inches from the prey, plucks it off the moss with a sudden, silent twitch, and draws it back into the fold. A single bead of pale ichor falls, freezing into a salt-crystal before it strikes the floor.

Yet none of these—not the swimmers, not the grazers, not even the porcelain-limbed ambushers—are the true keepers of the room. They are merely what grows in the margins.

The thing that actually *lives* here is larger, older, and far more deliberate. You usually see it first in the puddles.

When the cold shimmer passes through the light and the walls sweat their fat, slow beads into the floor’s dimples, look down into the water before it is drunk back into the stone. You will see a pale, long-limbed silhouette skittering swiftly across the ceiling—wide-jointed, narrow-bodied, moving with an eerie, sideways certainty—a full second before its shadow actually glides over the back of your hand.

If you look up quickly enough, you will catch it detaching itself from the glare.

It is roughly the size of a crouched hound, though its geometry belongs to nothing that walks under a sky. Its body is segmented and laterally compressed, built narrow enough to slip into the over-folded seams where two walls meet too tightly. Its carapace is not glossy like an insect’s, nor wet like a salamander’s; it has the chalky, faintly greasy sheen of freshly extruded bone, translucent enough along the spine that you can see a slow, pale pulse shifting inside, like milk stirred in water. Where a head ought to be, the shell tapers to a blunt wedge. It has no eyes—only two flat, milky panes set flush into the sides of its face, identical to the patches of wall that flicker into frosted glass.

Beneath that wedge hang heavy, overlapping mandibles, worn smooth along their inner edges from a lifetime of lifting stone.

This is the creature that builds the pebble rings.

Moving with a quiet, clicking persistence, it gathers the sharp, un-chipped fragments that flake from the newly set floor and stacks them, stone by stone, around the shallow dimples. The floor is always thirsty; left alone, it drinks the condensation almost as fast as it falls, hardening slightly with every swallow. By ringing the dimples with stone, the creature slows the drinking. It traps the water just long enough to create a lasting mirror—and then it bends its frosted-glass face over the teacup-sized pool, watching the second-ahead reflection of the room.

It does not use the puddles only to drink, though a pair of pale, feathery palps unfurl from beneath its mandibles to sip the mist. It uses them to *read* the chamber. In the reflection, it can see where a grazer will be before the grazer arrives, where a spatial crease is about to pinch shut, and—most importantly—where the ceiling above it is still solid.

Because the room is not finished, and the creature knows it.

Watch it when it approaches the patches of soft, un-set clay along the lower walls. It rears slightly on its hindmost joints, extends a single sharp, calcified spur from its forelimb, and drags it downward in a slow, rhythmic cadence: *tick, tick, tick, tick*. Not counting days—there are no days in this shadowless, refrigerated light—but testing resistance. Checking whether the wall is still clay, or whether it has hardened into limestone since the last cycle, or whether it has thinned into that sheer white absence that waits behind the stone.

When the spur meets a firm surface, the creature leaves a clean, deliberate tally-mark and moves on. When the spur sinks too easily, or passes into a patch of white absence without meeting resistance at all, the creature recoils. Its segmented plates shudder together with a dry *shhh*, and it retreats toward the center of the room, toward the calcified ribcage of the larger animal that wandered in before the pocket closed.

There, among the hollow vertebrae cradling the room’s only lush tuft of liverwort, it settles for a moment—but never for too long.

Even here, in the bright quiet it has claimed, the floor is trying to finish itself around it. Whenever the creature rests too long in one dimple, the wet-bone mineral creeps up its lower joints, stiffening the grease into crust. So it is forever scraping itself free against the sharp edges of the ribcage, shedding brittle, chalk-white husks that fuse into the floor beside the grazer skins, forever moving through the glare, stacking stones against the thirst of the rock, and scratching its quiet questions into the clay—trying to stay on the solid side of a room that hasn't yet decided to be real.
