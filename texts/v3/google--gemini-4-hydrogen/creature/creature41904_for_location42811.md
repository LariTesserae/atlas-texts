If you stay long enough for your eyes to adjust behind the bone-slits, you begin to see that the people of the bowl are built like the terraces they inhabit: low-slung, broad-stanced, and perpetually in the middle of a motion.

They are not tall, or perhaps they simply never stand all the way up. Decades of prying flagstones and ducking under low hide eaves have given most of them a permanent forward tilt, knees slightly bent, weight centered over the balls of their flat-soled boots so they can step sideways onto a catwalk without looking down. Where their skin shows—a strip of jaw between furred hood and collar, the backs of their wrists when a mitten slips—it is the color of smoked tallow, stiffened by cold and cured by a lifetime of mineral spray. White salt settles in the creases of their knuckles and along the rims of their nostrils.

You almost never see their eyes. Even indoors, where the brine-light filters dimly through oiled hide, they keep their bone-slit goggles strapped tight with sinew. The slits are cut no wider than a flint edge; the older inhabitants have narrow, horizontal grooves worn into the bridges of their noses where the bone has rubbed for forty years. Those who lose their goggles in a slide, or who look too long at the noon glare without them, go milky-eyed within a season. The blind do not become helpless here—they are given the long wooden scrapers and stationed along the cistern-troughs, where the work is all by touch and the wet stone never surprises them.

Because a shout can bring down a cornice of rime, their whole culture has folded itself into the space below sound. 

Their mittens are dark, greased sealskin or mountain-goat pelt, but across the backs of the fingers and along the outer edge of the thumb they sew small, flat tabs of bleached bone. Against the blinding white-and-gray of the basin, a flick of the wrist reads like a spark. Two workers on opposite tiers can settle a dispute over a sagging roof-beam with a sequence of sharp, economical tilts of the hand, punctuated by dry tongue-clicks that carry just far enough across the damp air without waking the ice.

When they need to say something that cannot be signed—a warning of deep sickness, a grief, a private bargain—they do not speak into the air. They step close, press forehead to forehead or jaw to collarbone, and hum low in the chest. Bone carries what the thin air refuses. You will sometimes see two figures crouched motionless in the lee of a retaining wall, skulls touching beneath their heavy hoods, sharing a long, wordless vibration while the slush sighs around their boots.

No one in the bowl owns a house. A structure that must be torn down and re-lashed every few days cannot belong to a single family. Instead, a household owns a *bundle*: a set of curved horn ribs, a coil of oiled sinew, three or four mats of pale, salt-cured reed, and a bundle of wooden wedges. The slate walls and notched-timber stairs belong to whoever is currently keeping them upright. When a fissure widens and begins to weep too warmly, undermining a tier, the families living there do not mourn the room; they unlace their roofs, roll their mats, shoulder their horn ribs, and wedge themselves into a drier pocket three yards to the left, where neighbors simply shift their own hides over to make room.

They sleep packed together in these stone pockets—four, six, sometimes ten to a hollow—layered between greased pelts with the fat-lamps turned down to a thread of blue smoke. The subsurface brine, for all the freezing air above it, emerges from the rock just warm enough to keep from solidifying on contact; the cistern-troughs give off a faint, mineral steam that takes the razor edge off the cold inside the shelters. The people smell strongly of rendered fat, wet wool, and the sharp, metallic tang of sulfur-rich salt.

They share their sleeping hollows with the birds.

The plump, flightless birds—mute, round-bodied things the size of loaves—have no fear of the inhabitants and very little distinction between wild and kept. Their plumage is extraordinary: dense, short, and velvet-soft, saturated with a natural oil that beads the brine into mercury-like drops. Not a feather rustles when they walk. They waddle right through the low hide doorways, stepping over outstretched boots, probing the chinks of the hearth-stones with their curved bills for stray crawlers. At night, they tuck their heads into their own oily down and wedge themselves along the edges of the human sleeping-piles, radiating a steady, furnace-like warmth. In return, they are tolerated—mostly. When a slush-slide crushes a bird, or when the winter shadows linger too long and the brine-pools crust over too deeply to probe, a bird is quietly taken by the neck, plucked for lining-down, and boiled in a horn-pot over a grease-wick.

The true engine of the basin, though, is smaller and flatter. 

Everywhere the brine weeps, the pale, flat-shelled rock-crawlers swarm. They look like thumbnails of wet limestone—segmented, eyeless, with dozens of fringe-like legs that grip the slick slate with astonishing force. They graze endlessly on the colorless, stringy brine-moss and on the invisible mineral film that coats the rock. When the sun flashes a puddle into needle-ice, the crawlers do not flee; they clamp their broad shells flush against the stone, sealing themselves into a microscopic pocket of liquid brine while the crystal blister grows over their backs like a glass dome. When the dome melts, they simply unclamp and resume grazing.

The people harvest them by the scoopful, scraping them off the cistern walls with dull flint blades. Ground into a paste with dried brine-moss and fat, the crawlers form the dense, salty mash that keeps the basin alive.

Living in the spaces between the unmortared stones—and inside the people’s walls—are the white-furred burrowers. Sleek, long-bodied, with black button eyes and whiskers stiff as spruce needles, they are fearless thieves. They will dart out from between two slate slabs to snatch a strip of oiled sinew right out of a worker’s lap, vanishing back into the labyrinth of dry-stone chinks before a wooden shovel can be raised. Nobody traps them. Nobody tries to drive them out. The burrowers’ whiskers and bare pink feet are sensitive to the faintest subsurface groan—the microscopic shift of limestone a minute before a shelf of rime shears loose. When three or four white heads suddenly pop out of a retaining wall and bolt upward toward the dry upper ridges, every worker on that tier drops their antler lever and steps onto the timber catwalks without even looking around to see why.

Up above the catwalks, where the brine never reaches and the black crustose lichen clings to the wind-scoured rim, nothing moves quickly. The ice-gnats rarely rise that high; they stay suspended in silent, shivering clouds over the sun-struck pools below, living out their single afternoon in the rising vapor before the mountain shadow drops across the bowl and freezes the air clean.

A visitor might wonder why anyone stays in a hundred-pace hollow where the ground itself is trying to dissolve beneath your feet, where a cough too loud can bury your neighbors, and where the sun blinds you if you blink too slowly.

The answer is waiting just over the rim. Outside the bowl, the high saddle is a howling desert of bare granite and gale-force wind that strips the heat from a living body in minutes. Inside, the rim turns the wind aside. The stone weeps endlessly, bringing up a faint, stubborn warmth from deep inside the mountain, feeding the moss, the crawlers, the velvet birds, and the silent, salt-cured people who tend them.

When someone in the basin finally grows too stiff to step aside from a slide, or dies quietly in the warmth of a sleeping-pile, they are not buried—there is no soil to dig, and nothing rots cleanly in this salt. Their family unlaces their greased pelts, saves the bone-slit goggles and the antler tools, and carries the body up to the dry, wind-scoured lip of the rim. There, laid out on the bare pale limestone, the thin air and the black lichen take them slowly, until only the clean white ribs are left—light enough to be carried back down into the bowl, lashed with sinew, and used to shore up a sagging roof against the next tide of slush.
