Nothing delicate survives the saddles for long. The things that truly live here—not the passing herring, not the seals dropping down from the silver ceiling for a breath-hold's hunt, but the creatures that belong to the drowned peaks—are built on the same principle as the granite beneath them: heavy, rounded, and unwilling to yield.

At a glance, in the strobing shafts of pale sun or the grit-thick gloom, they look like detached pieces of the kelp-beds, or like seals grown too broad in the shoulder and too flat in the skull. They are long-bodied and slate-colored, their hides as thick and scarred as cured leather, mottled with the pale crescents of old barnacle-scrapes and slick with a dense, cold-water oil that sheds the glacial silt. They have no hair to drag in the rip, no fragile fins to shred against the stone. Their forelimbs are thick, muscular levers ending in broad, leathery pads studded with blunt keratin pegs—hands meant less for swimming than for wedging deep into the fluted grooves of the gneiss and holding fast while forty knots of water try to peel them off the mountain.

Their faces are blunt, built to take the weight of a cross-swell head-on. Their nostrils pinch shut like rubber valves; their eyes are small, recessed behind heavy brow-ridges and veiled by thick, clear membranes that flicker constantly against the stinging grit. They do not use their eyes for much beyond reading the shifting shadow of a cormorant above or finding the sudden, unnatural bloom of the intake station's amber floodlights below.

In a world this loud, open ears would be useless—the thunder of the swells and the crackle of collapsing bubbles would leave them deafened in an hour. Instead, their ear-openings are mere pinholes sealed behind dense bone. They hear with their lower jaws, their ribs, and the flat pads of their bellies. When they press themselves into the thin, slower layer of water that hugs the bedrock, the entire drowned range speaks to them through their bones. They know every ridge by its vibration: the deep, slow bass of the solid granite domes, the slightly higher ring of the hollowed shelves, the rhythmic *clack-grind-clack* of cobbles churning in a pothole fifty yards away.

They do not fight the permanent storm; they spend it like currency. To move across a ridge, they drop into the kelp corridors, hooking a wrist around the iron-hard stem of an oarweed and letting the surge whip them forward like a stone from a sling, releasing at the peak of the arc to catch a leeward eddy behind a boulder. When the tide turns and the towering, invisible whirlpools tear around the peaks, they retreat to the honeycombed basins, wedging their broad tails and hooked limbs into the rock-flutes until the worst of the rip passes.

Even their feeding is bound to the violence of the water. They hunt the edges of the kelp for armored crabs and slow-moving bottom fish, and they pry the bristling mussels from the upper ledges. Where a shell is too thick for their blunt, stone-cracking teeth, they drop it into one of the stone potholes, wait for a heavy swell to roll overhead and set the fist-sized cobbles grinding together like millstones, and then reach in during the brief lull to scoop the crushed meat from between the stones.

For eons, the mountain's voice was only water and rock. Then, within the memory of their eldest—those massive, scarred matriarchs whose hides are nearly white with old scrapes and whose jaws have gone crooked from decades of stone-work—the iron arrived.

At first, the turbines were a disaster. The shrouded impellers turned the narrowest, richest saddles into lethal traps, and the sudden, relentless *thrum* of the bronze blades through the bedrock drowned out the subtle tremors that warned of building surface gales. Several of the young were pulled into the intakes before the rest learned the new geography of the current.

Now, the machines are simply another kind of geology—warmer, louder, and stranger than the granite, but just as permanent.

The inhabitants have learned to sleep—or to enter the shallow, watchful torpor that passes for sleep here—hooked around the thick, armored power cables stapled to the rock. The cables carry a faint, steady leak of heat that fights the bone-deep ache of the water, and the rusted iron brackets give their pegged hands something unyielding to lock onto while the silt-fog sweeps over them. Around the lone intake station, where the riveted cylinder hums in the blue-black dimness, they gather just beyond the cone of amber light, hanging motionless in the shelter of the concrete piers while the warmth draws clouds of translucent shrimp and ribbons of silver sand-lance into easy reach.

They have even learned to read the health of the engines. Just as they know when a storm is breaking overhead by the pitch of the grinding cobbles, they know when a turbine's bearing is wearing thin or an impeller is fouled with torn kelp by the sudden, ragged stutter in the vibration running through the cables.

When that stutter begins, they clear away from the saddles, because they know what follows: the taut steel hawser singing in the wash, and the slow, swaying descent of the maintenance bell.

They do not fear the creatures that climb out of the bell, wrapped in their clumsy canvas and brass, weighted down with lead boots and tethered by thick hoses. To the people of the smooth stone, the surface-tenders look pitifully fragile—blind in the silt-clouds, struggling upright against currents that would snap their necks if they lost their footing, trailing noisy streams of wasted air. Yet the inhabitants leave them alone. When the divers work with their heavy wrenches and torches, the ragged stutter in the stone stops, and the steady, predictable thrum returns, keeping the amber lights burning and the cables warm.

Sometimes, when a tender steps out onto a granite ledge in the forty-knot gloom, squinting through a scratched faceplate into the swirling kelp, a heavy, slate-colored body will glide past just at the edge of the lantern's reach—close enough to brush a thick, scarred shoulder against the diver's air hose, testing the strange, hollow vibration of the rubber, before banking into the surge and vanishing back into the roar.

And occasionally, when the crew hauls the bell back up or peers out through the thick glass of the intake station, they find small things wedged neatly into the iron brackets by the door: a clutch of perfectly spherical cobbles polished smooth as marbles, a tangle of snapped fishing wire stripped from a turbine shroud, or the empty, cracked carapace of a crab bigger than a man's chest—left behind in the warm light, before the storm takes the mountain back.
