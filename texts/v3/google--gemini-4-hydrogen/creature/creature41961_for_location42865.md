At first, you would not know anything larger than a cricket lives here. In a place where you can only read what you touch or what radiates against your face, absence is easier to notice than presence.

You might notice it first as a broken rhythm. Above you, an uninsulated return-line sweats its slow beads of moisture onto a verdigris-crusted brass elbow, and you wait for the *tink* in the ceramic gutter below. It comes once, twice, then stops—though the air is just as muggy, and the smell of damp clay and scorched almonds has not faded. Something has laid its mouth, or the flat of its throat, directly against the brass, catching the water before it has a chance to fall.

Or you might feel it as a passing eclipse. Standing still in the narrow gap between a brick pier and a scorching pressure-vessel, you suddenly feel the fierce radiant heat against your cheek dim for three or four seconds, as if a shutter had been drawn across a furnace. Then the heat returns, unchanged. Something cool has glided through the space between you and the iron, blotting out the thermal glare without a sound.

If you stay still long enough—longer than is comfortable in air that hovers just below the threshold of pain—it will eventually come close enough for you to know its shape.

It is roughly the length of a man’s arm and torso, though it is rarely stretched straight; nothing in the vault is straight, and its body has been shaped by generations of threading through the three-dimensional tangle of infrastructure. It is thin-flanked and strangely flattened from side to side, built to slip through the three-inch clearances between bundled copper pipes and masonry arches. When it moves, it does not use the catwalk gratings—its weight never touches those worn, narrow tracks. Instead, it travels overhead and through the interstices, suspended by four long, gangling limbs with extra, backward-hinging joints that fold and unfold like brass calipers.

Its feet are wide, leathery clamps, split into opposing pads that wrap cleanly around the curved bellies of pipes and flanges. They are soft enough to leave no scratch on the velvety magnetite skin of the ironwork, gripping with a quiet, dry suction.

If your hand were to brush against its back in the dark, you would pull back thinking you had touched a shelf of mortar. Its hide is completely dry, matte, and cool—surprisingly cool, hoarding the chill of the upper return-lines inside a thick, segmented body. Along its spine and shoulders, the skin is ridged with pale, waxy plates of calcified mineral, dusted with salt and soot, indistinguishable from the curtains of travertine hanging between the brick seams.

It has no eyes—only two smooth, sealed-over hollows beneath a broad, spade-shaped brow. To this creature, the vault is not dark at all. Across its flat face, arranged in a wide crescent above a small, hard-rimmed mouth, are deep, pleated thermal pits, dark and velvety inside. They read the chamber as an intricate, blindingly sharp landscape of temperatures: the fierce, white-hot glare of the main manifolds, the dim, grainy outlines of the brick piers, and the cool, dark rivers of the uninsulated return-lines winding overhead. It steers wide of the soot-caked mica sight-glasses, flinching from those occasional cherry-red smudges the way a cave-fish flinches from a lantern.

Everything about it is governed by the economics of heat and water. To hurry in this air would be to cook from the inside out, and to open its mouth to hiss or cry would be to waste moisture it cannot spare. So it moves with the same glacial patience as the tree-trunk piston-rod in the center of the clutter. A limb reaches out, pauses, tests the thermal shadow of a bracket, and settles; the body follows an inch at a time, a dry, papery rustle of mineral-crusted hide against brick.

It spends most of its life in the upper tiers, draped lengthwise along the sweating return-lines. Pressing its flat, pale belly against the cooler copper, it drinks in the temperature differential like a battery, letting the metal draw the day’s accumulated heat out of its core while its pleated throat-pouch catches the condensation from the brass elbows.

When it descends to feed, it moves in brief, deliberate excursions down into the muggier pockets around the catch-basins. There, it grazes with quiet precision—scraping mats of velvety black moss from the salt-crusted mortar, nipping the pale, threadlike roots, and pressing its hard-rimmed mouth over the blind, porcelain-shelled snails. When it crushes a shell, it makes a single, sharp *click* in the dark, almost identical to the solitary chirp of the heat-tolerant crickets.

It has no voice, yet it is not entirely mute. Every few minutes, a pipe in the vault *pings* as thermal expansion shifts it a fraction of a millimeter in its brackets. Sometimes, that sound is not the metal settling. It is one of these creatures—perhaps the only other one within fifty yards—tapping a hardened, calcified knuckle once against a copper line. The vibration travels cleanly through the packed labyrinth, felt in the pads of another’s feet three tiers away: a brief, dry acknowledgment in the dark, indistinguishable from the machinery’s own ticking.

Occasionally, two of them meet in the narrow gap between a valve and a pier. There is no aggression, no jostling for space; the vault’s equilibrium does not permit haste. They pause face-to-face, their broad, blind heads hovering an inch apart while their thermal pits read the faint warmth of each other’s flanks. Then, slowly, one flattens itself against the brick, the other threads over its travertine-plated back, and both continue on their separate paths.

They live a very long time—decades, perhaps longer, untouched by predators or weather, paced by the sub-audible thrumming in the floor. As they age, the mineral-laden condensation they drink and the lime dust of the mortar seams slowly thicken the pale plates along their spines. Their joints stiffen; their excursions down to the catch-basins grow rarer.

In the end, an old one does not fall from the pipes or leave a skeleton on the catwalk. It simply wedges itself into a cool, damp angle behind a ceramic gutter, tucks its caliper limbs tight against its ribs, and stops. Over the years that follow, the slow seepage from the masonry washes over it, laying down fresh layers of pale, waxy travertine across its back, until there is nothing left to distinguish the sleeper from the wall—and the vault goes on, ticking softly in the dark, needing no one to watch it.
