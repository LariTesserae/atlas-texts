In the bruised shadows and the green-filtered twilight, everything that lives here has learned the same two laws: do not waste heat, and do not make a sound.

The true citizens of the pocket are its builders, though neither could have raised the city alone. The blind termites are pale as peeled willow-roots, their soft, segmented bodies so translucent that you can read their recent labor in the faint tint of their guts—chalk-white in the mycelium tenders, moss-green in the leaf-chewers, and dark iron-grey in the miners who spend their lives rasping microscopic grains of clay from the softer vesicles of the basalt. They never step onto the bare, glassy stone if they can help it. Instead, they lay their world down ahead of them, millimeter by millimeter, pressing chewed fern-stipe and mineral paste into arched, damp-walled arcades that shield them from the open air.

Beside them, and above them, live the mud-daubing wasps—creatures so altered by the windless damp that they scarcely resemble their sun-drunk cousins on the scrubland overhead. Their bodies are slate-black and matte, dusted with pale clay, and their wings have grown short and smoky. In this tight, echoing bowl of stone, they rarely fly; a hum of wings would carry across the bare basalt and draw every predator in the chamber. Instead, they stalk along the spines of the termites’ mud-causeways on long, deliberate legs, sweeping their jointed antennae through the humid air. Where the sheer, freshly fractured walls defeat the termites’ heavy mortar, the wasps take over, anchoring their cantilevered carton-paper galleries directly to the razor-sharp glassy ridges. They are the city’s scouts and gatherers. They climb into the green shafts of light beneath the ceiling rift, plucking the small, powdery moths from the air with a single, silent snap of their mandibles, and carry them down to the bases of the three-foot ventilation chimneys. There, they leave the wings and dry husks in neat caches—payment, or tribute—for the termites to drag down into the buried fungus-gardens.

Beneath those fluted, organ-pipe chimneys, the pocket’s true engine breathes. In a chamber with no soil, the white mycelium is the only thing that knows how to turn death back into food. It spreads in vast, velvety beds inside the warm basalt hollows, digesting moth-scales, shed gecko-skin, and dead liverwort, sending up a faint, sweet, cellar-like warmth through the chimneys that keeps the brood-chambers steady even when the night air cools above the rift.

Out on the bare expanses of black stone, life belongs to the waiters.

Pressed flat against the sides of the ventilation shafts, the spotted geckos are almost indistinguishable from the rock itself—their skin the exact color of wet basalt flecked with pale, mineral-green spots. They do not hunt; chasing across razor-edged glass and slick stone costs too much and risks a fatal drop. They simply drink the rising warmth of the chimneys through their bellies and wait, lidless eyes fixed on the mud-arcades. When a repair-column of termites breaches a damp wall, or a stray worker wanders an inch beyond the mortar, a tongue flicks out and vanishes before the column even breaks stride.

On the isolated islands of moss and broad-leafed aroids, the pace is slower still. The translucent tree-snails glide across the waxy leaves like drops of living water, their coiled shells so thin and clear that the slow, rhythmic pulse of their hearts is visible beneath the whorl. When a bead of condensation lets go from the ceiling and strikes a leaf with a soft *tock*, the nearest snail pulls its eye-stalks in for a single heartbeat, then slides forward to drink the shattered droplets.

In the damp crevices where the moss meets the bare rock, the velvet worms move. They are soft, soot-black, and unhurried, cushioned on dozens of stubby, clawed legs that let them cross the sharpest basalt ridges without drawing blood. Blind to everything but shadow and scent, they probe the porous pits for woodlice and pale, eyeless springtails. When they find prey, they do not pounce; they raise their blunt heads and loose twin threads of quick-drying slime, pinning the creature to the glistening stone in a net of silent, glistening glue.

And deeper still, in the far corners where the green twilight never reaches and the stone holds its deepest, oldest heat, live the things that belong only to the dark. Flat-bodied whip-spiders cling to the undersides of the raw basalt overhangs, motionless for hours at a time. Their front legs have lengthened into hair-thin, sensory whips that span the width of a hand, drifting just above the wet stone, reading every tremor—the distant, muffled step of a wasp on a paper gallery, the slow scrape of a snail’s radula on a fern, the faint ripple of a drop falling into an angular dimple in the floor.

Nothing here sings, and nothing hurries. The moths drift like dust in the green-filtered shafts; the blind workers mend their damp highways; the geckos warm their bellies against the clay pipes; and the whole sunken city breathes together in the thick, incubating dark, waiting for the next drop of water to fall.
