Because the stone was laid dry, the city is not solid. Behind the battered faces of the walls, between the heavy outer slabs and the rubble cores, there is a second, hidden architecture of narrow voids and cold air-pockets. Nothing here lives entirely in the open; everything that survives on the plateau has learned to inhabit the seams.

In the dampest chinks, where the velvet moss swells like sponge, flat, slate-colored snails move at a pace that seems geological. They carry no heavy shells—only thin, calcified plates tucked beneath their mantles—and spend their lives rasping the sulfur and rust lichens from the windward stone, leaving behind faint, pale tracks that vanish as soon as the afternoon cloud bank wets the rock again. They are eaten by the small, thick-furred mammals that thread the hollow cores of the walls. These creatures are long-bodied and narrow-skulled, built to slip through drainage gaps no wider than two fingers. Their undercoats are dense and oiled, trapping a pocket of dry air against their skin even when they swim through the square settling basins. They rarely show themselves on the upper roofs; instead, they hunt by touch in the dark, listening for the scrape of a snail’s radula or the faint ripple of an amphibian rising to breathe.

Below the alleys, in the subterranean cisterns that honeycomb the bedrock, the temperature never quite reaches freezing, even when the upper terraces glaze with rime. Here, in water so still and clear it looks like black glass, the pale amphibians live out their entire lives. They are translucent, almost ghost-like, with feathery pink gills that flare gently behind their heads and long, delicate toes that never touch the silt. They feed on blind, thread-thin worms and the occasional midge washed down from the roof-runnels. Every few decades, one of them grows unusually large—thick as a wrist, slow and heavy, lingering in the deepest, coldest wells where the single *tock* of a falling drop is the only event for hours.

Above ground, the slate-colored birds—long-legged, thin-necked, with wide, splayed toes that distribute their weight across slick moss—patrol the ankle-deep roof-basins. They are patient to the point of stillness. A bird may stand for an entire morning with its beak poised an inch above the mirror of a settling trough, waiting for the cloud-shadow to pass so it can strike without casting a warning. When it does, there is almost no splash, only a brief, silent parting of the laminar film.

And then there are the keepers.

To call them a population would suggest a bustle the city could never bear. There are perhaps only a few dozen of them, scattered across miles of stepped slate, and they are so thoroughly matched to the plateau’s muted palette and slow rhythms that a stranger could walk the lanes for days and believe the place empty.

They are narrow-shouldered and lean, shaped by generations of moving through corridors where two people cannot pass abreast without turning sideways. They wear no hard-soled boots; leather or wood would clatter on the stone and betray them to the slick, algae-coated troughs of the carved stairs. Instead, they go barefoot through the warmer months and wrap their feet in braided sedge and oiled hide when the frosts come, their soles broad, toughened, and deeply calloused to grip the wet angles of the roofs.

Without timber or peat, there is no fire. The cold chimneys that rise above the corbeled eaves are not for smoke—they are draft-vents, drawing stale, damp air up from the lower rooms. The keepers survive the high-altitude chill not by burning, but by conserving. Their garments are heavy, stiffened cloaks felted from the shed winter fur of the wall-mammals and woven sedge-fiber, rubbed season after season with rendered fat until water beads and sheets off their shoulders just as it does off the pitched slate. When a keeper stands still on an upper terrace in the afternoon mist, hunched slightly to shed the condensation, they are indistinguishable from a weathered chimney-stack or a pile of stacked chinking shards.

They sleep in the windowless, low-ceilinged rooms of the lowest stories, tucked directly against the bedrock above the cisterns, where the stone holds a steady, cool equilibrium and the wind cannot reach. They eat cold, dense foods that require no hearth: pale, meaty fungi cultivated in stone troughs packed with the rich grit scraped from the alley silt-traps; tart, waxy rock-plants pickled in brine-water; and the pale cistern-amphibians, caught in woven baskets and dried on the upper roofs during the brief, flat glare of midday.

Their lives are governed by a single, quiet imperative: the water must not stop.

In a city held together only by gravity and friction, standing water is a slow-motion catastrophe. If a runnel clogs with sedge-roots or a fallen slate, water pools; when the clear night comes, it freezes, expands, and pries the dry-laid joints apart. A single wedged stone popping loose can send a ripple of failure down an entire three-story wall.

So the keepers walk the terraces. They move mostly in the afternoon, when the cloud bank rolls in and wraps the highland in cool, opaque grey. They do not carry tools of iron; iron rusts and snaps in the cold. They carry only heavy, flat-ended wooden pries—ancient, dark, and polished smooth by centuries of hands—and pouches of sharp slate shards. Moving silently along the cantilevered walkways, they feel the flow of the water with their heels and fingertips. Where a laminar sheet thickens or eddies around a clump of moss, they kneel and clear it. Where a lintel has settled a fraction of an inch, they tap a fresh chinking shard into the gap with the heel of their palm—a dull, muffled *thuck* that travels no further than the next alley.

They rarely speak. The narrow, stone-walled canyons distort voices into flat, uncomfortable echoes, and there is little to say that the stone does not already show. They communicate instead through small, deliberate placements. A pale limestone pebble set on a dark slate sill means a cistern below needs skimming. A knotted tuft of wiry sedge wedged into a wall-joint warns of a shifting lintel. If two keepers meet in a lane too narrow to pass, neither speaks; the one closer to a recessed doorway simply steps into the shadow and waits, breathing quietly into the collar of their oiled cloak, while the other brushes past without a sound.

Toward evening, when the cloud bank finally tears open and the low sun pours across the wet expanse, the keepers sometimes climb to the highest roofs. They do not gather in groups. Each finds a dry, sun-warmed slab—one on a western terrace, another a half-mile away across a labyrinth of sunken lanes—and sits with their back against a warm stone wall, letting the thin, silver light soak into their dark coats.

Below them, the city flashes like a vast, broken mirror. Water slides silently through the channels, drops *tock* into the dark wells, and thin veils of vapor rise from the moss-packed joints. The keepers sit motionless, watching the mist lift, breathing the cold air back into the sky alongside the stone.
