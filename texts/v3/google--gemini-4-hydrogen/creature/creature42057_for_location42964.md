If you kneel at the lip of the sinkhole and watch for ten minutes without blinking, the wall of noise begins to separate into voices, and the soup of motion below resolves into a strict, merciless architecture.

Nothing down there is wasted, and nothing down there is soft. Every living thing in the basin has spent uncounted generations trading away whatever moisture, fat, or tenderness its ancestors once possessed, replacing it with horn, mica, salt-crust, and iron-heavy shell. They do not merely live *around* the old machinery; they have fitted themselves into its tolerances.

Down in the ankle-deep silt of the floor, the first things you learn to distinguish are the **tallow-shields**. From above they look like dull, coppery coins scattered through the ash-grey dust, but they are beetles—broad, flat as a thumbprint, with carapaces so thick with ingested iron oxide that they clink like loose change when they collide. Their edges are finely serrated, and when the stubby intake cowls shudder and cough out one of their cold, dry cyclones, the beetles do not scatter. They lock their notched rims together into a single, flexible pavement, pressing their flat bellies to the bedrock until the gale passes, then uncoupling to resume their slow, scraping march toward the weeping pipe joints. They graze on the blackened, waxy crusts of ancient tallow and oxidized grease that seep from the seams, their mandibles working with a dry, rhythmic *snick-snick* that never stops.

Between them move the **stroke-worms**—long, blind, segmented burrowers the color of wet salt, though there is nothing wet about them. Their bodies are ringed with stiff, translucent plates that rattle like dry reeds when they flex. They are creatures of the three-second gap. While the underground compressor drives downward with its heavy *thunk*, the silt is empty of them; the vibration is too violent, the iron floor humming like a struck anvil. But the instant the *hiss* fades and the machinery pauses between strokes, the floury floor boils. Hundreds of them erupt at once, rasping up mouthfuls of rust-flakes and powdered mineral before diving back under as the next stroke begins. Their shed skins, left behind in the dust, are hollow, rigid tubes that never rot; smaller things—tiny, colorless mites that pour from the pitted holes in the iron—use those discarded husks as highways beneath the silt.

Above the floor, the sunlit stone ledges and the cool, shaded ducts belong to the **shingle-beasts**.

The most numerous are the **mica-skinks**, dry-skinned lizards no longer than a hand, packed so tightly along the horizontal pipes that they overlap like roof tiles. Their scales are not supple; they are thin, translucent plates of keratinized mineral that click together when they breathe. They have no water to drink, and no need of it—their kidneys are solid, chalky knots, and their bodies run on the thick, oily hemolymph of the beetles they ambush and the tiny scuttlers they snatch from the bolt-holes. In the hottest hour of noon, when the sun drops straight into the bowl, you can see them pressing their pale, porous bellies flat against the shaded undersides of the intake ducts, where the cold subterranean draft chills the metal just enough to keep them from cooking in their own shells.

Clinging among them, and often atop them, are the **bolt-crabs**. Spiny, six-legged, and drab as weathered lead, they have forelimbs flattened into heavy, notched wrenches designed for gripping curved iron and the square heads of old rivets. Some of the older crabs have grown so reluctant to give up a prime perch near a vent-hole that the rust-dust and salt-flour have cemented their rear plates permanently to the pipes. There they sit, living barnacles of shell and spite, snapping blindly at anything that passes within reach of their claws while the younger crabs clamber straight over their backs.

And then there is the air.

Because the pit is a steep-walled chimney and the cowls below never stop exhaling, a cold, dry updraft spirals endlessly toward the plain. Riding it are the **draft-kites**—pale, leathery-winged gliders with bodies no thicker than a quill and wings stretched over hollow, calcium-stiffened struts. They are all tendon and membrane, weightless as shed skin. To land on the pipes is to be torn apart by the shingle-beasts, so the kites simply do not land. They mate in mid-spiral, drop their leathery, salt-cased eggs into the crevices of the high rock walls, and even sleep on the wing, locking their shoulder joints stiff so the cold, metallic wind holds them aloft in tight, colliding circles.

Above them, just under the lip where the daylight is sharpest, hang the **clack-hawks**. Smaller, heavier-chested flyers with beaks like iron awls and stiff, wire-like bristles framing their faces to read the shifting pressure of the updraft, they do not ride the wind so much as fight it. They hover in the eddies near the great exposed gear-train, their eyes fixed on the ancient relief valve jutting from the housing below. Every forty seconds, the valve trips with a sharp, deafening *clack*. In that exact fraction of a second—masked by the shockwave of sound—the hawks fold their wings and drop like stones into the funnel, snatching kites out of the spiral or plucking lizards straight off the ducts before the echoes have even died against the stone.

Yet the hawks do not rule the pit. In the dark, tight elbows of the ducts, and wedged deep inside the pitted housings of the machinery, live the **caliper-jaws**.

You can stare straight at one for five minutes and see only a shadow or a bent bracket of cast iron. Their bodies are matte-black, pitted, and angular, with six long, jointed limbs that fold tightly against their sides like a surveyor's compass. They have no eyes at all. Instead, long, thread-thin feelers extend from their heads and press directly against the iron pipes, reading the whole basin through vibration. They know the weight of a lizard shifting three meters away by the tick of its claws on sheet metal; they know when a clack-hawk is diving above them by the sudden change in the duct's hum. When they strike, they unfold with a dry, mechanical snap, their heavy, serrated mandibles catching flyers out of mid-air and dragging them back into the dark bends of the iron before a single feather or wing-strut can hit the floor.

If you watch long enough, though, you notice one place in the pit that none of these creatures dare to crowd.

At the very bottom, where the cartwheel gear-train turns in its rusted casing, the outer iron is choked with shed husks and mineral grit—but the inner teeth gleam like fresh-honed silver. Nothing falls into that mesh and survives; a beetle or a lizard swept into the teeth is pulverized into dry paste in a heartbeat.

Yet something lives *inside* the gears.

If you catch the light at the right angle as the great wheel turns, you can see them: pale, eyeless, grease-slicked things no larger than a finger-joint, clinging to the roots of the gleaming teeth. They are soft where everything else in the pit is armored, their flat, rubbery feet gripping the polished steel. As the gears mesh, they dart forward in the fraction of a breath before the teeth lock, scraping away the stray salt-flour, the rust-flakes, the crushed bits of chitin that would otherwise jam the iron. They eat the grit that would kill the engine, and in return, the engine's relentless, crushing motion keeps every predator in the basin at bay.

They are the only silent things in the pit. While the lizards clatter, the beetles click, the relief valve *clacks*, and the compressor groans beneath the stone, those small, pale cleaners move in perfect, greased silence along the bright teeth—tending the heart of a machine that has outlived whoever built it, and will outlive everything else that crawls in its shadow.
