If you walk across the springy mat for half a day, you will swear the basin is empty. You will see the bone-pale lizards clinging beneath the fronds, the papery insects creeping along the damp bark seams, and the low, resin-daubed domes half-swallowed by the creepers—and you will think that whatever built them is long gone.

They are not gone. They are simply tuned to the same stillness as the plants, and they have learned that in a world without wind or shadow, the only way to survive is to become almost nothing at all.

They live both within the woven mat and beneath it, in the narrow, un-eroded space where the pale roots grasp the raw mineral seams. To see one at rest is to mistake it for a knot of cork-barked creeper or a pale, dusty shelf of quartz. They are lean, low-slung beings, light enough to cross the lattice of hollow canes without snapping a stem. Their skin is hairless, matte, and faintly waxy, the color of dried pith and milky stone, dusted with the fine white chalk of dried mineral puddles so that even in the bounced, luminous glare, they do not gleam. Their limbs are long and flat-jointed, ending in wide, padded palms and soles and broad, horn-thick fingernails—the hardest tools they possess.

Above all, they are creatures built to endure the light. Heavy, pleated brows shade their faces, and behind their outer lids sits a second membrane, translucent as frosted glass, which stays drawn against the relentless white glare. Through it, the world is only shapes of glowing chartreuse and pale, shifting depth. Sight is a secondary sense to them anyway; in a valley where the air never moves, the entire miles-wide floor of woven canes is a taut, silent drumhead. They press their bare palms and the sensitive ridges of their forearms against the hollow, fluted stems, and through the wood they *hear* the basin: the faint *tick* of a leaf expanding in the heat, the heavy *plink* of a condensation drop falling into a cupped rosette, the slow, unmistakable *give* of a cane bending under a lizard’s weight thirty paces away.

They do not stay in the domes you find. Those low, woven shelters are temporary things, built when a wanderer pauses for a few weeks or months to wait out a dry spell or tend to a brood. Using only their broad nails and the weight of their hands, they bend the living creepers together without ever cutting them—for to sever a cane is to spill hot, blinding sap and to weaken the living floor that keeps them suspended above the razor-sharp quartz below. They daub the gaps with warm resin scraped from the bark, creating the only dimness on the surface, and press shallow bowls of chalk-silt and sap beneath the heaviest, wettest paddles to catch the slow drip of condensation.

They leave the domes behind because the basin does not stop growing. Even without wind or seasons, the creepers thicken in the hot, muggy equilibrium. The walls of a dome slowly sprout inward, the resin softens and seals the entrance, and the canopy above shifts, stealing the drip from the bowl. When the shelter grows too tight and the bowl runs dry, the maker slips out—leaving the old nest to be swallowed by the mat—and crawls slowly onward, unhurried, toward a new seam of moisture.

Where the mat thins and the freshly fractured stone drops away into dark crevices, you will find their small cairns of sharp crystal shards. In a windless world, a delicately balanced stack of quartz will stand untouched for decades. They place them along the flat rock-shelves as quiet sentinels—markers for the safe ledges above the dark, mineral-heavy puddles, and tremor-bells that will clatter sharply across the silent valley if anything heavy passes too close. Occasionally, when a shard is needed to score a hardened plug of resin or pry open a dead cane, they take one from a cairn, leaving the living plants whole.

Most of their lives are spent *under* the mat, where the glare finally fails. Down among the grasping roots and the razor-sharp ledges, the air is heavy with the smell of wet chalk and old pith. There, they cling upside-down to the thick fibrous lattice to keep off the cutting stone, or crouch beside the warm, dark puddles, sipping the mineral-heavy water and waiting out the endless, unchanging heat. They are sparse and solitary; the basin offers too little water to support crowds. Yet they are not strangers to one another. Every few hours, if you press your ear to one of the hollow, fluted canes, you may catch a slow, deliberate rhythm—a series of soft, woody *knocks* traveling through the silent floor from half a mile away, one quiet presence letting another know it is still there, resting in the luminous dark.
