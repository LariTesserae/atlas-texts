If you watch the plateau long enough, you begin to see them—not as a sudden interruption of the landscape, but as another kind of movement within it, no more hurried than the mist or the grazing herds.

They are a tall, broad-chested people, built for thin air and long horizons. Generations at this altitude have given them deep ribcages and a slow, economical way of breathing; even when they climb the crisp geometric steps between residential tiers, their pace never quickens into a pant. Their skin is weathered to the tones of the rock—pale granite or dark, sun-burnished basalt—with fine lines around the eyes from squinting against the bright, ordinary sun reflecting off wet stone and morning ice. Their hair is thick and usually bound back or braided close to the skull, kept out of the sharp wind.

Because the rock is young and full of splinters, and the cold never truly leaves, they dress in heavy, layered practicality. Their outer coats are woven from the dense, water-shedding fleece of the plateau’s grazers, dyed in muted shades of slate, ash, and the copper of the crust-lichens, and rubbed with mineral oils until rain beads off them like mercury. Beneath, they wear fitted tunics of spun sedge-fiber and soft inner linings that hold body heat without bulk. Their boots are their most intricate possessions: thick-soled, articulated, and quiet, with gripping treads patterned after the cushioned hooves of the herds so they can cross slick, weeping basalt without slipping or making a sound.

They do not speak much outdoors. In the thin air, voices carry strangely—either swallowed instantly by the wind or ringing too sharply off the raw cliff faces—so most of their communication in the open is done with small, unhurried gestures: a tilt of the chin toward a gathering squall, two fingers raised to mark a slate-gray predator shadowing the ridge. When they do talk, their voices are low, chest-deep, and spare.

They live in the scattered districts like people who have made a quiet truce with emptiness. Each district has its own modest focus—some built around the broad agricultural basins where dark silt collects, others tucked into the lee of high ridges where the research halls sit, still others terraced into the sun-facing cliffs for warmth. Yet no one stays in one place for long. All day, figures stand calmly on the slender, pillarless causeways, wrapped in their heavy cloaks, gliding above the wet flats on silent repulsion fields. They lean slightly into the wind as the platforms carry them over the shallow pools and the churning filter-feeders, watching the herds below with the same detached, affectionate attention a sailor gives to the sea.

To them, the physics that sustains their cities is entirely beneath notice. There are no technicians bustling around the dark, mute monoliths out on the flats, no one squinting at readouts or adjusting dials. The people here interact with their infrastructure the way others interact with weather or stone. A woman stepping into a residential terrace does not look for a switch; the sintered wall simply recognizes the warmth of her palm and parts, sealing behind her against the damp. A group of surveyors waiting at a transit ribbon does not summon a platform; the causeway senses their collective weight and stillness, and a slate-gray disc drifts up to meet them without a whisper. If a basin’s thermal draw shifts or a conduit hums—though they almost never hum—someone will simply press a flat, glass-and-copper stone against the nearest rock face to listen, nod once, and leave the system to balance itself.

Their real work is slower, closer to the mud and the cold water.

In the agricultural basins, where the subtle warmth of the urban terraces keeps the pools from freezing solid at night, they wade knee-deep in mineral-rich water, tending vast, terraced mats of edible algae, cold-tolerant tubers, and thick-stemmed sedges. They work alongside the pale amphibians and segmented filter-feeders, never clearing them out, treating the little creatures as unpaid partners that keep the silt turned and the water sweet. Harvests are carried up in woven baskets—not in great noisy convoys, but in steady, quiet streams of workers moving up the stepped terraces.

In the research halls—long, bare-walled rooms of dark stone and seamless glass looking out over the raw plains—others spend decades watching the plateau age. Because the rock has never known glaciers or eons of wind, every tiny change is an event. They map the first hairline cracks widening under the night-frost; they catalog where copper lichen first takes hold on a bare shelf of granite; they measure the millimeter-by-millimeter deepening of the silt in the marshy hollows. Their records are kept not on glowing screens, but in dense, tactile archives—thin plates of sintered glass and stone etched with fine, heatless light, stacked neatly in dry alcoves.

Their relationship with the wild herds is neither mastery nor distance. They do not pen the long-legged grazers, nor do they hunt the slate-gray predators that follow them. Instead, during the brief, bright weeks when the sun warms the midday flats, groups of herders walk out onto the open stone with wide, curved shears, moving calmly among the beasts. The grazers, accustomed to the quiet, heavy-coated figures since birth, barely lift their heads from the lichen as their thick winter fleece is trimmed away. When a squall sweeps across the plateau, herders and herd alike simply turn their backs to the wind, stand shoulder to shoulder until the cold drizzle passes, and then move on.

At dusk, as the mist rises directly off the cooling stone and the surface water begins to stiffen into delicate lace, the districts draw inward. Inside the terraced homes, there are no harsh lamps—only the soft, heatless glow of mineral-infused wall panels, tuned to the amber of a low fire. Families and small communal groups gather around low stone tables to drink hot, salty broths thickened with algae-meal and roasted sedge-seed, their wet boots steaming gently on warm floor tiles.

They do not seem like a people waiting for the world to be finished, nor like conquerors of a harsh frontier. They have the unhurried patience of things that belong to a very young place—content to sit in the quiet, listening to the distant, muffled crunch of hooves on wet gravel, watching the first thin skin of soil form on stone that was pushed up yesterday.
