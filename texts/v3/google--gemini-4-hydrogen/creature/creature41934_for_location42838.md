If you stood on the rim of the basin at noon, looking down into the blinding white-and-rust labyrinth of ice, you would swear the hollow was empty. The glare is too fierce to look at anything for long, and the shadows thrown by the leaning blades are so sharp and ink-black they seem solid as slate. 

Yet something lives in those shadows, moving at the pace of the sun.

It is narrow enough to slip sideways through the gaps where the ice-blades crowd closest together, places where a person could barely wedge a flat palm. In posture it is low and folded, no taller than the gneiss windbreaks even when it rises to its full height, with a long, flat-ribbed torso and four spare, double-jointed limbs that end in broad, fissured pads. It has no fur—fur would mat with the midday brine and freeze solid by dusk—and no feathers. Instead, its hide is dry, overlapping, and papery, like flakes of weathered slate or dried salt-crusts. Iron dust from the bedrock and chalky mineral blooms have stained its flanks in muted bands of rust, grey, and bone-white, so that when it presses itself against the base of a blade or crouches in the scree, it disappears into the basin’s own geology.

Its head is a blunt, narrow wedge, almost eyeless. Two small, dark lenses sit deep behind overhanging ridges of bone, shielded from the hammer-blow of the noon sky; it sees the world mostly as sharp borders between heat and cold, glare and shade. What it lacks in sight it makes up for in stillness and listening. Thin, stiffened membranes tucked along the hollows of its throat and ribs register the faintest shifts in air pressure, the *ticking* of crystal lattices fracturing in the sun, and the distant *clatter* of frost-loosened gneiss.

By mid-morning, when the sun reaches the angles of the ice-blades and the hollow begins to sigh, it is already awake. It never steps into the direct light if it can help it; the unblinking glare dries its papery joints and blinds its recessed eyes. Instead, it threads the shifting, ink-black corridors on the leeward sides of the blades, gliding over the glassy ice on its wide, rough-skinned pads. As the sun’s warmth agitates the windward faces and thin beads of briny moisture creep down the shaded undersides, it follows them. 

It feeds with a long, narrow, grooved tongue, drawing it slowly up the freezing glass to catch the beads before they can reach the bedrock and crust into salt. Nothing grows in the basin, and no insect crawls under the scree, yet the creature does not starve: the cloud-sea below is full of life, and every afternoon’s mist carries upward a fine, invisible dust of pollen, fungal spores, and dried mountain-ash, trapping it in the rime. When the morning sun sublimates the frost, all that wind-blown detritus from the lower world concentrates in the bitter midday drip. The creature harvests it drop by drop, along with the iron and mineral salts that keep its papery hide tough and brittle-hard.

When the sun arcs westward and the shadows of the ice-blades lengthen and shift, the creature shifts with them, sliding silently from fin to fin like a darker seam in the ice. By late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the ridge and the temperature plummets, it abandons the labyrinth and climbs to the higher, ice-free rim along the northern ridge.

It knows the old marks of hands there, though it has no memory of hands and no sense of what they were. To it, the three low, curving windbreaks are simply stone that holds the faintest memory of the day’s heat, shaped just well enough to turn the thin hiss of the rising wind. It ignores the hand-hollowed bowl—the pale yellow salt caked in its bottom is too sharp and bitter even for its palate—and it steps carefully past the small cairns topped with milky quartz, whose smooth surfaces offer no grip.

It settles inside the curve of the northernmost windbreak, tucking its long limbs tight against its ribs and folding its wedge-shaped head beneath a thick, salt-crusted collar at its shoulders. 

A few paces away, the three slate discs on the crooked staff begin to drink the rising mist. As the cold, damp tongues spill over the lip of the saddle, the discs coat with frost, growing heavier, until the faintest draft sets them knocking softly against the hammered copper wire—a dry, deadened *clink* that carries across the scree.

The creature listens to that sound. As the mist reaches the windbreak and lays a fresh skin of rime over its papery back, sealing it into the stone, it closes the bony shutters over its eyes and lets the cold reclaim it until morning.
