At first, the noise and the dimness conspire to hide half of what lives here. The roar blurs the edges of things, turning the trembling puddles into mirrors of static and making every suspended cable look like it is vibrating out of phase with the wall behind it. Once your eyes adjust to the bruised-violet bleed and the dull amber of the floor-filaments, you begin to see the small, frantic traffic of the room—the grazers, the swimmers, the pale climbers wedged in the rigging.

It takes much longer to realize that the room is not merely infested. It is inhabited, from the sumps beneath the grating all the way up to the vibrating skin of the bulkhead itself.

Start at the bottom, where the work is simplest. The **vibration-sensing mites** are barely visible as individuals; they look like pale, moving rust along the weeping seams of the floor-grating, clustering wherever mineral-rich condensate seeps out. They have no eyes and no hardened shells, just soft, segmented bodies bristling with fine hairs tuned to the room’s baseline hum. When a human boot or a falling droplet disturbs that hum, the entire swarm scatters in a synchronized ripple, vanishing into the microscopic pits of the ceramic-alloy.

Hunting them through the shallow catch-channels are the **ribbon-swimmers**—blind, boneless things the length of a finger and the color of wet parchment. They have no fins; they move by throwing their flat bodies into rapid, sinusoidal waves that push against the warm runoff. Because the residual field-leakage from the manifold pulls the falling condensation sideways, the swimmers have adapted to a world where water does not strike vertically. They orient themselves by the slight, constant tug of the field, gliding sideways-tilted through the channels, snapping up mites with pincer-like mouthparts that click softly against the metal—a sound completely lost beneath the roar.

Above the grating, the vertical world belongs to the **armored grazers** and the **pale climbers**.

The grazers are slow, stubborn geometry. No larger than thumbnails, their flattened shells are built from the same pale, salt-like deposits they scrape from the heat-exchanger fins, giving them the exact pitted texture of the ancient walls. They have no distinct head, only a muscular, suction-cupped foot that seals them to the wet ceramic and a rasping radula that grinds away at the sparse mats of heat-drinking lichen. They are functionally deaf to the air, reading the chamber entirely through their soles. A single grazer can tell the difference between the steady thrum of the manifold and the approaching footsteps of a climber three baffles away.

The climbers themselves—those small, pale-skinned amphibians, or whatever evolutionary branch has folded them into this shape—are the sharpest minds in the lower rigging. Roughly the size of a spread hand, they are gaunt and long-fingered, with translucent eyelids permanently drawn against the slanted, mineral-heavy rain. Their skin is slick and nearly colorless, except at their throats, which swell into bruised, violet-tinted sacs when they press against the tension-cables.

They never use their voices. Instead, they communicate through bone-conduction, clamping their jaws and throats directly against the humming ribs of the room and tapping rapid, intricate rhythms with their calloused knuckles and chins. These codes travel cleanly through the ceramic-alloy, bypassing the deafening air entirely. They tap to claim dry hollows where cables cross; they tap to warn of rising heat; and, most strangely, they tap to keep time. A climber perched on a condenser-plate will often sit motionless for minutes, eyes shut, nodding in tiny, erratic increments—mirroring not the massive, steady roar of the fluid manifold, but the faint, irregular *ticks* and *pulses* that bleed through the wall from the computational lattice buried inside.

Yet even the climbers avoid the largest surface in the room.

To one side, the curved, matte-black bulkhead rises from grating to ceiling, acting as the chamber’s great diaphragm. Nothing grazes on it. No lichen takes root there. The heat is too fierce, and the vibration is violent enough to rattle the shells off the grazers’ backs. At first glance, the bulkhead looks utterly bare—just a vast, sweating expanse of black metal, scarred by a few worn patches where the sacrificial skin has thinned and the bruised-violet glow leaks out.

Look closer, though, at the widest of those violet-stained patches, and you will see that one of the bulges in the wall is breathing.

It is roughly two paces long and nearly as wide, shaped like a flattened tear or a heavy, wingless ray, and it is pressed so tightly against the bulkhead that its edges seem to melt into the metal. Its hide is matte-black and slick with condensation, crusted along its central ridge with the same pale mineral scale that coats the seams of the room. Only when the violet bleed catches its spine—a row of seven translucent, fluid-filled nodules running down its back—do you realize it is not a housing or a patch-plate.

It is the oldest thing in the pocket, and it has made its peace with the roar by becoming part of the wall.

Its underside is not skin at all, but a thick, corrugated pad of shock-absorbing cartilage and heavy mucus that couples directly to the bulkhead’s vibration. Rather than fighting the bone-rattling thrum, it rides it, letting the mechanical energy ripple through its tissues to drive a slow, heat-tolerant metabolism. It has no eyes. Its face, if it can be called that, is a blunt, sensory hood tucked beneath the leading edge of its mantle, studded with electro-receptive pits that drink in the stray fields and waste-photons bleeding from the worn computational lattice beneath it.

For seventeen minutes out of every twenty, it does not move. It simply lies over the bruised-violet glow, humming at the exact frequency of the wall, its spine-nodules pulsing in slow, asynchronous time with the sub-atomic work happening inches behind its belly.

Then, deep inside the manifold, the pressure-membrane shifts.

The bass *whump* drops the roar by half an octave, and the whole ecosystem snaps to attention. As warm, cloudy gray-water surges up through the floor-grating, the grazers clamp down hard, sealing their shells against the current. The ribbon-swimmers shoot upward into the flooded crevices, gorging on mites washed loose from the mineral crusts. The pale climbers scramble higher up the tension-cables, their throats fluttering as they tap frantic, overlapping warnings into the ribs.

And on the bulkhead, as the wall’s tension slackens for those three brief minutes, the great flat sleeper finally stirs.

With a wet, sucking hiss that is barely audible even in the lowered roar, it peels the lower edge of its mantle away from the matte-black metal. Beneath, its belly is a startling, luminous white, ribbed like the gills of a mushroom and faintly stained with absorbed violet light. From the gap, it extends a dozen long, pale, feathery palps—not rigid tentacles, but soft, drift-netting filaments that catch the rising steam and the slanted rain.

It sweeps them lazily through the humid air and down toward the swirling gray-water. It does not hunt with speed; it simply waits for the flood’s chaos to bring things to it—a drowned mite, a ribbon-swimmer swept too high, a careless climber that loses its grip on a slick cable and tumbles against the waiting, feathery net. Whatever touches the palps is numbed instantly by a mild, field-induced shock and drawn smoothly under the mantle to be dissolved against the heat of the bulkhead.

Within three minutes, the surge drains away through the lower sumps. The roar climbs back up half an octave, settling into its massive, familiar groove.

The pale climbers creep back down the dripping cables, resuming their nervous, rhythmic tapping against the ribs. The grazers unclamp and begin scraping at the fresh film of mineral slurry. And on the matte-black wall, the great flat shadow settles back over the bruised-violet bleed, sealing its edges tight against the metal until its crusted hide vanishes once more into the geology of the room.
