In the pewter twilight, you do not see them until they choose to shift.

At rest, they are indistinguishable from the fractured crust of the plateau—low, broad mounds of wet basalt and carbon-black slag, half-buried in the soot-colored moss. Only when a stray ribbon of violet lightning sheets silently across the clouds, or when a thermal fissure sighs warm mist beneath them, do their outlines separate from the stone. A faint, cold-green flicker runs along a ridge that turns out to be a spine; a cluster of what looked like copper-veined fern fronds unfolds into a broad, oil-slicked flank.

They have no hands, and nothing about them was made for shaping the world. They move on four heavy, multi-jointed limbs, wide-stanced and low to the spongy mycelial mat, keeping their weight beneath the restless, heated wind. Where fingers might have been, there are only broad, cloven hooves of dark, vulcanized horn rimmed with raw, oxidized copper—built to bite into the knife-edged basalt without slipping, and to bleed static safely into the earth with every step.

Their heads are featureless, sloped shields of dull mineral-bone, smooth as wet slate and pitted with dozens of tiny, fluid-filled pores. In a place where sound dies almost before it is born, they have no ears, and no mouths that open to call out. Instead, those pores read the plateau’s silent turbulence: the sudden slackening of tension before a gravity pocket lifts a pool of water into the air, the sub-audible hum of a nearby copper coil, the distant, pressureless shudder of an obsidian shelf peeling apart into hovering sheets. Where eyes might sit, there are only twin, sunken strips of pale tissue that glow a steady, muted amber—not to see by, for they navigate by field and current, but to speak.

They do not build shelters because the plateau’s heat is already a roof, and its fury is their larder.

When the ambient charge in the air grows thin, they gather along the sheer cliff faces where native copper and iron have braided themselves into natural coils. They press their broad, plated chests directly against the humming metal, closing their sensory pores and drinking the high-voltage current into dense, spongy organs packed along their ribs. The air around them thickens with the smell of hot ozone and scorched sap as their internal capacitors fill, until a dull, slag-orange glow radiates faintly from between the seams of their hides.

At the churning thermal vents, they wade knee-deep into the chemical vats—not to forge, but to graze. Extending long, fibrous palps from beneath their sloped mineral shields, they skim the iridescent films and freshly precipitated metallic crystals from the boiling condensate. The metals are not merely food; their bodies weave the raw copper and iron slowly into their own skeletons and horn-plates, mending what the razor-sharp rock and stray arcs wear away. When a gravity pocket slackens nearby and a pool of warm condensate rises into a trembling, waist-high sphere, they do not cup it or flee it; they simply step forward and press their sensory shields into the suspended globe, drawing the mineral-rich water in before the gravity snaps back and the sphere splatters across the stone.

Even the fraying physics of the place has a rhythm they know how to inhabit.

Beneath the great, unweathered slabs of lodestone that hover and spin in silence, they gather to sleep. There, in the immense, steady pull of the magnetic flywheels, the iron in their blood and plates aligns with the stone above. They settle into the moss with their limbs tucked tight, hovering a fraction of an inch off the ground—just like the rock—suspended in a motionless, humming trance while the silent storm turns overhead.

And when the air shimmers and a patch of terrain stutters into a spatial echo, they do not recoil like the blind flyers or phase past it like the translucent grazers. They step deliberately into the overlap. For a few breathless minutes, two identical, oil-slicked forms stand side by side in the steaming ferns, touching their amber strips together in the dimness, sharing the brief, strange warmth of a duplicate self before the echo collapses and leaves only one, slightly heavier shadow behind.

They move in loose, silent congregations, never crowding. Too many charged bodies in a single crevice will arc and burn, so they space themselves across the jagged ridges with the careful, measured distance of nodes in a circuit. When one finds a newly opened thermal vent or a vein of raw metal tearing free of the crust, it pulses—a slow, rhythmic ripple of amber down its flank. The nearest neighbor catches the light, translates it into a cold-green flicker, and passes it on. Within heartbeats, the signal races across miles of dark, tangled brush, weaving seamlessly into the silent bioluminescent chatter of the lesser beasts.

They are neither the masters of this unbuilt foundry nor its scavengers. They are its living insulation—its grounds, its valves, its quiet, steady bleed. Where a copper coil threatens to overload and shatter a cliff face, they gather to drain it; where a chemical vat thickens with too much slag, they scour it clean.

And when one of them finally wears out—when its internal capacitors crack from a surge too fierce, or a spatial collapse catches it mid-stride—it does not rot into soft soil. It settles into a narrow fracture in the basalt, and the copper-veined ferns thread through its hollow mineral ribs, and the pale, segmented crawlers strip its hide for glassy slag, until nothing remains of it at all—only another jagged, conductive ridge of the plateau, humming quietly in the warm dark.
