If you stand still long enough for the water to stop welling around your ankles, and if you let your breathing slow to match the *plink* and *pat* of the canopy-drip, you begin to notice that the empty alcoves are not quite as abandoned as they seem.

The creatures who weave them are rarely seen—not because they are shy in the way of hunted things, for nothing here knows the terror of a chase, but because they belong so thoroughly to the pace of the plateau that a hurried eye slides right past them.

They are long-limbed and slender, built for a world without hard ground or open sky. When they stand on the moss-mattress, they stoop slightly, their weight distributed across broad, splayed feet with deeply grooved soles that grip the slick bryophytes without breaking the surface skin. Their skin itself is bare, damp, and faintly translucent at the knuckles and ribs—much like the cartilage-soft walls of the siphon-reeds. It is the color of steeped tea and wet amber lignite, mottled across the shoulders and spine with pale, velvety patches of harmless lichen that they share with the older columns. Along their forearms and the sides of their necks grow sparse, thread-thin filaments—not hair, but sensory fringes that stir in the faintest thermal drift rising from the composting floor.

Their faces are quiet and unhurried: wide, soft-lipped mouths concealing flat, chisel-edged front teeth, and large, lidless eyes the color of cloudy pondwater. In the bruised olive twilight, those eyes see little of shape or edge; they are tuned instead to light and shadow, reading the dim glow of the water-bladder epiphytes overhead like distant, submerged lanterns.

To know the plateau, they rely on two other senses entirely.

The first is vibration. The thirty-meter mattress of peat and roots beneath them is waterlogged and continuous, carrying low-frequency tremors the way a pond carries ripples. Through the sensitive pads of their feet and the long, four-fingered palms of their hands, they feel the plateau’s pulse: the hour-long *groan* of a shifting reed-column half a mile away, the rhythmic *thump-crawl* of a grazer herd, or the sharp, alien *suck* of a heavy boot lifting from the moss. Before you have even crossed the hundred-pace rim of the haze, anyone resting in a nearby alcove has already felt your approach through the living wood of the reeds.

The second is scent. In a world with no wind to speak of, smells do not blow away; they hang in thick, invisible strata between the columns. The weavers read the air through flared, gill-like folds along their cheekbones, tasting the layered history of a place—who passed through three days ago, whether they were heavy with young, or whether they had recently fed on the sweet, faintly luminescent cores of the epiphyte bladders.

---

They do not speak. Sound dies too quickly in the wet moss, and a loud noise feels violent here, like a tear in the mat. Instead, they converse through touch and chemistry. When two meet along a root-bridge, they press their palms against the same siphon-reed column and squeeze in slow, deliberate rhythms, sending private pressure-waves down the water-filled tubes inside the plant. At parting, they rub the scent-glands at their wrists against the bark, leaving a faint, musky signature of wet bark and warm peat that will linger for weeks.

This is why the teardrop alcoves are always empty when you look inside.

A weaver does not build a shelter when it is tired; it *grows* a shelter for a future self, or for a cousin who will pass that way a decade later. Moving in loose, silent kin-groups along vast circuits that take twenty years to walk, they tend hundreds of these living chambers across the plateau. A weaver will find a promising fork in two young siphon-reeds, gently bruise the rubbery shoots with its flat teeth so they bend without snapping, and hook them together with its blunt, curved claws—claws shaped not for tearing flesh, but for parting roots and tucking stems. Then it moves on.

Years later, another weaver—drawn by the faint, lingering musk—will pause at the same fork, see how the bruised shoots have thickened and fused, and braid in the next layer. Over fifteen or twenty years, the living wood closes into a dry-ish, hollow cradle suspended above the dampness of the floor. They are used only briefly: for a few days of deep, motionless sleep, to wait out the birth of a single, slow-developing young, or to shed the thin, silvery outer film of skin they slough off every few decades.

And if one happens to be curled inside when a stranger’s noisy footsteps disturb the peat, it does not fight or flee in panic. It simply uncurls, slips out through the upper weave like water through a sieve, and pulls itself silently up into the curtains of aerial roots overhead—hanging motionless among the glowing green bladders until the strange vibrations fade back into the drip of the canopy.

---

Their days are spent in an unhurried forage that blurs into the life of the plateau itself. They peel the soft outer rind from young siphon-reeds to chew the sweet, watery pith; they tap rhythmically on the moss with their knuckles to mimic heavy rain, coaxing the blind, pale segmented things up from the peat-channels; they pluck the translucent epiphytes, leaving a faint, ghostly green glow on their lips and fingertips for an hour afterward.

With the wide, moss-backed grazers, they keep a quiet, ancient truce. They travel alongside the crawling hillocks, grooming the tough, choking vines and parasitic tree-snails from the grazers’ backs—eating the snails, leaving the moss undisturbed. In return, the grazers allow the weavers to rest against their warm, composting flanks, or to drink the clear, mineral-rich condensation that gathers in the deep folds of their necks.

Even the broad-winged drifters serve them, though only in death. When an old drifter’s paper-thin membranes finally tear and it settles onto the moss to become part of the floor, the weavers gather silently around it. They do not eat it—there is little to it but dry membrane and air—but they carefully strip away its long, thread-thin feelers, using them to tie the first delicate knots of a new alcove before the living shoots are strong enough to hold on their own.

---

Only one thing on the plateau puzzles them.

Once in a great while, where a shallow pool has worn the moss thin, a weaver will come across one of the smooth, soap-soft nubs of ancient granite poking an inch above the peat. To beings who know only cartilage-soft reeds, spongy moss, and yielding water, stone is an impossibility: cold, dead, unyielding, and utterly devoid of an internal pulse.

They have no concept of tools, or of anything stacked or carved. To them, these rare nubs are simply places where the floor forgot to be alive. Yet they are drawn to them. A wandering group will sometimes pause around a granite nub for hours, pressing their sensitive palms against the slick, green-coated rock, marveling at its silence. Before they move on into the olive haze, one of them will almost always rub its wrist against the stone—for stone, unlike wet bark or peat, holds their musk for months at a time, a stubborn little marker in a world that otherwise swallows everything back into itself.
