If you stand still in the dark and press your ear against a felt-wrapped strut, you begin to realize that the quiet is crowded.

Nothing here has wet flesh, or blood, or lungs. Animal life would desiccate in an hour or boil in the updrafts. Yet the valley is not empty; it has simply grown its own inhabitants out of the same dry, oiled, high-tension stuff as its walls.

The smallest of them live in the dust and the waste heat. Where the thermal whirlwinds corkscrew up through the narrow gaps between the towers, things hang from the cane lattices like soot-blackened paper lanterns. They are anchored by single, taut threads of copper-veined bast that hum with static. When a hot gust roars past, carrying ash and millions of papery winged seeds, these hanging things unfurl ribs of cured reed stretched with thin, oil-cured membranes. They catch the swirling chaff, fold shut, and use the buffeting of the wind itself to rock internal gizzards of vitrified resin—grinding the dry seeds into meal and squeezing out the last microscopic beads of oil before spitting the carbonized husks back into the draft.

Down along the overheated friction-bearings, where the wood glows a dull, ember-red, something else gathers. They look at first like stray knots of braided vine or discarded wooden couplers, no bigger than a clenched fist, resting on six or eight jointed legs of split cane. They do not eat wood; they feed on excess heat. One will scuttle up to a smoking axle, clamp its legs around the housing, and sit utterly still. Inside its hollow shell, a tight spiral of layered heartwood and pith—two woods that expand at different rates when baked—slowly curls tighter and tighter in the oven-heat. When the coil is wound to its limit, the little thing releases its grip with a sharp, dry *tick*, indistinguishable from the pressure-valves all around it, and skitters away across the swollen phloem-pipes on its stored spring-tension. It spends that borrowed energy in the cooler crevices, drilling tiny holes into oil-ducts to siphon a drop of volatile lubricant, or shedding its own dry spores into the nearest updraft.

Then there are the ones that walk the tiers.

You can stand ten feet from one in the ember-lit dark and mistake it for a supporting strut or a bundle of spare belting. They are tall, narrow, and slightly hunched to clear the low, claustrophobic ceilings of the stacked galleries. Their frames are grown rather than carved—articulated lattices of iron-hard burl-wood and cured cane, wrapped tightly in overlapping bandages of deadened root-felt so that their movement makes almost no sound at all. Only a soft, dry *shush-shush* of oiled pith-sockets sliding in bark sleeves, lost immediately in the ambient hush of the factory.

They have no eyes; in a lightless valley, eyes would only dry out and crack. Instead, where a face might be, there is a smooth, blank shield of amber-dark vitrified resin, slightly concave like a listening dish. Along its rim, and trailing from the backs of their jointed wrists, grow fine, nervous fringes of copper-veined fibers. These bristles twitch constantly, reading the faint blue static charges that gather on the drive-belts and tasting the rising heat of a bearing about to seize. Through the broad, flat pads of their wooden feet, they feel the whole valley as a single, continuous vibration—a map of low thrums and sudden, discordant shudders.

They have no stomachs, and they do not sleep. Instead, every few hours, one of them must dock with the engine.

You will see one step out of the shadows beside a massive, spinning burl-wood flywheel. It braces its feet against the petrified floor, leans in, and extends a notched wooden key from a housing in its ribbed, gourd-wood chest, slipping it neatly into the teeth of a drive-gear. For half a minute, it stands completely rigid while the valley’s machinery *winds* it. A rapid, rising *tick-tick-tick-tick* chatters from inside its ribs as a heavy torsion-bundle of springy heartwood twists tight. At the same time, it presses a hollow, needle-like tap into a nearby phloem-pipe, letting the line’s own pressurized sap and mineral oil surge into its joints, flushing out the wood-dust and keeping its sockets supple. Then it disengages, steps back into the dark, and walks away on the borrowed torque of the city.

Without them, the valley would strangle itself. The pale weaver-vines that surge out to patch broken gantries are blind and frantic; left alone, they would wrap every flywheel in scar-tissue, seal every valve, and choke the conduits with wild, crooked scaffolding. The wound-ones are the valley’s pruners and tuners. They move through the tiers with long, hooked blades of vitrified resin, slicing back over-eager tendrils before they can foul a belt, re-packing smoking axles with oil-soaked felt, and tapping clogged seed-meal pipes with heavy wooden mallets until the dry powder flows again.

They do not speak—the thin, hot air carries voices poorly, and they have no wet throats to shape them. When two of them meet on a narrow catwalk, they halt, lean their blank resin faces close, and touch the copper-veined bristles at their temples together. There is a tiny, sharp blue snap of static, a brief smell of ozone and scorched dust, and an exchange of dry *ticks* from the fluidic sap-valves packed inside their skulls—a whole report on belt-tension, ruptured vats, and collapsing tiers passed in a single jolt of voltage and pressure. Over longer distances, they tap rapid, wooden codes directly onto the taut phloem-pipes, sending messages vibrating down the miles of conduit.

When one of them wears out—when its pith-axles grind down to powder, or its cane ribs grow brittle and snap under the torque of its own wound chest—it does not rot. Nothing rots here. It simply slows as its spring runs down for the last time, wedges itself into a quiet corner of a press-yard, and stops. Within hours, its kin—or the blind weaver-vines themselves—are upon it. They drain its remaining oil, strip out its copper-veined nerves for re-splicing, and weave its dry, petrified frame directly into the nearest retaining wall or gantry. New ones are already growing in vertical wooden jigs down near the simmering amber resin-vats, trained slowly around rotating cores until their joints set, cut loose from the wall only when they are ready to take their first winding.

And deeper down, beneath the shifting tiers and the constant *crack* and rebuild of the upper galleries, live the oldest things in the valley.

In the sunken press-yards, where the ancient heartwood has been eroded and vitrified into dark, geological mesas, the machinery stops moving—or rather, it moves too slowly to see. Here, coiled around the deep, heat-shielded taproots that drink geothermal warmth from the earth below, sit vast, motionless knots of braided wood and swollen resin-bladders. They are the size of houses, fused seamlessly into the floor, laced with thick cables of copper-veined root that plunge into the walls and rise up through every pillar in the basin.

They have no hands to turn a valve, no legs to walk a catwalk. They are simply immense, dry brains of static capacitor-wood and pressurized sap, listening through the soles of the valley to the endless, felt-muffled thrum. They do not know there is a sky above the charcoal-barked ceiling, or what water is, or what a bird might be. They feel only heat, friction, tension, and failure. When a tier far above begins to sag, or a main belt snaps in the dark, it is a pulse of static from these sunken ganglia that surges up through the copper veins—sending the pale vines erupting from the walls, and calling the silent, ticking walkers through the ash to keep the wheels turning.
