At first, you think the city is only inhabited by the swarm. The climbers, the gliders, the pale amphibians, the needle-winged clouds—they fill the air so completely, and their clamor is so absolute, that it takes a long time to notice the quiet evidence of *hands*.

Look down at the braided bridge beneath your boots. The vines are not merely tangled; they are spliced, three-ply, knotted at regular intervals with a tension that keeps the span from sagging. Look at the gourd-chambers hanging in tiers: their oval doorways are not chewed by insects or split by rot, but pared back while the rind was still green, the edges calloused over into smooth, lipped thresholds. Even the dead fronds at the base of the reed-pillars are stacked in neat, circular collars, rotting cleanly into mulch where the pale new shoots need it most.

Someone tends this place. Someone has been tending it for centuries.

You only see them when you stop looking for movement in the open voids and start watching the seams where the daylight frays.

A breeze drifts across the basin, warm and damp, and ripples the taut leaf-wall of an awning across the street. For half a second, the leathery green turns translucent, revealing a deeper gallery behind it—a corridor that cannot possibly fit inside the root-cliffs. And standing in that corridor, motionless as a reed-joint, is a figure. Before the leaf settles back into opacity, it steps forward—not through a doorway, but simply *out* of the thinning membrane, onto the sunlit balcony opposite yours.

At a glance, you might mistake it for a pale, elongated knot of peeled bark. It is roughly the height of a person, though leaner, built with the spare, hollow-boned lightness of a wading bird or a climbing ape. Its limbs are long and double-jointed at the wrists and ankles, ending in broad, splayed feet and heavy, curved claws—the exact shape that has worn the lower arcades smooth over a thousand years. Its skin is hairless, waxy, and cool-looking even in the heavy warmth, mottled in the damp greys and faded celadons of old cellulose, dusted lightly around the joints with pale spores from the mulch below.

It has no external ears. Its head is narrow and smooth, tapering toward a blunt, lipless snout, with small eyes set wide on the sides of the skull. Even in the clear, ordinary daylight, it keeps those eyes veiled behind milky, translucent lids, as if bright, direct sight were an irritation—or a liability.

It does not look at the bridge it is about to cross. If it looked directly, perhaps the space would stiffen, locking into the forty-pace stretch you struggled across. Instead, it tilts its head sideways, letting its veiled eyes slide past the edges of the void, and steps out onto the braided vines. It crosses in twelve unhurried strides, its claws clicking softly against the bark.

Only you don't hear the clicks when its feet touch down. You hear them *before*—a dry, rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* arriving from somewhere inside the hollow gourd hanging above your left shoulder, two seconds before the creature even reaches the center of the span.

These are the keepers of the basin, though they seem less like masters of the city than its resonant organs.

As more of them emerge—some detaching themselves from the shadows of the pillars, others stepping out of the shimmering, wind-rippled walls—you begin to see how their bodies are built for the roar. Between their slender ribs, where muscle and fat would be, there are taut, drum-like membranes stretched pale and tight over hollow chest cavities. As the warm air rushes through the reeds and sets the basin humming, their flanks visibly vibrate in sympathy, pulsing with the same low organ-drone that rattles your teeth.

They do not endure the noise; they read it. You watch two of them meet at a cupped leaf-cistern below. Neither opens its mouth. Instead, one raises a long-clawed hand and presses its palm flat against the damp trunk of an oak-thick reed. It holds it there for a heartbeat, its chest-membranes fluttering as it drinks in the vibration of the wood. Then it taps its middle claw twice against a porous ridge in the bark.

Three tiers higher, on the far side of the basin, a third keeper stops mid-stride and turns its narrow head—having heard those two taps ring out clearly from the water-viaduct beside its elbow, a fraction of a second before the hand below ever struck the trunk.

They have mapped the city’s broken acoustics the way sailors map currents. In a place where airborne sound is a solid, deafening surf, they speak through the *slips*—tapping specific reeds, plucking fibrous cables, or pressing their throats against hollow gourds to send dry, intimate messages that bypass the roar entirely, arriving early and elsewhere.

And when they need quiet, they know how to make it.

A cloud of needle-winged insects boils past a keeper’s face, its high-pitched hum sharp enough to ache. The keeper does not flinch. It simply raises both hands and cups them around a vibrating frond beside its head, pressing its waxy palms against the leaf’s tension-points. The frond goes dead. Within a handspan of the creature’s skull, the deafening roar of the basin drops away into a sudden, startling pocket of still air, as if a heavy blanket had been thrown over the world. The insects veer around the dead zone, leaving their shimmering afterimages buzzing harmlessly in the wake.

They live in the equilibrium of the fray, moving with an unhurried, ancient precision.

You watch them go about the endless, gentle maintenance of the city. One climbs vertically up a buttress, its claws sinking effortlessly into the springy bark, and pauses to pare away a patch of yellowing rot with a thumbnail, dropping the scrap down to the mulch-bed below. Another kneels by a grooved viaduct, clearing a knot of shed insect-chitin from the water channel so the condensation can resume its steady, rhythmic dripping. A third reaches out into empty air as a swarm of iridescent flies banks past a pillar; its hand passes through the shimmering afterimages and closes cleanly around a real body half a pace behind them, popping it into its mouth without breaking stride.

They do not bother the gliders that dive through the solid bark, nor the amphibians barking in synchronized choruses around the cisterns. Sometimes a glider will drop onto a keeper’s shoulder, folding its leathery wings while the keeper’s long fingers absently groom parasites from its membrane, before launching itself back into the void—vanishing into a leaf-wall and bursting out of a gourd two tiers down.

Most of the keepers, you realize, are not even *here*—not in the small, sunlit bowl you can take in from the rim.

The visible basin is only the outer shell: the cistern-yard, the resonator, the porch. Whenever the warm mist drifts down over the root-cliffs, or a breeze ripples the taut awnings into brief translucency, you catch glimpses of the rest of them. Dozens—perhaps hundreds—moving through stacked, shadowy galleries and wide, quiet-looking streets that extend far outward beyond the perimeter of the cliffs. In those folded spaces, the light falls at angles that have nothing to do with the noon sun, and the gourd-chambers hang in vast, impossible clusters, glowing softly with filtered green light.

They step in and out of those deeper streets effortlessly, slipping through the seams of the air whenever the wind loosens the walls.

One of them walks past you now, along the damp, braided balcony. It passes close enough that you can smell it—not sweat or musk, but the sharp, clean scent of crushed stems, wet cellulose, and old rain. Its milky, veiled eyes do not turn toward you, though its chest-membranes flutter slightly as it registers the disruption your solid, un-resonant body makes in the warm air.

Its shadow falls straight down into the void beneath the walkway, even as yours stretches east.

As it reaches the far end of the balcony and slips sideways into a patch of air where the leaf-wall is rippling, you hear the soft, wet *slap* of its bare foot against the damp bark behind you—and turn just in time to see it vanish, already gone.
