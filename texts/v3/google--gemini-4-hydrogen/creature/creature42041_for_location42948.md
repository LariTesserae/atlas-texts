At first, in the clear, shadow-sharp daylight, you might think no one lives here at all. The broad courts are empty, the heavy slate doors of the hall are shut against the wind, and the only movement is the pair of black choughs walking the retaining wall.

Then a grey shape detaches itself from the parapet of the watch-platform—not a cairn, as it seemed, but a person seated on a folded fleece, knees drawn up, watching the hazy air below the drop. Down in the plaza, a wooden lid scrapes softly over stone as someone kneels to lift a dipper from a cistern.

They are few—seldom more than a dozen or fifteen at a time—and they keep to the same spare economy as the place itself. Small-boned and lean, they have the dry, weathered look of the bone-coloured timber, their faces neither sun-browned nor pale, but cured by the cold wind to a muted ochre. Their knuckles are broad and their nails worn short from stone-work and ropes. They dress in layers of heavy, undyed wool—slate-grey, cream, and the dark brown of peat—bound close at the wrists and ankles so the flat hiss of the wind cannot find a seam. On their feet they wear soft-soled felt boots that make no clatter on the flagstones, though generations of those same soft soles have worn the stair-treads into shallow troughs.

They move without hurry. In this thin air, haste wastes breath and moisture, so they walk at an even, measured pace, always singly, following the worn centre of the stairs and stepping cleanly over the chiseled runnels so their heels will not chip the stone edges. When two of them pass in the wide emptiness between the storehouses, they do not stop to crowd together; they incline their heads, or lift two fingers from a woollen sleeve, and keep walking. Speech is rare and pitched low, kept for what cannot be shown by a gesture or the placement of a tool.

Their days are governed by the sweat of the stone and the drift of grit. In the late afternoon, before the chill deepens, two or three of them cross the plazas with stiff brooms of bound wiry grass, sweeping the dust and blown gravel away from the runnels and off the edges of the terraces. The work is quiet and rhythmic—a long, dry *shhh* against the flagstones—done so that when the morning cloud brushes the saddle, the condensation will run clear into the bedrock cisterns. 

The row of stone-lined basins on the bare upper terrace is theirs to tend at mid-morning, once the sun has lifted the mist. There they ladle the first, silt-clouded wash of water from the upper catch-drains and leave it to stand in the cold light until the fine limestone dust settles to the bottom, before skimming the clear water into leather buckets for the cisterns. Nothing is spilled; even the grey dregs left in the basins are scraped out onto the patches of silver-leaved saxifrage tucked along the retaining walls.

They live mostly out of the three windowless storehouses, which hold what the damp, warm world far below cannot keep. Inside, in the dry, steady dark, sit sealed clay jars of seed-grain, oiled iron blades wrapped in wool, bundles of dried roots, and wooden tallies notched decades ago. Some of it is their own provision, hauled up the switchbacks in rare, silent pack-trains; much of it belongs to people down in the haze, brought up to wait out lean years, or wars, or simply the rot of the lowlands. The people of the saddle do not own these things, nor do they guard them with weapons. They are simply the ones who stay up in the chill to see that the lids remain tight and the roofs stay weighted down with river-stones.

In the evenings, when the shadows turn cold enough to sting, they gather inside the low-eaved hall. There is no hearth-fire to waste scarce wood—only small tallow lamps that throw a steady, grease-scented light across the dry-laid slate walls. They eat sitting on low benches or woven mats: dense cakes of barley, strips of dried mutton, and warm infusions of bitter mountain herbs, drunk from wooden cups. A child is a rare sight among them, raised in the quiet of the hall and taught early not to run on the terraces or kick grit into the drains. The old do not step aside; they simply take the slower tasks—mending wool, watching the hazy drop from the platform, or keeping count of the water levels with a notched stick—until they grow too frail for the wind, and are laid away in the dry clefts of the upper ridge, where the cold air keeps them light and whole.

They do not seem to belong to the town as builders or masters, any more than the lizard belongs to the slate it warms itself upon. They are simply another part of its long equilibrium—quiet, unhurried, and sparing, keeping the stones clear so the water can gather, and keeping the silence so the place does not wear out.
