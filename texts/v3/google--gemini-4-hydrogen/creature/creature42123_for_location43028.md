If you hover in the dark between the condenser blocks, watching the murky halo of an amber gantry-bulb, you can easily spend an hour believing the basin is empty of anything larger than a dinner-plate crab. Nothing darts. Nothing flinches from the light.

It takes patience to realize that one of the pale, lime-crusted stanchions bolted to the catwalk has shifted three inches to the left.

They are not easy to separate from the town, because they are made of the same patience and, increasingly, the same geology. Roughly the height of a stooped person, they are heavy-boned and dense enough to walk the slate-hard silt of the alleys without drifting. Their backs, shoulders, and outer limbs are plated in overlapping wafers of pale-grey aragonite and iron scale, laid down over decades like the crust on the girders. Where those plates meet at the knees, elbows, and neck, the joints are dark and slick with a thick, waxy secretion that smells faintly of the mineral oil in the gear-housings.

They have no eyes. In a sinkhole lit only by clouded filaments and the watch-dial flicker of lead anodes, eyes would be an extravagance. Instead, the front of each rounded, featureless skull is a smooth, dish-shaped plate of porous bone-mineral, pitted with tiny dark pores that read the town the way a tongue reads a battery. They feel the sub-audible hum of the braided power cables in their ribs. They know, from three alleys away, when a copper roller is beginning to bind, or when a silt-bank has grown heavy enough to slide.

Their hands end not in fingers, but in blunt, heavy pincers—one side curved like a caliper, the other flat and beveled like a cold-chisel. They move with the exact, fluid-damped economy of the machinery around them. Hurrying through water this cold and viscous is a waste of heat; a single reach toward a brass valve takes half a minute, smooth and deliberate, leaving scarcely a ripple in the brine.

They are the reason the town has not seized.

Long after the builders vanished—or forgot what they had sunk down here—the dwellers stayed, tending the basin not out of loyalty, but the way an animal tends a burrow or a winter hearth. Without the town, the water would freeze them solid or starve them into stillness. So they walk the iron lattices in silence, scraping the thicker crusts of lime off the valve stems with their chisel-thumbs. When a ceramic bearing begins to grind, they kneel in the dark and pack it with their own oily secretion mixed with fine silt. When a pipe-joint weeps pressure, they gather handfuls of the colorless algae and the stiff, glass-spined sea-lilies from the rust-crusts and tamp them into the seam until it holds.

Even the town’s uncollected product passes through their hands. Whatever the silent conveyor chains carry out of the electrolytic vats—heavy, regular shapes of dull metal or sealed ceramic, arriving one by one over the course of hours—cannot be allowed to pile up and jam the tracks. The dwellers wait at the dead-end hoists, lift the cold burdens off the rollers, and carry them away. They have no use for the things as they were meant to be used. Instead, they stack them into neat, towering cairns along the edges of the central courtyard, or lay them in low, blind retaining walls along the curbs of the lowest alleys to keep the syrupy cold-streams from spilling into their resting trenches.

When they need to feed, they descend into the sunken vats where the lead plates hang. Hanging upside down from the overhead grates by their hooked hind-limbs, they part the heavy mineral plates across their bellies and chests, exposing the only soft part of themselves: pale, ribbed folds of tissue like the undersides of mushrooms. Suspended there in the dark, they let the ghostly, warm plumes of dissolved sulfur and copper rise through their ribs, drinking the heat and the minerals while their pitted faces soak up the stray current bleeding off the anodes. They may hang like that for four or five days without moving a muscle, dimly lit from below by the cold-blue sparks.

The rare fauna of the basin treats them as part of the furniture. The blind, slate-colored crustaceans sometimes clamber right over a dweller’s plated back to reach a busbar, and are only gently nudged aside if they block a latch. Now and then, one of the translucent eels will thread itself into the hollow space behind a dweller’s shoulder-plates to shelter from the cold, tolerated because it eats the tiny parasites that gather in the oiled joints.

They rarely have reason to speak, and they have no voices to do it with. If one needs to summon another—to help clear a clogged intake tower or brace a sagging cable—it places a heavy knuckle against the nearest brine-pipe and taps out a slow, low-frequency rhythm, or turns a distant sluice-valve just enough to send a single, unmistakable pressure-pulse rolling through the basin.

Once every few decades, when the thermal plumes run unusually warm, two of them will meet in the murky halo of one of the amber gantry-bulbs and release a faint, milky cloud of spores into the still water. Those few that survive drift onto the salt-clouded glass cages of the lamps, clinging to the faint warmth like pale, flat limpets until they grow their first wafers of scale and drop down into the alleys to find a tool-less place on the walkways.

And when one of them grows too old—when the lime-crust finally wins, locking its hip-plates or stiffening its caliper hands until the oil can no longer keep the joints turning—it does not linger in the vats. It walks slowly down to the lowest, darkest streets, steps over the low retaining walls of stacked, uncollected cargo, and wades into the syrupy, refractive pools of the cold-streams. There, in the burning, near-freezing brine, it settles onto its knees and stops. Within a few years, the pale-grey scale covers it completely, welding it to the basalt floor, leaving just another smooth, stony shape in the dark while the rollers above keep turning.
