For a long time, you might believe the shelf belongs only to the thumb-sized amphibians and the patient, mineral-shelled crustaceans. In the glare of midday, when the heat rises off the basalt in a thick, shimmering wall, there is nothing else to see—only the bare, razor-edged stone, the islands of plum-colored fern, and the four or five strange geometries of the artifacts.

It is only when the afternoon shadows lengthen across the terrace, cooling the air by that single, critical fraction of a degree, that one of the rock-forms near the weeping back wall begins to uncoil.

At first, it looks like a slumped buttress of wet basalt, no larger than a crouched bear, draped in a ragged mantle of the translucent bladder-moss. Then a joint flexes with a sound like two pieces of wet slate sliding together, and it lifts itself clear of the steaming crevice.

It is built for a world without soil or softness. Its body is broad, low-slung, and heavily plated in overlapping shields of a dark, matte mineral—not grown from bone, but precipitated over decades from the scalding, silica-rich water it lives in. Between the heavy plates, its true skin shows in narrow, pleated seams: waxy and pale, the color of the bruised-plum ferns, glistening with a thick, milky oil that keeps the simmering damp from poaching it alive. 

Because the rock beneath it is freshly fractured and sharp enough to shear flesh, it does not walk on pads or claws. Its six limbs are long, crooked, and surprisingly slender, ending in narrow, vitreous points—dense needles of grown glass, cloudy white and hard as flint. As it steps out onto the open shelf, each foot finds purchase in the tiny, flat pockets between the basalt blades with a delicate, deliberate *click*.

It has no eyes. In the high-altitude glare and the constant, swirling steam, eyes would be useless things. Instead, the broad, shovel-shaped cowl of its head is fringed with dozens of pale, thread-thin cilia, no thicker than spider silk, that tremble constantly in the rising thermals. They read the map of the terrace in heat and humidity, in the weight of the condensation, and in the sub-audible hum of the matte-gray disc out in the center of the floor.

As the shadows deepen and the weeping walls begin to run faster, the creature goes to work.

It moves at the pace of a drifting cloud, tracing a slow, circuitous path along the margins of the pools. Where a cluster of silica reeds has collapsed across a mineral vein, choking the trickle of runoff, it pauses. Using a blunt, spade-like forelimb, it nudges the fallen stalks aside, clearing the channel so the hot water can glide unimpeded down to the basins. It is not altruism, nor is it entirely instinct; it is the quiet maintenance of a body that happens to be larger than itself. If the veins choke, the pools cool; if the pools cool, the loop breaks.

Along the way, it grazes—not on the tough, waxy ferns, which it ignores, but on the swollen, glass-bright bladder-mosses. It shears them cleanly with a pair of flat, grinding plates inside its mouth, swallowing the warm, distilled water trapped inside their cells along with the pulp. A few of the small, white-shelled crustaceans cling to the joints of its hind legs, riding it across the jagged stone like barnacles on a slow ship, scraping away the excess mineral scale that builds up along its seams.

When it reaches the center of the terrace, it steps carefully into the shallow, simmering basin that holds the matte-gray disc. 

Here, the creature stops. It lowers its heavy, plated belly until it hovers just an inch above the seamless gray surface, straddling the edge where the disc interrupts the rock. The sub-audible hum—the vibration you feel in the soles of your feet—passes straight up through its glassy legs and into the hollow, resonant chamber of its chest. Its pale sensory threads go utterly limp, drooping toward the metal.

Where the basin’s water laps over the disc, the droplets bead into those perfect, trembling spheres, skittering across the surface before leaping into the air with a soft *tink*. Slowly, the creature parts the cowl beneath its head and extends a broad, grooved tongue, pale as tallow. It holds it suspended just above the disc, catching the leaping pearls of water as they jump—pure, hot, and stripped of the heavy salts of the pools. It drinks them one by one, patient as a stone, while the steam curls around its mossy back and glows like amber in the slanting sun.

It stays at the disc until the last of the direct sunlight slips over the jagged western rim, and the evening chill begins to rise like a tide from the void below.

Even for a creature of the scalding damp, the night’s heavy condensation is a danger. When the air cools too quickly, the moisture thickens into a cold film that can rot the waxy seams between its plates and weigh down the mosses on its back until they tear loose.

So, as the terrace settles into twilight, the creature lifts itself from the basin. *Click, click, click*, its glass feet carry it toward the cliff-lip, where the three bone-white struts lean inward over empty air.

It steps carefully between the slender struts, entering the small, ash-dry circle beneath their triangle. Here, the bent light feels thick and strange, and the rock is warm and completely arid, untouched by the wet that rules the rest of the shelf. With a long, wet exhalation—a soft *hiss* of spent steam—the creature folds its six glassy limbs beneath its body and lowers its plated belly onto the dry basalt.

The mosses on its back immediately begin to shrink and tighten in the dry air, sealing their moisture inside. The milky oil on its skin cures into a thin, protective skin. Beneath the silent, leaning tripod, the creature tucks its cowl down, stills its trembling threads, and becomes once more indistinguishable from the jagged, volcanic dark—waiting, in the one dry place on the mountain, for the sun to return and start the loop again.
