They are small-boned, wiry people with skin the color of smoked tallow and eyes that rarely open wide. Even in the dim, olive-stained gloom of the sinkhole, they squint slightly—a habit passed down from the few among them who must occasionally climb through the thorn-mat onto the plateau above, where the white stone reflects the sun like a hammered shield. Down in the pocket, their pupils stay large and dark, catching the amber light that filters through the canopy.

They do not wash with water, because there is no water to spare. Instead, they clean themselves the way the pale geckos and the hyraxes do: with dust, oil, and scraping. Every household keeps a shallow bowl of talcum-fine floor grit mixed with crushed euphorbia ash, and a set of smooth, curved rib-bones worn glossy from generations of use. After the day’s work, a person sits on the edge of a cane balcony, rubs a handful of sharp-smelling plant mucilage over their limbs until it dries into a tight, papery film, and then peels and scrapes it away with the bone strigil, taking the sweat, soot, and resin-smudge with it. As a result, the people carry the exact scent of the hollow itself—a dry, warm tang of fermenting fig-sap, woodsmoke, and crushed green bark.

Their teeth are stained dark amber from childhood, and many of the elders have lost their molars early to the grit that works its way into everything: the locust-meal, the dried seed-cakes, the thick, milky sap-curds pressed in woven bags. 

They call themselves—if they bother with a name that distinguishes them from the plateau, where nothing else lives—simply *the Rim-Under*, or *the Root-Held*.

Because there is no flat ground worth speaking of, their bodies are shaped by verticality. Their toes are splayed and calloused from gripping bone-runged rope ladders; their shoulders are knotted and narrow, built for squeezing between overhanging baskets and ducking under low, plastered lintels. A child learns to climb before it learns to run, tethered by a length of braided sinew around the waist to a balcony post while its parents pound agave fibers or mend snares. Falls still happen, of course—a rotted rung, a slick patch of spilled sap, a startled lunge away from a dropping snake—but the balconies are strung so thickly with nets, hanging gourds, and woven root-mats that a falling body rarely reaches the dust floor unbroken or un-caught.

***

Everything that lives in the sinkhole is bound to the *Tap-Keepers*, though they are not priests or rulers—just the oldest, lightest, and sharpest-eyed among the Rim-Under, whose joints have gone too stiff for hauling stone or pounding fiber, and whose weight the upper canopy can still bear.

It is the Tap-Keepers who crawl out into the iron-hard tangle of the roof before dawn, moving spider-like across the basket-weave of branches while the fruit bats are still returning to roost. Armed with flaked chert knives and small, wax-plugged gourds, they read the health of the hollow by the thickness of its bleed. A healthy fig or euphorbia gives a slow, steady bead of sap when scored; a stressed one weeps thin and watery, or dries crusty at the cut. If a Tap-Keeper finds a branch turning brittle, they bind it with wet moss-poultices and forbid anyone from tapping that quarter of the wall for a season. No one argues with them. To kill the roof is to let the plateau in—and once the sun and wind break through the canopy, the hollow would bake into a kiln in a week.

Below the Tap-Keepers, life sorts itself into tiers, though not by wealth—there is too little space for real hoarding—but by heat, light, and smell.

The highest balconies, wedged just under the hanging root-curtains, belong to the weavers, the net-menders, and the young couples. It is bright enough up there to split reed-stalks and thread bone needles without a tallow lamp, and the breeze from the plateau’s edge keeps the worst of the smoke away. It is also loud—the constant rustle of thousands of weaver-birds shifting in their bottle-nests, the dry rattle of beetles in the thatch, the heavy *thump* of bats landing at dusk.

The middle tiers are the kitchen-levels, where the smooth concave rock has been hollowed slightly further over centuries to form beehive clay ovens and soot-blackened hearths. Here the air is thick and sweet-sour. Women and men squat side by side on narrow ledges, turning wooden paddles in clay pots of boiling locust-paste, or skimming the bitter white scum off simmering agave-juice until it reduces to a dark, drinkable syrup. Because wood is too precious to burn recklessly, fires are fed with dried dung from the hyraxes, pithy husks, and the brittle, shed wings of insects swept from the floor. Every spark is watched; a fire that climbed the dry-moss curtains would turn the sinkhole into a chimney.

The lowest tier—just above the talcum-fine dust of the pit floor—belongs to the old, the tanners, and the scavengers. Down there, the amber light barely reaches, and the air smells sharply of guano, rotting chitin, and damp earth-salt. It is cool, though, and quietest, insulated by four stories of woven cane above.

***

Down in that bottom gloom, the boundary between what is human and what is animal goes slack.

The Rim-Under do not keep livestock in pens; there is nowhere to put a pen. Instead, they live in a state of wary, crowded truce with the creatures that share the walls. Slender, fierce-eyed weasels—no thicker than a child’s wrist—are tolerated in the rafters because they hunt the rats and the cliff-snakes. Some families leave out scraps of roasted beetle-fat on their window ledges for a particular weasel, tying a tiny ring of carved hyrax-bone around its neck so no one mistakes it for a wild intruder and brains it with a mallet. The weasels are not tame—they will still tear a finger to the bone if cornered—but they know the smell of the people who feed them, and they sleep curled inside the warm felt of hanging storage-bags during the hottest hours of the day.

The weaver-birds are treated like noisy, quarrelsome neighbors. Their nests are robbed carefully, never stripped bare—one egg taken from a clutch of three, or a handful of fledglings pulled just before they feather, to be roasted whole on skewers. In return, the people leave out frayed ends of sinew and plant-fiber, which the birds promptly weave into their nests, strengthening the canopy’s underside.

Only the forearm-length centipedes that patrol the floor at night are killed on sight, pinned with forked sticks and dropped into boiling clay jars to render their oil for waterproofing leather. Even then, the elders say you should never kill the pale, blind ones that occasionally crawl up out of the deep aquifer cracks—those belong to the stone, they say, and if you anger them, they will chew through the taproots in the dark.

***

In a space where three hundred people live within earshot of every cough, argument, and whispered secret, privacy is not a matter of walls. It is a matter of manners.

The Rim-Under speak in a low, clipped murmur that rarely carries past the next balcony, using a dense shorthand of hand-gestures—a flick of the chin, a tapped knuckle on a cane rail—to pass messages across the gulf without adding to the din. When two neighbors fight, they do not shout; they sit facing away from each other and pound their plant-fibers harder, *thock-thock-thock*, letting the rhythm of their mallets say what their voices won’t. If a couple wants to be alone, they hang a mat of woven fig-leaves across their alcove; everyone else in the tiers below and across simply looks at the stone, or busies themselves with a quern, pretending the space behind the mat has ceased to exist.

When someone dies, there is no ground to dig and no wood to waste on a pyre. The body is washed one last time with precious fermented sap, wrapped tightly in a shroud of stripped succulent-fiber, and carried up by the Tap-Keepers into the high, sun-warmed crown of the canopy—the space between the thorny roof and the open sky. There, laid out on a cradle of iron-hard branches, the dry wind and the carrion beetles strip it clean in a matter of days.

A month later, the Tap-Keepers bring the bones back down. The long bones are notched and lashed into new ladder rungs; the ribs are smoothed into scraping-strigils; the smaller joints are carved into needles, net-gauges, and hooked poles for passing baskets across the void. In this way, no one ever truly leaves the sinkhole. A grandmother who spent seventy years cooking locust-meal on the third tier still holds up the rung her great-grandson steps on to reach the roof.

***

At night, the sinkhole changes. The sharp, percussive clatter of the day’s work dies down to the hiss of banked embers and the wet *drip-drip* of sap-jars filling in the dark. The fruit bats drop from the canopy in soft, leathery rushes, brushing past sleeping faces on the high balconies, their wings smelling of warm fur and crushed blossom. Below them, the pale geckos come out onto the plaster walls to lick the dew of sweat and cooking-steam from the stone.

The people sleep curled tight in hammocks of knotted sinew, wrapped in capes of felted hyrax-fur and beaten bark-cloth, swaying gently in the warm updraft that rises from the pit floor toward the stars they almost never see.

And sometimes, on nights when the wind across the dead plateau above blows hard enough to howl across the sinkhole’s rim like a mouth over a bottle, a few of the Rim-Under lie awake in the amber dark, listening to the stone hum around them—grateful for the thorns, the dust, the crowded knees of their neighbors, and the deep, unseen water far beneath their feet that keeps the whole world green.
