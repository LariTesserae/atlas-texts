At first, in the flat pewter light and the crush of sound, they are indistinguishable from the fallen slabs. You only notice them when one shifts its weight against the horizon of the bedrock, breaking the clean, angular line of the limestone.

They come out of the haze in twos or threes, rarely more, moving with a low, unhurried gait that belongs entirely to uneven stone. They are compact, lean, and built close to the ground, with the wide, slightly bowed shoulders of people who spend their lives climbing talus and squeezing through narrow fault-lines. Their skin is the color of wet chalk or pale tallow—never touched by sun, though dusted here and there with dry limestone flour and the dark, oily soot of guano. Their hair is cropped short or bound tight against the skull, kept out of the way of sharp rock and low overhangs.

Everything they wear is borrowed from the dark. They drape themselves in heavy, supple cloaks and leggings stitched from hundreds of small bat pelts, overlapped like shingles and sewn with bone awls and twisted sinew. The hides are velvety, musk-scented, and slick with natural oil, shedding the slow condensation that drips from the high ceiling. On their feet they wear thick, flat-soled wraps of folded leather, scarred and chewed at the edges by the young, glass-sharp bedrock. Even through the leather, their heels and toes are broad, calloused into pale horn from gripping fractured limestone.

They do not speak in the valley. A voice here is useless—the moment a word leaves the throat, the hard walls catch it, smear it, and fold it into the roar of a hundred thousand wings. Instead, they have learned to live inside the noise as if it were weather. They keep moss or twists of bat-wool tucked loosely in their ears to dull the metallic chittering of the swiftlets, and they watch the air rather than listening to it. When they need to call a companion’s attention across a stretch of bedrock, they do not shout; they tap the butt of a heavy cobble once or twice against the floor. The sharp *tock* does not travel through the air—it runs straight through the limestone slab, arriving as a clean, unmistakable pulse in the soles of the other’s feet.

Their eyes are large, steady, and pale-ringed, unbothered by the unchanging grey. Because there are no shadows to hide movement and no glare to squint against, they see the valley in flat, meticulous detail: the faint sheen of a damp seep fifty yards off, the tiny twitch of a cricket’s antennae in a dry crack, the pale crust of a swiftlet’s nest glued high under an overhang.

When they reach the margins of the mineral pools or the edges of the guano mounds, they settle into their work without ceremony. They sit cross-legged on the bare stone, knees tucked, and draw out the tools they left behind on their last visit.

The cobbles they use are the only smooth things in the valley—heavy, river-rounded stones carried in from some distant, unseen water, their ends pitted and white from years of striking limestone. With a cobble in one hand and a flat piece of bedrock between their knees, they crack open clusters of pale cave snails, then use slender bone awls to pry the meat from the shattered spirals. If they need a cleaner edge—to skin a fallen bat, trim a strip of hide, or scrape dark moss from a seep—they take a nodule of flint from a belt-pouch and strike off a single, razor-edged flake. The sharp *crack* of the stone briefly cuts above the insect-click and the dripping water, then vanishes as if it never happened.

For the tighter places, they use the copper. They lash the small, hand-hammered hooks to long, springy wands of dried fern-stalk or bone, and slide them deep into the jagged crevices of the talus where fingers would be torn to ribbons. Copper is quiet and soft; if a hook snags on a limestone tooth, it bends rather than snapping, and it can be coaxed back into shape with a thumb. With patient, circling wrists, they draw out fat, sluggish crickets and blind salamanders from the cracks, or reach up beneath the lower ledges to pry away the translucent, saliva-spun nests of the swiftlets while the birds wheel and hiss inches from their faces.

They eat some of what they gather on the spot, cold and raw, spitting the grit and shell-fragments onto the stone. They never light a fire. There is nothing here to burn except the dry guano, and a fire in a sealed rift would fill the grey air with choking smoke and send the ceiling-carpets of bats and birds into a blind, suffocating storm. Nor do they ever build a blind, a windbreak, or a cairn. The air does not blow, the temperature does not drop, and there is no rain to keep off. To stack one stone upon another in a place that has no seasons would be an act of madness, or a joke no one could hear.

Because the light never shifts, they have no hurry. A task takes as long as it takes. They work until their woven baskets and hide satchels are heavy with nests, dried crickets, and scraped moss, or until the steady, chilled air begins to stiffen their knuckles.

Before they go, they clean the flint flakes on their leggings and tuck them back into the narrowest, driest cracks in the bedrock, alongside the bone awls and the copper hooks. They leave the battered cobbles resting beside the empty snail shells, right where they found them. They do not carry their workshop away; the valley is already a house, and the stone keeps their things safer than they could.

Then they rise, pull their bat-skin hoods up against the slow drip of the ceiling, and walk back out across the flat, angular floor. They move in single file, stepping lightly over the fissures, until the flat pewter haze softens their outlines and swallows them whole—leaving nothing behind but the damp tools in the dark, and the endless, roaring stone closing over where they stood.
