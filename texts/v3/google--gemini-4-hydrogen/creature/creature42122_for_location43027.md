If you stood on one of the grated catwalks for only an hour, you might leave believing the cavern held nothing larger than the gliding things in the rafters. The residents do not announce themselves, and they do not flee. They are the color of wet lime and tallow, draped in the same dull patina as the brick piers, and they keep the same glacial tempo as the counterweighted pistons.

You notice them first as absences of symmetry—a heap of tarred felt slumped against a tiled pump-house wall that, when you look back ten minutes later, has shifted three feet toward the warmth of a transformer housing.

There are perhaps fifteen or twenty of them in the whole hollow, though they are rarely seen together. Roughly the size of a person when unfolded, they almost never unfold; they move in a deep, liquid crouch, their long, supple spines curved like the slack cables overhead. Beneath their outer layers, their skin is pale, damp, and faintly translucent at the wrists and ankles, toughened at the knees, palms, and wide, flat feet into thick, grey-violet calluses that grip the wet iron grates without a sound. Over their shoulders and backs, they wear no woven cloth, but rather a living patchwork they have pressed onto themselves over years: mats of dark thread-moss, scraps of salvaged tarred canvas, and broad plates of the arthropods’ pearlescent wax, which adheres to their cool skin and cures into a flexible, waterproof shell.

They have no eyes—only smooth, shallow depressions beneath a heavy brow of bone. In a place where the amber bulbs are spaced so far apart, sight would be a poor instrument anyway. Instead, their broad, flat faces are built to read the cavern’s quieter languages. Wide nostrils flare slowly to taste the balance of ozone, damp limestone, and weeping oil; along their jaws and the sides of their necks, fine, gill-like creases quiver at the sub-audible hum of the dynamos. They walk the narrow walkways with their long, verdigris-stained fingers trailing lightly along the iron railings, feeling the micro-tremors of distant bearings through the metal, and feeling the faint electrostatic prickle of the DC current lift the fine, pale hairs along their forearms.

They are not operators. They have no memory of why the copper-wound dynamos were lowered into this vault, or what the numbered brass plaques once meant. To them, the machinery is not a system to be run; it is simply the climate, and they tend it the way a badger tends its sett or a bird tends a nest.

When a pipe joint begins to weep too briskly, one of them will pause beside it, reach up with infinite patience to pluck two or three palm-sized arthropods from a warm porcelain insulator, and set them gently beside the leak, waiting hours if need be until the creatures begin to graze and lay down their rubbery seal. When a tuft of fleshy fern grows thick enough to catch the iron-bound paddle of an overshot waterwheel, a resident will wade knee-deep into the sluiceway—unbothered by the black water or the eyeless swimmers brushing against their shins—and peel the fern away in a single, unbroken mat, preserving the steady, soothing turn of the wheel. Now and then, one will stand before a pressure gauge and press a broad palm against the yellowed, condensation-beaded glass, not to read the needle, but to feel the slow pulse of the water behind it.

Their lives are woven tightly into the cavern’s other flesh. In the tiled pump-houses on the two central islands—where the floors are slick with mineral dust and the air is a degree or two warmer—they share the space with the velvety gliders. While the gliders hang like wet rags from the upper pipe-runs, the residents sit below against the brick housings of the hydraulic accumulators. Sometimes, if a glider drops clumsily onto a cold iron grate, a resident will scoop it up in both hands and lift it back to a warm tin roof, lingering only long enough to stroke the damp membrane between its digits.

Down at the waterline, when the slow dynamos pulse and the bruised-violet bioluminescence ripples through the black basin, the residents gather along the lime-crusted piers. They kneel at the edge, dangling their long arms into the charged water, letting the ribbon-like swimmers knot around their wrists and tickle their calloused palms with feathery gills. They do not hunt the living ones, though they gather the pale, spent swimmers that drift against the sluice grates, eating them raw and unhurried in the orange pools of light beneath the caged bulbs.

They have no spoken language; a voice would echo too sharply off the wet stone and unsettle the quiet. When they need to acknowledge one another across the wide, cold expanses of dark air between the central islands and the perimeter walls, they use the cavern’s own bones. A knuckle tapped gently against a catwalk girder sends a soft, dull *tink-tink* racing through fifty yards of riveted iron—audible only if your ear or your sole is pressed to the metal. More often, they communicate simply by sharing stillness: two or three of them leaning shoulder-to-shoulder against a warm transformer casing, their moss-and-wax shells touching, their slow, deep breathing falling into time with the hours-long rise and fall of the great pistons.

In the deepest alcoves behind the bricked archways, where the residual heat of the grid collects in pockets, the younger ones stay hidden. They are smaller, almost entirely translucent, without the heavy moss-and-wax carapace yet, curling like pale grubs in beds of dry lichen and shed glider-membrane. An elder sits beside them, one hand resting flat on the brickwork, teaching them without a sound how to tell the healthy, velvety hum of a well-oiled bearing from the dry, thin whisper of one that needs its grease.

Nothing here is waiting to be relieved from a shift, and nothing is trying to leave. In the dim, damp equilibrium of wet stone and slow iron, they have found a world that asks only to be kept humming—and in return, keeps them warm.
