At first, even under the clear, direct light from above, it seems as though only the plants live here—and even they seem half-mineral, standing in their wide margins of gleaming stone like monuments to patience. Nothing darts between the plum-and-jade stalks. Nothing breaks the mirror of the ankle-deep pools.

Yet if you kneel beside one of the shallow pans and watch without moving, long enough for the thermal breath of the rock to warm your shins, you begin to notice that the water is not uniform.

In the center of the clear pool, where the dark slate dips into the cup-shaped hollows ground out by ancient hands, the water seems slightly thicker—not cloudy, only denser, like a pane of old glass slumped to fit the curve of the basin. Along its edges, a faint line of refraction trembles and holds.

What lives here has no blood, no skeleton, no feet to lift it from the wet stone. It is a flat, broad body of clear, water-logged tissue, no thicker than a thumb at its center and tapering at its margins to a skin as fine as a soap film. It does not breathe air, and it has no heart to drive it; instead, it is powered by the fever of the shelf itself. The stone beneath its belly is warm, while its upper surface touches the slightly cooler air. Inside its translucent body, the clear mineral water it has drunk from the rock rises in slow, silent plumes, cools along its back, and sinks again—a perpetual, bloodless circulation driven by heat alone.

Suspended within that clear, convective tide are fine, branching threads, pale as peeled willow and faintly knotted with amber where old food has settled. It has no eyes, yet its whole upper skin is sensitive to the weight of the overhead light. It has no ears, yet its flat belly, pressed tight against the slate, feels the smallest shudder of the ridge—the distant groan of cooling stone, or the tiny, wet pop when a bladder-sac on one of the tall plants finally splits three yards away.

When that happens, and a cloudy wash of spores glides across the wet stone floor, the pool-dweller stirs. It cannot hurry; it moves by shifting the tension of its own outer membrane, drawing water in at one edge and weeping it out at the other, creeping across the slick slate at the pace of a spreading stain. Over hours, it slides into the path of the runoff. It flattens itself across the stone, letting the milky spore-water wash over its back, then slowly rolls its translucent margins inward like a closing eyelid to trap the catch. Most of the spores it dissolves over weeks, turning them into new amber knots along its inner threads. A few, coated in a slick of clear mucilage, pass all the way through its body and are left behind in the next fissure it crosses, already cushioned for germination.

They are solitary, like the plants, rarely sharing a pool. Each keeps to its own pan of warm water, growing over years to the breadth of a platter, perfectly matched to the hollow it inhabits.

Only in one place do they grow differently: beneath the vast, deep-green cushions of liverwort, especially the great mound on the back wall where a wedge of knapped basalt redirects a weeping seam of water. Out of the direct light, in the dark, perpetually wet cradle between the moss and the hot stone, the dwellers thicken. They lose their glassy flatness and swell into pale, pillowy forms the size of a loaf of bread, veined with rose and slate-gray mineral streaks. They do not creep across the floor here; wedged beneath the velvet weight of the liverwort, they simply pulse, expanding and contracting by a hair's breadth every few minutes as they draw in the rich, moss-filtered runoff and sweat it back out into the roots above them.

In their own way, they are as much stone-makers as the tall plants whose bases calcify into fluted pillars. As a pool-dweller ages, its underside—pressed for years against the feverish slate—begins to lay down a thin, pearlescent crust of dissolved lime and silica. Layer by microscopic layer, it plates the bottom of its hollow with a smooth, faintly iridescent scale, like the inside of a river-mussel shell. When a basin grows too shallow from its own deposits, or when a seam of water shifts, the creature slowly peels itself upward and creeps away over the wet stone, leaving behind a pale, empty saucer fused to the dark rock.

That is what the rare climber comes for, every few years, rising out of the flat bright floor of cloud below.

The dwellers know the climber long before a shadow falls across the pools. They feel the approach through the bone of the ridge—a dull, rhythmic vibration against their bellies, entirely unlike the steady hum of thermal drift. When the bare feet step onto the shelf, the clear panes in the pools draw their margins slightly inward, stiffening their skins, while the great, pillowy one beneath the moss cushion goes still.

The climber never steps into the pools, and never tears the living tissue. Kneeling beside the cup-shaped hollows with the flat scraper of dark stone, the visitor gently pries up the abandoned, pearlescent saucers of mineral scale—thin, brittle plates infused with years of trapped spores and deep rock-heat—and lays them out to dry on the western ledge. With a round stone, the climber grinds a little deeper into a catch-basin that has grown too shallow, or taps the basalt wedge firmer into its vertical fissure to keep the moss cushion fed. A few dead, fibrous ribs are stripped from the oldest plum-and-jade plants and braided into coarse cordage to bind the harvested plates.

Then, with the bundle slung across a shoulder, the climber steps back off the lip of the shelf and disappears into the clouds.

The vibration fades from the slate. In the sudden, total silence, the clear panes in the pools relax their edges, spreading back out to fit the newly deepened hollows. A single drop of water, redirected by the basalt wedge, beads on the underside of the great liverwort cushion and sinks into the pale, pulsing flesh beneath it. Everything on the shelf returns to its own slow time—drinking the heat, waiting for the next bladder-sac to swell and split, and laying down, grain by invisible grain, the next thin layer of stone.
