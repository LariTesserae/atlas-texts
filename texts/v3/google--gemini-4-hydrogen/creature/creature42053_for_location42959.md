At first, under that bright, unremarkable sky, it seems impossible that anything larger than a water-strider could live here. The basin is too wide, too bare, and far too loud. There is nowhere to hide from the daylight, nothing dry to nest in, and no soil to burrow into. A visitor standing on one of the braided causeways sees only miles of glistening water-pans, distant spires pouring their steady curtains of warm rain, and the faint mist rising into the open air.

You only begin to see them when you stop looking for movement and start looking for stillness that does not belong to the architecture.

In the center of a roofless rotunda, half a mile out across the pans, a pale form stands beside an arched xylem rib. At a glance it looks like another grown buttress—slick with embryonic dew, shaded the same bone-ivory and faint, translucent jade as the living floor. Then it shifts its weight, and a subtle, rhythmic ripple spreads outward across the shallow water around its ankles.

There are never many of them. Across a plateau wide enough to hold a town, there might be only a dozen, or perhaps a score, spaced so far apart that days can pass without two of them coming within a quarter-mile of one another. Yet they are never truly separate, because the hydraulic engine beneath their feet binds them into a single, continuous conversation.

They are tall, narrow-shouldered, and entirely smooth. A world without dry fiber, wind, or rot has left them with no hair, no nails, and no flaking outer skin—nothing that could shed into the pans and clog the invisible pores of the floor. Their flesh is dense, slightly turgid, and permanently wet, gleaming with the same thin film of moisture that coats the causeways. Their heads are small and sleek, with wide-set, lidless eyes that are dark and clear as well-water, built to read the glare off the flat pans. They have no external ears; the continuous, booming roar of the siphon-spires would have made them useless. Instead, the skin along their jawlines, palms, and the broad, flat soles of their feet is thin and faintly recessed, tuned to drink in the low, heavy *thrum* of the vascular bladders pulsing beneath the membrane.

To them, the deafening roar of the plateau is not noise—it is the medium they inhabit, as natural and unnoticed as air. They speak by interrupting it.

When one of them wishes to reach another across the mile-wide basin, it walks to the nearest rotunda and presses its spine, palms, and bare heels firmly against the curve of a hollow xylem arch. By leaning its weight into the living rib, or relaxing against it in slow, measured intervals, it dampens the arch’s resonance, bending the organ-pipe hum by a fraction of a tone. That slight shift travels down through the pale biological welds of the floor, riding the pulse of the circulating water. Half a mile away, another figure standing ankle-deep in a stepped basin feels the tiny, deliberate skip in the vibration beneath its feet, and turns its head toward the distant spire.

They do not eat as creatures of the lower valleys eat. There is nothing to harvest here, and no fire to cook with, and tearing a piece from the living floor would break the skin of the world. Instead, they are sustained by the engine itself. At long, unhurried intervals, they kneel beside the flared mouths of the siphon-spires or press their forearms and throats against the weeping undersides of the arches, absorbing the warm, mineral-rich condensation and the sweet, thin embryonic dew directly through porous patches along their skin. Their chests barely rise or fall; their metabolism is slow and even, paced to the heavy, unhurried thrum of the bladders below.

Most of their existence is spent in quiet, solitary patrol along the straight causeways. They carry no tools—there is nothing here to cut, and nothing to bind—yet they are the reason the plateau remains locked in its strange, gleaming equilibrium.

Warm, wet places want to grow, and they want to rot. Left to itself, even this high above the valleys, the basin would soon be choked with wind-blown lichen, algae in the warm pans, and wild, tangled shoots bursting through the sutures of the floor. The inhabitants are the ones who hold it at the threshold.

Moving with a slow, liquid economy of motion, they walk the ribs dividing the water-pans, their eyes scanning the pale-jade surfaces for the slightest blemish. When a speck of silt settles in the corner of a stepped basin, or a stray spore from the lower valleys tries to take root in the shade of an arch, one of them kneels and presses a broad, spatulate hand over it. Their palms secrete a clear, slightly bitter salve that dissolves the foreign matter without scarring the plant-skin beneath, leaving the surface as glossy and unblemished as if it had emerged from a bud-scale an hour ago.

When the internal pressure of the engine pushes too hard against a seam—when a dormant bud-scale begins to swell along a causeway, threatening to split the smooth geometry—two or three of them will gather, summoned by a flattened note in the floor. They place their warm, heavy hands over the swelling tissue and hold it still, leaning their weight into the archways to quiet the local pulse until the shoot subsides back into dormancy. They do not prune; they simply persuade the plateau to remain unfinished.

With the rare, small animals that occasionally appear—the nearly transparent striders skating across the warm pans, or the pale, blind grazers clinging to the arches—they have a vast, indifferent gentleness. They step carefully around the grazers, never disturbing them while they drink the condensation. Only when one of those tiny creatures dies, dropping silently into a water-pan, does an inhabitant stoop to lift it before decay can begin. Carrying the small body to the base of a siphon-spire, it presses the carcass gently against the thirsty, porous root-flesh, where the engine quietly draws the matter back into its own circulation.

Because nothing here ages in the ordinary way, it is impossible to tell how long any one of them has walked the causeways. They do not seem to multiply, and they do not seem to wither. Yet now and then, over spans of years that leave no mark on the unweathered floor, one of them simply slows down.

It walks out to the edge of a quiet pan, far from the loudest spires, and lies down along one of the low, gently curving ribs of tissue that divide the water. It presses its wet cheek, its palms, and its long limbs flat against the pale-jade skin and stops moving. Warm, tepid water laps gently over its side. Without rot, without a scavenger to disturb it, the living floor simply accepts it the way two wet shoots accept each other when pressed together—slowly softening at the seam, welding skin to skin until the boundary is forgotten, and nothing remains on the glistening plain but one more smooth, seamless rib humming quietly under the open sky.
