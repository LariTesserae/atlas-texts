You would not hear it coming—nothing can be heard over the room’s permanent thunder—and in the murky amber, your eyes would skip right past it, fooled by the detached shadows swimming lazily against the current.

You would notice it only when a crack in the floor pulses copper-bright, and the brief light catches a pale, broad-knuckled hand closing around a bone wedged into the obsidian.

It clings there in the lee of an overhang, anchored against the shearing tide, looking less like a swimmer than a climber caught in a vertical gale. Its body is compact, heavy-boned, and built for impact rather than speed. Its skin is the dull, ash-white of wet rhyolite, mottled with darker patches of mineral callous along the shoulders, shins, and spine—and mapped everywhere with thin, silvered seams where the young glass has opened it and healed over. Its limbs are long-jointed and thick-muscled, ending in blunt, spatulate fingers with broad nails like horn, the palms darkened and leathery from a lifetime of gripping stone and shell.

Where its eyes ought to be, there is only smooth, taut skin, faintly scarred and unbroken. Sight would be a liability here, where light is scarce and shadows lag a second behind the rocks that cast them. Instead, it reads the basin through its skeleton. Its bones are dense as ballast, heavy enough to keep it from being hurled into the churning ceiling, and they act like tuning forks in the concussive roar. When it wants to know what the room is doing, it does not turn its head; it presses its chin or the flat of its sternum directly against the obsidian, listening past the water’s chaos to the deeper, slower groans of the mountain’s crown.

It does not belong entirely to the water, nor to the storm above it. When its lungs grow heavy, it unhooks itself from the bone-grips and launches into the violent mid-water, timing its ascent to the scream of a rising air-pocket. In the dim, silt-choked murk, it intercepts a boulder-sized bubble of trapped storm-air just before the conflicting currents can tear it apart—clamping its jaw around the silvered skin of the pocket, gulping down the cold, thin mountain breath before letting the scalding updrafts carry it back down toward the floor.

It feeds on what little the basin offers. In the narrow seams of calm behind boulders, it crops the stubby knots of pale, rubbery kelp with flat, grinding teeth. Better still are the blind, heavy-shelled drifters that hug the bottom rubble. It stalks them not by chasing, which is impossible in the shearing currents, but by waiting in the silt until a thermal vent blows open and scatters them. It catches the flattened nautiluses and armored eels in its bare hands, wedges them into a fracture in the cliff, and waits—holding the shell against the stone until a stuttering collapse sends a delayed shockwave slamming through the water, cracking the armor clean open against the rock.

The thickest of those shells, and the longest of the calcified bones, it does not discard. It carries them to the sheerest walls of black glass and drives them with the heel of its palm into the narrow fissures, building a sparse, secret ladder of handholds across the drowned nave—the only path through a room of fresh blades that will not slice its flesh to the bone.

When the cold downdrafts of rain-fed brine pour through the roof and the currents turn savage, it retreats to the lower ledges. There, it wedges its curved hip or the dense ridge of its shoulder into one of the half-moon hollows it has spent years grinding smooth with a broken nautilus shell. Curled into the only dull cradle in a world of razor edges, it rocks gently in the backwash, letting the warm vents wash over its scarred hide while the thunder rattles its teeth.

Most strangely, it tends the room’s fraying physics. 

It cannot stop the cliffs from shearing away, dissolving into silt, and snapping back whole. Yet it seems to know, by the ache in its heavy bones, where gravity has gone hesitant and begun to pool. In those quiet eddies beneath the overhangs, it gathers razor-edged shards of basalt—handling them with a slow, meticulous respect—and stacks them one atop another. It does not balance them by eye, nor against any true down, but by the faint, trembling hum the shards make as the current brushes past them. When the cairn is finished, it stands against all reason, leaning slightly toward whatever wall currently holds the weight, while loose pebbles drift into slow, obedient orbit around its peak.

What it makes of these small anchors—whether they are landmarks for a blind climber, or quiet places where the basin’s violence is briefly persuaded to hold still—is impossible to say.

Sometimes, clinging to a bone-grip in the copper-lit dark, it will lift a smooth stone and tap it sharply twice against the obsidian. The sound cannot travel through the roaring water, but it races cleanly through the solid glass of the mountain’s crown. And now and then, from the far side of the sunken nave—out of the dim amber and the swirling ash—a faint, sharp *tap-tap* answers back through the rock. 

Whether there is another one over there, waiting in its own half-moon hollow, or whether it is only the mountain stuttering—returning the same knock a few moments late from a fracture that has not yet finished breaking—it waits for the answer all the same, chin pressed to the stone, before sliding back into the warm, deafening dark.
