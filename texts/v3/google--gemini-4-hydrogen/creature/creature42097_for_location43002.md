At first, the basin seems to belong entirely to iron, water, and leaf. Nothing scurries when a sluice gate groans; nothing flinches when a steam vent shrieks. Yet if you stand on one of the pitted catwalks long enough for the deafening roar to flatten into a kind of background pressure, you begin to notice that not all the movement in the black lagoons belongs to the machines.

Watch the concentric ripples radiating from the submerged hydraulic rams. Every so often, a ripple breaks early—warped against something soft and heavy just beneath the mirror-slick surface. Or you will see a slow, V-shaped wake creeping *against* the current from a brick spillway, steady and unhurried, moving with a purpose that has nothing to do with gravity.

What lives here has no nerves to jangle, no eyes to scald, no bones to rattle loose under the endless sub-bass shudder of the engines. It is neither animal nor plant, though in the olive-amber gloom it borrows the look of both: a sluggish, hydraulic thing built from the same copper, salt, and waxy membrane as the basin itself.

When one surfaces along a submerged brick causeway, it looks at first like a sunken bolster or a mound of verdigris-crusted slag. Its hide is thick, matte, and mottled in the basin’s own palette—bruised violet where the skin stretches thin, dark copper and pale salt-bloom along its upper ridges. There is no head, no mouth, no symmetry to speak of, only a cluster of fused, leather-tough lobes perforated by tiny, rhythmic pores. Inside, there is no flesh in the animal sense: only a labyrinth of fluid-filled chambers suspended with dense mucilage and fine metallic grit.

Because it has no nerves, it does not hear the roar of the boiler houses or feel the scalding heat of the water. Instead, it inhabits the sound physically. The staggered thudding of the submerged rams sets up standing waves inside its fluid chambers, turning its entire body into a slow, resonant gauge. When a sluice gate dumps a fresh sheet of mineral water, or when a heavy seed pod *plops* from an overhead creeper into the lagoon, the interference pattern inside the creature shifts. Its internal grit settles toward the disturbance. Without a single thought or reflex, the whole mass swings slowly toward the source, like a compass needle turning in thick oil.

It moves by heat and pressure—a living extension of the basin’s own plumbing. By pressing a flank against the scorching brick of a boiler vault or a submerged steam line, the fluid in its rear chambers warms and expands, forcing its bulk forward into the merely hot water of the open lagoon, where it cools slightly and draws in fresh broth through its underside. Along the wet spillways and brick ledges, it hauls itself out of the water in slow, bulging heaves, shifting turgor from chamber to chamber with the patient, blind regularity of a piston.

They gather where the machines are most violent. Every few minutes, when a rotating scythe clears a clogged spillway or a burst of superheated steam strips a manifold clean, a rain of shredded fern-fronds and half-cooked creeper pulp washes down into the lagoons. The creatures converge on the fallout. Having no mouths, they simply drape a heavy lobe over a drift of wet pulp or a fallen seed pod, secrete a hot, sour exudate that breaks down the waxy plant skins, and drink the slurry directly through their copper-flecked hides.

Even the machinery’s defenses cannot truly harm them; they only make more of them. Now and then, a younger one creeps too far up a spillway in pursuit of the hanging moss and is caught by a rotating scythe. There is no recoil, no thrashing, no pain. The iron blade slices cleanly through the lobe, and the severed piece tumbles back into the hot, copper-rich lagoon. Within minutes, the heat and dissolved minerals seal the wound into a tough, glassy skin, and the fragment rounds itself into a new, fist-sized bladder—already pulsing faintly in time with the rams beneath the water.

The oldest of them no longer move at all. Over decades of siphoning the mineral-heavy broth, their hides plate themselves in layers of solid verdigris, silica, and green copper carbonate, growing too heavy for their internal pressure to shift. They settle permanently onto the submerged arches, the intake grates, and the brick footings of the octagonal pump-towers.

From the catwalks above, these ancient ones are indistinguishable from the architecture—bloated, salt-crusted bolsters wedged into the stonework, gently expanding and contracting with the rhythm of the pumps. The pale creepers even root into their mineralized backs, mistaking them for iron or brick, and drop their heavy seed pods directly onto the creatures’ broad, unfeeling crowns. Still alive at their gelatinous cores, the old ones simply absorb the gift, filtering the hot, copper water in the steaming twilight, part of the self-perpetuating organ that keeps the basin breathing.
