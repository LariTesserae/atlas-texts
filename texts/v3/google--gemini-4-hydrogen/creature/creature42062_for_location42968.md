They are a quiet, dry-skinned people who move as though they are always listening to the hum of a wire.

To live in a basin that has never known rain is to unlearn water. The citizens of the valley—whom the few outsiders who have ever descended call the *Grounded*, though they call themselves simply the *Tenders*—are tall, lean, and remarkably still when at rest. Their skin is pale, matte, and cool, with the fine, dry grain of seasoned ash wood or unglazed porcelain. They do not sweat; in this brisk, bone-dry air, moisture is too precious to waste on the surface of the body. Instead, their pores secrete a faint, protective wax, and each morning they rub their knuckles, elbows, and faces with refined amber resin from the mills, leaving them with a faint, clean scent of dry cedar. When they grieve or are stung by chalk-dust, they do not weep saltwater, but blink away a single, slow bead of clear, fast-hardening gum.

Their clothing is as crisp and architectural as the papery canopies above them. Woven on the silent electrostatic looms from silver-grey frond-fiber and conductive spider-silk, their coats and tunics hang in stiff, dry folds that never cling. Every garment is stitched with fine copper thread—not for ornament, but for survival. In a valley saturated with high-voltage induction and carpeted in insulating lichen-felt, a careless walker builds a charge strong enough to stop a heart or spark a firestorm. So the Tenders braid copper wire into their hair and trail bronze grounding-straps from their heels, bleeding their static safely into the iron-framed causeways with every soundless step.

Because the corky plant-mat swallows sound like thick wool, they have no use for shouting. They speak in low, dry, unhurried murmurs when standing shoulder-to-shoulder, and rely on a brisk, precise vocabulary of finger-signs across a room. When they must send word across the wide, empty bark-plains between districts, they do not use horns or bells; they step up to the tensioned steel bracing-cables that anchor the factory-towers and strike them with weighted brass mallets. A clean, coded *thrum* travels for miles through the taut metal, vibrating through the soles of the boots of anyone standing on a distant causeway.

Their lives are divided between the mill and the root, for in this city, engineering and arboriculture are the same craft. 

There are the **Pruners**, who climb the skeletal towers in total silence, armed with ceramic shears that will not short a live busbar. Their work is endless: the chalk-cane buildings are alive, and their electric tropism is relentless. Left unwatched for a single season, a tower’s living struts will coil hungrily toward the copper lines, swallowing porcelain insulators in woody knots and twisting the iron frames out of true. The Pruners trim back the eager tendrils, seal the cuts with hot wax, and coax the pale trunks into straight, load-bearing columns.

There are the **Silk-Reapers**, who patrol the high struts alongside the long-legged loom-spiders. The Reapers wear insulated leather gauntlets and carry small, hand-cranked Leyden-wands. With a gentle, rhythmic tick of static, they coax the spiders to spin thicker, purer threads of conductive silk across the copper frames, harvesting the shimmering grey skeins onto wooden bobbins while tossing the spiders stunned chalk-moths as payment.

And out in the jarring, deliberate emptiness between the districts walk the **Plain-Wardens**. The wide expanses of living bark-plain are not wasted land; they are firebreaks and magnetic watersheds, keeping the high-voltage mills far enough apart that a single arc-flash cannot turn the bone-dry valley into a furnace. The Wardens walk these silent, pale rugs for days at a time, testing the lichen-felt with bronze probes for dry-rot, clearing dead coral-wood before it can bridge an insulation gap, and drinking small, rationed measures of warm, oily sap-broth from sealed glass flasks.

The Tenders do not own this basin alone; they have merely woven themselves into a dry-chemistry ecology that was already waking up when the first arc-lamps were strung.

Out on the open bark-plains, where the Wardens walk, live the **Grit-Backs**—slow, low-slung grazers the size of calves, plated in overlapping shingles of pale, porous cork. They have no voices and almost no eyes, navigating by the faint magnetic pull of the distant power lines. They shuffle across the lichen-felt on broad, padded feet, tearing up dead thickets of petrified coral-wood and grinding them in twin muscular gizzards. As they move, they vent fine clouds of pulverized limestone dust through slits along their flanks, reseeding the dry air with the exact mineral grit the surrounding flora needs to drink.

In the papery canopies above the causeways, where the six-limbed velvet-hided grazers cling to the undersides of the root-bridges, stalks a sleek, silent predator: the **Snap-Fox**. Roughly the length of a human forearm, covered in soot-grey down and articulated plates of amber chitin, it hunts not by scent or sight, but by the long, copper-rich whiskers fanning from its snout. It feels the tiny bio-electric flicker of a grazer’s nervous system through the bark. When it strikes, there is no snarl—only a sharp, localized *crack* of discharged static that stuns the grazer before it can drop into the safety of the lichen-carpet below.

And far above the city, lost in the jagged, unweathered ceiling where cool daylight filters through the translucent selenite plates, live the **Plate-Gliders**. Pale, nearly weightless creatures with broad, mica-thin membranes stretched between elongated ribs, they never touch the valley floor. They ride the silent, dry thermal updrafts rising from the distant geothermal shafts, clinging to the crystalline fissures like living frost, and swooping down only to snatch the chalk-moths that drift too high above the industrial arc-lamps.

At the end of a shift, when the slow flywheels are locked and the Tenders climb the stepped living quarters to their dry, cedar-scented beds, the valley settles into a bright, motionless hush. The arc-lamps never dim; there is no night here, only the cool, even light of the ceiling and the faint, sub-audible hum of copper busbars feeding the dark roots below. Outside the woven windows, the chalk-cane quietly thickens around its iron bones, a Grit-Back vents a soft plume of white dust onto the plain, and a loom-spider repairs a broken thread—all of them, builders and beasts alike, still learning the shape of a world without rain.
