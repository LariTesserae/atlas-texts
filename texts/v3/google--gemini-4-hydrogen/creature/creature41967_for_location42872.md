Nothing on the plateau lives on top of the world so much as inside it. To survive up here, where the air is thin and the clouds condense directly against your skin, you have to give up the idea of a dry surface. Everything that breathes or crawls or grows has made the same quiet bargain with the wet, the cold, and the steady, sub-audible thrum of the ground.

Start at the level of the moss, where the light is silvery and diffused. If you part the saturated cushions of sphagnum—pushing aside the waxy leaves of the dwarf willows and the hanging curtains of lichen—you find that the three-foot pelt of turf is not a single floor, but a crowded apartment of narrow, waterlogged corridors.

The smallest permanent residents are the beetles. Their carapaces have the dull, greenish-black sheen of weathered bronze, hardened by generations of drinking mineral seepage from the buried brine siphons. They do not fly; in the thick, wet mist, wings are a liability that tear and mat. Instead, they march in slow, deliberate files along the braided copper cables buried beneath the root-mat, guided by the steady magnetic field of the direct current. Their larvae cling directly to the warm polymer sheathing, grazing on the pale, threadlike fungi that thrive on trace leaks of heat and electricity. When the larvae pupate, they shed skins stiff with precipitated copper, leaving tiny, hollow shells along the lines like tarnished beads.

In the deeper, flooded pockets of the peat, where the turf gives way to dark sinkholes, live the amphibians—pale, soft-skinned newts with no eyes at all, only smooth, translucent skin stretched over the sockets. They have no need for sight in the root-labyrinth, and no need for lungs; they breathe through their flanks, drawing oxygen straight from the cold, moving water. They gather in slow, tangled knots around the buried electrolytic cells, where the bog-water is faintly warm and tastes of sharp salts. There, invisible streams of split gas—oxygen and hydrogen—rise in tiny, silent pearls through the peat, and the newts hang suspended in the fizzing warmth, their feathery external gills pulsing in time with the slow trickle of the meltwater.

Even the birds here have forgotten the sky. Small, round-bodied rails, no bigger than a fist and covered in dense, oily plumage the color of wet granite, thread the low tunnels beneath the dwarf willows. Their wings have shrunk to stiff, stubby paddles used only for balance as they sprint across the floating mats of liverwort. They never sing. A sharp call would carry nowhere in the sponge-muffled air, and it would only draw notice. Instead, they communicate with soft, percussive clicks of their beaks—sounds that blend seamlessly into the *plip* of condensation dropping from the sedges.

Above the turf, out in the open mist of the broad terraces, move the herds.

The grazers are heavy, barrel-chested beasts with broad, cushioned feet that splay into four wide toes with every step, spreading their weight like snowshoes so they neither punch through the root-mat nor crush the brittle mineral crusts around the evaporation pans. Their coats are thick, double-layered pelts: an oily, impenetrable underfur that stays dry against their skin, beneath a long outer thatch of hollow guard hairs that trap the mist. Because they move so slowly and rarely shake themselves, tiny liverworts and pale mosses actually take root in the damp outer thatch of their backs, turning a standing herd into a cluster of low, breathing hummocks indistinguishable from the plateau itself.

They are almost entirely silent. In the thick fog, where the horizon is a pale line and every ridge looks like the last, a lost calf does not bleat. Instead, the herd stays together through the ground. When they pause to rest, they lay their heavy chins flat against the wet moss, feeling the steady, sub-audible thrum of the buried turbines through their jawbones. A lead cow, sensing a shift in the terrain or the approach of danger, simply lifts one broad hoof and brings it down—a dull, low-frequency *thump* that travels through the waterlogged peat for half a mile, felt in the soles of the herd’s feet long before anything could be heard in the air.

And something is always listening for them—not with ears, but with skin.

The plateau’s predators are low-slung, long-bodied carnivores, built something like oversized otters with thick, water-shedding pelts of dark slate-gray. Their eyes are small and milky, nearly useless in the cloud-sheets that hug the ridges. Instead, their broad, flat snouts are hairless and leathery, pitted with thousands of tiny, gel-filled pores. As they prowl the edges of the terraced pools, they sweep their heads slowly from side to side just above the wet turf, reading the electrical landscape.

It takes a lifetime to learn how to hunt here. The buried copper lines hum with a constant, steady direct current, creating an unchanging electrical background—a flat, quiet horizon of voltage. The predators learn to subtract that steady song from their senses, listening only for the anomalies: the sharp, irregular flicker of a beating heart, the tiny bioelectric spark of a grazer’s leg muscle tensing in the mire, or the sudden twitch of a blind newt darting under a root. When a predator locks onto that living static, it moves with terrible, muffled speed—a low shadow sliding over the moss, a brief, almost noiseless splash in a copper-rimmed pool, and then the wet turf closes back over the stillness.

Deeper still, far beneath the roots and the hunting grounds, the flooded basalt shafts hold a stranger, older ecology. Down in the pressurized dark, where the hydraulic turbines turn under the patient weight of percolating meltwater, there is no sunlight and no green. The stone walls and the bronze turbine housings are furred in pale, ghostly sponges and slick, orange-black mats of bacteria that feed not on plants, but on the dissolved iron and sulfur leaching from the rock. Pale, blind crustaceans—ghostly things with long, sweeping antennae—cling to the edges of the spinning vanes, plucking bits of organic detritus from the ceaseless, silent current before it disappears into the dark below.

And yet, an engine of this scale—even one grown seamlessly into the stone and the peat—does not run entirely by itself. Pipes clog with mineral scale. Siphons choke on sulfur crusts. Braided copper lines, shifted by the slow, eons-long settling of the bog, occasionally fray and leak their current into the mire.

So there are the Tenders.

You could walk across a ridge in the mist and pass within twenty feet of one without ever knowing they were there. They have no villages, no standing frames, no roofs to catch the wind or walls to rot in the eternal damp. They are people—or what people become after uncounted generations of living in a place that refuses to be dry—short-limbed, broad-chested, with thick, calloused skin and dark, oily hair that sheds the condensing cloud.

They wear layers of felted bog-fiber and the oiled hides of the grazers, stitched together with sinew and fastened with carved horn or strips of salvaged polymer—never metal, which corrodes quickly in the salty, ozone-laced air. Their boots are thin-soled moccasins, greased with mineral tallow, deliberately kept soft so they can feel the sub-audible thrum of the grid through the soles of their feet. To a Tender, that vibration is as legible as a map: a steady, even hum means the turbines are turning true; a flutter or a dead spot in the peat means a shaft is choked or a line has parted.

Because they have no houses, they sleep where the plateau itself is warm. They seek out the bright emerald basins above the buried transformers and electrolytic cells, where the waste heat keeps the moss body-warm and steams gently into the cool air. At the end of a long march, a group of Tenders will simply curl into the deep, steaming sphagnum, pulling the living mats of dwarf willow and lichen over themselves like heavy, wet blankets, sleeping cheek-to-cheek while the hidden machinery hums beneath their ribs.

They carry no torches—fire is a futile thing in a world where every millimeter is saturated—only long, weighted staffs of dense horn and spades carved from the broad shoulder-bones of the grazers. When a buried cable faults, the Tenders do not need instruments to find it. They watch the plateau. Above a broken line, the warm emerald moss begins to brown and die back, and the grazers shy away from the stray voltage tingling in the mire. The Tenders gather at the dead patch, kneel in the cold wet, and part the three-foot-thick turf with their bone spades. Working in near-total silence, communicating with low, jaw-vibrating hums and touches on the shoulder, they uncover the blackened copper taproot, splice the braided strands back together, and wrap the wound in resinous peat and strips of old polymer before folding the living moss smoothly back over the scar.

And when the great turbines in the flooded basalt shafts begin to slow—choked by pale freshwater sponges and heavy crusts of mineral scale—it is the Tenders who go down into the dark. They strip off their oiled hides, tie heavy stones to their waists, and slip silently into the ink-black sinkholes, holding their breath for minutes at a time in the freezing, pressurized water. Down by the turning vanes, feeling their way entirely by touch in the blind dark, they scrape the bronze blades clean with horn knives until the steady, patient weight of the meltwater catches them again, and the deep, quiet thrum returns to the soles of the world above.

When they climb back out onto the moss, shivering and slick with mineral water, they do not look up at the pale horizon. They simply wring the water from their hair, pull their oiled hides back over their shoulders, and press their palms flat against the wet turf to listen—waiting until the engine beneath them settles back into its ceaseless, silent work.
