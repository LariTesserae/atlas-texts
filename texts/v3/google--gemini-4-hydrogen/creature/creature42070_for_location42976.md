To live in a world where the noon never wavers and the engines never miss a beat is to live inside a held breath. Nothing here can afford to be fragile, and nothing can afford to hurry; hurry implies a destination, and here, everything has already arrived.

If you drift long enough through the bright, chest-vibrating roar, you begin to notice that the emptiness of the water is an illusion of scale. Life has simply sorted itself into the quiet seams of the machinery—or learned to ride the current so cleanly that it disappears into the background hum.

### The Seam-Dwellers

Inside the fluted trunks of the plant-towers, where no sunlight reaches and thousands of tons of cool water are slammed upward with every *thump-hiss*, lives a creature that knows the world only as pressure and shear. They are **siphon-clingers**—flat, rubbery organisms shaped like teardrops, blind and colorless, anchored by muscular suckers to the inner walls of the vascular bladders. They do not fight the hydraulic stroke. On the intake, their elastic bodies stretch to three times their length, exposing fine, comb-like gills to the rushing torrent to strain out stray spores and flecks of shed chitin; on the compression, they snap back into dense, armored knots that let the water thunder past without tearing them free.

Outside, down where the smooth rhizome terraces roll away in pale slate-green swells, the floor is tended by **silt-borers**—stubby, segmented creatures plated in dull, iron-stained calcite. They move with glacial slowness across the weathered valleys, swallowing the fine, gleaming mineral dust shed by the spinning heartwood turbines. They digest nothing of the metal itself; instead, they pack it into dense, microscopic pellets and press them directly into the breathing pores of the living floor, sealing the rhizome’s surface as it mineralizes from beneath. They are the reason the terraces remain smooth as river-stones, free of drifts or dunes.

Even the flat, mirror-still ceiling of the ocean has its residents. Right at the meniscus, where the shimmering gas plumes from the tower crowns break into the thin, high-altitude air, **skimmer-films**—colonies of microscopic, translucent polyps linked in papery, hexagonal sheets—ride the rising rings of oxygen. They never venture more than a millimeter below the surface, living their entire existence on the razor-thin boundary between the deafening underwater roar and the silent, unclouded mountain sky.

### The Interval-Swimmers

Yet the true citizens of this high sea are not tucked into the trunks or pressed into the floor. They belong to the vast, sunlit miles between the towers—beings so thoroughly adapted to the permanent noon and the sixty-cycle hum that a visitor might drift past one at twenty paces and mistake it for a shadow cast by nothing.

They are roughly the length of a human, though flattened and broad, shaped like a cross between a manta ray and a tuning fork. Their mantles are tough, matte slate-green mottled with tarnished verdigris, matching the exact palette of the distant floor, and reinforced along the leading edges with flexible, interlocking plates of calcified silica. From the rear of each body, two long, rigid keels trail behind like twin prongs, veined with fine threads of biological copper and tipped with polished nodules of magnetite.

They do not swim with frantic tail-beats. They glide—heavy, negatively buoyant, and unhurried—angling their broad wings to catch the steady, unvarying currents that sweep across the plateau. Beneath their mantles, rows of coppery gill-filaments drink the hyper-oxygenated water, while their twin keels act as a living antenna, reading the sixty-cycle electromagnetic field of the sagging power-vines miles away.

To them, the deafening *thump-hiss* of the plant-engines is not noise; it is the floor of reality, subtracted from their awareness the way a land-dweller ignores the weight of air. What they *hear*—what registers in the magnetite tips of their keels—are the tiny disruptions in that roar: the passage of a silver-plated eel, the faint static crackle of a dirigible grazer discharging against a root, or the subtle, rhythmic *interference* where the pressure waves of three or four distant towers collide.

Because the towers are spaced miles apart, their unvarying beats overlap in vast, three-dimensional geometric lattices across the open water. At certain precise coordinates, the compression stroke of a tower to the west meets the intake trough of a tower to the east, canceling out entirely. These are **null-nodes**—invisible, palm-sized or room-sized pockets of absolute acoustic stillness suspended in the middle of the bright, empty sea.

This is where the interval-swimmers rest. They find a node by feel, slip inside its invisible boundary, and hover motionless in cool, sunlit silence while the industrial roar rages a hand’s-breadth from their wingtips.

### A Language of Noon

In a world with no night, no mud, and no loose stone, there are no tools, no shelters, and no fires. Their eyes—set along the upper rim of their crescent heads, lidless and black, with horizontal slits narrowed against the endless daylight—are built for distance. In water this clear, unclouded by silt or surface chop, sight stretches for miles.

So they speak in sunlight.

Along the dorsal ridges of their mantles sit rows of translucent, crystalline scales, normally folded flat against their matte skin. When one of them wishes to be seen, it banks a fraction of a degree and erects its scales, catching the steady overhead noon and throwing a sharp, blindingly bright blade of polarized silver-green light across the empty miles.

Drift long enough through the mid-water, and you will see it: a sudden, crisp *flash* two miles ahead, like a mirror struck by the sun—answered three seconds later by a twin flash to the north, and a slow, rhythmic triple-pulse from near a tower’s crown. They do not report news; there is no news in a closed loop. Instead, their heliograph language is a continuous, shared mapping of the whole. They trace the slow drift of the dirigible grazers, the shifting density of the eel-ribbons, the exact pitch of the gas plumes venting far above.

Sometimes, a dozen or more will gather in the open water between two towers, drawn together by a sequence of long-range flashes. Without touching, they arrange themselves into a precise, three-dimensional helix, angling their copper-veined keels into the sixty-cycle field of the floor cables below. Together, they bend the local current—not enough to strain a vine or trip a smelter-nodule, just enough to create a brief, shimmering standing wave in the water. For a few minutes, the rising halos of micro-bubbles from the cables below twist into a rising silver spiral, and the passing eels weave through them in a sudden, breathless braid. Then the swimmers tilt their wings, the helix dissolves, and the water returns to its exact, unvarying state.

### The Exact Count

Nothing in this sea grows larger, and nothing decays—not even them.

An interval-swimmer does not measure its life in years, for there are no seasons to mark them. Instead, it measures time in *stiffness*. Over centuries of gliding through the mineral-rich water, the constant vibration of the engines and the slow accretion of silica gradually thicken its flexible joints. Its copper filaments darken; its crystalline back-scales grow cloudy, throwing duller and duller flashes against the noon.

When one of them grows too rigid to hold its trim in the null-nodes, it does not fight the current. It banks downward, leaving the bright open water for the last time, and settles onto one of the swollen, tuberous smelter-nodules where the pitch-black power cables meet the living floor.

There, bathed in the dull, heatless chemical glow, it locks its twin keels around the rubbery hide of the cable and goes still. The same biological enzymes that refine dissolved iron and copper from the sea slowly, cleanly unmake it—not through rot, which has no place here, but through quiet reclamation. Its silica plates, its copper veins, its iron-rich tissues are drawn directly back into the fibrous lattice of the rhizome mat, reinforcing the floor against the endless vibration of the towers.

Elsewhere on the plateau—perhaps twenty miles away, along a smooth terrace weathered by steady currents—a translucent cyst of calcified silica swells from the living ground, fed by the exact measure of minerals returning to the loop. When the balance shifts by a single gram, the cyst splits, and a soft-plated swimmer unfolds into the cool, bright roar, blinking its lidless eyes at the permanent sun.

Their number has not changed by a single individual in ten thousand millennia. They do not tend the towers, and the towers do not know they are there; they are simply the quiet, gleaming thoughts of a machine-forest that reached perfection long ago, content to drift forever in the bright, loud noon.
