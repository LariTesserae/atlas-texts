If you watch the tank for only a minute, you will see nothing that moves at all, save for the trembling refraction of the sunbeams and the occasional, mercury-bright bead of oxygen detaching itself from a junction box. You would swear the place is empty.

You would have to wait through two or three cycles of the weighted brass ratchet—twenty minutes, perhaps half an hour—before you noticed that one of the vertical bundles of pale roots has quietly unlatched itself from its hydraulic cylinder.

It is not an animal. There is no blood in it, no gut, no frantic, twitching nerve-fire. It has no mouth to mar the symmetry of the kelp fronds, and no claws to scratch the glazed white tiles. Whatever lives here is something the tank itself might have dreamed up in the long, warm silence between its gears: a solitary, slow-moving *conductor*, born of the same marriage of mineral, current, and sun.

At rest, it is almost indistinguishable from the apparatus it tends. Its body is a tall, slender lattice of smooth, porcelain-white struts—neither bone nor ceramic, though it shares the unstained, geometric crispness of the floor tiles—bound together by tight, braided sheaths of pale, fibrous root-tissue. Stretched taut across this frame is a membrane of translucent cellulose, clear enough that the fierce mountain sunlight passes straight through it. Inside, in place of organs, you can see a slow, tidal circulation of clear sap, carrying suspended flecks of pure, glinting silver, and a neat, vertical stack of dark, velvety leaf-plates that angle themselves silently toward the overhead skylights.

It has no head in any familiar sense. At its apex sits a delicate, crown-like cluster of bevelled glass prisms, set into rings of tarnished bronze. These are its eyes, though they do not blink or dart. They simply gather the unfiltered high-altitude light and focus it downward onto the dark leaf-plates within its chest, drinking the sun to charge the low-voltage current that hums through its silvered veins.

Because there is no open water to swim through, it never swims. It navigates the dense, suffocating weave of pipes and rubbery emerald curtains by *bridging*. Over the course of an hour, a long, jointed limb—more like an articulated copper busbar wrapped in living moss than an arm—extends from its frame, threads between two banks of stainless-steel piping, and anchors its tip against a riveted iron catwalk. Where its silver-plated digit meets the iron, a faint, microscopic fizz of electrolysis blooms, a tiny halo of hydrogen pinpricks that anchors it electrically as well as physically. Then, with the patience of a growing vine, it draws its weight across the gap by shifting the hydraulic pressure in its hollow struts.

It is the tank’s living relay, its silent, solitary keeper. 

Though the machinery is unworn and the plants are unblemished, equilibrium is not effortless; it must be *persuaded*. As the copper-veined kelp grows thicker in the thirty-degree warmth, a heavy frond will inevitably drift too close to the razor-sharp teeth of a cast-bronze gear, or begin to choke the intake of a submerged valve. The keeper does not cut it. It has no shears, and nothing here is meant to be broken. Instead, it drapes itself across the nearest junction box, presses its porous, root-wrapped palms to the frond, and bleeds a tiny, calibrated trickle of direct current into the leaf’s metallic ribs. Over the span of an afternoon, the plant’s own tropism responds to the faint magnetic nudge; the heavy emerald ribbon curls gently away from the gear teeth, guided back into the open shafts of light without a single bruised cell.

When the spherical air-bladders clustered around the wiring grow overly taut, swollen with hours of trapped oxygen, the keeper reaches out with a fine, hair-thin copper filament from its wrist and touches the membrane. The excess charge grounds harmlessly through its porcelain frame, and the bladder relaxes, releasing its single, measured bead of gas in perfect time with the hydraulic valves.

It does not sleep, because there is no need to rest when you never hurry. Its consciousness—if a thing made of sap-pressure and slow-charging capacitors can be said to have one—moves at the speed of the brass ratchet. A thought begins when a cloud passes over the jagged mountain ridge outside, plunging the cathedral of glass into sudden, cool shadow. The keeper’s internal current dips; its prisms dim; it freezes mid-reach, suspended between a copper busbar and a curtain of amber kelp. While the shadow lingers, it simply listens through its bronze-ringed frame to the deep, velvet hush of the water and the subterranean thrum of the induction coils. 

When the cloud clears and the fierce sun punches back through the skylights, illuminating the crystalline depths in bright, trembling shafts, the keeper’s dark leaf-plates drink the warmth, its silver sap stirs, and its limb completes the millimeter of movement it began ten minutes ago.

It knows nothing of the thin, freezing air outside the leaded glass, nor of the treeline far below the ridge. Its entire universe is thirty degrees Celsius, unstained tile, and the *tock* of the gear. Locked inside its bright, silent box, the keeper tends the metallic forest and the turning engine alike—neither master nor prisoner, merely the one living circuit that binds them both in their endless, sunlit patience.
