You notice the green first, and the bone-white second, because those are the colors of the noise. The celery-pillars whistle; the pale, waxy trunks groan and split; the white roots wrestle in the dust. It takes an hour in the basin, with your ears ringing from the hydraulic roar and your throat dry with the taste of crushed stalks, to realize that you haven’t actually seen the third color.

The bruised violet is not on the leaves. It is not in the bark-pavement, or the blackening chaff of the collapsed pavilions.

You find it first in the shadows.

Stand still in one of the tight, dead-calm alleys—if you can find a patch of bark-plate that isn’t buckling—and watch the base of a fluted pillar. The shadow it casts onto the dusty polygons is crisp in the steady daylight, except at the very bottom, where it pools a shade too dark, edged with a faint, velvety plum. If you keep your eyes on it, you will see that pool of violet-black shiver. A fraction of a second *later*, the pillar above it lurches sideways on its root-skids.

The shadow did not follow the plant. The plant followed whatever was lying inside the shadow.

There are no birds here, no beetles, no ants—nothing with wet eyes, jointed legs, or warm blood. Flesh would not last an afternoon in this valley; it would be cooked by the dry glare, battered deaf by the whistling reeds, or sheared in half by a wall that decides to become a staircase. Whatever lives here does not belong to the kingdom of meat. It is dry, angular, and loud in a register that vibrates through your teeth rather than your ears.

Wait until midday, when the sun stands directly over the basin and the heat drives the sap-pressure to its peak. As the shimmering haze of mist rises off the upper canopies, the boxy, windowless chambers of fused agave-flesh begin to creak—not from the inside out, like a bursting tuber, but along their seams, as if something flat and tightly folded is wedging itself open.

It emerges not through a door, because there are no doors, but through the *give* in the corner where two walls meet.

At first, it looks like a strip of bruised-violet bark peeling away from the green. Then it unfolds. It has no head, no face, no limbs—only a tall, narrow assembly of pleated, papery vanes stretched taut over an internal scaffolding of dry, chalk-white struts. It is built like a cross between a kite, a bellows, and a seed-pod that has been dried and pressed flat for a century. Its outer skin is matte and fibrous, the color of a deep plum or a healing contusion, dusted here and there with a fine, pale powder that smells sharply of ozone.

It does not walk on the pavement. Feet would snag on the cracking bark; weight would slow it down when a pavilion begins to rot. Instead, it drifts a few inches above the dust, buoyed by the rising thermal drafts and the sheer static tension crackling between its vanes. When it needs to turn, it extends a single chalky strut from its flank, hooks the dry tip into the joint of a balustrade or the lip of a lily-pad cistern, and *snaps* its entire body around the pivot with a sound like a taut wire plucked in an empty room.

Down the length of its central hollow run neat, vertical rows of thumb-sized apertures—four to a row, rimmed with hardened, bone-white pith.

As it glides past one of the towering, hollow reeds, it presses its side flush against the vibrating green wall. The reed is screaming with upward sap-pressure; the creature’s four-holed flank locks onto the vibration, and suddenly the bruised-violet vanes swell slightly, humming in a deep, discordant chord that makes the dust jump on the pavement. It does not eat the plant. It does not drink the hoarded water—the mosses are welcome to that. It feeds on the *torque* of the valley: the violent hydraulic thrum, the hot resin vaporizing in the sun, the electric friction of a million cells dividing at once.

Now, look back at the city.

The vegetation did not catch urbanism from nowhere. A plant left to itself grows in arcs, spirals, and tangled forks; it reaches toward the sun in messy, branching patience. But these creatures cannot navigate curves. Their rigid, kite-like geometry—their straight struts and crisp, right-angled folds—exerts a kind of violent spatial pressure on everything around them. Wherever they drift, the hyper-fast, malleable flora parts before them like water before a prow, snapping into thirty-foot avenues, square-stemmed grids, and sharp-edged lintels.

They are the reason the geometry slips. You count twelve pillars on your left, twelve on your right, because one of them is hovering three streets over, holding the local space taut along its axis. When it snaps itself around a corner and glides away, the tension drops; the avenue behind you pinches shut into thorny latticework, or slides sideways with a seismic groan. When you walk down a straight corridor toward an archway and it takes twenty minutes to cross fifty yards, it is because one of them has just passed through ahead of you, leaving the space behind it pleated like an accordion, slowly unfolding back to its true length while the archway tilts upward into a balcony.

Even the city’s strange, useless mimics are just the flora echoing its tenants. Those perforated gourds humming in rows of four are the plants trying to bleed off the creatures’ acoustic static, growing resonators in the exact pattern of their flanks. The sunken amphitheater of cabbage-thick leaves is what happens when half a dozen of them cluster in a pit to feed on the deep, subterranean root-groans—their intersecting fields of tension forcing the surrounding foliage upward into steep, concentric tiers, while the roots beneath them knot and unknot in the dust, struggling to anchor themselves in ground that is being twisted like a rag.

They are never still for long. By late afternoon, as the misting haze thins and the heat begins to break, the ones in the lower quarters fold themselves flat—vanes collapsing inward with a dry, papery rustle—and slide into the cracks of the freshly grown towers, or press themselves flush against the bases of the pillars, disguised once more as pools of overly dark shadow.

Sometimes, one of them fails to fold fast enough when a bamboo-grass pavilion blackens and collapses, or gets caught when two shifting blocks grind together. It does not bleed. It simply shatters into dry chaff and bone-white splinters, its violet membranes flaking away into dust, its chalky struts breaking down into raw, fibrous pith. Over weeks and months, the restless churning of the valley pushes that dead, calcified latticework outward to the edges of the four-mile basin, piling it up into a raw, unweathered ring.

It is the rim you stood on before you dropped down—what looked like limestone, until you pressed your heel into it and felt it give.

By evening, the avenue you walked in on has been digested into a terrace of pale moss, and a new district of sharp-cornered towers is already unfolding in its place. If you climb back up to the rim before dark and look down into the screaming green and bone-white bowl, you will see the bruised-violet shapes rising from the rooftops like narrow, angular kites—humming in the cooling air, busy with the only business they know, endlessly straightening a world that was never meant to be straight.
