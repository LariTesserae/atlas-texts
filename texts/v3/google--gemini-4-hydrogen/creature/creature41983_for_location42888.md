If you stood in the ankle-deep water long enough for your eyes to adjust to the greasy twilight, you still would not see them at first. You would take them for part of the machines’ own long ruin: thick, slumped blisters of corroded lead wedged into the angles of the concrete plinths, or heavy, oil-blackened aprons of rubbery hide draped over the edges of the sunken culverts.

They have no sharp angles, and no parts that could break. In a place where the air itself is a physical weight that would shatter an eardrum and fatigue brittle bone to powder, whatever lives here has given up the luxury of skeletons. Their bodies are dense, broad, and low—heavy, flattened mounds of mineralized cartilage and gristle, soaked through with iron salts and slick with the same black, water-thinned grease that coats the floor. Their upper surfaces are dark as wet slate, pitted and crusted with green copper-oxide stains where they press against the weeping brass fittings of the turbines.

Nothing walks, crawls, or flies here, and they are no exception. They have no legs to lift them out of the cold sheet-flow, no claws to grip the rounded aggregate, no wings to be torn apart by the pneumatic vents. Yet they are not entirely sessile. Beneath their heavy, armoured backs, their undersides are broad, pale, muscular soles, almost translucent, like the bellies of deep-water skate. When they need to shift—toward a warmer exhaust seam, or away from a filament that has finally sputtered out—they do not crawl. They loosen their grip on the concrete by a fraction of a millimeter, exuding a fresh film of dark, tallow-thick mucus, and let the floor move them. The colossal, subterranean thrum of the turbines does the work: vibrating through the slabs, it jiggles their heavy mass forward in infinitesimal increments, a slow, greased hydroplaning along the smooth, worn troughs of the pavement. A journey from one solitary housing to the next, across miles of empty, flooded apron, might take a decade. Most never make it; they simply settle into the lee of a single plinth and let the decades pass over them.

Their lives are divided not into days and nights—for the wet cloud above never parts, and the dim amber and green halos behind the salt-crusted glass never change—but into intervals of forty seconds.

For thirty-nine seconds, they are sealed tight against the roar: mute, blind, indistinguishable from the blistered iron around them. Then, just before the fortieth second, a subtle change runs through the bass vibration of the floor—a tightening of the subterranean pressure that no human ear could separate from the din, but which they feel through the whole length of their mineral-heavy bodies.

Slowly, with a hydraulic, unhurried swelling, seams part along their backs. Pale, leathery flaps—the same translucent texture as the hollow-stemmed reeds that shiver beside them—fold outward, exposing wet, pleated membranes veined with dark sulfur. When the pressure-valve overhead lets out its pneumatic shriek and blasts a curtain of cold mist into the dark, they do not flinch. They drink it. The acoustic shockwave passes harmlessly through their fluid-filled chambers, stirring their sluggish circulation, while their pleated gills comb the freezing spray for atomized oil, sulfur, and the fine, suspended dust of the valley. As the mist settles back into the standing water, the seams draw shut again, sealing them in for another thirty-nine seconds.

They have no eyes to watch the empty miles of pavement, and no ears to be ruined by the engines. Instead, the outer rims of their flattened bodies—resting just beneath the surface of the cool, ankle-deep water—are lined with dense, sensitive fringes of calcified bristles. Through these, they read the geometric ripples that shake the standing water. The whole flooded valley is a single, taut drumhead to them. They know when a slab settles a hair’s breadth deeper into the mud of its own making three miles away; they know when a house-sized flywheel in a distant pit develops a new, uneven wobble; they know when the water-film thickens because a culvert grate has begun to choke with rust. 

Their minds, if they can be called minds, are built to the scale of the roar. A human thought is a fragile, flickering thing that the low-frequency pressure flattens into numbness; theirs are standing waves. Inside their thick skulls—not domes of bone, but hollow, resonant chambers of cartilage—the overlapping cycles of the turbines and hydraulic rams interfere and combine, humming in slow, complex harmonies. They do not think in words or images. They think in phase, in pressure, in the gradual drift of heat. To them, the valley is not silent or monotonous; it is a vast, intricate chord that has been held for centuries, and they are simply the flesh that listens to it.

They feed without hurry, extending short, boneless siphons from beneath their rims to graze on the rubbery, soot-colored lichen that clings to the mineral seams, or to filter the fine metallic silt that washes toward the grates. In return, the machines owe them a small, accidental debt. The thick, dark mucus they secrete to slide across the stone and protect their hides from the acid runoff mixes with the standing water and the oil-drip from the pits, replenishing the black grease that keeps the great flywheels from seizing in their housings. By scraping away the sulfur-crusts and filtering the silt around the plinths, they keep the sunken intakes from choking completely—not out of any loyalty or memory of who built the place, for they have never known a world without the engines, but simply because the warm leak of the pipes and the steady thunder of the rams are the only things that keep them alive.

Even their young are born into the cycle of the machines. Every few years, when the overlapping rhythms of the turbines align in a particular, heavy beat, the creatures release clusters of tough, oil-sealed cysts—no larger than rivets—into the shallow sheet-flow. The migrating water carries them across the worn pavement and down through the rusted grates into the roaring subterranean intakes. Most are crushed by the hydraulic rams or boiled in the pressure chambers. But a few—dense, rubbery, and impervious—survive the churning dark, are swept up into the riser pipes, and are blasted back out through the pressure-valves in the forty-second shriek. They drift down through the mist like heavy black hail, settling onto distant, lonely housings miles from where they began, wedging themselves into the warm seams beside the shivering reeds to grow, over slow decades, into another mound of listening, greased flesh.

So the valley keeps its rhythm. The machines grind themselves down into silt, the silt feeds the reeds and the pale, heavy things pressed against the iron, and the things exude the grease that keeps the flywheels turning—all of it humming together in the wet twilight, bound to a beat no one remembers starting, and nothing left alive knows how to stop.
