The builders are no taller than a human finger, and they move as if the air itself were thick as tallow. 

Seen up close, their stooped posture is not a sign of age but of architecture: generations spent beneath thumb-sized lintels and low, weeping vaults have curved their spines into permanent, practical arches. Their limbs are short and corded with dense muscle, ending in broad, flat-nailed fingers hardened at the tips like horn—tools meant for prizing pebbles from clay pipes and gripping wet, slick slate. Beneath their heavy, lime-crusted hides—stitched from the tough, pale skins of sump-newts and stiffened by years of mineral spray until they hang like curved plates of armor—their own skin is the color of wet chalk, translucent enough at the wrists and throat to show the slow, sluggish pulse of dark blood.

They have no use for ears. In a room where the water’s roar is a physical weight, an open ear would only madden or rupture. Instead, the sides of their heads are smooth, scarred-over hollows of thickened cartilage. They hear with their bones. When a builder pauses in a narrow, two-finger alley, they will often press their palm, their bare elbow, or the flat of their cheekbone directly against the wet masonry. Through the stone, the deafening white noise of the cavern resolves into a legible map of vibrations. A clear clay conduit carries a steady, deep-throated thrum; a weir choked with lime-grit sends a thin, nervous chatter up through the bone struts; a loose flagstone rattles with a dull, hollow knock three tiers away. Their entire world is a chord played in stone, and they spend their long, unhurried lives keeping it in tune.

Their eyes are small, milky, and set deep beneath heavy brow-ridges to shield them from the ceaseless, atomized mist. They cannot see the far side of the pocket, nor do they need to. To them, the upper fissure is merely a faint, cold bruise in the ceiling, and the tiny blue-white flames in the grease saucers are not lamps for reading, but sharp, cold beacons marking the steepest drops and the mouths of the most dangerous sluices. 

Instead of sight or speech, they navigate by a dense, overlapping grammar of touch. The waist-high parapets of the alleys are carved with notches—some sharp and deep to warn of a missing step, others worn smooth by thousands of passing thumbs to indicate a safe handhold or a shared cistern. Overhead and along the cantilevers, the braided sinew cables are knotted at irregular intervals. A builder crossing a bridge in the dark runs their fingertips along the cord, reading the tension of the sinew and the spacing of the knots: this many knots to the next landing, a double-knot for a branching flume, a frayed loop to tell of a weak strut. When two builders meet in a passage too narrow to pass, neither steps back blindly; they touch knuckles, palms, and shoulders in a brief, silent negotiation of weight and balance, before one hooks a heel into a wall-groove and lets the other edge past.

Nothing green grows to feed them, yet the storehouses wedged into the overhangs are rarely empty. Their agriculture is strictly mineral and cold. Down in the flooded sump and along the undersides of the travertine shelves, where the water slows to a film, a pale, greasy curd of iron-slime blooms on the rock. The builders tend these slick beds with scrapers of split horn, clearing away excess sinter so the slime can thicken. It is grazed upon by the mineral-shelled snails—slow, heavy creatures whose coiled houses grow thick with gray limestone crust. The builders herd them along the damp terraces, harvesting the largest for their tough, salty meat, pounding their shells into grit for lime-mud mortar, and pressing the dried slime-curd into dense, dark wafers that keep indefinitely in the cold.

In the deepest, darkest cisterns at the base of the pocket, they keep the pale, eyeless newts. Sluggish and cold-blooded, the newts feed on the blind silt-worms that breed in the sump-mud. They are rarely killed for meat; they are far too valuable alive, and when one finally dies of age or cold, every scrap is claimed. Their tough, porous hides are scraped, cured in mineral brine, and sewn into the stiff cloaks and bridge-cables that hold the town together. Their meager fat is rendered over slow, smoky embers of dried worm-casings into the cold, pale grease that feeds the saucer-lamps—the only fire the pocket ever knows.

Occasionally, once in a decade or a lifetime, the unreachable fissure high in the ceiling yields something from the rumored world above: a drowned cave-cricket, a splinter of bat-bone, a thread of pale, dead moss washed down by some distant surface thaw. These fragments are retrieved from the catch-basins with reverence, dried, and locked in the highest granaries—not for food, for they have forgotten what such things taste like, but as rare, lightweight materials for needle-work, splints, and the delicate triggers of their sluice-gates.

Because nothing changes here—no season, no storm, no shift in the aquifer’s steady pulse—time does not pass in days or years, but in the accumulation of stone. A builder’s life is measured by how thick the flowstone has grown over their threshold, or how many times they have had to re-chip the rim of their own doorway to keep the lime-glaze from sealing it shut. 

When they grow very old, the damp and the minerals settle into their joints, stiffening their limbs until their movements become as slow as the snails they tend. They do not fear this; it is simply the way of the pocket. When an elder can no longer clear a weir or read the fine knots on a cable, they retreat into one of the high, dry alcoves tucked beneath the cavern’s ribs. They pull their lime-crusted hide tight around their knees, press their jaw one last time against the humming stone to feel the steady, unbroken roar of the water, and go still. Over the decades that follow, the mist settles over them, laying down layer after patient layer of translucent sinter, until the builder is glazed smooth into the wall—another small, hunched buttress holding up the ceiling of the world.
