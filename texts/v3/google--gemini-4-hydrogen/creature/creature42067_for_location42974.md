The people who live in the cleft look, at first glance, like parts of the machinery that have learned to unbolt themselves.

They are small-boned, deep-chested, and dry as cured hide. Generations of living in air too thin to waste have bred out every unnecessary gesture: they do not wave, they do not pace, and they never shout. Even their laughter is a brief, economical hiss through the teeth. When a rigger pauses on a swaying gantry to wipe slate-dust from their eyes, you see the faint glitter of mica caught in the creases of their knuckles and along the rims of their ears, catching the amber bulb-light like frost that never melts.

Their skin is the color of oiled ash, kept supple not with water—which is too precious to spend on washing—but with tallow, beetle-oil, or a dry rub of fine talc-flour brushed off with stiff horsehair. Around their necks, almost everyone wears a *catch-rag*: a folded square of tight-woven linen, often stained dark at the center, that they pull up over their mouths when they sleep or haul on a heavy chain. In a place without dampness, a person’s own breath is a resource; the cloth traps a ghost of warmth and moisture against the lips so the lungs do not crack from the inside out.

Water itself is never seen in the open. It arrives from far below the lowest catwalks, drawn up through sealed copper risers from a deep-vein bore-hole beneath the spire’s root, tasting faintly of sulfur and warm iron. It is measured in brass thimbles, traded in stoppered glass flasks, and boiled only inside heavy-lidded pressure kettles that hum quietly without ever letting a wisp of steam escape.

To step into a sleeping-locker or a cook-stall is to enter a world ruled by static. The dry air and the endless friction of leather drive-belts charge everything with a restless, invisible prickle. Unchecked, it makes loose threads float like underwater weeds and turns a wool coat into a nest of blue sparks. The cleft-dwellers answer this with copper. Men and women alike braid thin, bare copper wire into their hair, or shave their heads close and wear felt skullcaps sewn with brass mesh. From the hem of every work-apron trails a short, fine grounding-chain that whispers across the iron catwalks as they walk, bleeding their charge back into the frame of the town with a faint, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick*.

Because the air carries sound so poorly—and because the town’s builders have spent decades wrapping every bearing in felt and lining every chute with hide—the inhabitants have largely given up on the human voice for anything farther than an arm’s length. Instead, they speak through the bones of the city.

Every steel guy-wire, tension rod, and turnbuckle in the cleft is tuned taut. A rigger wanting to call a mate three tiers down does not lean over the rail and holler; they draw a brass knuckle-ring or a wrench-butt across the nearest suspension cable. A sharp *plink* followed by two dull taps travels faster through iron than breath ever could through thin air. In the dimness, you can watch a whole crew pause, one felt-soled boot pressed flat against a girder, reading a message up through the soles of their feet before they nod and reach for their spanners.

Nothing in their lives is bolted down for good. A family’s entire world—three sleeping-bunks, a copper kettle, a shelf of stoneware jars, a framed charcoal rubbing of a child’s hand—is built inside a single riveted iron frame with lifting-eyes at all four corners. When the slate shifts, as it always does, they do not flee; they simply unhitch. 

Above and below every tier sit the **Plumb-keepers**, usually elders whose eyes have been dimmed by decades of arc-welder glare and floating mica. They spend their shifts in suspended wicker-and-iron cradles at the quietest intersections of the shaft, one bare palm pressed against the raw rock wall, the other resting on a weighted copper plumb-bob. They know the difference between the mountain’s ordinary settling—a slow, harmless *creep* of half an inch a season—and the tight, singing strain that means a fresh fracture is about to open. When a Plumb-keeper taps three slow, heavy beats on the main cable, an entire neighborhood of workshops and lockers uncouples itself in synchronized silence, winches itself twenty feet higher or across to the opposite wall, and re-anchors before the stone can even finish groaning.

The cleft-dwellers share this vertical labyrinth with its animals not as masters, nor quite as neighbors, but as fellow components of the same dry engine.

The **translucent spiders** that drape the undersides of the catwalks are tended like quiet livestock. Riggers never sweep away their grey, dust-heavy blankets of silk while they are still thin; they wait until the webs have drunk enough floating slate-powder to hang like thick, drooping curtains of felt. Then, with horn knives, they slice the curtains down, press them flat between oiled boards, and use the resulting *web-felt* to pack motor bearings, line the insides of their boots, and pad the cradles of the Plumb-keepers.

The **copper-shelled beetles**—called *oil-ticks* by the mechanics—are equally indispensable. When a new electric hopper or sorting-mill is bolted into place, the machinist brings a tin of beetles from an older casing and shakes them onto the warm iron. The insects keep the housings clean of stray grease, and they serve as living gauges: as long as they cluster in a calm, sandy shimmer over the motor, the coils are healthy. If the beetles suddenly stream outward across the girders in a panicking bronze wave, someone cuts the power before the bearings seize.

Even the **rock-voles** and the **cliff-swifts** have their places in the town’s narrow customs. The voles are treated as clever, infuriating cousins—too smart for simple traps, bold enough to unlatch a brass lunch-tin clasp with their teeth while a rigger’s back is turned. Old mechanics sometimes keep a half-tame vole in a fleece-lined breast pocket to warm their hands between shifts, feeding it crumbs of dry biscuit in exchange for the velvety fur shed in spring, which makes the finest wiping-cloths for goggles. The pale cliff-swifts, meanwhile, are the subject of a quiet, unspoken reverence. They are the only living things in the cleft that regularly rise up through the razor-thin ribbon of pale sky at the top of the shaft, vanishing into the lethal, roaring wind outside and returning hours later with dust from another world on their moth-pale wings. No one harms a swift; riggers leave pinches of suet on the warm hopper-rims where they nest, and it is considered good luck if a swift chooses the underside of your sleeping-locker to raise its blind, shivering chicks.

At the very bottom of the spire, hundreds of meters below the lowest amber bulb, the cleft pinches narrow enough that a tall man can touch both walls at once with his elbows. No machines run there; the rock is too restless, the space too tight. It is where the town sends its dead. Wrapped tightly in worn-out drive-belts and packed in dry mineral wool, the bodies do not rot in the bone-dry dark—they simply desiccate until they are light as paper, tucked into the lowest, narrowing V of the slate so that, over years, the young mountain slowly closes its stone jaws around them.

Up above, in the amber-lit dimness, the living keep moving—unbolting, winching, tightening turnbuckles, listening to the cables hum. They have forgotten what a horizon looks like, and the idea of an open plain, with nothing to hold onto and no wall within reach, fills them with a quiet, breathless vertigo. Home is thirty paces wide, held together with copper wire and felt, and forever shifting an inch at a time in the dark.
