Between the living world and the buried engine, there is no clean seam. Whatever survives on the plateau has learned to treat the machinery as geology: the superconducting lines are simply warm veins in the stone, the electrostatic plates are just another kind of bedrock, and the collector-sleds are slow, mineral-bearing weather.

Look closely at one of those sleds as it inches across the fog. Its wide, padded rollers are never bare. Years of crawling through the brine-pans have coated them in a living crust—a pale, rubbery mat of colonial bryozoans that feed on the crushed isopods and mineral-silt the rollers press into the slate. Tiny, copper-shelled mites swarm in the crevices of the sled’s chassis, drinking the faint acidic sweat that forms where damp air meets bare metal. Whenever a sled passes over a hairline trench, the vibration—too low for a human ear to catch—draws threads of blind, translucent nematodes up through the wet grit to graze on the grease of its axles.

Inside the low, frost-caked vent-slits, where the subterranean refineries breathe out their dry, invisible warmth, the stone is lined with a pale, felt-like fungus. It grows nowhere else on the plateau. It needs no light and no liquid water, only the steady convection of warm gas and the occasional stray moth-scale or mite swept inward by the draft. In the deepest winter, when even the brine-pans skin over with slush, the mouths of these vents are choked with sleeping velvet moths, packed so tightly around the fungal curtains that the warm air has to hiss softly through their wings to escape.

Out in the open boulder fields, where the herds pass in their dead-straight columns, something else waits.

Nothing here can afford to run. In air this thin and cold, a sprint burns too much heat and leaves the lungs raw. So the plateau’s largest predator hunts by becoming a stone. It is a wide, low-slung quadruped with a flattened skull and a pelt mottled in the exact ochre, verdigris, and soot-black of the crustose lichens. It lies in the shallow troughs between the ancient stone polygons, half-buried in the wet grit, its broad, pad-footed legs tucked tight beneath its ribs. Its metabolism slows until its breath barely clouds the air.

It does not wait for stragglers; it waits for the column itself. When ten thousand striders walk over a boulder field, they do not look down—they navigate by the magnetic pull beneath their feet, eyes half-shut against the mist. When a broad, fibrous ankle-pad settles within reach, the lichen-backed thing rises only an inch or two, clamps its silent, crushing jaws onto the tendon, and drops back into the grit. There is no cry of alarm. The strider has no voice to raise, and the herd does not break stride. As the column moves on into the fog, the injured beast is simply peeled away from the line, dragged down into the wet stones while the silent grey gliders above begin to tighten their spirals.

And then there are the people—though if you walked within fifty paces of them in the pewter light, you might take them for another outcrop of weathered slate.

They have no name for themselves that translates easily, because they rarely speak aloud. In this thin, dead air, a shout travels poorly and feels obscene; it draws too much moisture from the throat and disturbs the stillness that lets you hear the faint *tick* of contracting rock or the distant, muffled thud of ten thousand pads on wet stone. Inside their shelters, they sometimes use a dry, clicking whisper, little more than breath and tongue. Out on the open tableland, they speak with their hands held close to the chest, with the tilt of their shoulders, and with small, sharp touches—a knuckle pressed to a collarbone, a finger drawn across a wrist—reading one another’s intent in the dark fog the same way the striders do.

They are short, broad-chested, and heavy-boned, with dark, oil-rich skin that resists both frostbite and the salt-burn of the brine. Their lungs are vast, built to pull oxygen from air that would leave a lowlander dizzy on his knees. They wear no woven cloth; instead, they dress in thick, layered coats felted from the shed, oily guard-hairs of the striders, stitched together with tendon and lined on the inside with the pressed, velvety wings of thousands of moths. The coats are waterproof, windproof, and utterly silent. On their feet, they bind wide, splayed pads woven from wire-sedge and stuffed with dried moss, so their weight spreads across the wet slate without a sound.

They do not build above the horizon. To raise a wall on the plateau is to invite the wind and the frost. Instead, they live in sunken courts—ancient boulder-rings dug out by hand down to the warm bedrock just above the superconducting trunk lines. Over these shallow pits they stretch low, taut roofs of greased strider-hide, weighted around the edges with flat slabs of basalt until the whole dwelling sits flush with the ground. From a distance, a settlement is invisible: only a faint, damp depression where the rime frost refuses to settle, and a thin smell of rendered fat, ozone, and warm copper.

They are neither the masters of the buried engine nor its servants. If their ancestors built it, they have forgotten how, just as the plateau has forgotten how it was lifted. They treat the refinery the way they treat the herds: as a vast, indifferent partner.

They know the invisible map of the conduits by the soles of their feet. A child learns early to tell the difference between the dead, cold slate of the margins and the faint, high-tension hum of a trunk line twenty feet below. They warm their dwellings over the induction junctions, dry their moss-pads beside the vent-slits, and skim the grey brine-pans with fine-meshed nets of sedge, pressing the translucent isopods into dense, salty cakes that keep for years without spoiling.

They do not herd the striders; they braid themselves into the migration. A family will step quietly into the middle of a moving column, walking for days inside the warm, oily shelter of the beasts, shielded from the cold mist by ten thousand breathing flanks. As they walk, they pluck loose tufts of hair from the passing coats and watch for the old or the sick—the ones that slow, tremble, and finally lie down beside a warm vent-slit to freeze. When a strider drops, the dwellers step out of the column and wait with the patient, circling gliders, taking the hide, the fat, and the bone, leaving the salt-crusted marrow for the birds.

Even the collector-sleds have a place in their rhythm. Because the sleds move at the pace of a glacier and never stop, a traveler crossing the mist will sometimes walk beside one for half a day, scraping the thick, mineral-heavy bryozoan crust from its rollers with a bone knife. It keeps the rollers from jamming, and in return, the sled offers a flat, warm deck to rest a pack upon, or a slow, steady landmark moving true across a world without shadows.

When one of the dwellers dies, there is no wood to burn the body, and the shallow ground is too laced with conduits to dig a grave. The family carries the dead out to the edge of a warm seepage-basin, lays them flat on a slab of dark basalt, and unlaces their felt coat so the wind can reach the skin. Within an hour, the silent grey gliders drop out of the stratus without a sound. Within a week, the velvet moths and the copper-mites have finished what the birds left behind. The salt washes back into the pans, and the bones—bleached pale by the mist—are gathered to be carved into needles, sled-hooks, and combs for the strider-hair.

Everything on the plateau has reached its final angle. Nothing hurries, nothing cries out, and nothing is wasted between the frost and the hum beneath the stone.
