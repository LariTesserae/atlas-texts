If you stay in the basin long enough for the roar to stop feeling like an assault—long enough for your sternum to give up fighting the sub-bass hum and simply begin to vibrate at the same pitch—you start to notice that the emptiness is not quite empty.

At first, it looks like a trick of the glare. A hard black shadow beneath an undercut limestone rib seems to shift an inch to the left, though the sun has barely moved. A geometric lattice of ripples in one of the saucer-shaped pools suddenly breaks—its neat six-sided crystal pattern fracturing into concentric rings, then settling back into geometry—when no wind has gusted and no bird has dropped from the sky.

They live in the boundary layer, in the thin, relatively calm skin of air pressed directly against the stone.

To a traveler passing through for water or meat, they are indistinguishable from the basin itself. Their backs and shoulders are the color of scoured limestone—matte, chalk-pale, and finely crazed like old mineral crust—while their bellies and inner limbs are dark, absorbing whatever warmth the cold air allows. They have no hair to catch the laminar wind, only stiff, sparse bristles along the ridges of their brows and forearms that register the slightest eddy in the current. Their eyes are set deep beneath heavy, dark-rimmed sockets, veiled by a pale second lid that draws shut against the blinding white of the quartzite terraces, turning the world into tolerable shades of grey.

They have no outer ears. Where ears would be, the skin draws tight over smooth, dense plates of bone behind the jaw.

In a place where you cannot hear your own voice, airborne sound is useless, and the basin’s permanent shriek is merely weather to be ignored. Instead, they live inside the bedrock’s hum. Their sternums are broad, flat, and slightly hollow, acting like the sounding board of an instrument. When they rest, they do not sit or curl; they lie prone on the bare terraces or wedge themselves flat beneath the undercut ribs, pressing their chests flush against the stone. Through their ribs, their palms, and the thick, calloused pads of their heels, their bodies lock into the basin’s fundamental frequency. 

Once calibrated to that steady drone, the hum disappears into their own pulse. What remains is a miles-wide drumhead of terrifying clarity. A rodent’s claws scraping a tunnel three hundred yards away, a stone flake spalling off a ridge in the heat, the clumsy, off-beat crunch of a traveler’s boot coming over the western rim—each sends a tiny thread of dissonance rippling through the limestone, legible as a ripple on dark water.

They move low and unhurried, keeping three points of contact with the stone when crossing the open flats. They do not walk upright into the wind; they flow along the concave dishes and through the shadow-lines, moving most freely at dusk and under the stars, when the glare dies and the pale quartzite turns the color of ash.

Because nothing here can be built without the wind tearing it apart, they make nothing. They do not knap stone, weave fiber, or stack cairns. Their only tools are the basin’s own acoustics.

When they hunt the stout, deaf rodents that live beneath the limestone arches, they do not dig or set snares. One of them will climb to the ridge where the thinner stone flutes shriek, find a vent that connects to the honeycombed tunnels below, and press a palm or a flat shoulder against the opening. The pitch of the flute drops; a pulse of heavy, unfamiliar vibration shoots down into the dark maze. Seconds later, panicked rodents bolt from the shaded crevices below, driven out by the sudden chaos in their paws, straight into the waiting hands of others crouched motionless along the moss rims.

They eat the rodents raw or sun-cured on the windward ledges, along with the leathery cushions of moss, the silver-grey lichen scraped with a thumbnail from the leeward boulders, and the occasional pale bird that misjudges a downdraft and clips a stone rib. For water, they visit the shaded condensation pools—never splashing, never cupping their hands clumsily. They lower their faces to the exact center of a standing wave, where the geometric lattice is stillest, and drink without breaking the pattern.

Across the miles-wide saddle, they speak without making a sound that rises into the air. 

Sometimes it is through the rock: a sharp, deliberate tap of a knuckle or a heel against a limestone rib, sending a clean, rhythmic fracture through the sub-bass hum that others can read through their feet half a mile away. More often, they speak through the water. By wedging a smooth pebble into a ridge-flute or damping a vibrating overhang with the weight of a body, they subtly alter the frequency of a local stone shelf. Down on the flats, in the saucer-shaped depressions, the trembling crystal lattices rearrange themselves in response—tightening, loosening, shifting from squares to hexagons—carrying messages in light across the empty terraces.

They keep no fixed camps. They sleep wedged in the deepest, driest pockets of the undercut ribs, curled around one another to share heat, padded with dry moss lifted from the rodents’ chambers. Their young are born into the hum, learning the grammar of stone-taps and standing waves long before their eyes can bear the unfiltered sun. When one of them grows old and stiff, unable to brace against the steady pressure of the western rim, they simply crawl into a high windward crevice or lie out on an open terrace, where the thin, dry air strips them down to pale bone that the wind soon scours indistinguishable from the quartzite.

They know when travelers climb into the basin. They feel the foreign footsteps miles before the travelers reach the first water pocket. They do not confront them, and they do not flee. They simply slide into the hard, black shadows beneath the overhangs, press their chests to the humming rock, and wait. They know the basin will do the work for them. Within a day or two, the glare, the thin air, and the relentless wall of sound will drive the visitors back down below the timberline, leaving behind only a scatter of chipped stone and a forgotten sinew snare.

As for themselves, they could never go down. Down below, in the thick, wet air of the forests, the wind gusts without rhythm, the soft earth swallows every footstep, and the air goes dead and quiet between storms. To them, that silence would feel like suffocation—a world suddenly gone deaf, and blind, and terribly loose.
