If you stand on one of the great stone ribs long enough for your eyes to adjust to the dim, shadowless grey, you might still walk straight past them. They do not huddle against the cold, and they do not stir when you approach. From thirty paces away, one of them looks like nothing more than a weathered knob of the arch itself—the same pale, slate-colored surface, slicked with the same thin film of supercooled moisture.

Only when you come close enough to feel the mist soaking into your sleeves do you notice that the shape has knees, and that the knees are tucked beneath a broad, heavy torso.

They are roughly the size of a person, though built with a squat, low-slung solidity that makes a human frame look brittle by comparison. Their skin is entirely hairless, pale as wet chalk and dense as cured hide, with no visible pores for the cold to bite into. Over decades—or however long time takes to pass here—the valley’s constant dampness has given their backs and shoulders a faint, mineral sheen, so that water beads and sheets off them exactly as it does off the stone. Their feet are wide, flat-soled, and tough-heeled, gripping the slick rock without slipping, and their long forearms end in blunt, thick-nailed fingers worn smooth at the tips.

They have no external ears. Behind the hinge of the jaw, there is only a tight, unmoving seam of skin. In a place where the air is locked in a deafening, teeth-rattling roar, ears would be useless; instead, their skulls are thick and their ribcages are wide, rigid cages of bone that act like sounding boards. They listen through their heels, through their palms, and through the cartilage of their chests. When one of them shifts its weight on a stone bridge, it is reading the standing waves of the valley’s low iron chord—feeling where the rock is solid, where a hollow opens beneath, and where the air ahead has begun to fold.

Their eyes are small, sunken, and pale as milk-glass, usually kept half-shut. Sight is a poor tool in a basin where distances lie and reflections show the wrong sky, and they seem to have little use for it.

Mostly, they sit.

In the sheltered alcoves where the rock is worn into shallow bowls, they kneel or fold their legs beneath them and simply remain. They do not sleep, because there is no night to sleep through and no morning to wake for; instead, they let their bodies settle into the frequency of the stone until their breathing slows to a single, shallow draw every few minutes. No steam rises from their mouths. Their blood is thick and sluggish, running nearly as cold as the mist itself, so they burn almost nothing. A visitor watching one for an hour might conclude it is a statue, or a corpse preserved by the altitude, until a slow, deliberate blink crosses the milky eyes, or the blunt fingers shift half an inch along the polished stone.

That is how the alcoves were worn smooth—not by a single lifetime of sitting, but by stillness stretched across centuries.

When they do move, it is without haste or rhythm, as if they are only changing posture between one long thought and the next. They creep out onto the undersides of the stone arches, clinging with broad palms and flat feet to harvest the pale, rubbery lichen that grows in the dampest corners. They peel it away in strips and chew it slowly, or snap the translucent, hollow stalks at the base to drink the cold, mineral-heavy condensation trapped inside. Occasionally, when one of the four-winged moths drifts too close—suspended in mid-air by the valley’s acoustic pressure—a pale hand will rise with startling, sudden precision and close around it. They eat the moth whole, wings and all, with a dry crunch that vanishes instantly beneath the static hiss of the basin.

Now and then, you will see one walking the length of a stone rib, carrying three or four river-smooth stones cradled against its chest.

They are the ones who build the cairns. They do not place them to mark trails—trails here have a habit of looping back into themselves—nor as monuments. They build them wherever the standing wave of the roar feels uneasy, where two competing vibrations cross and the space between one pace and the next threatens to stretch or double. Kneeling on the slick rock, they balance one smooth stone atop another, then a third, then a fourth, with agonizing patience. In a valley where nothing loosens and nothing falls, a balanced cairn is a quiet test of gravity: if the top stone sits true without sliding toward an invisible incline, then *here*, at least, the rock agrees with itself.

When the distance folds and brings them face-to-face with a cairn they built twenty paces back, they show no confusion. They simply pause, rest one blunt fingertip against the uppermost stone to feel its steadiness, and walk on.

There are not many of them—perhaps a dozen, perhaps several dozen, though it is impossible to count anything in a place where two stone arches can pass cleanly through one another without touching. They do not speak, and they have no names. When two of them meet on a narrow bridge, they stop a pace apart, then lean forward until their foreheads or the flat heels of their palms meet. For several minutes they stand locked like that, letting the hum of their ribcages pass through bone and skin—trading whatever small shifts they have felt in the valley’s chord, or simply confirming that the other is solid.

Even the flat, chitinous creatures that creep through the crevices seem to recognize them as part of the stone. Sometimes, while one of the inhabitants kneels in an alcove, a hound-sized thing will slide out of the mist and press its slate-colored plates against the inhabitant’s calf, its fleshy flank-filaments trembling in time with the slow, resonant thrum of their ribs. Neither moves away.

The only time their posture changes into something like attention is beside the tilted pools.

In the shallow basins where dark water leans at twenty degrees against no visible slope, you may find one of them kneeling at the edge, leaning far forward with their palms pressed flat to the damp rock. Their half-shut, milky eyes open wide over the glassy surface. They do not drink from these pools, and they do not touch them. They simply stare down into the reflection of a sky crowded with too many stars, or lit by the bleached smear of a sun that isn’t there—watching it with the same patient, unblinking stillness they give to everything else, as if waiting to see whether something on the other side of the water might one day lean down to look back.
