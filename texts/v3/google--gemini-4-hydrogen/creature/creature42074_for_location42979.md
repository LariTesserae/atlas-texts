At first, even in a room with no shadows, you would not see them. 

The white-green glare flattens every surface into a single plane of brightness, and the creatures that live here have learned the oldest trick of a shadowless world: they do not hide from the light, they match it. Along their narrow spines and flat shoulders, the same chalky biofilm that coats the walls has taken root, radiating the exact clinical luminescence of the stone. Pressed against a travertine nodule, one of them is simply another glowing bulge of mineral. It is only when a patch of the wall detaches itself from a calcite blade—gliding an inch to the left with the unhurried precision of a clock hand—that the eye catches the seam between rock and flesh.

There are three of them, never more and never fewer.

Each is no larger than a human hand splayed flat, with a slender, reed-like body and a long tail that tapers into a pale, calloused hook. Their skin is slick and translucent, the color of wet gypsum, stretched taut over delicate ribs that show faintly beneath the surface like the veins in frosted glass. Where eyes should be, the skin is seamless and unbroken over a smooth, wedge-shaped skull. In a chamber of relentless, unblinking noon, sight is a useless expense; instead, a pale, crystalline spot at the crown of the head drinks in the ambient glare, keeping their slow metabolisms idling at a fixed, cool hum.

To move through a lantern packed with razor-edged teeth requires a body built for geometry. Their four limbs are long and thinly jointed, ending in three opposable digits whose tips have hardened into translucent, calcified horn. They never grip the sharp edges of the crystals. Instead, they place their glassy fingertips flat against the slick, damp facets, bridging the gaps between gypsum needles with the careful, weightless balance of spiders walking on oil. 

They do not hear the room’s deafening roar as noise. To them, the standing chord is the medium they inhabit, as water is to a trout.

Beneath the jaw and along the sternum of each creature lies a hollow, cartilaginous pouch—a biological sounding-box tuned to the exact metallic frequency of the pocket. As the thousands of blind amphipods graze and the nymphs’ ribbed abdomens hum, the creatures’ own chests vibrate in quiet sympathy with the stone. They navigate by the architecture of the sound. When an aging millipede loses its footing on a damp rosette, or a nymph fails to molt cleanly and its tiny scraping rhythm falters, the standing chord ripples by a fraction of a hertz. The creature feels that microscopic dissonance in its ribs before the dying insect has even fallen, turns its eyeless head toward the disturbance, and glides across the lattice.

They are not hunters; a chase would scatter the swarms, snap the crystal needles, and sour the room’s equilibrium. They are gleaners. With wide, underslung mouths lined with fine, comb-like ridges rather than teeth, they pluck only the sluggish, the mis-molted, and the newly dead from the moving skin of chitin, leaving the healthy lattice undisturbed. Between circuits, they dip their chins into the thimble-sized cups of condensation to wet their throats without spilling a drop, or gently pare away ribbons of overgrown biofilm so the light never dims or pools.

Even their spacing is governed by the chord. Each keeps to its own shifting third of the pantry. If two drift too close, the vibration of their throat-pouches falls slightly out of phase, creating a faint, physical flutter in the air between them; without ever touching, they turn away and settle back into their separate nodes of stillness.

Long before the last fissure pinched shut, something else passed through this pocket and left behind its small, patient arrangements. The three creatures did not build them, yet they have folded the relics into the clockwork of their lives. Every few years, they descend to the soft alabaster vein along the low wall and draw their ever-growing, horn-tipped digits down the shallow, parallel grooves, honing them to a precise bevel along paths scratched ages ago. They rub the slick undersides of their tails against the three finger-tall cairns on the floor, leaving a faint, bitter mineral oil that keeps the grazing swarms off the pebbles.

And high in the dry crevice, the half-dozen cup-shaped nests of gray mud and dried saliva serve a rarer purpose. Time here is measured not in seasons, which do not exist, but in the slow accumulation of mineral. Over decades, a creature’s joints stiffen with calcified salt, and its sounding-box begins to fall flat against the room’s note. When it feels its resonance failing, it climbs to the high crevice, scrapes a nest-rim clean of biofilm, and lays a single, thumb-sized, translucent egg in the dry cup.

Then it drops to the flattest patch of floor and grows still. The blind swarms strip it cleanly to pale bone, which the damp, mineral-heavy sheen slowly coats and cements into the stone, indistinguishable from another nodule of travertine. Above, in the mud-and-saliva cradle, the egg splits open, and a blind, slick hatchling crawls out onto the crystal teeth—tuning its soft throat to the metallic hum, slipping into the empty third of the room, and keeping the single, unvarying note unbroken in the dark beneath the earth.
