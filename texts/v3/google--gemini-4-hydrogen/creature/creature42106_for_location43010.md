At first, the basin seems to belong entirely to the flora. You can walk for hours—your boots sinking into the spongy bryophyte with a muffled, wet *thump*—and see nothing that is not root, column, vat, or vine.

But an equilibrium this vast leaves margins. And in those margins, something else has learned to live without disturbing the load.

You would not notice them by sight. Even when a swollen transformer node gives its tiny, muted pinprick of blue-white static, the flash is too brief and too weak to separate one tallow-pale shape from another. In that split second of cold light, a thing hanging beneath a braided conduit looks like nothing more than a swollen resinous gall, or a cluster of fused, waxy leaves. 

You notice them first by the channels.

If you kneel beside one of the dark, mirror-still waterways that wind through the basin floor and reach your hand beneath the surface—through the lukewarm, mineral-heavy brine—your fingers will not strike the razor-edged basalt right away. Instead, they will find a bed of smooth, heavy, thumb-sized pellets. They are cool, dense, and utterly unweathered: nodules of pure, unoxidized copper and iron, resting in the dark like river-stones in a world that has never known a river.

Something is dropping them there.

It takes patience—standing still in the warm, mineral-sweet dark until your own breathing feels too loud—to catch the faintest deviation in the forest’s rhythm. A transformer node above your head, humming with high-amperage direct current, will drop its pitch by a fraction of a tone. Not a break in the circuit, just a slight, sudden *draw*, as if a new branch had quietly come online.

If you reach up toward that node, your hand will meet skin.

It is not bark, and it is not leaf. It is thick, smooth, and slick with a hydrophobic wax that beads the ambient moisture just as the foliage does, keeping the surface dry enough to prevent a short-circuit. Beneath that tallow-pale hide, the body is soft, heavy, and segmented into deep, horizontal pleats like the bellows of an accordion. 

They have no heads, no eyes, and no mouths. In a world with no light and no rot, none of those would serve. Where a head might be, the top of the pleated torso narrows into a crown of fine, wet, copper-tipped filaments that sway infinitesimally in the dead air, tasting the mineral-sweet vapor and reading the faintest shifts in the surrounding electric fields. 

They are living bridges. 

For months, sometimes years at a time, one of these dwellers will hang motionless in the middle canopy, indistinguishable from the machinery. Its long, multi-jointed limbs—thin as the braided tendon-vines, sliding on internal sheaths of mucilage—thread carefully between the live conduits. One limb anchors itself into the wet, mineral-rich basalt or dips into a mirror-still brine channel below; another presses a broad, pale, pad-like organ directly against the translucent casing of a transformer node, or slips a fine probe into the seam of a peristaltic sap-pump.

Once connected, it becomes part of the circuit. 

A steady, controlled trickle of direct current flows through its pleated body. To keep from cooking in the amperage, it regulates its own electrical resistance hydraulically—pumping dark, electrolytic fluid in and out of chambers along its limbs, matching the forest’s own osmotic engineering. Inside its translucent torso, powered by the stolen current, it runs a quiet, continuous electrolysis: splitting the heavy-metal brines it draws up from the channels, binding the sulfur and carbon into its tissues, and precipitating the excess iron and copper into dense, neat nodules that it eventually releases back into the water below.

It has no reason to hurry. Nothing hunts it; nothing flees from it. When a basalt fissure shifts by a millimeter, or a fluted trunk thickens enough to alter the tension on a vine, the creature simply adjusts. 

Its limbs detach from the bark with a sound no louder than a wet valve-leaf seating itself—*shhhk*—and it swings its pleated weight across the dark gap in slow, hydraulic arcs, its flat, rubbery foot-pads finding purchase on the mossy substrate without making a ripple in the air. If you didn’t know what to listen for, you would swear it was only the forest settling into its own weight.

They are not entirely solitary, though they never touch. Miles apart in the pitch dark, two of them tapped into the same braided trunk-line will occasionally introduce tiny, rhythmic fluctuations in their draw—modulating the amperage in slow, deliberate pulses that travel silently through leagues of copper-plated column and resinous node. It is a conversation conducted entirely in load and resistance, so faint that the forest’s own valves never register it.

If you stand near one long enough, its copper-tipped crown will tilt slowly toward you. It cannot see you, and it has no interest in your flesh. But in a basin of absolute, steady-state equilibrium, the erratic, chemical crackle of a human nervous system—and the warm, frantic thump of a heart—is a tiny, noisy storm. For a long moment, the filaments will hover an inch from your skin, tasting the salt of your sweat and reading the strange, useless static of your body. 

Then, finding no current worth drawing, it will turn back to the humming vine, settle into its own waxy folds, and return to the work of being still.
