At first, the chamber seems to have already shown you everything it holds: the hexagonal plate-backs shuddering on the iron housings, the blind bellows-hangers breathing in curtains from the ceiling, the braided knots of ribbon-crawlers, the brittle wire-scrub leaning toward the copper hum. In a room this loud and this tightly packed, the eye settles on what moves and what gleams, and assumes the rest is only dust, stone, and machinery.

Nothing here, however, is merely stone or machinery. Where every cubic meter is spoken for, life has had to colonize the margins—the air currents, the mechanical tolerances, and the narrow, shifting corridors of space that open and close with the beat of the engines.

In the dense, stratified bands of dust suspended mid-air, there lives a population of things no larger than fingernails, so light and dry they never touch the floor. They are pale, spoked, and radially symmetrical, like tiny snowflakes spun from shed silica and translucent chitin. They do not fly; they ride the room’s rhythmic convection, drifting endlessly along the invisible shelves of warm air. As they float, they trail fine, hair-thin filaments charged with static, combing the metallic chaff of the wire-scrub out of the air before it can thicken and foul the turbine flywheels. When a filament grows heavy with dark, conductive dust, the drifter reels it in, compacts the metal into a neat, dry pellet, and lets it drop—a tiny, precise rain that falls straight through the lower air into the waiting knots of the ribbon-crawlers below.

Closer to the noise, inside the cast-iron housings themselves, live the things that keep the engines from dying. If you could pry open the dry-lubricated sleeves of the reciprocating pistons, you would find them lined with thousands of flat, eyeless, graphite-colored organisms shaped like overlapping scales. They thrive in the brutal, rhythmic friction, grazing on the microscopic iron filings worn away by the slamming metal and excreting a fine, dry, velvety powder of carbon and waxy wax-chitin. They are the reason the pistons *clack* sixty times a minute without ever seizing, and why no rusted-out hulks litter the floor. The machines are not immortal; they are simply inhabited by their own lubrication.

And then there are the ones who walk the room.

At first, in the bruised amber light, you might mistake them for stanchions, or vertical bundles of conduit bolted between the floor and the overhangs. There are only seven of them—or perhaps nine, for they never gather in one place—and they are built to occupy the narrowest possible column of space. Tall, thin, and sharply articulated, they stand on long, reverse-jointed legs that end in split, three-toed pads of hardened anhydrite. Their bodies are sheathed in narrow, overlapping slats of pale gypsum and dark, oil-sheened chitin, bound together by taut bundles of matte-black fiber indistinguishable from the stems of the wire-scrub.

They have no faces, and no eyes. Where a head would be, each carries a tall, narrow, slotted crest—a hollow acoustic cowl of fused mineral and parchment-thin membrane. They do not see the amber bulbs or the curtains of bellows-hangers; they read the chamber through the standing waves of its own noise. In a room where sound never dissipates, every overhang, every braided knot of ribbon-crawlers, and every vibrating plate-back bends the turbine howl and the piston-shock into a shifting architecture of pressure. The tall ones navigate by the nodes and antinodes of that pressure, feeling the exact shape of a crowded ledge by the way the brass valve’s hiss echoes back into their crests.

Because the room is packed shoulder-to-shoulder, they cannot move continuously. Instead, they step in exact lockstep with the sixty-beats-per-minute *clack* of the pistons. On the beat, when the ceiling curtains contract and the ribbon-crawlers tighten their knots, a tall one will lift a jointed leg, slide sideways through a gap no wider than a handspan, and plant its foot before the air swells back. Their motion is neither hurried nor mechanical; it is the patient, rhythmic choreography of something that has never known an empty room.

They are the chamber’s tuners, though they do not know the word. Watch one pause beside a cast-iron housing: its long, multi-jointed arms unfold from its ribs, ending in delicate, three-pronged tips like drafting calipers. With surgical stillness, it reaches between two shuddering plate-backs and plucks out a single grain of loose salt that was beginning to dampen their high-pitched stridulation. It tucks the salt into a pouch beneath its sternum. A moment later, it leans toward the hissing brass pressure valve, where a pale crust of pulverized gypsum has begun to narrow the reed. Timed to the next *clack* of the piston, a chitinous blade-tip flicks the crust away. The hiss snaps back to its exact, harsh pitch.

To keep moving, they drink from the sharpest edges of the room. Now and then, one will tilt its slotted crest toward a jagged overhang where the dusty violet sheen of static discharge is thickest. Tiny blue-white sparks leap across the gap, threading into the fine, copper-dark bristles along the rim of its cowl. Its joints give a dry, satisfied tick as the charge settles into its fibers, and then it steps back into the amber dimness.

When two of them meet in the narrow aisles between the conduits, they do not speak—there is no room in the air for another voice. Instead, they stand chest to chest and touch the rims of their slotted crests together for three beats of the piston, letting their bodies share the same vibration, checking that neither has drifted out of pitch with the turbines or the floor. They have no names, no memories of an overburden above them, and no sense of time passing, because nothing here passes. They are simply the chamber’s equilibrium given legs and calipers—the quiet, clicking intelligence that keeps the closed circuit closed, so that the roaring, dry, sunlit-less cathedral can go on humming in place forever.
