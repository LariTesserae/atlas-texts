You can stand on a catwalk for an hour before you realize you are being watched—or, more accurately, felt.

From a distance, the people who live in the basin are nearly indistinguishable from its hardware. They dress in the palette of the terraces: heavy canvas aprons stiffened with pale lime-wash, oiled wool coats the color of wet iron, and wide-brimmed hats of waxed leather that shed the lukewarm mist. They move so slowly that the eye slides right past them, mistaking a figure leaning over a copper manifold for a valve-housing or a weathered bollard. Only when a broom sweeps a thin crust of ochre silt off an iron grating, or a gloved hand reaches out to nudge a sluice-wheel a quarter of a turn, does the silhouette resolve into a person.

They are few—perhaps only two or three hundred scattered across the numbered sectors—and they prefer the distance. Each cluster of pump-houses and settling pans has its own acoustic footprint, a particular blend of turbine-thrum and diffuser-pitch that a tender learns the way a lowland farmer learns the slope of a field. To crowd too many people into one sector is to muddy the floor with stray footsteps.

Life here begins with a surrender of the ears.

No one born in the valley keeps their air-hearing past childhood. The relentless, rib-pressing roar of the diffusers and the hours-long hiss of the vapor stacks wear the eardrums down until airborne sound—a shouted warning, the snap of a cable, the wind along the upper ridges—becomes a distant, muffled static, if it is heard at all. In its place, the basin-dwellers develop an extraordinary, whole-body literacy in vibration.

They wear boots with thin, split-leather soles shod in flat plates of brass at the heel and ball, keeping the foot coupled directly to the stone and iron. Even more essential is the sounding-rod: a six-foot staff of seasoned ash tipped with a blunt copper ferrule. No tender walks the catwalks without one. By planting the copper tip against a girder, a porcelain insulator’s bracket, or the rim of a settling pan, and resting their thumb against the ash shaft, they can read the health of a machine three hundred yards away. The tremor travels up the wrist, through the collarbone, and into the jawbone. A clean, steady hum tastes faintly of salt on the back of the tongue; a dry bearing chatters against the molars like grit.

Because they cannot speak over the roar, their language is split between distance and touch. Across the wide, sun-bleached stretches of wet rock, they use a broad, unhurried sign—tall, sweeping arcs of the arms and flat-palmed gestures meant to be legible through rising mist at half a kilometer. Up close, they rarely bother with their hands. Two tenders meeting on a narrow viaduct will simply press the tips of their sounding-rods together, or lay a palm flat against the other’s shoulder blade, sharing a few seconds of stillness before moving on.

When they need to summon a neighbor across sectors, they use the city itself. A series of slow, deliberate raps with a brass wrench on a main steam-conduit carries for miles through the bolted ironwork. Better still, a tender can lean into a diffuser’s baffle-gate and briefly alter its aperture, sending a recognizable stutter—a momentary collapse and reformation—rippling across the geometric lattices of the ankle-deep settling pans. Anyone watching the water in Sector Four knows immediately that Sector Two is asking for spare packing-grease, or that a storm is building on the rim and the upper sluices need watching.

Their bodies are shaped by the mineral damp as much as by the sound. Their skin is pale, tough, and kept slick with lanolin and light machine oil to keep the warm brine from cracking their knuckles and chapping their lips. Where they neglect to oil, a fine, chalky bloom of lime gathers in the creases of their elbows and behind their knees. Their hair is shorn short or bound tight under oiled caps, both to keep it out of slow-turning gears and because wet hair wicks the lukewarm air into a permanent chill.

Their work is not labor in the frantic, lowland sense; it is a kind of patient grooming. The turbines turn at two or three revolutions a minute, and the tenders match their pace. They spend their days scraping pale scale from the throats of the perforated copper diffusers before it can choke the standing waves, tightening gland-nuts a fraction of a turn, and skimming the fine, metallic silts that the sound coaxes out of the brine. They press this silt into dense, heavy cakes, which are stacked on the high viaducts to wait for the quarterly cable-car that descends from the basin’s rim—the city’s only thread to the dry world above, bringing down bolts of wool, barrels of clear oil, and salt-free grain in exchange for the mineral harvest.

When the sun drops behind the western rim and the high-altitude light turns flat and violet, the tenders retreat into the acoustic shadows.

They do not live in the iron-and-steel structures of the industrial age, which ring like bells through the night. Instead, they hollow their dwellings out of the oldest layer of the city: the cyclopean travertine buttresses and thick-walled masonry vaults that predate the power lines. Here, behind six feet of porous stone, the standing waves break and scatter. The rooms are small, dry, and strangely plush, lined with sealed slate and hung with heavy, oiled-felt curtains that drink up the residual hum. They sleep in canvas cots slung by hemp ropes from damped iron springs, hovering a foot above the floor so the deep, subterranean thrum of the hydraulic rams cannot rattle their ribs while they dream.

Yet even in these quiet pockets, they are never truly alone with silence. On every kitchen table sits a shallow, wide-rimmed bronze bowl filled with brine from the nearest pan. As the city breathes outside, the water in the bowl holds a miniature, trembling lattice of ridges—a living clock and barometer. If the ridges tighten into sharp, nervous diamonds, a tender knows a pressure-relief stack has jammed somewhere down-valley, and pulls on their boots without needing to be told.

Alongside them, in the warm, baffled cisterns tucked beneath the buttresses, live the only creatures they keep: pale, sightless, flat-bodied amphibians bred from the wild stock that clings to the undersides of the settling pans. Too sluggish to flee and deaf to the roar, they graze on the leathery olive-black moss that the tenders cultivate on submerged stone slabs. They are eerie, translucent things—you can see the slow, mineral-tinted pulse of their hearts through their bellies—and they provide the basin-dwellers with a bland, fatty meat and a clear oil prized for waterproofing boots.

The tenders rarely leave the valley. On the rare occasions when one must ride the cable-car up to the rim to negotiate with the lowland factors, the experience is quietly terrifying. Up there, where the air is dry and thin and the wind makes a thin, whistling noise through the scrub, the absence of the city’s roar feels like a sudden loss of gravity. Without the steady pressure against their chest and the hum beneath their heels, they grow dizzy, breathless, and profoundly lonely, as if the world above the rim has died and gone hollow. They finish their business as quickly as courtesy allows and ride back down into the mist, visibly relaxing the moment the brass plates of their boots touch the trembling travertine again.

In a place where a counterweight takes an afternoon to fall and a trickle of water bridges a lip over a season, people do not age with urgency. Their tempers are slow to rise and slow to cool; their griefs are quiet, heavy things, worn down gradually like stone under a steady drip. When a tender dies, there is no dry earth to dig and no wood to spare for a pyre. Their tools and brass-shod boots are passed to an apprentice, and their body is carried down into the deep, unlit fissures beneath the oldest terraces, where the warm, mineral-rich water weeps through the dark. Laid out on a shelf of limestone, over the course of decades, the city slowly encases them in pale, shelflike deposits of lime and ochre—folding them, layer by patient layer, back into the floor that holds the hum.
