If you stand still long enough for the roar of wings and frogs to become a kind of weather, you begin to notice that some of the clay pillars are breathing.

They are easy to miss at first. In the gray-green gloom, a crouched figure pressed against a buttress looks like nothing more than a slumped repair—a rounded boss of damp earth, furred in velvety lichen and dusted with pale spore-ash. Only when a swiftlet whips past within an inch of it, and the lump slowly shifts its weight to let a heavy drop of condensation fall onto its upturned palm, do you realize it is alive.

They are the tenders of the equilibrium, though they have no word for themselves, and no use for words at all. In a place where the air is a solid, vibrating wall of clicks, rattles, and frog-song, speech would be swallowed before it left the throat. 

Instead, they are built to listen with their bones.

They are heavy, low-centered creatures, roughly the size of a crouched human, with broad, slightly humped backs and long, flat-boned forearms. Their heads are blunt and wedge-shaped, completely lacking external ears; behind the hinge of the jaw lie only narrow, muscular slits that can be pinched shut against the din. Their eyes are small, dark, and deeply recessed beneath thick brow-ridges that shield them from falling grit and dripping water. In the near-darkness of the lower galleries, sight is good for little more than telling the pale, dusty shaft of a ventilation chimney from the black mouth of a tunnel. 

To know where they are, and what the city is doing, they press their heavy lower jaws or the flats of their broad chests against the nearest wall. Through the continuous, fused skeleton of clay and root, the deafening noise of the air becomes something clean and legible: a low, steady hum. A healthy pillar rings with a dull, dense weight. A wall that has thinned under centuries of condensation hums a fraction too high. A fig root slowly splitting an upper vault sends a dry, rhythmic creak down through the buttresses, long before a single flake of mud falls to the floor.

Their coats are dense, short, and coarse, oiled by glands along the spine so that moisture beads and runs off rather than soaking in. Over decades, the grit of the walls and the floating spores of the fungus gardens work their way into that oily pelt, until an older tender carries a living crust of pale moss and tiny, thread-rooted ferns across its shoulders. Blind crickets and millipedes stream across their broad, calloused feet without breaking stride; tree frogs cling motionless to their cool flanks, mistaking them for the damp undersides of arches.

They carry no tools. Everything they do is done with what their bodies make and bear. Their hands end in four stubby, immensely strong fingers tipped with broad, squared-off nails like horn chisels, worn smooth at the edges from generations of scraping and patting. Under their wide tongues sit swollen glands that produce a thick, mineral-rich saliva, faintly sweet and quick to set when exposed to the warm interior air.

Their days—if you can call them days, in a place where the only clock is the slight cooling of the mist at the chimney mouths—are spent at the pace of the drips. 

You will find them in the deep, kiln-warm fermentation chambers, where the air smells richly of wet rot and yeast. There, they gather the city’s leavings: fallen swiftlet chicks, shed snake skins, the husks of dead beetles, and the sodden leaf-litter that filters down through the living roof. With their blunt teeth, they chew tough vine-stems and old comb into a wet mash, spitting it into neat, steaming beds where the white mycelium thrives. They do not hunt the bats or the frogs, and the snakes in the hanging moss ignore them; they are the city’s composters, turning the frantic waste of a million short lives into steady heat and soft, edible caps.

When a wall needs mending, they do not hurry. A tender will squat beside one of the dark, mineral-rimmed catch-basins, scooping up handfuls of fine silt and kneading it together with chewed wood-pulp and its own glutinous spit. Moving with a slow, rolling gait—always keeping one palm or shoulder brushed against the clay—it climbs the worn terraces to the scar. With long, patient sweeps of its broad thumb-nail and the heel of its hand, it plasters the new layer over the old, smoothing the seam until no sharp edge remains for the drafts to catch. A single patch may take three days to lay and another week to cure.

They live in loose, quiet kin-groups that span three or four generations, tucked into the warm alcoves beneath the oldest fig roots. Because nothing in the city is written or spoken, their memory is entirely architectural. An elder does not tell stories; it leads a young one through the roaring dark, takes its broad hand, and presses the young one’s palm against a particular hollow in a buttress. Together, they stand motionless, eyes half-shut, feeling the slow, deep vibration of a repair made a century ago, or the faint, wet percussion of a catch-basin that has never gone dry. When they need to call to one another across the galleries, they do not shout. They strike the hollow clay walls with the heels of their hands—slow, deliberate thuds that travel *under* the din, rippling through the floors and pillars like stones dropped into a deep well.

Because they move so slowly, and because the air inside the city never freezes or dries out, they live for a very long time. Only when the moss on their shoulders has grown thick and heavy, and their horn nails have worn down to the quick, do they leave the warm lower chambers for the last time.

They do not die in the open, where the beetles would strip them in a night. When a tender feels its joints stiffening toward stillness, it climbs to the outer edges of the plateau, where the ancient arches sag under the weight of the mountain clouds, or seeks out a hollow, crumbling pillar that has begun to hum too high. It wedges itself deep into the gap, curling its heavy limbs inward. Its kin follow, carrying mouthfuls of chewed pulp and wet silt from the basins. Silently, patiently, they plaster warm clay over the elder’s back, over its moss-crusted shoulders, over its blunt, earless head, sealing it into the wall while its chest is still rising and falling in slow, rhythmic breaths.

Within a season, the clay dries and fuses with the older work. The warm body inside feeds one last, quiet bloom of fungus before settling into bone—and the wall, braced from within, hums deep and true again, holding up the roof for another hundred years.
