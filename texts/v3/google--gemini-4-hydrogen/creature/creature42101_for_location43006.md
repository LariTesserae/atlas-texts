To look at the wide plazas in that flat, shadowless light is to see only the middle of the machine. The flyers, the shell-bearers, and the blind white scavengers are merely the creatures that live in the open air, where the mist is thin enough to see through. Every other surface of the basin—the dark throats of the drains, the roaring bellies of the concrete blocks, the high, humming web of iron overhead—is just as crowded, and just as tightly bound to the rhythm of the pistons.

Nothing here is wasted, and nothing lives apart from the circuit.

Even the creatures in the plazas have hidden halves to their lives. The translucent flyers that wheel in their endless figure-eights above the flagstones never touch down to nest; instead, they shed microscopic, glassy cysts into the thermal updrafts as they pass over the vents. Most are swept away into the cool mist and dissolve on the slick stone, where the scavengers sweep them up as oily grit. A precise, unchanging fraction are caught in the warm eddies behind the smokestacks, where they swell in the steam, split open, and unfurl four damp, papery wings before they ever lose altitude. The shell-bearers on the walls, too, are bound to the stone until the exact moment they fail: when an old grazer’s broad muscular foot finally loses its grip on the wet concrete, it drops with a dry *clack* onto the pavement, and the porcelain-white scavengers have it stripped to a hollow dome before the next unison shudder ripples across the masonry.

Follow the lukewarm runoff from those flagstones, past the rounded curbstones and into the straight, hand-cut drainage channels, and you find the next layer waiting just beneath the iron grates.

The grates never clog, even though softened carapaces, shed wings, and mineral grit wash into them without pause. Wedged into the dark stone throats directly below the bars are the siphoners—pale, foot-long sacs of corrugated flesh, anchored to the wet mortar by rings of small, chitinous hooks. They have no heads and no limbs, only wide, pleated mouths pressed upward against the underside of the iron, drinking the mineral-heavy trickle as it falls. Their rubbery hides are marbled with dark, rust-colored veins where the iron-rich water circulates through them. As they strain out the dissolved chitin and the sheen of machine oil, they exude a clear, slick mucus that coats the stone culverts, keeping the pipes from silting shut with lime. Every few hours, a siphoner swells with a dense, chalky pellet of compacted slag and indigestible grit, and ejects it deeper into the dark cisterns beneath the basin, where the heavy sediment settles out before the water is drawn back up to the boilers.

Behind the slatted vents of the windowless buildings, the climate changes abruptly, and the life changes with it.

Inside, there is no cool mist—only a dry, blistering heat, thick with atomized oil and the sharp, metallic tang of hot copper. Here, in the deafening dark beside the whining dynamos and the hypnotic, spinning flywheels, live things that would stiffen and die in the clammy air of the plazas. Clinging to the stationary iron housings, a hair’s breadth from the blur of the shafts, are flat, hexagonal plates of tarnished bronze chitin no larger than a fingernail. At a glance they look like loose washers or flakes of scale, until you notice them creeping in slow, deliberate spirals around the bearings on dozens of tiny, hooked legs tucked beneath their rims. They feed on the black, gummy varnish that forms when hot oil breaks down, and on the fine carbon dust shed by the dynamo brushes. By scraping the housings clean, they keep the tolerances exact; the engines never seize because the animals eat the friction away. Below them, in the warm, churned emulsions of oil and water pooling beneath the reciprocating pistons, blind, amber-skinned worms no thicker than pencils coil and uncoil, breathing through pores along their flanks and keeping the sumps clear of sludge.

Above the plazas, far from the damp stone and the hot engine rooms, the sparse web of steel catwalks and insulated power conduits appears empty. In the flat, grey light, it is easy to mistake the shapes hanging beneath the walkways for transformer boxes or lumps of weathered concrete.

They are neither. They are the largest things in the basin, and the slowest.

Roughly the size of a crouched hound, they are built entirely for hanging, with four long, jointed limbs ending in heavy, twin-pronged hooks of dark chitin—curved to the exact thickness of the catwalk railings and ladder rungs. Their bodies are encased in thick, slate-grey flesh, ridged along the spine with overlapping plates of dull, porous shell that catch the rising mist and look like damp mortar. They never walk on top of the catwalks; they move exclusively underneath them, swinging hand-over-hand with a greased, unhurried precision that matches the pace of the flywheels below.

They have no eyes. The front of the head is a smooth, blunt wedge of grey flesh, pitted with tiny sensory dimples that read the faint warmth of the arc-lamps and the sixty-cycle hum of the power lines. Along their shoulders and ribs, they carry tufts of pale, branching cartilage—damp, translucent filaments that look like exposed nerves. When one of these creatures reaches a tall ceramic insulator, it hooks its hind limbs around the steel bracket, lets its torso hang down into the zone of crackling blue-white static, and unfurls those damp filaments.

The static charges the tufts, raising them into a pale, trembling halo. The ozone keeps the creature’s damp hide clean of rot, while the hum of the arc-lamps and the crackle of the insulators draw the highest-flying flyers out of the plaza updrafts. When a flyer’s papery wings brush the charged filaments, it sticks fast and goes limp from the shock. The hanger does not rush. Every few minutes, it curls a forelimb back, combs the stunned flyers off its shoulder-tufts, and presses them into a wide, lipless mouth lined with flat, grinding plates of shell.

Between feedings, they hang motionless against the humming transformer casings, using the serrated edges of their chitinous hooks to scrape away the white mineral crust that forms around the rivets, licking the salty, iron-rich dust from their claws. When one finally grows too old to hold its grip, it simply lets go, dropping fifty feet onto the wet flagstones with a heavy, wet *thud*. Within minutes, the porcelain-white scavengers swarm the carcass, stripping the grey flesh and porous shell down to the heavy chitin hooks, which the mineral mist softens and the drains swallow.

From the bottom of the cisterns to the top of the insulators, there is no spare room and no idle creature. The basin does not merely tolerate its inhabitants; it runs on them, an immense, deafening clockwork of wet iron, boiled stone, flesh, shell, and chitin, turning over at the exact same speed forever.
