At first, you think the scavengers in the rafters are all there is. You watch a pale, long-legged grazer pick its way across a row of rivets, or catch the silhouette of a bladder-winged glider banking silently through a hollow foundry, and you assume the city is an abandoned engine—left running by something that packed up and left a thousand years ago.

It takes an hour of standing still in the pewter gloom to realize you are being watched—not by eyes, and not from the shadows, but from the *architecture itself*.

Start with the water. Those wide aprons of ochre and pale verdigris staining the basalt aren’t just mineral deposits. If you crouch by a drainage channel and press a boot-tip against the edge of a yellow-green crust, the whole sheet recoils—slowly, with the thick, sluggish ripple of a sea anemone—before oozing back over the warm stone. The city is skinned in these heat-loving mats. They drink the lukewarm, rust-colored water spilling from the rooftop cisterns, digest the iron and sulfur, and sweat out a thin, lipid-rich oil. That is why the rising mist spirals in tight, greasy ribbons: the city is breathing out a fine aerosol of living wax, coating its own girders so the damp doesn't eat them down to red dust.

Look up from the stone, then, into the hollow frames of the buildings. Look at those awkward, waist-high catwalks stacked tier upon tier in the empty air.

What you mistook for bundles of wet tarpaulin or corroded counterweights slung beneath the iron rails are not machines.

One of them shifts. There is no suddenness to it—the air here is too thin for haste, and the heat is too heavy—only a slow, hydraulic unfolding of limbs that makes a soft *clack* of wet horn against iron.

They are long, low-slung things when they come down to the flagstones, though *tall* when they uncoil to reach a high junction box. Their hides are the color of wet slate, mottled with pale salt-scars and slick with the same greasy condensation that coats the plateau. Four primary limbs—long, double-jointed, and dense with cartilage—end in wide, cleft pads that grip slick basalt as effortlessly as dry wood. Tucked higher against their narrow, keeled chests are two smaller, finer arms, multi-jointed and tipped with blunt, ceramic-hard nails built for scraping crust from copper and teasing apart jammed valves.

They have no faces. Where a head should be, the spine rises into a broad, anvil-shaped crest of dark, mineralized bone, grooved along its flanks like a fish’s lateral line and fringed with hundreds of pale, feathery filaments. Those filaments are never quite still. They shiver faintly in the muggy air, tasting the sixty-cycle hum of the overhead cables, reading the pulse of the deep-rock pumps through the soles of their feet.

Along their ribs, beneath overlapping plates of slate-grey hide, sit rows of dark crimson gill-lungs. They open and close with a patient, bellows-like rhythm, drawing in the thin, feverish air and exhaling thin wisps of vapor through spiracles along their backs.

Once you see one, you see them everywhere—and the strange geometry of the city suddenly locks into place.

They do not use floors because floors trap stagnant heat and block the rising thermals. Instead, they *rack* themselves. A tender climbs one of those spiral stairs that seem to lead nowhere, steps out onto a waist-high catwalk, and threads its long, flexible torso between the rails. It hooks its hind limbs over the bar above, rests its belly on the grating below, and lets its crimson gill-flanks hang down into the rising steam. There it stays for hours, motionless as a girder, soaking in the mist from the overflowing rooftop cisterns while its crest-filaments drink the hum of the nearest substation. A single hollow foundry might hold thirty of them, stacked in the dimness, invisible until one shifts its weight and sends a shower of warm droplets pattering onto the stone below.

Out on the broad, doorway-less avenues, they never crowd. That is why the grid was laid out with half-mile buffers of empty pavement between one district and the next.

Watch two of them meet on the wet flagstones beneath the faintly glowing amber lamps. They do not approach within twenty paces of each other. They stop, plant their wide pads, and lower their anvil crests toward the ground. For a long moment, nothing seems to happen. Then you feel it in your teeth and through the soles of your boots: a low, sub-audible thrum, distinct from the rhythm of the underground pumps. Across the wet salt-film on their crests, tiny static sparks tick faintly—*tiss, tiss, tiss*—like water hitting a hot housing. They are speaking in resistance and vibration, modulating the hum of the plateau between them. If they stood any closer, their fields would blur together, deafening them in a wash of static.

Their lives are bound to the slow maintenance of the balance. You see them moving with unhurried, deliberate grace along the rows of pylons, carrying long, ceramic-tipped poles. When one of those horizontal stalactites of pale mineral salt grows too close to a live copper wire, a tender reaches up and snaps it cleanly at the base before it can bridge the gap and arc. Others wade into the shallow, steaming basins to clear excess biofilm from the sluice gates, keeping the massive, beltless paddle wheels turning at their steady crawl. Still others climb the slender iron legs of the rooftop cisterns, moving straight up through the shimmering curtains of rust-colored water to scrape the rims clean—clearing away the blind, iron-red ribbon-worms that breed in the warm tanks and threaten to clog the overflows.

And there are places you cannot follow them. Every windowless substation has a low, narrow hatch near the base, sealed with heavy brass dogs, and the deep-rock shafts where the underground belts descend are wide enough to swallow a locomotive. Down there, away from the eternal pewter gloom, the air is thicker, hotter, and loud with the roar of the pumps. That is where the young ones stay—soft-hided, pale, and entirely blind to anything but current—clinging to the warm pipes until their bone crests harden and their gill-plates toughen enough for the thin air above.

Up on the plateau, the rhythm holds. A counterweight drops in the haze with a muffled *thud*. A steam-relief valve lets out its long, wet sigh. On the ironwork overhead, a dozen slate-grey shapes shift slightly in their racks, turning their feathered crests toward the humming wires, tending the great, sweating machine that keeps them alive.
