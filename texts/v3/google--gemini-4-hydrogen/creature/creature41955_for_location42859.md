If you stay past the first hour—past the second fog bank, into the long, silver afternoon when the cold settles into your joints—you stop looking for people in the empty streets and begin to look at the seams where the world has come apart.

That is where the life is.

Nothing here has blood or breath. The sky remains empty, and the fern-brakes never stir with anything that flees or hunts. Yet the plateau is not uninhabited. Over eons, as the rules of slope and shadow and time wore thin, whatever lived here did not die out. It simply stopped obeying the boundary between what is alive, what is built, and what is merely old stone.

You notice the smallest ones first, if you kneel beside one of the orphaned shadows left behind by a drifting fog bank.

In the bright, ordinary daylight, the patch of shade lies on the herringbone basalt like a dropped cloak, crisp-edged and cold, while the cloud that cast it is already half a mile away. If you watch the edge of that shade without stepping into it, you will see its surface ripple. Flat, soot-colored things—no thicker than wet felt, shaped like the irregular gaps between flagstones—peel themselves up from the stone. They have no heads or limbs, only a slow, rippling edge like a dark lichen that has learned to crawl. They graze on the dry bronze-and-slate dust left beneath the workbenches, or gather in tight, overlapping plates around the bases of the wooden sluice-gates to drink the chill. They can only hold a shape inside darkness that has come unpinned from its cloud. The moment the shadow finally snaps back across the plaza to rejoin the fog, they press flat into the pores of the basalt and vanish.

Stranger things cling to the underside of the world. Where the bedrock beneath a plaza peels upward in thin, translucent sheets, exposing the inverted undersides of the cobblestones, pale membranes hang in the damp air between the layers. They are cartilaginous and veined like quartz, pulsing at the same slow, brisk pace as the rubbery morning fungi. They have no eyes, only wide, muscular mouths pressed against the upside-down stones, filtering the uphill water that seeps through the cracks.

And then there are the ones who keep the cities.

You will never see them in the normal, uncolored daylight of the surface. In that light, the windlass turns itself, the bronze chisel sits beside dry stone dust, and the street is empty from wall to wall. That is because the surface layer—the bright, wind-scoured top of the plateau—has worn too thin to hold them.

To see them, you have to take the wrong distance. You have to walk toward the neighboring city five miles away and pass through its outer kilns a second time, or step into the narrow seam of a street that is three hundred years older on its left side than on its right.

In that second pass through the kilns, the air is no longer empty. There is still no smoke rising into the pale sky, no sound of voices, no warmth of animal breath—only a dry, mineral heat, and the quiet, unhurried presence of the Tenders.

They are roughly human in height, though they have been here so long that their proportions have drifted toward the geometry of the basins. They are lean and narrow-shouldered, wrapped in heavy, damp-proof felts woven from reed-fiber and the iron-dark wool of the giant cushion-mosses. Where their skin shows at the wrists and throat, it has the pale, matte grain of lichened slate, faintly translucent at the knuckles like the peeled-up sheets of bedrock. Their joints do not bend smoothly; they move with the dry, deliberate *snaps* of midday seed pods, pausing between motions to let the cold mist settle on them.

They have no faces to speak of—only smooth, weathered planes beneath hoods of woven reed, and no mouths at all. Whatever language they once used has closed up over the millennia, replaced by the *clack* of sluice-gates, the *thud* of counterweights, and the precise, mortarless fit of stone against stone.

They live in the tucked-under layers of the city—the duplicate alleys, the underside of the plazas, the folded centuries—where the air is thicker and the rules still hold enough weight to support a body. They do not step out into the bright, exposed daylight of the top layer. Instead, their tools do.

The bronze chisels, the oak-pinned windlasses, the copper-bound buckets, the heavy timber sluice-gates—these are old enough and dense enough to sit in every layer of the delaminating city at once. When a Tender’s slate-gray hand grips the crank inside the folded dark of a workshop, the wet hemp cordage rises in the empty daylight above, glistening and dripping, with no hand visible upon it. When a broom of bound fern-stems sweeps the flagstones in the three-hundred-year-old half of a street, the stone dust clears on the sunlit side as well.

They are not ghosts, and they are not memories. They are simply working from the inside of the seam.

In the older districts, where the dry-stacked walls soften into living moss, some of them have stopped moving altogether. You can see them if you look closely at the retaining walls—places where a vertical course of slate swells slightly, the stones stacked in the unmistakable posture of a kneeling shoulder or a bent back, already half-braided with root-mat and green-patinated copper wire. They do not seem to distinguish between building a wall, tending a bed of freshly sprouted reeds, and becoming part of the basin itself.

If you stay in one of the cities long enough, they will eventually notice you.

Not with alarm, and not with curiosity—there are no beasts here to startle or investigate. They notice you the way a mason notices a loose flagstone, or the way the mosses notice a flat rock in their path. You will feel it first in the afternoon, when your own shadow begins to lag a half-step behind your boots, lingering on the cobblestones after you have moved on.

And if you turn back quickly, before the shadow catches up, you may catch a glimpse of a tall, reed-hooded figure standing quietly at the edge of an alley—not looking at you, just waiting with a bronze chisel in its pale, mineral hand, as patient as the wind, waiting for you to either move on or settle deep enough into the cold stone to be built around.
