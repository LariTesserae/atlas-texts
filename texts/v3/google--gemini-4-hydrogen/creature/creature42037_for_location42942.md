To say that nothing lives here is only to say that nothing here breathes, bleeds, or leaves behind the wet evidence of a metabolism. The pocket has no use for lungs or stomachs. Anything that relies on warmth and moisture would crack like dry leather in an hour and blow away as powder.

Yet the hollow is not empty.

If you stay past the first few minutes—past the initial shock of the windless threshold and the flat, pewter stillness—your eyes begin to notice what they had dismissed as a trick of the dim light. Look again at the corner where the three walls meet at an angle that is somehow both acute and obtuse. Look at the shadows pooling there: short, blurry, and slightly detached from the banded gneiss.

One of those shadows has weight.

It does not huddle against the stone so much as it occupies the fold itself, tucked into the impossible crease where the mountain was bent like heavy cloth. At a glance, it is indistinguishable from the dark metamorphic rock—a low, angular mass, roughly the size of a crouching person, though it has no clear head or shoulders. Its surface is layered like sheared slate, dry and matte, with the same lightless quality as the two knee-high pylons in the center of the room. If you were to reach out and touch it—assuming you could bring yourself to cross the dust-covered floor—you would feel neither the bite of the cold rock nor the warmth of living flesh. You would feel nothing at all, just that same unsettling absence, as if your hand had met a gap in the air.

It is waiting. Or rather, it is existing at the speed of the room.

Here, where time itself feels slightly viscous, an hour is not a long wait; it is scarcely a single breath. To whatever lives in the fold, a human visitor who steps under the overhang, looks around for five minutes, and steps back out into the gale is no more noticeable than a spark striking a cold anvil—a brief, hot, noisy flicker of moisture and sound that vanishes before it can be properly registered.

Only if you stay until the cycle turns do you see it wake.

First comes the sub-audible shift in pressure, the sudden, cold ache behind the teeth. The pale-gray mineral dust lifts half an inch from the floor, hanging in a rigid, three-dimensional lattice of invisible lines.

And in the corner, the shadow unfolds.

It does not uncoil like an animal, with the wet snap of tendons or the rustle of scales. It opens the way a hinge opens in a vacuum, or the way a geometric proof resolves itself on a page. Thin, faceted planes slide apart without friction and without sound. Long, multi-jointed limbs—if they can be called limbs, resembling nothing so much as slender, slate-dark calipers or the ribs of a folded fan—extend smoothly out from the impossible corner. They do not touch the floor. They never disturb the dust. Instead, they thread themselves cleanly through the open spaces of the suspended lattice, aligning with the ghostly scaffolding that the idling machinery beneath the stone has briefly revealed.

Now, for the first time, it has a clear shape, though it is a shape built for this room alone. Its body is a narrow, segmented spine of dark, non-reflective facets, hung with delicate, papery membranes of mineral-gray tissue so dry they look like shed mica. Where a face might be, there is only a smooth, roughly hexagonal wedge, tilted slightly toward the two central pylons. It has no eyes—there is nothing here worth seeing in the dim, tarnished light—and no mouth.

For the few seconds that the dust hangs suspended in the air, the creature drinks.

It does not consume matter. It feeds on the tension in the lattice, the faint, cold gradient humming between the two pylons and the bedrock. Its extended limbs tremble almost imperceptibly, tuning themselves to the rigid geometry of the invisible lines, drawing in the brief surge of order the way a deep-sea creature filters warmth from a thermal vent. If you watch closely, you can see the pale-ochre crust of silicate needles at the base of the smaller pylon shudder slightly in sympathy, as though the creature and the mineral crust are both drinking from the same sparse, invisible well.

It is the true keeper of the threshold. It is the reason the wind fails at the lip of the overhang, stopped by an unseen pane of motionless air. A single gust from the ridge outside would scatter the pale-gray dust, and without the dust, the hourly lattice would remain blind and empty, unanchored in the physical room. So the creature holds the stillness together, trading its own patient immobility for the silence it needs to survive.

Then the pressure eases.

The cold ache behind your teeth fades. Slowly, gracefully, the faceted limbs retract, folding back into one another along seams that vanish as soon as they close. By the time the fine mineral dust has drifted back down into the worn, concentric grooves around the pylons, the creature is already back in its corner, tucked once more into the angle that does not add up.

The light remains flat and dim. The dust lies undisturbed. To anyone stepping under the slate lip a moment later, there is nothing here at all—only bare stone, two silent pylons, and a shadow in the corner that doesn't quite match the rock that casts it.
