If you stand in one of the shoulder-wide alleys long enough for your breath to stop steaming—long enough for the chill to settle into your joints and slow your eyes down to the pace of the dripping water—you begin to realize that some of the knots in the timber are not wood.

They are there, wedged into the forks of the root-bridges, hunched beneath the eaves of the living gutters, or curled so tightly into the corners of the vaulted galleries that your eye slides over them as just another swelling of wet bark. To a hasty visitor, the city seems empty because a hasty visitor moves at the speed of blood and fire, and whatever lives here moves at the speed of sap.

They are small-bodied—no larger than a crouching child, and built flat and narrow so they can slip sideways between two bulging walls of moss without bruising the shoots. Their skin is thick, pebbled, and the color of wet slate, though you rarely see much of it bare; wherever a shoulder, a spine, or a crown stays still for more than a season, thin films of translucent lichen and dark, velvety algae take root upon it, blurring the creature into the walls. Only their palms, their knees, and the undersides of their forearms are clean, worn smooth into pale, rubbery calluses the exact shade of the healed graft-knuckles in the juniper.

They have no fur to mat in the freezing fog, no feathers to ice over, and no outer ears to catch the frost. Their eyes are small, dark, and set wide on flat, wedge-shaped heads, veiled by a thick, milky second lid that blinks horizontally to clear away sleet and dripping condensation. Below the eyes, instead of lips or teeth, the lower jaw ends in a broad, slate-hard plate of horn, edged like a carpenter’s gouge.

This is how the city is built, and why it has no need of knives or rope.

When a young juniper shoot reaches out into the cold air at the top of the notch, one of the dwellers climbs up through the dim twilight to meet it. It does not pull the branch—a frozen juniper bough pulled in haste will snap like glass. Instead, the dweller wedges itself into the neighboring timber, braces a calloused foot against the shoot, and leans. It may hold that lean for a day, or a week, shifting its weight by the width of a fingernail each time a warmer bank of fog softens the wood. When the branch is bent into place beside its neighbor, the dweller lowers its jaw and shaves a strip of outer bark from both stems, exposing the pale, resinous green beneath. It licks the wounds with a thick, bitter saliva that seals the sap against rot, presses the two raw faces together with its broad palms, and then simply goes still.

If a sleet squall sweeps over the notch while it is holding a graft, the dweller locks its joints and lets the rime armor its back, indistinguishable from the glazed sedge around it. It may stay there for a month, its pulse dropping to two or three sluggish beats a minute, until the cambium knits and the smooth pale knuckle forms around its knuckles. Only then does it peel its palms away and crawl back down into the dark.

They cannot make fire—a hearth would suffocate the alleys in smoke and turn the living walls to char—and they carry almost no warmth of their own. Their bodies hover only a degree or two above the freezing water in the runnels, just warm enough to keep their blood from crystallizing. To do any real work, they must descend into the lower tiers, down past the dripping root-vaults into the crushed, airless chambers at the bottom of the notch.

Here, where the dead moss and timber slowly ferment into dark, steaming peat, the air is thick and almost mild. The dwellers bury themselves up to the neck in the warm rot, letting their stiffened muscles thaw while pale springtails swarm harmlessly across their cheeks and eyelids. This is where they feed. They graze on the dense mats of cellar-fungi that thread through the dying wood, scoop up mouthfuls of springtails with their broad tongues, and drink from the deep, root-lined cisterns where runoff from the upper roofs collects—water dark with tannins, steeped with fallen needles, drowned beetles, and the droppings of the ash-colored birds.

Nothing is wasted, which is why there are no middens. Their own droppings are left in the peat-vaults, feeding the same slow rot that keeps the lower chambers from freezing solid. Even the pale, heavy-bodied birds that hop along the upper sills are left alone—or rather, they are tolerated as keepers of the timber, plucking out the frost-beetles that would otherwise bore into the load-bearing roots. A dweller will lie motionless on a mossy ledge for hours while one of the birds steps across its lichen-covered back, pecking at the crevices of its shoulders.

Without lungs built for shouting, and without any need to break the muffling hush of the moss, they have no voices. Yet the city is not silent to them. Because every beam, buttress, and retaining wall is grafted directly into its neighbor, the entire settlement is a single continuous frame of living wood. When two dwellers meet in a narrow gallery, they do not speak; they press their pale palms against the same juniper rib, or rest their flat brows against each other’s wet shoulders. When one needs help bending a forearm-thick root-cable, or when ice begins to dam one of the bark gutters near the windward rim, it taps a slow, heavy rhythm into the wood with the heel of its hand. That dull tremor travels down through the grafted knuckles, through the vaulted ceilings and the cistern walls, until others in the dark below feel it in their ribs and begin the long, patient climb upward.

They live for decades—perhaps for centuries, since nothing that moves so slowly in the cold wears out quickly. When one of them grows too stiff to thaw, even in the deepest peat, it does not climb back up toward the slate-green twilight. It crawls into one of the lowest, oldest chambers, where the weight of the three stories above is already crushing the timber flat. There it curls its long, flat limbs around a dying root, closes its milky lids, and lets the fermentation take it, becoming part of the warm, dark floor that keeps the city alive.

Above it, in the dim light where the fog combs through the needles, the younger ones are already reaching outward—guiding the new green shoots across the cold air, scraping the bark, and holding the wet wood together with their patient, moss-backed bodies, adding one more living roof over the old.
