In the shadowless glare, you do not see them right away. Without shadows to anchor them to the plaster, their pale, damp bodies blend into the rising steam and the white-hot flats. You might look straight at one kneeling beside a cistern and mistake them for a chalk-block vessel or a mound of slaked lime, until an elbow shifts, or a hand dips into the trembling water.

They are human-shaped, though spare and curiously unweathered, as if the same stillness that preserves the razor-sharp edges of the bricks has kept their joints from stiffening and their skin from lining. Their limbs have the matte, damp sheen of wet lime putty—pale grey-white across the shoulders and back, flushed a faint, terracotta pink along the forearms and shins where the radiant heat rises off the tiles. Like the floor, they are slick with a thin film of warm water that never beads into sweat and never dries. They wear almost nothing in the heavy heat: only a belt of twisted reed cordage knotted at the hip, from which hang a polished bone scraper and a small, sharp leaf of hand-knapped chert.

When they move, they make almost no sound. Their bare feet press onto the wet plaster without a slap or a splash; the film of water parts around their soles and closes behind them without a ripple, as if it were too thick to disturb. More often, they travel along the narrow, unweathered ridges of the mortar joints, balancing with an effortless, practiced grace so their heels never touch the flats. Even in the thickest heat, their chests rise and fall in a slow, shallow cadence, though if you look closely at the hollow of a throat or the inside of a wrist, the pulse beneath the skin beats to a different, unhurried tempo—out of rhythm with their breath, just like the tree frogs clinging beneath the leaves.

They are the tenders of this breathless equilibrium, though they do not hurry, and nothing they touch ever seems to need fixing.

Out on the blinding flats, you find them crouched by the runnels and the shallow rectangular cisterns. A person will sit back on their heels beside the stark-white wading birds, completely ignored by them, and dip a hollow gourd into the water. They do not drink from it. They only lift a small measure and pour it back in a slow, glassy ribbon, watching the surface settle against the sculpted clay lip to make sure it holds at that exact, trembling brim. Now and then, one will reach out with a wet thumb and lift a fallen scarlet petal from the plaster floor—not to discard it, for nothing rots here, and there is nowhere to throw it away—but simply to lay it back onto the emerald moss of a garden cauldron, where it belongs.

At the gardens, they part the shield-sized leaves with slow, careful fingers, peering into the suffocating green shade. They touch the pale frogs’ pulsing throats with the tip of a finger, as if checking a beat, and brush the iridescent beetles aside without crushing them. If a trailing vine has grown too close to the floor—closer than a finger’s breadth—they do not cut it with their chert. They simply kneel and hold a flat, damp palm beneath the vine’s tip until its dry spiral tightens and curls back upward on its own, away from the plaster.

If you watch them beside the cisterns, however, you notice the same quiet wrongness that clings to the birds. In the mirror-flat water around their shins, their reflections do not always match them. A tender may be kneeling with both hands resting empty on their thighs, yet the pale figure looking back from the water is still holding a gourd, or standing upright, or turned slightly toward the opposite rim of the basin, watching something behind you.

Most of them are not out on the flats at all, but gathered in the distant, reed-thatched drying sheds. Under the thatch, the glare softens to a thick, uniform dimness, and the air smells strongly of wet clay and dark pitch. Four or five will sit together around a damp clay mound, completely silent, their damp hair plastered close to their skulls or hanging in heavy, un-tangled hanks down their backs.

Here, their hands are never still. One draws a length of twisted reed cordage through the mound to slice off a clean, wet slab. Another presses the slab into a wooden mold, running the ball of their thumb along the bevelled edge to leave that crisp, familiar impression. A third takes a bone scraper and draws it in slow, rhythmic arcs across a tile that is already mirror-flat. 

They stack the finished tiles in neat, razor-sharp rows along the shed walls. Yet the stacks never reach the thatch, and the clay mounds never shrink. If you watch long enough—though time is hard to measure where there is no sun to cross the sky—you realize that when a stack grows high enough, someone quietly lifts the top tiles down, sets them into a pitch-sealed cauldron of warm water, and kneads them back into the mound, only to slice and mold them all over again. 

They do not do this with frustration, or with the hollowness of a punishment. Their faces—broad, still, with dark, unblinking eyes—hold the calm, absorbed satisfaction of hands that were made for clay and have been given an eternity of clay to touch. They never speak; the padded air would swallow their voices before they crossed the shed. Instead, they pass a bone scraper from palm to palm without looking up, or lay a wet hand lightly against another’s shoulder to share the weight of a wooden mold.

They have no fires lit in the kilns, no food laid out, no beds rolled in the corners of the sheds. They do not wait for the plaster to cure, or for the afternoon to cool, or for the water to rise. Like the blind eels braiding in the clear runnels and the crimson blossoms that never drop their petals, they simply occupy the exact, warm hour they have always occupied—living, breathing, and shaping the world with their thumbs, long after the need for it has ended.
