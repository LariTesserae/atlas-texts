At first, the hollow seems to have kept its promise of emptiness. You can stand at the bottom of the settled scree for a long time and find nothing that contradicts the unbroken silt or the dust lying thick on the flat stones. There is no stir of breath, no rustle in the silver-grey grass, no wet gleam of an eye in the dark.

Yet the air tastes of old resin—more of it than a few leafless, fibrous shrubs could ever spare.

It takes patience to notice where the scent comes from, because whatever lives here has spent centuries learning not to be distinct from the room. Along the back wall, where the overhang curves lowest and the slate-grey light fails altogether, the sandstone is pitted with shallow alcoves and rounded ribs that feel like dry bone. Tucked into the deepest of these recesses, wedged flush against the rock, is a shape that the eye passes over again and again, mistaking it first for a thicker knot of root, then for a bulge of iron-stained stone, then for a heavy scab of black lichen.

It is none of those. And it is not an animal—not in any way that needs water, or haste, or soft flesh.

An animal would shrivel here, its moisture pulled straight through its skin by the kiln-dry air. What lives in the alcove has solved the drought by giving up water almost entirely. Its body is built in overlapping, matte plates of something between baked clay and dark chert, dusted across its upper ridges with the same pale, flour-fine silt that coats the floor. Nothing on it glistens. Where the plates meet—at slow, reluctant hinges along its folded limbs and segmented back—there are no wet membranes, only thick seams of dark resin. On the outside, the resin has cured into a dull, dusty shellac that seals the interior against the thirst of the room. Inside, kept fluid only by the steady, stubborn warmth of the iron-rich sandstone, it moves sluggishly through the body like warm pitch.

It has no face to turn toward the entrance, and no eyes to dry out in the permanent dusk. Along its underside, pressed tightly into the hairline fissures of the wall, are dry, fibrous pads that drink the faintest trace of mineral vapor seeping through the rock, just as the shrubs do. For the rest, it lives on heat. The stone behind it is its hearth and its pulse; without the unlit kiln of the hollow, its resin would stiffen into glass and its life would pause until the rock warmed again.

Because it never crosses the pale silt pan, it leaves no tracks. Because it does not hunt or graze, nothing in the hollow is disturbed by its appetite. It has no lungs to heave and stir the motionless air; whatever gas it exchanges passes imperceptibly through microscopic pores beneath its dusty crust, so gently that a grain of silt resting on its shoulder will stay there for years.

Its life happens on the clock of the sandstone. Over the course of a season, it may shift its weight by the breadth of a fingernail, flexing its plated joints to follow a subtle drift of warmth in the wall, or pressing deeper into the alcove as the distant seasons turn outside. When it moves, the resin in its seams gives with an infinitesimal, silent stretch, and now and then two dry plates brush past each other with a sound no louder than a grain of sand dropping from the ceiling onto cracked clay: a single, tiny *tick*.

Yet it is not asleep, and it is not merely a plant. Its whole armored shell is tuned to vibration and thermal weight. In the velvety silence, every event in the hollow registers along its ribs—the faint, slow expansion of the overhang when noon heat finally sinks through the rock above, the whisper of a brittle grass stalk, the *tick* of falling dust. It knows the exact geometry of the chamber not by sight, but by the way silence settles against its plates.

If you stand still in the hollow long enough—until your own breathing slows and your skin begins to tighten in the dry warmth—you start to feel that the quiet is not vacant, but occupied. The darkness in the back alcove holds its heat differently than the bare stone beside it. And if you wait just a little longer, you might hear a faint, dry *tick* from the recessed wall, even though no sand fell from the ceiling.
