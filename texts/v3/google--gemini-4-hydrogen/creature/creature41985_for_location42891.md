Nothing in a room this loud and this cold can afford to be merely decorative. Between the booming pressers, the groaning rotors, and the snapping crowns of the barrelers, the open water looks empty—just shafts of high-altitude sun slicing through freezing clarity. Look closer, though, and the spaces between the great living machines are busy with smaller, stranger shifts, all of them plugged into the same circuit of stone, salt, and shock.

Hanging almost motionless in the bright mid-water, suspended between the sunlit ledges of the capacitor-barrelers, are the **arc-ribbons**. At rest they are nearly invisible: two-foot strips of pale, wet cartilage, no thicker than a leather belt, drifting slowly on the faint convection. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no fins—only a single, branching lattice of silver-gray nerve tissue visible beneath their translucent skin. You do not notice them until a barreller’s ion-stacks reach threshold. In the final seconds before discharge, the water around the copper-tipped crown begins to prickle with rising voltage, and the nearest arc-ribbon stiffens, swinging around like a compass needle to align itself along the invisible field lines. When the blue-white bolt finally snaps across the gap, it does not strike the granite. It strikes the ribbon’s leading edge, races down its branching silver spine—lighting the creature from within like a flooded filament for a fraction of a second—and leaps from its tail to the neighboring barrel. The concussive *pop* tumbles the ribbon backward in a cloud of tiny, fizzing hydrogen bubbles. Stunned and faintly smoking, it drifts down toward the rotors, slowly metabolizing the jolt it just swallowed.

Higher up, wedged directly into the groaning hub-joints of the spinning rotors, live the **gasket-walkers**. They are palm-sized, flat-bodied creatures shaped like split washers, their backs armored in dull, dark slate-colored plates that match the basin walls. In water this rich in dissolved minerals, any slow-moving joint ought to weld itself shut with scale within a week. The gasket-walkers prevent it. Riding the agonizingly slow, thirty-second revolutions of the turbines, they brace their hooked legs against the calcified spokes and work their mouthparts—stiff, twin rasps tipped in natural garnet, harder than quartz or aragonite—into the grinding axles. Day and night, they scrape away the creeping mineral crust that would otherwise seize the rotors solid. They do not digest the grit; they pack the scraped quartz and calcium into the segmented plates of their own tails, growing heavier and stonier with every month. Eventually, a gasket-walker becomes too heavy to cling to the hub. It loses its grip, drops silently through the bright water past the yellow conduit-worms, and lands in the rubble below—where a presser’s toothed valves slowly draw apart to take it in, garnet teeth and all, smashing its armored body back into the basin’s metallic slurry.

Down in the shadowed bottom, where the water sits just a fraction of a degree above freezing, the sulfur-yellow conduit-worms are rarely bare. Clinging to their undersides in thick, spongy mats are **lagging-slugs**—blind, lumpish things that look at first glance like patches of pale, bubbly rust. They scarcely move an inch in a week. Their tissues are packed with microscopic pockets of trapped gas—hydrogen and methane scavenged from the chemical churn in the silt—turning each slug into a living jacket of foam. By crowding shoulder to shoulder along the bellies of the conduit-worms, they trap the cables’ meager electrical warmth against their rubbery hides, keeping the worms’ internal fluids from stiffening in the glacial dark. In return, the slugs drink the faint, metallic sweat that seeps through the yellow skin. Wherever a falling shard of slate scrapes a conduit-worm bare of its slugs, white needles of frost immediately begin to feather out from the exposed hide.

And moving slowly through all of it—never swimming, only walking the talus on three heavy, jointed legs—is the basin’s largest mobile thing: a **salvager**, roughly the size of a kneeling person, built like a squat, asymmetrical tripod of fused, slag-gray carapace.

It has no eyes, despite the blinding clarity of the water. In a room this small and this loud, light is less useful than vibration. Its broad, flat front is a concave dish of bone-white bone, rimmed with hundreds of hair-thin copper filaments that tremble constantly in the *thrumm* of the water. Every *boom* of a presser, every *whir-groan* of a rotor, every sharp *crack* of a barreller’s discharge washes over that dish, painting the jagged granite walls and crowded ledges in crisp acoustic relief.

The salvager lives on failure. It patrols the bottom rubble and the lower ledges, listening for the wrong kind of sound—a rotor whose groan has gone shrill and erratic, or a capacitor-barreller whose sharp, percussive *crack* has softened into a wet, fizzing sputter because frost or overcharge has split its magnetite ribs. When it hears a crack in the engine, the tripod lumbers toward it, stepping carefully over the insulated cables, its joints clicking faintly. It wraps its heavy, pincered limbs around the dying barreller, pries open the fractured shell, and feeds on the sour, salt-heavy membranes spilling out into the cold.

When it has stripped the interior clean, it does not leave the empty drum to clutter the shelf. It hauls the hollowed magnetite shell down into the shadowed dark, wedging it directly into the jaw-path of the largest presser—feeding the dead battery back into the grinder, so the basin’s iron and calcium can be smashed into dust, stirred by the slow convection, and drawn up once more into the freezing light.
