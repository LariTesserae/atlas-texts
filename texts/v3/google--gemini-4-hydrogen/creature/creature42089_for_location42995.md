At first, you would not see them at all. Against the iron-dark shale and the churning, coffee-colored bores, they look like nothing more than exposed ribs of bedrock or tangled mats of black kelp-grass waiting out the tide. Only when the sheet-tide thins and the stone-bed momentarily slows do they uncoil from the crevices—low, broad, and heavy-limbed, shedding curtains of warm brine as they rise.

They are built for the ball-mill, not the sky. Nothing tall could survive the colliding walls of water or the sideways shear of the wind, so their bodies stay close to the floor of the world: wide-set hips, sloped shoulders, and long, immensely muscular forearms that let them move on four limbs as easily as two. Their skin is thick, slick, and hairless, mottled in the bruised pewter and wet charcoal of the shale, with pale, horn-calloused bellies and shins scarred white from a lifetime of rolling stone-eggs. Where human ankles would snap in the shifting floor, their joints are supple and wide-angled, ending in broad, splayed pads with four stubby, iron-strong fingers. They do not step *against* the rolling stones; they step from crown to crown with a practiced, unhurried roll of the hips, or else drop flat onto their bellies and let the current carry them along with the millstones, steering with their palms until they feel the motionless thrum of bedrock beneath the chaos.

They have no external ears to be torn or deafened by the world’s weight. Instead, the bones of their skulls, jaws, and collarbones are dense and spongy, tuned to read the tectonic chatter of the stone-bed through their feet and fingertips. Their nostrils are heavy, muscular valves that snap shut when a bore slams overhead; they can hold a single breath of the sulfur-heavy vapor for a quarter of an hour, wedged shoulder-to-shoulder in a shale seam while towering ridges of foam rear up and collapse above them. Their eyes are small and set deep beneath translucent inner lids that stay drawn against the flying silt. Sight here is a poor guide, and mostly a liar.

Yet they do open their eyes—not to look at the dragging cloud-ceiling, which they know well enough, but to peer into the rare, still hollows left behind between bores. Because reflections here cannot be trusted, they have learned to read the lies as instruments. Kneeling in ankle-deep water, one will stare down into a quiet puddle—never seeing its own face, but watching the ghostly, tilted double of a wave that will not arrive for another minute, gauging its height and angle before the air even begins to hum. They live leaning slightly forward in time, bracing their bodies in the ten-second stillness between the piled-up crack of thunder and the violet flicker of lightning that caused it.

They have no spoken language; a shout here is swallowed whole before it leaves the throat. When two of them meet on a high hump of shale, they press their foreheads or ribs together, trading a rapid, rhythmic vocabulary of clicks, hums, and bone-tremors—names tapped out in three-beat patterns against the sternum. For longer conversations, or simply for the luxury of stillness, they wait for the world to fray.

When the silt and brine suddenly remember a shape and freeze into a hollow dome or a spiraling chimney humming like struck bronze, they do not flee it. Those ten heartbeats of solid quiet are the only rooms this world ever builds. They slip inside the vibrating wall the instant it forms, pressing their backs to the mud-water. Inside, the roar of the continent-wide puddle drops to a deep, enclosing drone. In that brief, resonant dark, they can share a handful of chewed kelp-sap, check each other’s hides for deep stone-bruises, or press their skulls together to pass along news of where the bedrock is exposed—before sliding back out on the ninth heartbeat, just as the dome dissolves into boiling foam. Even the stuttering waves—those rigid, roaring knots of water caught in a three-second loop—are useful to them; they crouch in the lee of a knotted wave as a windbreak, harvesting the pale bladder-reeds exposed in its trough.

They carry no tools, no metal, no packs. Whatever they need is already tumbling at their feet or braided into the rock. They eat the tough, rubbery kelp-grass, chewing it slowly for its mineral salt, and drain the leathery bladder-reeds for the cleaner, filtered gel inside. Sometimes they follow the blind, house-wide rays that plow through the shifting stone-bed, swimming in their turbulent wakes to gather the small, pale worms and crushed shell-things turned up from the shale. When the sky-darkening double-bores approach, they crawl to the higher bedrock seams and wedge themselves tightly against the leeward rims of the pale, boulder-sized shell-creatures clamped there, sharing their weeks-long stillness until the worst of the violence passes.

Because nothing can be owned or kept in a place that never stops grinding, they drift—alone for months at a time, or in loose, shifting bands of three or four, crossing paths by chance on the higher humps of shale. And wherever they pause, they leave small, deliberate anchors behind.

They braid the black kelp-grass and knot it tightly around bedrock spurs—not just as tethers to hook a forearm through while sleeping in the tide, but as resonant strings. Different braids hum at different pitches in the rushing brine; a traveler passing weeks later can lay a palm on the knot and feel the speed of the current, or sense whether a stutter is building nearby.

They stack the smooth stone-eggs into tight, squat cairns, wedging them with dried bladder-weed and packed silt until they hold against the bores. A cairn is a seed of stillness: it forces the racing brine to eddy behind it, creating a tiny, quiet lee. More than that, if a cairn survives long enough, the silt around it learns its shape. Long after a monstrous tide finally scatters the stones, the mud-water in that spot will sometimes freeze for ten heartbeats into a hollow ghost-cairn, sheltering whoever happens to be passing.

And before they slip back into the waist-deep current, they press their splayed palms or coiled bodies into the softest mud, leaving shallow rings beside the cairns. In a world too old to remember its own shape, a ring in the mud is a wordless greeting across frayed time—a way of saying, *someone with hands was here, and held still for a breath, before the water took them again.*
