Up close, in the shadowless glare of the basin, the builders look almost carved from the same pale limestone they haul. Their skin is the color of wet chalk, cool to the touch and perpetually dusted with fine mineral grit that settles into the creases of their knuckles and elbows. Beneath deep-set brows, their pale eyes are ringed with thick, dark smears of ground fungal soot—the only black thing on their bodies—painted on each morning to cut the blinding reflection off the stone. When they look up toward the white sky, a second, milky lid flicks sideways across the eye.

They do not speak. Their throats are narrow and quiet, and their mouths stay shut against the dust, breathing only through their noses. In a bowl of slick, concave rock where a shout would ricochet for seconds and even a sharp clap can unsettle a pocket of trapped air beneath the silt, sound is treated like a leak in a cistern—a waste, and a danger. Instead, their hands are never still. A crew moving at a hunched trot across a causeway is a blur of small, crisp signals: a two-finger flick to call for a wedge, a flat palm pressed to the collarbone to warn of a soft seam underfoot, a sharp jerk of the chin to redirect a stone-sling. Even in the darkest alleys, three levels down where the glare finally softens into grey twilight, they converse by tapping patterns into one another’s forearms and shoulders.

Their lives are governed by the arithmetic of weight and moisture. Nothing is owned for long, because nothing stays dry or wet for long. A family—if the tight, shifting work-crews that share a sleeping alley can be called that—possesses only what it can carry when a cistern goes dry: a length of braided fungus-fiber, a flint scraper, a bone lever worn glossy by three generations of palms, and perhaps a single cold-hammered copper pin, prized above any stone. There is no fire in the basin; there is nothing to burn. They eat cold, dense cakes of pressed black fungus, cured in salt-crusts scraped from the dry flats and washed down with mineral water that tastes faintly of iron and old rock. Their tunics of felted fungus-fiber are never truly dry, clinging to their lean ribs like damp bark, keeping their bodies cool while they work.

When the light above dims to a flat, pewter grey—the only sign of night in the sinkhole—the frantic motion slows but never quite stops. Some crews curl together in the narrow, roofed compounds, packed tightly for warmth against the clammy air, while others work on by the faint, residual gleam of the pale stone, listening with the soles of their padded feet for the subtle *shudder* that means a subterranean air-pocket is shifting.

They are rarely alone in the alleys. The long-necked grazers wander through the city with the unhurried entitlement of animals that have never known a fence. Their hides are pale, pebbled, and slick with condensation, beading water that sometimes drips into the runnels as they pass. They have no horns and no true voice—only a low, wet *huff* when they clear their nostrils of chalk-dust—and their broad, flat teeth make a constant, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* as they rasp the velvety black fungus from the damp walls. The builders never tether them. Instead, they steer them gently with long bone staffs toward fresh fungal blooms, and clear sharp flint chips from their paths so their soft, pad-like feet won’t split. When a grazer grows too old to climb the stepped terraces, it is killed quietly with a flint blade in a dry cistern; nothing is left behind. Its hide wraps the heavy mallets, its long ribs become levers for prying up flagstones, and its sinews are braided into stone-slings.

Filling the spaces between the builders and the grazers are the scavengers. By day, most of them are invisible, wedged flat into the dark, unmortared chinks of the terrace walls, their pale, translucent shells mimicking the chipped edges of the limestone. You only see how many there are when a crew begins to dismantle an old retaining wall. As soon as the top gypsum slab is pried loose, the scavengers spill out from the gaps in a silent, pale tide—hundreds of segmented, dog-sized bodies scurrying on delicate legs, the dark grey pulp of digested grit and fiber faintly visible through their backs. They scatter without a sound save for the dry, papery patter of their feet, streaming across the open plaza to vanish into the joints of the nearest standing tower.

In this way, the city digests itself and grows back in the same breath. Even the dead among the builders are not buried—there is no soil to hold them, and a body sealed in a dry-stone tomb would only waste good limestone. Instead, when one of them stops moving, their crew strips the fungus-felt tunic, takes the bone levers and copper pins, and lays the body in one of the deepest, wettest crevices at the basin’s rim, where the cold condensation drips thickest. Within weeks, the velvety black fungus creeps over the pale skin, and the scavengers work through the joints, returning everything to the thin, damp paste of the floor—to be scraped up, trodden down, and built into the next wall.
