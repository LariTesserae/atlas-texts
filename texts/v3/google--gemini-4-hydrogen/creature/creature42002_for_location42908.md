At first, and even at second glance, it looks like part of the scale. 

Along the seams of the cast-iron pump housings, and wedged into the narrow slots between the finned transformer radiators, the pale crust of calcium carbonate is slightly thicker than it ought to be. In most places the mineral deposit is uniform, a thin frost over the bolt heads and flanges. Here and there, though, it gathers into low, overlapping shelves—chalk-white at the edges, darkening to slate-gray where it meets the iron—that have the quiet symmetry of bracket fungi growing on a dead log.

Nothing about them moves. They do not pulse or retract if a shadow passes overhead. If you were to touch one, it would feel as hard and cold as the limestone floor. Yet they are not mineral alone.

They are creatures of the boundary layer, neither wholly animal nor quite plant, that have taken root in the narrow margin where the machinery meets the lake. What shows to the water is only their outer architecture: stacked, brittle plates of precipitated lime, laid down over years in ridges no thicker than a fingernail. Beneath those plates, tucked into the shaded crevices of the castings and the undersides of the copper busbars, lies the living body—a flat, cool-toned ribbon of tissue, pale blue-gray and nearly translucent, pressed so tightly against the metal that not even a hair’s breadth of open water separates them.

They have no mouths, no gills, no eyes for the muted daylight overhead. In a basin with no current and no prey, swimming would be a waste of energy, and pumping water to breathe would stir the silt. Instead, they live by the hum.

Each of the outer lime plates is grown to a precise, rigid thickness, tuned by trial and slow accretion to the sixty-hertz vibration of the transformers. Pressed against the iron, the plates catch the steady mechanical thrum that a human ear can only feel in the bones of the skull. They shiver at an amplitude too small to cloud the water or trouble the nearest stalk of stonewort, yet strong enough to keep the creatures’ internal fluids turning in a slow, continuous circuit. The machinery’s wasted vibration is their heartbeat; without it, they would suffocate in the still cold.

From the underside of their pale bodies, fine, velvety-brown filaments—stained the same dark shade as the oxidized copper—creep along the steel skids and thread into the gaps between the sacrificial zinc plates. They do not eat the metal, not directly, but they drink the faint galvanic current that leaks between the copper and the zinc in the mineral-heavy water. Trace metals drawn from the anode racks are woven straight into their own nerves, forming a simple, conductive net that runs the length of each ribbon.

To live like this is to inhabit a world without weather. They do not know morning from afternoon, except perhaps as a sub-degree shift in the density of the water pressing down on their backs. They do not know the surface exists. Their entire reality is the sixty-hertz chord—flat, unvarying, unbroken—and the faint, steady warmth radiating a millimeter off the transformer fins before the cold lake swallows it.

Growth happens on the timescale of the silt. A single plate may take three years to widen by the breadth of a grain of sand. When a sacrificial zinc plate finally pits away to nothing and the local current fades, the creature beneath it does not thrash or detach. Over months, it simply thins out on the dead side, letting its older lime shelves go empty and brittle, while its brown filaments creep fractionally along the steel skid toward the next freshest anode. What it leaves behind is indistinguishable from ordinary mineral scale, a pale ridge that slowly darkens with aquatic moss.

There may be dozens of them in the basin, or only a few, branched and connected beneath the armored cables where they climb the northern rock lip. They make no sound of their own, cast no shadow worth noting, and ask nothing of the water except that it stay cold, still, and faintly charged. They are simply the last, quietest piece of the circuit—listening to the shore station’s distant, steady load, holding their place in the iron seams as the years settle around them.
