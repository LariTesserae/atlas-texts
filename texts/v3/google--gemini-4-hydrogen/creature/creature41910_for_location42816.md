What lives here does not fight the mountain or the machines; it wedges itself into the narrowest margins between them and becomes as patient as the stone.

In the crowded dark of the pocket, the thousands of arthropods—the flat-bodied beetles, the blind crevice-scorpions, the bristling runners and armored mites—form a single, living crust. Because there is no water to drink and no breeze to cool them, they survive by sharing what little moisture their own bodies hold. Pressed carapace to carapace in dense, layered mats, they trap a microscopic, stagnant stillness between their bellies and the stone, hoarding every molecule of dampness exhaled through their tiny breathing-vents. Their wing-cases are fused shut into seamless domes; their joints are rimmed with thick, heat-cured wax. To move quickly would be to crack that wax against a razor edge of quartz and bleed out into the kiln-dry air as vapor. So they drift like tectonic plates, nudging and climbing over one another across hours, grazing on flakes of shed exoskeleton and the desiccated husks of their dead.

Yet even in a chamber this tight, there are places the crowd avoids: the sharpest, hottest ridges of magnetite where the stray voltage from the ridge-top transformers pools and snaps, and where the grey rock flour stands on end like iron filings. 

That is where the *flat-backs* live.

They are scarcely thicker than a sliver of mica, no wider than a thumbnail, and shaped like broad, segmented teardrops. At a glance—if there were any light to see them by—they would be indistinguishable from the freshly cleaved stone. Their outer plates are not the glossy amber or black of ordinary insects, but a dull, matte grey, pitted and rough. Along the edges of their segments grow rows of stiff, metallic-looking bristles, hardened with iron oxides scavenged from the dust. 

When a flat-back wedges itself into a raw crystal seam, it pulls a coat of the loose magnetite flour over its own carapace. The iron-rich dust clings to its bristles, standing up in tiny, rigid spikes that align with the invisible magnetic fields humming through the basalt. In this way, the creature builds itself a living shield. When the stray current surges through the mountain and a blue-white spark snaps across the cleft, the electricity does not cook the flat-back’s soft insides; it skitters harmlessly across the dust-coated armor on its back, tracing the edges of its plates in a brief, ghostly halo before grounding back into the stone.

They are entirely blind, and they have no use for ears in a chamber filled with a deafening, unbroken roar. Instead, their flat undersides are pressed flush against the basalt, reading the world through vibration. To them, the bone-deep bass of the crushers and the high, metallic whine of the engines are not noise—they are the steady, unchanging pressure of the universe, like deep water to a fish at the bottom of a trench. Because that mechanical drone never drops in pitch, the faintest disruption stands out with razor clarity: the microscopic *tick* of a mite’s claw on quartz, the dry *hiss* of a beetle’s shell scraping an overhang, or the sudden, sharp *snap* of a static discharge nearby.

That snap is often how they eat.

The flat-backs do not hunt by chasing. They wait in the electrified seams, motionless for days at a time, until the press of the crowd pushes a silverfish-runner or a young beetle too close to a charged ridge of magnetite. When a spark arcs across the gap, it locks the stray arthropod’s muscles in an instant, stunning it or knocking it into the flour-soft dust. Only then does the flat-back stir. Sliding out from its cleft fraction by fraction of an inch, keeping its belly low to avoid the static, it reaches the stunned prey. It has no jaws to chew with—chewing would expose wet flesh to the parching air. Instead, it unfolds a needle-thin, heavily armored beak from beneath its head-plate, slides it cleanly into the soft membrane between the prey’s leg segments, and seals the puncture with the rim of its own face before drawing out the thick, syrupy fluid inside. Not a drop is lost to the heat.

Everything about them is built for an endurance that outlasts seasons, because there are no seasons here. There is no day or night, no rain, no cool dawn—only the steady thermal warmth bleeding from the bedrock and the endless shifts of the unseen machinery above. A flat-back may spend years in the same two-inch crack of quartz. When it finally grows too tight for its skin, it splits its old shell down the back, waits for the new plates beneath to dry and stiffen in the kiln-heat, and then immediately consumes its own shed husk so not a grain of protein or moisture is wasted. The females carry their few leathery eggs tucked tightly beneath their broad tail-plates until they hatch, pale and soft, and immediately roll in the grey rock flour to gather their first coat of iron dust.

Together, the crowded mats of beetles and scorpions and the spark-sheathed flat-backs in the seams form a single, closed, humming knot of life—breathing hot ozone and pulverized silica, drinking only each other, and listening through their bellies to the tireless thunder of the engines outside.
