At the heart of the city, wedged into the oval alcoves of the towers, live the **Masons**—though they have no name for themselves, and no eyes to see the avenues they have raised.

To touch one in the dark is to meet two entirely different textures at once. The lower half of a Mason is a heavy, spiraled cone of dense aragonite, fused directly into the wall of its alcove like a knot in petrified wood. From the mouth of this shell extends the creature’s true body: a thick, segmented column of pale, muscular flesh, as long as a human arm and ringed with fleshy, pleated collars. It has no head in any familiar sense. Instead, its upper end opens into a broad, scalloped mantle—a wet, velvety hood lined with hundreds of tiny, beating cilia and a single, crescent-shaped slit of a siphon.

A Mason lives by drinking the city’s breath. When the thermal tide is at ebb, it withdraws mostly into its spiral anchor, leaving only the rim of its mantle protruding like a pale lip. It pumps water steadily through its siphon, straining out stray bacteria and organic dust with a low, rhythmic *thrum* that vibrates through the limestone. When the deep geological pressure shifts and the warm, mineral-heavy plumes surge up through the avenues, the Masons unfurl. Their bodies stretch outward into the current, swaying slightly like blind anemones. Special glands along the edges of their mantles drink in the dissolved calcium and sulfur, secreting a thick, pearlescent mucus that hardens on contact with the cooler surrounding water.

They do not build by plan, yet they build with precision. Each Mason’s mantle is studded with pressure-sensitive pits that read the speed and warmth of the current. If an eddy strikes too hard from the left, the creature angles its body and lays down a thicker lip of shell-cement on that side; if the warm plume drifts away, it extends its secretions toward the heat. Over decades, thousands of neighbors responding to the same invisible currents naturally curve their towers inward to meet one another, bridging the alleys with buttresses and raising the fluted chimneys that draw the heat upward.

A Mason never leaves its tower, yet it slowly climbs it. As the lower tiers of the city fill with pale silt and the old alcoves grow cramped, the creature secretes a dissolving acid from the base of its spiral shell, detaches itself by a fraction of an inch, and inches upward, sealing the abandoned chamber behind it with a plug of calcified waste. The oldest Masons—massive, scarred patriarchs near the chimney rims—sit atop ten stories of their own discarded, fossilized infancy.

***

The Masons are the architecture, but they are not its only tenants. Packed into the porous mortar-joints between their shells are the **Thread-Eels**—ribbon-bodied fish no thicker than a finger, entirely devoid of pigment or eyes. Their skin is slick and scaleless, the color of skimmed milk, and along their blunt snouts grow clusters of branching, feathery barbels. 

A Thread-Eel spends its life woven through the walls like stitchery. It anchors its flattened tail inside a Mason’s abandoned chamber and lets its forebody drift out into the alley, its barbels tasting the water for the faint electrical twitch of passing shrimp. They are fiercely territorial over their cracks; when two eels meet in a shared tunnel, they do not bite, but press their flanks together and shudder, sending rapid, high-frequency vibrations through the stone until the smaller one backs down into the silt.

Below them, on the smoothed limestone plazas and the knee-deep drifts of pale sediment, patrol the **Silt-Coins**. These are flat, circular crustaceans the size of dinner plates, with thin, translucent carapaces that flex slightly as they move. They have no need for heavy armor here; instead, their edges are fringed with stiff, sensory bristles that sweep the floor like brooms. They glide over the bottom on dozens of paddle-like legs, shoveling fallen scraps—dead shrimp, flakes of shed shell, wisps of bacterial mat—into their underside mouths. When a thermal surge hits and the currents quicken, a Silt-Coin simply presses its broad, concave belly flat against the velvety stone, creating a seal so tight that even the strongest updraft cannot peel it loose.

Hanging above the plazas, caught in the warm updrafts of the chimneys like dust motes in a sunbeam, are the **Plume-Shrimp**. They are scarcely larger than grains of rice, entirely glass-clear save for a tiny, pulsing knot of orange-pink organs in their chests. By the tens of thousands, they tread water in the rising heat, combing the current with feathery front legs. They are the city’s perpetual fog, thinning when the water cools, thickening into dense, rustling clouds the moment the avenues grow warm.

***

When the Plume-Shrimp thicken, the **Listener-Squid** descend.

They drop from the pitch-black water column above the chimneys like pale ghosts, each one roughly the length of a forearm, with a slender, torpedo-shaped mantle and eight long, trailing arms. Because light does not exist here, they have shed the ability to change color; instead, their skin is a stark, chalky white, and they navigate by a different kind of camouflage. Their bodies are covered in tiny, muscular papillae that can instantly rise into rough ridges or smooth into glass, allowing a squid to drape itself over a ribbed limestone buttress and become indistinguishable—to the touch of a passing crab or the lateral line of a fish—from the stone itself.

Their eyes are gone, replaced by two broad, dish-shaped pits on either side of the head that read the faintest displacement of water. A Listener-Squid can hang motionless in an arched alleyway, feeling the individual *ticks* of Silt-Coins on the street below and the collective hum of a thousand Plume-Shrimp vibrating in the updraft. When it strikes, it does not jet forward; it simply uncoils two long feeding tentacles, guided by the eddies, and plucks a Thread-Eel from its crack or sweeps a handful of shrimp from the current in total silence.

Sharing the dark alleys with the squid are the **Vane-Fish**—gaunt, narrow-bodied hunters with oversized heads and jaws that unhinge like traps. Their bodies are lined from gill to tail with a bright, silvery seam of exposed lateral-line pores, sensitive enough to map the shape of a tower wall from the way water bounces off it. They cruise slowly through the avenues, mouths slightly agape, letting the warm current carry them past the alcoves. Now and then, a Vane-Fish emits a brief, pale-blue spark from a gland beneath its chin—not to see by, for it is blind, but to startle a hiding crustacean into twitching, betraying its position to the fish’s hyper-sensitive flanks.

***

Far above the streets, where the fluted chimneys vent their spent warmth into the freezing weight of the abyss, lives something that never enters the city at all.

The residents below know it only as a slow, sub-audible pressure wave—a heavy *whoomp* that rolls down the avenues every few minutes, stirring the settled silt and making the Masons pull back slightly into their shells.

Above the rooftops, in the boundary layer where the blood-warm plumes meet the cold black ocean, glides the **Abyssal Manta**—though it is no true ray, but a vast, cartilaginous grazer as wide as a city avenue is long. Its body is a thin, undulating sheet of pale, rubbery flesh, speckled with stray colonies of luminescent bacteria that cling to its skin like faint, dying embers. It has no teeth and no eyes. Instead, its broad, crescent mouth stays permanently open as it flies in slow, effortless circles above the chimney tops, straining the thick bacterial filaments and startled Plume-Shrimp that rise on the thermal exhaust.

It rarely drops low enough to brush the chalky rims of the tallest towers. It belongs to the open dark, a living roof passing endlessly over a blind metropolis—leaving behind only the gentle roll of its wake, drifting down through the warm, quiet water to settle the dust.
