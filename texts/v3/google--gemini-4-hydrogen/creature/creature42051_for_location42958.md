At first, if you drifted through the lower galleries with a lantern, you would think nothing larger than the wrist-thick eels lived here. You would see only the shrimp rowing in the gloom, the blind catfish nosing out of their clay jars, and the faint, milky-green smudges of the snails.

Then you would notice the absences.

On the undersides of certain lintels, the brittle white thickets of tube-worms have been sheared away in neat, crescent-shaped patches, as if something with a broad, blunt edge had scraped them clean. Along the sunken thresholds, where the pale mineral silt lies undisturbed for years, there are occasional shallow troughs—two parallel drags, and between them a wider, smoother furrow, already softening as new silt settles grain by grain. And here and there, among the hundreds of terracotta jars mortared into the walls, you would find one that holds no salamander and no fish, its mouth plugged instead from within by a tight, pale bolster of skin, motionless as a rolled bolt of wet linen.

What lives here does not hurry to be seen. It has been here long enough that its stillness matches the masonry.

If you waited in the dark—waiting as the water waits, through the slow, hours-long turn of the convection loop—one of the pale bolsters would eventually shift. Not with a start, and not with the sudden whip of a startled eel, but with the heavy, deliberate ease of a stone settling deeper into mud.

First come the hands: broad, flat-palmed, and six-fingered, the digits stubby and joined by thick webs of skin. The nails are blunt, horn-colored plates, worn smooth and rounded at the tips from millennia of gripping basalt. They reach out, press gently against the lip of the clay jar to test the current, and pull the rest of the body forward.

It is roughly the size of a curled-up child, though stretched longer and heavier through the ribs—shaped less like a fish than like a salamander that forgot to stay small, or something that once walked on dry ground and gave up the sky for warm stone. Its skin is the color of wet tallow, seamless and loose, hanging in soft, pleated folds along its flanks. In the faint, bruised glow of a nearby snail, you can see the skin is thin enough at the webs of its fingers and along those flank-folds to show a dense, feathery net of dark capillaries beneath—the faintest, slowest pulse of blood, beating once every dozen seconds.

It has no eyes. Where eyes might have been, the pale skin stretches unbroken over two shallow, pearly domes that can tell only the difference between the slate-blue twilight of the collapsed dome above and the absolute black of the floor. Instead, its broad, blunt muzzle is pitted with dozens of tiny sensory pores, like a catfish’s lateral line gathered into a face, and two short, fleshy barbels hang from the corners of its mouth. Behind the hinge of its jaw, where ears would be, sit two sunken, slit-like pits.

It does not swim if it can walk. Swimming stirs the silt and spends heat it cannot easily spare. Instead, it steps out of the jar and moves along the gallery wall with a slow, four-limbed gait, its webbed palms cupping the pillowy curves of the basalt, its long, flattened tail dragging lightly behind it to feel the drop-offs and doorways.

There are nine of them in the cistern. Perhaps ten, if the youngest—no larger than a forearm, translucent as a shrimp—has survived its first year behind the fallen capitals. They do not gather in crowds; eighty paces of stone is large enough for solitude, and small enough that none of them is ever truly alone. Each knows the geography of the pillars by the texture of the recessed mortar and the taste of the water rising from the floor-cracks. They know which arches are low enough to brace their backs against while they sleep, and which jars are cracked just wide enough to wedge a hip into against the gentle pull of the current.

They are not fierce. Nothing in this room can afford ferocity.

Mostly, they graze and wait. One will drape itself across a pillar like a wet cloak and stay there for two days, its mouth slightly open, letting the drifting shrimp row straight into the current of its breath. Another will slide a single hand into an occupied clay jar, fingers curled loosely like anemones, and wait with geological patience until a blind catfish mistakes the pale digits for worms. When it eats the bioluminescent snails, it peels them from the stone with a neat, scraping sweep of its flat-nailed thumb—never clearing a whole pillar, always leaving a few behind, though whether out of dim husbandry or mere fullness is impossible to say—and cracks the thick shells between pebble-flat molars. For an hour afterward, the cold, milky-green glow of the snail’s flesh lingers in its throat and belly, shining softly through the thin skin of its chest so that it drifts through the dark galleries like a faint, drowning lantern.

They have lungs, small and leathery, tucked behind those slow-beating ribs, though they scarcely use them. The vascular folds along their flanks drink enough oxygen from the warm water to keep them down in the lower chambers for days at a time. Only rarely—once a week, perhaps, or once a month when the sulfurous fissures run hotter and thin the water’s breath—does one of them climb.

It ascends the thick, squat pillars hand over hand, moving up into the dull slate-blue glow of the upper arches, past the schools of eyeless fish that part around it like pale dust. At the very crown of the vault, where the trapped, stale air pockets lie pressed flat and silver against the highest stones, it breaks the surface without a ripple. It presses its nostrils into the sour, dead cushion of air, takes three or four deep, rattling breaths that echo faintly down the submerged dome, and then lets itself sink back into the warm dark, heavy and unhurried.

They do not build, and they do not speak, yet the ancient room is not merely a shelter to them; it is an extension of their own slow bodies.

They tend the floor-cracks. When the pale mineral crust grows too thick over the fissures in the hexagonal flagstones, choking off the fever-warm water that rises from the mountain’s bones, one of them will crawl down to the bottom, brace its webbed feet against the stone, and scrape the lime away with its blunt nails until the warm current pulses free again. Then it will press its soft, pleated belly directly onto the crack, soaking in the heat and listening through its jaw-pits to the deep, distant *thrum* of the rock.

They also gather small things that do not belong to the water. In the recessed mortar grooves of the lower pillars, tucked neatly out of the current, you can find their hoards: a bronze chisel-head worn to a green nub by centuries of warm water; a fragment of woven reed basket, petrified in mineral silt; a spiral snail shell twice the size of any living snail; a smooth, dark pebble that must have fallen through the collapsed seam in the dome a hundred winters ago. When one of them passes its cache in the dark, it will sometimes pause, lift a finger, and tap the bronze nub once against the stone, listening to the dull *tink* fade into the thick silence.

They rarely touch one another. When two meet in a narrow, corbelled alley, they stop a hand’s breadth apart, letting the current carry their scent between them. Slowly, they extend their blunt muzzles and brush their barbels across each other’s flanks—reading age, fullness, and the slight, seasonal shifts in the water’s chemistry—before stepping aside, one climbing the pillar to let the other pass beneath.

Once every few years, when the water warms by half a degree and the mineral taste sharpens, one of them will retreat to the deepest, tightest chamber at the base of the retaining wall. There, inside a large, intact jar, it will lay a clutch of seven or eight leathery, pearl-gray eggs, each the size of a plum. It wedges its broad, flat back across the mouth of the jar, sealing the opening with its own body, and stays there for months—neither eating nor climbing for air, breathing solely through the crimson-veined folds of its flanks, fanning the eggs with slow, rhythmic sweeps of its webbed hands until the pale, gilled young wriggle out between its ribs.

The oldest among them scarcely moves at all anymore. It has claimed a niche behind a fallen capital in the deepest gallery, where the darkness is absolute and the snail-light never reaches. Its skin has thickened and clouded to the color of old bone; along the ridges of its motionless shoulders and the crown of its skull, a faint crust of white calcareous tubes has begun to take root, as if the room were slowly mistaking it for another pillar. Yet when the lazy convection loop completes its turn and brushes past the capital, the feathery crowns of the worms snap shut, and the old thing’s barbels twitch once in the dark—tasting the sulfur, the silt, and the patient, unchanging warmth of the only world it has ever known.
