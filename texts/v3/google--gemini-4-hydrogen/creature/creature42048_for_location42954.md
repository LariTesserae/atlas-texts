At first, the glare makes it seem as though nothing here could have a body at all—only stone, water, and the white, directionless light that fills the room like a held breath.

If you kneel beside the basin and let your eyes settle, the life here reveals itself not by motion, but by color. Along the dampest, slightly cooler crevices at the water’s edge, the pale silicate is crusted with a sparse, chalky-orange film. It looks like rust or dried clay, but it is alive—a thin, gritty skin woven half from dissolved minerals and half from simple, heat-loving microbes. Where the stone is hot enough to hiss, the rock stays bare. Only in the narrow band where the weeping water pools and cools by a fraction of a degree does the orange crust take hold, drinking in the mineral-heavy moisture and the fierce, steady light, growing so slowly that a fingernail’s width of it might represent months of quiet work.

Moving across that orange crust—and occasionally darting over the clear water between one ledge and the next—are the pocket’s only other residents.

They are small, flat-bodied arthropods, no longer than a thumbnail, segmented into seven overlapping plates of pale, translucent amber. In a room without shadows, they have none; the white light passes straight through their shells, turning their insides into faint, shifting threads of darker ochre. When one pauses on the pale stone floor of the basin, it looks less like an animal than a drop of warm resin caught in glass.

They are completely blind. Even if they had eyes, there would be little use for them here: there is no night to hide in, no approaching shadow to warn of a predator, no horizon to steer toward. Instead, they read the room through their undersides and through a pair of short, stubby palps that tap the wet rock ahead of them. They know the pocket by temperature, by the slickness of the mineral film, and by sound. The soft *tiss* of a drop hitting a hot upper shelf means nothing to them—it is too dry, too far above. But the bright *plink* of water striking the basin sends a clean ripple across their backs, a brief pulse of cooler water that stirs loose flecks of the orange crust and sets them grazing faster.

Their bodies are built for tight places. They did not originate in this open room; the fracture is too young for that. They arrived the same way the water does—squeezed flat as leaf-veins through the hair-thin seams of the basalt, following the invisible, frictionless pull of moisture through the mineral lattice until the rock opened into this sudden, blazing pocket. Even now, with three paces of open air above them, they behave as though the ceiling might press down at any moment. They hug the jagged lip of the basin, tucking themselves into the lee of stone overhangs—not for shade, since there is no shade, but for the slight shelter from falling drops and the fractionally cooler water trapped against the rock.

Their legs are fine, pale hairs splayed wide to distribute their weight, tipped with tiny pads that grip the glassy, razor-edged stone without snagging. Most of the time they move unhurriedly, scraping the chalky-orange film with delicate, comb-like mouthparts, leaving faint, clean trails in the crust behind them. When two meet on a narrow submerged ledge, there is no contest and no alarm; they tap palps briefly, and one either slides flatly over the other’s amber back or lets go of the stone altogether, kicking sideways across the warm basin in a sudden, crisp dart before settling onto the opposite rim.

Because the water is shallow and kiln-warm, oxygen is thin. Beneath their flat segments, rows of feathery gill-plates beat in a constant, barely visible rhythm, drawing what breath they can from the surface film. Every so often, one will climb halfway up a weeping wall—just into the taut, glassy sheet of water clinging to the vertical rock—and sit there for an hour, half-submerged in the moving current where the air and moisture meet.

They molt on the submerged shelves, slipping backward out of their tight amber plates. The empty husks drift like flakes of mica across the basin until they catch on a sharp tooth of silicate, where they slowly soften in the heat or are nibbled down by another grazer for the minerals in the shell. Now and then, a female presses a cluster of pearl-white eggs directly into the chalky-orange crust, gluing them into the living mineral matrix so that when the hatchlings emerge—no larger than grains of salt—they wake up already surrounded by food.

There is no urgency in them, and no strangeness beyond the fact of their being here at all. In the steady, shadowless glare, to the quiet *tick* of fresh stone and the *plink* of circulating water, they simply graze, molt, and dart from ledge to ledge—small, sun-warmed creatures tending the edges of a puddle that happens to be sealed beneath tons of rock.
