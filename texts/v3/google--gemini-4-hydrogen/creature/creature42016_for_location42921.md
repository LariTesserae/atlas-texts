You do not see whoever lives here right away, not because they are hidden—there is nowhere to hide in a room stripped so clean—but because the stretched emptiness between you and the far wall makes distance unreliable, and because they have learned to match the stillness of the wood between sap-tides.

They are crouched near the curve of the pale cambium floor, one palm resting flat against the taut membrane as if steadying a drumhead, their weight spread low so they do not make the room boom. Lean, spare, and long-jointed, they look less like a creature of the deep underground than someone who has spent a very long time indoors on a bright, windless afternoon. Their skin has the pale, slightly waxy clarity of an inner shoot grown beneath bark—neither sunburned nor cave-bleached, though the stolen noon fills every corner of the chamber. Only their eyes belong to the brightness: clear and sharp, the pupils drawn down to tight black seeds against a light that has no sun behind it.

Their shadow does not lie beneath them. It pools in a tight dark stain under their raised wrist, or lifts slightly upward along the freshly torn ridges of heartwood, following the chamber’s confused sense of sky.

They wear almost nothing—a scrap of stripped bast knotted around one hip, nothing loose that might snag on the splintered walls or gather the clear, weeping resin. The air is warm and close enough that clothing would be a nuisance, and clutter is impossible here; anything dropped onto the floor would rattle and thunder every time the walls swelled. Instead, everything they own is pressed into the architecture of the wound itself: the three knotted fibers funneling sweat from the root-node, the shallow basin scraped into the living floor, and the single chipped flake of pale stone wedged into the weeping seam.

Watch their hands. The knuckles are broad and scarred with tiny, pale lines where wood-splinters have been dug out; the nails are worn flat and stained dark at the quick from scraping wet cambium. Clear resin has dried along the backs of their wrists in thin, amber crusts. When they move to adjust the stone flake, they do not reach straight across the open air—they know better than to trust the stretching void in the middle of the room. They stay close to the wall, moving with a slow, deliberate economy, and they time their touch to the stutter of the chamber.

Just before a heavy drop detaches from the ceiling, ripples stir across the lukewarm water in the basin. The dweller sees them and freezes, jaw slightly slack so the low, teeth-vibrating hum of the xylem does not ache in their skull. The drop falls. It strikes the floor like a mallet on wet hide. In the ringing, deafening aftermath—while the wood groans and a fresh fiber parts somewhere behind the bark with a pistol-sharp *crack*—the dweller works. A quick, practiced twist of the pale stone keeps the seam from healing backward over itself. A thumbnail clears a bead of thick sap from the knotted bast so the funnel stays open. Then they settle back onto their heels, motionless again before the echo has even died.

They do not seem to mind the noise, though they rarely add to it. If they speak, it is only in a dry, breathy murmur pitched beneath the hydraulic groan of the roots, or perhaps they have forgotten how to use a voice in a room that turns every footstep into thunder. They listen instead—with their palms, their bare soles, the flat of their cheekbones pressed briefly to the warm wall—reading the massive biological engine running just behind the bark.

Beside the scraped basin, the thumb-sized, blind woodlouse shifts its segmented legs, waiting for the same tide. The dweller does not brush it away, nor do they harm it. When the sweet, lukewarm water rises by a fraction of an inch, they dip two fingers into the basin and bring the moisture to their lips, leaving the rest for the pale creature at the rim. In the bright, shadow-sharp air, with daylight passing straight through the woodlouse’s thin carapace, the two of them look less like keeper and pest than two different kinds of patience sharing a single, temporary lung.

How long they have been inside this blister is impossible to tell; in a place where torn wood un-splits itself for a few seconds before snapping open again, a day and a year feel much the same. Perhaps they were here when the pocket first ruptured, or perhaps they followed the sound of straining xylem through the dark and pried this room open themselves, carrying that single shard of vanished stone from a world that no longer has geology.

Either way, they will not stay forever. When the root-system finally remembers which way is down, or when the wound grows too stubborn to wedge open any longer, they will pull the pale stone from the seam, unknot the three lengths of fiber, and press their ear to the groaning wall—listening for the next thin place where the sap is straining, ready to tear open another pocket of stolen noon.
