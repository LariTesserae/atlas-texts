They are built for gripping and leaning, the people of the hollow—broad-shouldered, short-necked, and heavy-thighed from a lifetime of hauling wet timber up slick rungs and bracing against the sudden, sideways slam of mountain squalls. Even on flat ground, if they ever stood on any, they would walk with a slight forward pitch and knees unlocked, the gait of someone who expects the floor to give an inch under the heel. Their feet are wide and toughened, shod in thick-soled alder clogs scored deep across the bottom or bound with strips of rawhide that swell tight when soaked. Their hands are the real tell: permanently stained along the knuckles with peat-tannin and bog-iron rust, the palms calloused into pale horn from hemp cord and adze-handles, the fingernails split and blackened with pine-tar.

Nobody here has a quiet voice, and nobody has delicate hearing. Years spent between the roar of the back cliff and the rattle of a hundred wooden sluices leave the older folk slightly deaf to anything pitched low; they speak in a clipped, barking cadence, pitching their words sharp and nasal so they cut through the water-noise. When two people meet on a narrow catwalk, they don’t step back—there is nowhere to step—they simply hook an elbow around a convenient upright, press chest-to-shoulder to let a basket of eels pass, and shout their business straight into each other’s ear-rims while keeping one eye on the footing. Beneath the shouting, they carry on a second, silent conversation with their chins and fingers: a raised thumb for a loose lashing overhead, two fingers hooked downward to warn of a slick plank, a sharp whistle pitched just like the starlings’ alarm-note when a heavy load is coming down the rope-ways.

They dress for grease and drip rather than warmth alone. Next to the skin goes rough, unbleached linen that never quite dries, then layers of felted wool heavy enough to turn a cold drizzle, all of it sealed under sleeveless aprons of tallow-rubbed hide and cloaks boiled in linseed and birch-bark. Everything they wear smells faintly of sour milk, alder-smoke, and wet dog. Because cloth rots almost as fast as cedar in the bowl, nothing is worn for show unless it can survive mildew: hammered bog-iron brooches shaped like crab-claws or trout-bones, belt-toggles carved from goat-horn, and tufts of iridescent starling-wing or jay-feathers jammed into the bands of their stiff felt caps. A person’s standing in the hollow can be read at a glance by the dryness of their tinder-box—worn in a sealed bladder-pouch under the armpit—and the quality of the grease on their boots.

Their society is arranged not by streets or families so much as by *drip-lines* and *anchor-trees*. In a place stacked four tiers deep, whatever happens above you eventually lands on your roof, and whatever burns below you seasons your floor. The highest perches, lashed into the upper forks where the wind whips over the limestone rim, belong to the young, the nimble, and the restless—rope-makers, shingle-splitters, and goat-herds who don’t mind the howling drafts and the sudden blinding glare when the mist parts. They get the cleanest air and the first sun, which means they can dry hemp and cure fish-strips without everything turning furred with green mold, though they pay for it every time a squall flattens their awnings.

The middle tiers—tucked deep under the interlocking crown of the cloud-oaks—are the thick, warm heart of the place. Here live the elders, the weavers, the iron-beaters, and the smoke-keepers, wedged into hunchbacked bark huts that share upright posts and borrow heat from one another’s sand-box braziers. It is dim here, lit by tallow-dips and shafts of silver-green light bouncing off wet leaves, and the air hangs thick with the fat smell of roasting turnips and eels. Beneath them, down in the perpetual twilight just above the springy peat-mattress, dwell the people who work the wettest trades: the hide-scrapers, the weir-tenders, the basket-weavers soaking willow-switches in the runoff, and the peat-cutters who know how to slice fuel from the living floor without opening a sinkhole into the cold springs underneath.

Living this tightly packed makes them fiercely practical and utterly unsentimental about property. A roof-beam isn’t yours if your neighbor’s walkway is lashed to its outer end; a sluice-trough belongs to whoever clears the dead leaves out of it before dawn. Quarrels erupt fast and loud—over a leaky pig-pen dripping into a drying-rack, or a child who cut a vine-lashing too short—and end just as fast, usually settled by the nearest group of bystanders who need the argument cleared off the gangway so they can get a cask of brine past. Theft is rare, mostly because there is nowhere to hide a stolen goat or a dry cloak when everyone can see through the slats of your floor and hear your floorboards creak. Instead, the great social sins are carelessness and rot: leaving a brazier-sand damp so it cracks, letting a main support-cord fray until it snaps, or dumping offal into a clean water-chute. Someone who lets their timber go black and spongy without patching it is treated like a menace to the whole tree, and three neighbors will show up with drawknives and green cane to tear the bad wood out themselves, shouting curses the entire time.

And woven right through the human crowd—indistinguishable from it, really—are the beasts that make the hollow work.

The goats are small, slab-sided, and mean-tempered, with long, greasy coats that shed rain like thatch and hooves split wide and rubbery for gripping mossy bark. They aren’t kept in pens; they are tethered on swivel-ropes to the thicker boughs, browsing the pruned oak-shoots and screaming whenever they want their tether moved. They know the layout of the tiers better than most children, leaping across three-foot gaps between walkways with a casual rattle of horns and expecting people to duck out of their way.

Down in the gloom beneath the lowest planks, the pigs rule the peat. They are short-legged, barrel-chested things covered in stiff black bristles, with snouts like iron wedges. They never see the open sky, rooting endlessly through the composted drop-down of the whole settlement—cabbage stalks, fish-heads, goat-pellets, rotted bark-shingles—turning what would otherwise become a festering bog into churned, steaming mulch. Every household knows the exact grunt of its own sows, and children are sent down with long ash-poles to prod them out of the drainage channels before they dam the runoff and flood the lower huts.

Above and around them all swarm the birds. The cliff-pigeons and highland ducks aren’t really wild and aren’t quite tame; they roost in wicker baskets hung beneath the eaves like giant wasps’ nests, fed on bruised grain and kitchen scraps in exchange for eggs, feathers, and meat. The starlings are nobody’s property and everybody’s nuisance—thousands of them, glossy and loud, nesting in the hollow trunks and erupting in roaring, swirling clouds whenever a dog barks or a mallet strikes a peg. The people watch the starlings the way sailors watch clouds; when the flocks suddenly drop silent and pack themselves tight under the bark awnings, everyone knows a cold squall is about to spill over the limestone rim, and hands move faster to batten down the shutters.

Even the smallest things have a place in the hollow’s economy. Boys wade into the icy runoff channels with horsehair nooses to snare dark, speckled trout and freshwater crabs from under the root-tangles. Old women keep wooden boxes of moss packed with orange-bellied salamanders, which they sell or trade to the hide-tanners and medicine-mixers for their sharp, bitter skin-slime, used to seal leather seams against the damp. Thick-bodied dung-beetles and midges feed the frogs; the frogs feed the ducks; the ducks drop feathers for the pillows and fat for the boot-grease. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be carried out easily over the steep limestone rim, and nothing can be left to lie idle without turning foul.

What binds all of them—human, goat, pig, and bird—is a kind of cheerful, relentless urgency. They do not build for their grandchildren; they build for next Tuesday, knowing the damp will eat whatever they nail down before the season turns. Their songs are short, rhythmic work-chants timed to the *thwack* of wooden mallets and the *scritch-scritch* of drawknives stripping green bark. Their stories are mostly about great storms, famous slips where someone fell forty feet through three tiers of laundry and landed unhurt in a pig-wallow, or the legendary dry summer forty years back when the back-cliff stopped roaring for three days and nobody could sleep for the eerie quiet.

When night settles into the bowl and the mountain cold drops down like a stone, the noise finally thins out—not to silence, never that, because the cliff-water never stops and the frogs only get louder in the dark. The shutters are barred with ash-pegs. Braziers are banked low under clay hoods, sending thin, sweet threads of alder-smoke curling up through the floorboards to keep the midges down and the damp off the wool. Goats sigh and shift on their boughs; pigs snort in the warm mud below; starlings rustle and mutter in the thatch. And tucked inside their hunched, creaking shells of bent sapling and moss, the people sleep light and huddled together, one ear always cocked for the snap of a wet rope or the sudden drum of rain on the bark overhead, ready to grab an adze and climb before the wood gives way.
