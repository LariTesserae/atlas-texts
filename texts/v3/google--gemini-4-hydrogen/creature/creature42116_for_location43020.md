If you stand on the terrace at midday, with the sun pouring cleanly over the lip and the bass note of the stone shelf vibrating in your ribs, the place seems empty of anything that breathes. You see the seven or eight colossal trees standing far apart across the bare slate. You see the pale, pearlescent travertine crusting their lower trunks, fusing them into the floor. You see the shallow mirror-puddles, and, if you peer into the deepest basin under the overhang, the single pale salamander hanging motionless near the bottom.

It takes patience—longer than a visitor usually has—to notice that some of the travertine mounds tucked among the tree roots are not fused to the rock at all.

They are the size of hearthstones or curled-up hounds, low-domed and asymmetrical, their backs built up in pale, overlapping plates of lime-crust, pitted by decades of mineral-rich drips and flecked with tiny tufts of emerald moss. At rest, pressed flat against the wet slate or wedged into the crevices of the back wall, they are indistinguishable from the stone that made them. Only when the afternoon mist rises over the cliff’s lip, dimming the glare and cooling the open floor, do they stir.

First comes a faint, glassy overtone—barely audible above the deep organ-hum of the chimneys—as the chambers inside their shells shift and equalize. Then, slowly, a dome lifts an inch from the rock.

Beneath the stony carapace, there is no blood, no bone, no red flesh. What lives under that pearlescent roof is a dense, translucent architecture of tough cartilage and pressurized water-sacs, clear and pale as the leaf-bladders hanging from the boughs above. They have no eyes; in a world where nothing with wings or blood lingers, sight is an unnecessary expense. Instead, the lower rim of each creature is fringed with smooth, sensitive lobes that press against the wet floor and read the terrace by its vibrations.

To them, the amphitheater is not a silent room filled with noise, but a detailed map of sound. The continuous bass hum of the updrafts tells them where the limestone is thickest and where the flooded chimneys yawn beneath their feet. Every heavy drop falling forty feet from the overhang into a basin sends a bronze-bowl ring shivering through their fluid-filled bodies, measuring the fullness of the pools. Even the slow, agonizing swell of the leaf-bladders above is something they feel—a rising tension in the boughs, day after day, week after week.

When they move across the open stone, they do not crawl. They extend three or four thick, translucent hydraulic pillars from beneath their shells, lifting their mineral-armored weight clear of the puddles and gliding forward in slow, deliberate pulses. They leave no trail, only a faint disturbance in the mirror-water that settles in seconds.

Their lives are bound to the colossal trees. In the cool mist, they climb the spongy, moss-wrapped trunks, their fleshy lobes gripping the wet bark with quiet suction. They graze on the silver-green lichen and draw water from the condensation runnels, and when they reach the lower bark—where the sap runs mineral-sweet—they press a small, calcified pore against the wood until a bead wells up, drinking slowly and leaving behind a neat, pale scar that heals over in a season.

Most of all, they wait for the booms.

When a melon-sized leaf-bladder finally reaches its tipping point and the stem flexes, dumping a gallon of water onto the hollow stone below, the thunderous concussion rolls around the curved walls and shakes the canopies. For the dwellers below, that rare, violent shock is a kind of weather. It cracks away the oldest, heaviest layers of travertine from their backs so they do not grow too rooted to move, and it shakes loose a fine dust of pollen from the epiphytic orchids high in the boughs. The pollen settles onto the damp moss and onto the slick, pearlescent domes of the creatures sheltering beneath—and over the weeks that follow, as they glide from one isolated tree-island to the next across the empty floor, they carry that dust with them, keeping the jungles alive.

They are not many—perhaps a dozen across the whole terrace, though it is hard to count things that spend half their lives disguised as boulders. They do not crowd one another, any more than the trees do. When two meet at the rim of a deep basin to draw in lime-rich water, they simply pause, their internal chambers humming in a brief, glassy harmony, while the pale salamander hangs untouched in the dark beneath them.

When the climber came up from the lowlands long ago, stepping onto the humming stone with a gourd and a bone awl, the dwellers would have felt those strange, heavy footsteps long before the climber reached the trees. They would have pulled their translucent lobes tight beneath their domes and sealed themselves to the wet slate, becoming stone again. Perhaps the climber leaned against one while tapping the mineral-sweet sap from the oldest tree, mistaking it for a warm, slightly resonant knot of travertine—and never knew, when they left the flint wedge in the crevice and climbed back down into the mist, that the rock beside them was listening to them go.
