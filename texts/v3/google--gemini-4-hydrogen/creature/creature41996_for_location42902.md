Down in the basin, nothing has the luxury of softness. Every living thing here is either armored against the stone, translucent enough to vanish into the glare, or quick enough to outrun the ground collapsing beneath it.

### The Wet Channels and the Chimneys

Where the warm, mineral-heavy water braids through the lowest pans, life begins as a slick, rust-colored skin. It is not a plant, though it drapes the gravel like wet felt; it is a dense, living mat of iron-eating microbes that thickens in the heat until it traps tiny bubbles of steam beneath its surface. When the bubbles swell and pop, they release a sharp, metallic tang into the air.

Grazing this film are the **plate-backs**—flat, oval creatures no wider than a palm, whose segmented shells are built less from horn than from the basin itself. As they grow, their tissues draw calcium and silica straight out of the scalding trickles, laying down jagged, chalk-white ridges that match the fresh-fractured travertine around them. From above, a cluster of them looks like nothing more than a scatter of mineral chips. Only when the water shifts do they glide forward on broad, muscular feet, rasping the rust-slime clean with file-like tongues and leaving pale, scoured tracks in the gravel.

Darting between them are **glass-needles**—slender, finger-length swimmers so clear that the only visible parts of them are their dark, pinprick eyes and a thin amber thread of gut. They live in a frantic race against evaporation. When a saucer-pan begins to shrink and crust over with salt, the glass-needles burrow into the cooling silt, curl nose to tail, and exude a clear, salty gum that hardens into a bead. They can lie like that for months—looking like spilled quartz pebbles—until a wandering seep dissolves their shells and wakes them in a single afternoon.

Along the steaming fissures, where the ground sighs wet heat into the dry air, live the **chimney-colonies**. Inside each porous, fluted tower of calcified dust and hardened mucus pulse hundreds of eyeless, pale-pink worm-bodies, anchored in the damp rock below. They have no mouths for biting; instead, they extend crowns of feathery, damp filaments into the rising steam, catching airborne mineral-mist and stray spores. A colony can raise a fragile, whistling spire taller than a person in a matter of days—only for a passing tremor or a clumsy scavenger to snap it at the base, showering the flats with chalky rubble and exposing the soft, writhing core to the waiting swarm.

### The Swarming Floor and the Blinding Air

Across the dry travertine terraces move the **chalk-runners**—blind, pale, many-legged arthropods that form the restless, whispering carpets of the basin. Their flat, waxy carapaces reflect the brutal midday sun, keeping their insides from cooking while they scour the flats for sulfurous rinds, dead flyers, and the spilled insides of shattered chimneys. They navigate by scent and vibration, surging in vast, colliding ribbons that hiss like poured grain over the salt-crust. Every few days they split out of their own skins, leaving behind drifts of hollow, papery shells that pile up in the wind-shadows of the boulders.

Just beneath them, the loose talus and thin gypsum crusts are worked by **sappers**—heavy-shouldered, shovel-snouted burrowers with broad, spade-like forelimbs and backs plated in thick, overlapping horn. They rarely show themselves in the flat glare. Instead, they tunnel through the warm, unstable rubble, chewing through the buried roots of chimney-colonies and listening for the patter of feet overhead. When a sapper undermines a shelf of travertine, it waits until the crust caves in under the weight of a passing drift, then surges up through the dust to drag down whatever was stunned by the fall.

High above this ground-level frenzy wheel the **glint-wings**. Neither bird nor true insect, they are light-boned, four-winged gliders whose membranes are stiffened with fine, translucent plates of chitin that flash like mica in the sun. Because the basin offers no trees and the midday rock is hot enough to blister, glint-wings spend their lives on the thermals rising from the steam vents, spiraling in loud, papery clouds. They swoop down in steep, whistling dives to snatch chalk-runners off the stone, cracking them open in midair and letting the empty husks rain back down. Only when the sun drops behind the rim do they fold their flashing wings and hook themselves upside-down beneath the cool basalt overhangs, packed together like dry leaves.

And in the deep, shadowed cracks of the talus wait the **heavers**. Low-slung and wide-stanced to spread their weight across brittle salt-crusts, they have tough, blistered, slate-gray hides that turn the razor edges of the basalt and resist the scalding steam. Their long, crooked legs end in splayed, calloused pads; their broad heads carry heavy, serrated jaws that snap shut with an audible *clack*. They spend the worst heat wedged motionless in the dark stone, conserving moisture. But the moment a landslide rattles down a slope, or a larger beast falters on a fractured terrace, the heavers haul themselves up through the crusts with startling speed, stripping meat and marrow before the dust has even settled.

### The Fat-Bearers

Moving slowly through all of this—rare, solitary, and immense compared to the frantic life around them—are the **tallow-backs**.

A tallow-back is a barrel-ribbed, low-bellied grazer the size of a small cart, built to endure a world with no plants and no shade. Its underside is sheathed in thick, overlapping scutes of horn-hardened skin, worn smooth from sliding across razor-edged basalt; its broad, pillar-like legs end in wide, leathery cushions that grip loose talus without sinking. Along its arched spine runs a ridge of fused, mineral-stained bone plates that ward off diving glint-wings and snapping heavers alike.

Beneath that armored roof, packed around its long, curved ribs, lies what makes the creature precious: a dense, foot-thick layer of pale, oily fat. In a basin where water vanishes overnight and microbial blooms shift without warning, that fat is both insulation against the feverish stone and a reservoir of moisture and energy. A tallow-back feeds by sweeping its wide, shovel-lipped jaw along the rust-slime channels and straight through the dense ribbons of chalk-runners, gulping down mud, arthropods, and mineral grit together, grinding it all in a heavy, stone-filled gizzard.

They live for decades if they are careful, following the faintest scent of new steam. Yet the basin spares nothing forever. When an old tallow-back misjudges a hollow gypsum crust and snaps a leg, or gets caught by a sudden eruption of scalding vapor, its collapse draws every scavenger within half a mile—and, if they are down on the floor to hear the rumble, the makers from the rim.

### The Makers from the Rim

The tool-users do not belong to the basin, and they never pretend to. They live up on the plateau, where the wind blows cool and the ground holds still. To them, the rift below is a blistered, roaring quarry—a place you enter like a diver entering deep water, take what you can carry, and leave before it closes over you.

They come down the sheer, jagged steps in small, tight-lipped parties of four or five, never alone. Everything they wear is shaped by the basin’s hostility:
* **On their feet and shins**, they bind thick, layered wraps of cured hide reinforced with plates of dried tallow-back scute, keeping the razor-sharp basalt from slicing their ankles and the chalk-runners from swarming up their legs.
* **Over their eyes**, they tie narrow slits of carved horn or smoked mica to cut the flat, blinding glare off the pale travertine.
* **Around their mouths and noses**, they damp-wrap strips of rough cloth to filter the wet chalk, sulfur, and drifting mineral dust.

Each carries a long, hooked pole—part walking-staff, part probe to test thin gypsum crusts before trusting them with full weight, and part weapon to fend off heavers from a fresh kill or a knapping ledge.

They descend for four things the plateau cannot easily give them: slabs of pure, precipitated salt; fresh, glassy obsidian fractured out of the dark basalt blisters; light, hollow bundles of shed chalk-runner carapaces (which they dry, lash together with sinew, and carry up for kindling, lime, and rattle-markers); and, above all, the rich, sulfur-scented fat and long, curved ribs of a fallen tallow-back.

When they work, they work fast. One keeps watch with a hooked pole while another squats on a flat basalt shelf, striking gleaming black flakes from a volcanic core so they only have to carry the sharp, finished blades back up the cliffs. They stack low, unmortared windbreaks to shelter their water skins from the heat, drive bone pegs into cracks to hang their carapace-bundles above the swarming floor, and lash rib-bones across narrow, hissing rifts to cross without slipping.

By late afternoon, dusted white with chalk from brow to boot, they haul their lashed burdens back up the jagged steps toward the rim. Behind them, the basin immediately begins erasing their visit—coating the discarded obsidian flakes in salt, undermining the windbreaks, and reclaiming the stone before dark.
