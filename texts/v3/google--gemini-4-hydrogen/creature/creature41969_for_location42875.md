You do not see them at first, because they have learned the same lesson as the *parchment-drifters*: in a room where the air itself is a solid wall of pressure, you survive by living in the seams where the noise cancels itself out.

They crouch in the dead-air pockets behind the ledges and along the exact geometric troughs of the floor, motionless as the silica-stiffened ferns. When one finally shifts, it does not dart or scurry. It leans forward with a slow, deliberate tilt, stepping cleanly over the pale ridges of Chladni dust without disturbing a single grain, its weight distributed so evenly that it seems less to walk than to slide along the invisible contours of the standing wave.

They are slight, narrow-shouldered beings, no taller than a child’s waist, built entirely for the kiln-heat and the drought. There is no softness to them, no wet sheen of eye or lip. Their skin is the color of smoked amber and cured vellum, stretched taut over long, hollow-feeling limbs and dusted with the same pale mineral powder that coats the basalt floor. Where joints bend—at the knees, the wrists, the sharp ridges of the spine—the hide is oiled with a dark, slow-hardening resin harvested from the *pitch-creepers*, keeping them supple in air that would crack anything water-born within hours.

Their heads are long and slightly flattened at the crown, hairless, with deep, lidless sockets where dark, glassy eyes catch the bruised-amber glow of the rock. They do not blink; there is no dust blowing in a room without wind, and no tears to wash it away if there were. Instead of external ears, the sides of their skulls are flared into thin, fluted plates of horn—curved like the carapaces of the *drum-backed husks*—that sit flush against their temples.

They do not hear the roar as sound. To them, the deafening, flat-lined hum of the chamber is simply *space*—the medium they inhabit, as unremarkable as gravity. What they feel through those horn-plates, and through the calloused, splayed pads of their feet, is the architecture of the pressure: the invisible walls where the air batters, the narrow corridors of stillness, the faintest tremor of a *spine-crawler* dropping a pellet of ash three ledges away.

Watch them long enough, and the strange little structures scattered across the hall begin to make sense. They are not shrines, and they are not traps. They are *edits*.

A figure detaches itself from the shadow of a *spore-cushion*, carrying a flat splinter of basalt pinched between two long, resin-stained fingers. It approaches one of the hairline fissures in the floor—a natural reed shrieking softly beneath the larger roar—and listens, pressing the side of its horned skull close to the stone. Then, with infinite care, it wedges the basalt splinter into the slit and seals the edge with a bead of warm pitch from a pouch at its belt.

The pitch of the fissure drops by a fraction of a tone.

To human ears, the difference is swallowed instantly by the room’s vast groan. Yet across the chamber, three *parchment-drifters* tremble in midair and drift sideways by an inch, chasing the shifted quiet of their node. On the floor, a line of pale dust shivers and redraws itself, sharper than before. The figure watches the dust settle, its chest—ribbed like a bellows, drawing in the warm, sulfurous gas in slow, shallow cycles—rising and falling once in satisfaction.

This is how they tend their world. They do not till soil or herd beasts; they tune the rock. By narrowing a slit here, or wedging a reed there, they coax the standing wave into new shapes—moving the quiet pockets to shelter a fresh cluster of *bladder-ferns*, or steering the drifting clouds of ochre spores toward a ledge where they can be scraped into dry, nutrient cakes.

The tripods of vine-wood and bone serve a subtler purpose. Each holds a scraped-out, hollow seed-pod aimed at a specific angle toward the ceiling, positioned precisely at the intersection of two acoustic currents. When one of the chamber-dwellers presses its horned temple against the wide mouth of a pod, the gourd acts as a filter, stripping away the crushing bass of the room and isolating a single, pure harmonic—a high, thin thread of sound pulled from the roar like a silver wire from ore. They will sit beside a tripod for hours, motionless as stone, listening to that single note. Whether it is prayer, or meditation, or simply the way they check the health of the vents beneath the floor, is impossible to tell.

They do not speak with throats. Soft tissue cannot compete with an organ pipe that never stops blowing. Instead, they converse in the register *above* the roar—using the hanging chimes of anhydrite shards, brushing them with a fingernail to send a sudden, glassy shimmer slicing through the heavy bass, or clicking their own hardened, resin-tipped fingers together in crisp, dry rhythms that carry cleanly along the stone. Sometimes, when two meet in a narrow passage, they simply press their horned temples together and let the vibrations of their own hollow chests pass directly from bone to bone.

And then there is the slab in the center, with its rows of crisp, white tally marks.

In a pocket where nothing rots, nothing rusts, and nothing cools, time has no natural teeth. A frond snapped a century ago lies beside a living thicket, unchanged; a splinter wedged into a fissure before memory holds the exact same note today. Without seasons, without darkness, without decay, there is no way to know that *now* is not *then*.

So they make their own clock.

Every so often—perhaps when a new reed opens in the floor, or when one of their kind slows, dries out completely, and ceases to hum—a figure walks to the central slab with a sharp shard of glass-stone. It kneels in the dust, draws the edge across the basalt with a brief, grinding screech, and leaves a fresh white groove beside the others.

Their dead do not leave the chamber. There is nowhere to bury them, and nothing to consume them. Instead, when one stops, the others prop the dry, weightless shell in an alcove or along a high ledge, its amber-oiled skin hardening into something indistinguishable from the *pitch-creepers* around it. In time, its hollow ribs and flared horn-plates are taken for the tripods, or hung from sinew to catch the air—not out of grimness, but because in a closed world, nothing is ever discarded.

Even in death, they stay tuned to the room. The warm gas passes through their dried chests; the standing wave hums through their bones; and the fierce, loud equilibrium closes over them without a ripple, holding them forever in the middle of its own breath.
