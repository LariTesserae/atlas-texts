If you only stay for a few minutes, you will swear nothing lives here at all—nothing, at least, that has a will of its own. The amber bulbs cast no shadows that twitch. The ferns hang heavy and still under the weight of condensation. Even the water seems to fall in fixed, predictable curtains.

To see what inhabits the basin, you have to stand on the perforated iron grates and wait through the engines. You have to let one stroke finish—the thirty-second sink of the piston, the groan of the sluice, the chest-rattling *thoom* of the cistern—and then wait through a second, and a third.

Only then do you notice that one of the moss-hung stanchions on the third-tier catwalk has shifted half a foot closer to the boiler housing.

It does not walk so much as it *settles* forward, one heavy, deliberate transfer of weight per stroke of the counterweights. Up close, it is easy to mistake for a length of bundled conduit or a corroded valve-column wrapped in wet burlap. It stands roughly the height of a man, though it stoops slightly beneath the low-slung copper pipes, its upper half draped in a broad, cowl-like mantle of overlapping shelves. Those shelves are made of the same translucent, copper-tinted fungus that rings the dynamo housings, slick with mineral dew and faintly warm to the touch.

Beneath the mantle, there is no bone, no meat, no animal heat. Its frame is a dense, braided column of pale, fibrous root-matter, toughened by decades of boiled-iron steam and darkened on the outside by a crust of black engine grease and verdigris. It has no eyes to trouble with the bruised twilight overhead, and no mouth to draw breath. Instead, it works on the same principle as the basin itself: warm water and pressure.

When it needs to lift a limb—a thick, cable-like appendage ending in a splayed fan of wet fibers—chambers deep inside its core draw up warm groundwater from its base, swelling until the joint stiffens like a charged hose. As the limb reaches its extension, a tiny, quiet hiss of steam vents from a seam along its flank, and the fibers relax. Nothing about it twitches. Nothing about it hurries.

It perceives the pit through the iron grates underfoot. The broad, pad-like soles of its base press flat against the metal, reading the *thoom* of the cisterns and the low, steady hum of the oil-cooled transformers. To it, the basin is not a room; it is a single, resonant body, and the rhythm of the beam engines is the only pulse that matters.

When that pulse falters—when a sluice gate sticks on a knot of mineral scale, or a cast-iron trunnion runs dry and begins to whine against its housing—the vibration through the grates changes. The tender feels the dissonance and turns, slowly, over the course of ten or twenty strokes, toward the source.

It has no tools, because it does not need them. If a copper flange has begun to weep too fast, wasting pressure from the line, the tender presses its fibrous flank against the gap. Over an hour, it weaves a thick, pale mat of its own living root-tissue tightly around the bolted collar, swelling the fibers with warm water until the leak slows to a bead, then stops. When it pulls away, the new gasket stays behind, severed cleanly from its side, already stiffening into the permanent crust of the pipe. If a frond of dark-emerald fern has grown too far into the teeth of a gear-wheel, the tender lays a heavy, grease-slicked limb across the stem and leans its weight until the plant yields and tears away, dropping into the flooded sumps below to feed the next generation of moss.

There are four or five of them scattered across the four tiers—perhaps more, hidden in the permanent veil of mist near the highest trusses. They have no names for one another, and no speech. When two of them meet on a cantilevered catwalk too narrow to pass, they simply lean together for a dozen strokes of the engine. Their damp, fungal mantles touch; a slow trickle of mineral-rich water and a faint, stray charge from the dynamos pass between them; perhaps a few spores of the copper-tinted fungus trade places in the steam. Then, with the patience of two shifting walls of brick, they slide past one another—one descending toward the warm sumps to drink, the other rising toward the boilers to dry its crown.

Around them, smaller things share the same quiet kingdom. Fist-sized, translucent bladders of membrane and root cling to the undersides of the sweating copper pipes, swelling and contracting in exact time with the water hammer. Along the brick piers, flat, crusted plates of mineral and mycelium creep an inch a day toward the amber bulbs, not for the light, but for the faint, dry warmth of the glass.

None of it crawls. None of it flies. None of it leaves a bone behind when it finishes. When one of the tenders finally grows too heavy with mineral scale to lift its own hydraulic limbs, it simply stops beside a pier or a boiler housing, sinks its root-fibers into the nearest seam of mortar, and lets the waxy vines climb its flanks. In a year, it is indistinguishable from the brick. In ten, a new shoot of pale, fibrous root has already detached itself from the base, swollen with warm groundwater, and taken its first slow step onto the vibrating grates.
