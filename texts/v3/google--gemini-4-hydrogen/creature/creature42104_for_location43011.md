At first, you would swear the basin is empty of anything that thinks. You are looking for things that dart, that breathe audibly, that break the surface of the water or leave tracks in the slick silt. Nothing here does that. 

Instead, you have to notice the heat.

The travertine walls hold the warmth of the air, radiating a dull, even fever against your palm. Yet if you walk the colonnades long enough, trailing your fingers along the bone-pitted stone in the dark, you will eventually touch a pillar that is *too* warm—not the dead, stored heat of rock, but the steady, slightly higher warmth of a living core. Its surface is softer than the rest, yielding a fraction of an inch beneath your knuckles like damp chalk, and slick with a thicker coat of that oily mineral film.

If you stay still beside it, you will feel it *draw*—a faint, rhythmic suction through the pinholes in its crust, pulling the humid air inward without a sound.

They are not animals, and they do not move like animals. For hours at a time, while the lukewarm water sits low in the lagoons, they are indistinguishable from the architecture they inhabit. They stand in the colonnades, or sit along the submerged tiers of the amphitheaters, or wedge themselves into the honeycomb niches of the windowless towers. With every silent tide that recedes, the mineral-heavy water leaves another paper-thin skin of fresh travertine over their bodies, sealing them into stillness. Between tides, they are calcified, patient, and blind.

It is only when the water rises again—oozing up through the porous floors and weeping out of the walls—that they loosen.

As the warm flood climbs their legs and softens the fresh crust, a single, muted *tock* breaks the silence. A hairline crack opens down the side of what looked like a buttress, leaking a dull, greyish phosphorescence for three or four seconds. In that brief, ash-colored glow, you see what lives here: a tall, narrow figure, pale as wet lime, peeling a sheet of half-dissolved stone away from its shoulder.

They have the rough, upright geometry of people—heads, torsos, long-fingered limbs—because the city was grown for that shape, or because that shape is what the city remembers. Yet there is nothing of flesh-and-blood hurriedness about them. They have no eyes; the unbroken darkness has bred smooth, pitted domes of cartilage where faces ought to be, scored only by fine vertical slits that open slightly to taste the dissolved minerals in the steam. Their skin is porous, fluted like the columns, and wet with the same heavy fluid that fills the basin. Beneath the chalky outer hide, they are mostly hydraulic—chambers of warm, pressurized brine and flexible lime-cartilage that stiffen in the dry-air intervals and go supple in the flood.

When they step into the water, there is no splash. They lower broad, flat, silt-colored feet with such agonizing deliberation that the thick liquid parts around them without breaking, sliding up their shins in silence.

Even then, they rarely walk the distances between districts. Walking is for things that live in fixed space.

The basin's strange physics are not an accident of the drowned valley; they are the wake of its citizens. When one of these pale, porous dwellers stands at the edge of a honeycomb gallery and wishes to reach a solitary tower half a mile out in the dark, it does not trudge through the knee-deep mud. It plants its feet, opens the slits along its throat, and *pulls*. The distance between the two buildings thins out like stretched tallow. The humid air compresses, growing heavier against the chest, and the tower simply arrives—while behind the traveler, the open lagoon folds quietly into a sunken courtyard that wasn't there a moment ago.

The tilted lagoons are their work, too. You will find them standing knee-deep at the edge of a sloping sheet of water, long arms submerged to the elbow, holding the liquid at an impossible angle against gravity. They are herding the mineral load, pooling the saturated water against a mudbank or a half-formed terrace so that, when the next tide sinks away, a new set of steps or a wider causeway will precipitate out of the stillness. They do not own chisels or mortar. They shape the water, and the water builds the city around their intentions.

Their gatherings are equally quiet, conducted in a language of pressure and tension that leaves the air untouched.

In the solitary amphitheater out in the dark water, dozens of them will sit along the damp, concentric tiers during the height of the tide. None of them speak; sound dies too quickly in the feverish mist. Instead, they lean forward and lay their long, bone-pale fingers flat upon the lukewarm surface of the central pool. All at once, they begin to *draw*—not with their mouths, but with the porous chambers of their chests and palms.

Concentric ripples appear out in the darkness and travel *inward* toward the center of the arena, converging on a single pinhole of flat water where they vanish without a sound. Through those returning rings of pressure, they read one another: the state of a distant causeway, the slumping of a mudbank, the slow, collective memory of a plaza that sealed itself shut a century ago. Now and then, one of them flexes a calcified joint too sharply, fracturing its own skin in a brief flash of grey light—a silent, luminous punctuation in the dark.

Between these gatherings, they tend the basin's meager garden. You can feel them moving softly past the bases of the colonnades, grazing their porous palms over the patches of black, velvet-textured crust that drink the condensation, absorbing the concentrated nutrients directly through their skin. They linger beside the rings of pale, leafless stalks, waiting patiently through the hours as the translucent bladders swell with warm gas. When a stalk finally splits without a sound, the dwellers lean into the rising plume, drinking the fresh heat that keeps the basin hovering near the temperature of a living body.

They do not seem to age in any way that matters, though they do eventually *settle*.

Sooner or later, one of the citizens grows tired of the effort required to crack its shell when the tide rises. It finds a place where a wall needs thickening, or an arch needs a buttress, or a sunken plaza is missing a column. It plants its broad feet in the silt, closes the slits in its skin, and stops drawing the warm water inward.

The next tide coats it. And the next. The pale, chalky cartilage hardens into true travertine. The black velvet crust creeps up its shins, and the pale gas-stalks take root in the crevices of its ribs. Within a few decades, the heat inside it fades to match the stone around it, and it is simply another part of the city—ancient, weathered, and seamlessly melted into the terrace beneath it.

And the rest of the basin goes on as it always has, in the steaming, breathless dark: the living walls slowly shifting the water, folding the distances, and growing a city out of their own bones.
