If you were to stand in the center of this pocket, you would not know at first that you were not alone. There is no rustle of cloth, no scrape of a boot, no shift of shadow. Even if your outstretched hand came within an inch of the one who lives here, you would feel no rush of body heat; the skin of this place and the skin of its inhabitant are the exact same blood-warm temperature, slick with the same tepid film of lime and iron.

You would know them first the way you know the chamber itself: through the air. Beneath the rare *plock* of falling drops and the low, geological hum of the mountain’s core, there is a rhythm—a slow, deep intake of breath, held for a long moment in a broad ribcage, and then released in a wet, unhurried sigh.

Whoever lives here breathes like someone who has made peace with air that never quite finishes the job. Their chest is deep and wide, built over years—or perhaps generations—for pulling what little oxygen exists out of a thin, saturated stillness. They take only a few breaths a minute. Between one exhale and the next, they are as motionless as the travertine shelves.

They are folded, more often than not, into a natural hollow near the back of the chamber, where the raw basalt has cooled into a slightly smoother curve. They sit with their knees drawn close, their heels resting in the inch of coppery water on the floor, their spine pressed against the wet stone. They wear nothing; cloth in this mist would either rot into slime or stiffen with mineral crust until it sawed the skin raw. Their bare body is pale as tallow, softened by perpetual damp, though the skin of their palms, knees, and the soles of their feet has thickened into a tough, leathery horn from decades of gripping unweathered rock.

In the creases of their elbows, behind their ears, and along the ridge of their collarbone, the weeping water has left faint, chalky traces of lime, as if the mountain were slowly trying to coat them the same way it coats the grass cords. Their hair—what remains of it—hangs in heavy, pale ropes, the ends stiffened and calcified by the mist. Their eyes are kept shut. Whether they are blind from birth or simply ruined by years of mineral-heavy damp does not matter here; lids closed against the wet wool of the dark are a mercy, keeping the lime-water out.

Instead, they see with the backs of their fingers, with the soles of their feet, with the fine hairs along their forearms that register the slightest displacement of still air.

When they move, there is nothing sudden about it. A sharp jerk in a room lined with mineral needles is a death sentence. They unfold with a practiced, liquid patience, testing each space before committing their weight to it. Their hands—long-fingered, scarred across the knuckles with old, pale lines where the basalt bit them years ago—reach out to find the familiar landmarks. They know every fin and shelf by heart now, the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth, yet they still pause at the central rib of basalt to run a broad thumb across the horizontal notches ground into the stone.

Every so often, when the fresh lime threatens to fill those notches and smooth them away, they take a sliver of old bone from a crevice and scrape the grooves clean. The calcified grass cords stretched between the pillars they leave untouched, letting the mineral mist turn them to brittle stone. They no longer need the cords to keep from falling, though perhaps they leave them there out of habit, or memory—a remnant of a time when their own hands were new to the dark, or when another traveler still passed this way.

Their days—though there are no days here, only the slow turning of the water’s weight—are spent in a state that is neither sleep nor quite waking. They tend the low stone lip of the catching-basin, clearing away any flake of crust that might breach it, and drink by dipping their cupped palms into the tepid pool. They eat sparingly, as the chamber dictates. With the edge of a chipped stone or a fingernail, they peel away strips of the pale, rubbery mats that grow along the warmest sulfur seams, chewing them slowly for their metallic, cartilaginous pulp. When their fingers, sweeping the undersides of the travertine shelves, encounter one of the blind, soft-shelled creatures clinging there, they pry it loose with a gentle twist—never taking more than one, leaving the rest to their long wait.

Sometimes, they reach up to where the hair-thin roots hang from the ceiling like damp thread. They do not pull them down. They only pinch the very tip of a root between thumb and forefinger, or touch it briefly to their tongue, tasting a faint, sharp bitterness—a rumor of soil, of frost, of wind and sun from meters of solid stone above. It is the only news they ever receive from the outside, and it requires no answer.

They are not waiting to be found, and they are not trying to escape. Like the chamber itself, they have settled into a humid equilibrium—neither growing nor crumbling, held intact in the warm, wet dark, listening to the mountain breathe around them.
