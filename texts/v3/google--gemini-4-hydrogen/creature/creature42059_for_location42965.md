At first, there appears to be nothing here at all, because whatever lives in the hollow has long since agreed to the hollow’s terms.

It rests near the back wall, half-sunk in the dampened silt, and in the flat, secondhand dusk it is easily mistaken for one of the fallen boulders. Its back is a smooth, pale dome, the color of bone-white limestone dusted with warm grey. There are no sharp angles to it—no jutting shoulders, no pointed elbows, no bristling hair or scales where dust might snag or a spider might anchor a thread. Like the stone around it, its edges have been rounded by time into a single, settled curve.

If you watch long enough—longer than most people are willing to stand in a warm, silent room—you notice that the dome rises and falls. Not with the quick, anxious rhythm of a mammal’s ribs, but with a tide so slow it seems almost geological. One breath takes the space of three or four water drops. When it exhales, the air does not stir; it simply adds a small, unnoticed measure of warmth to the heavy pocket trapped behind the scree.

It is roughly the size of a person curled tightly against the cold, though there is no cold here to curl against. Its skin is matte and chalky, dry across the upper slopes of its back and shoulders, and faintly darkened—the color of wet slate—along the flank where it leans against the sweating seam of the back wall. Over decades of stillness, a thin, pale crust of lime has precipitated into the creases of its joints, and in a shallow hollow behind one ear-ridge, a patch of the yellowish-green moss has taken root, no thicker than a fingernail.

It has no use for hurry. Its limbs are thick and heavy, padded with dense, slow-moving flesh that tapers into broad, blunt hands and feet. There are no claws or nails, only smooth, calloused pads worn down to the shape of river pebbles. When it decides to shift its weight, it moves with an economy so complete that it looks less like locomotion and more like a boulder settling deeper into its bed. It lifts a limb, sets it down, and moves on; behind it, the fine silt holds the faint, rounded impression of its passing for an hour or two before smoothing flat again.

Its face, when it turns it toward the dim light of the scree ridge, is broad and quiet, dominated by a heavy, overhanging brow. Its eyes are rarely open. Most of the time they remain two pale, horizontal seams, because in a room where nothing changes from hour to hour, sight is an unnecessary expense. Instead, it knows the hollow through the soles of its feet and the flat of its palms pressed against the cool floor—feeling the minute tremor of each water drop striking a saucer, the slow creep of moisture down the back wall, the distant, muffled vibration of wind passing over the ridge outside.

It does not eat the way animals eat, which is why the hollow keeps its mineral cleanliness. There are no scraps, no droppings, no scent of rot to summon flies or beetles. Instead, it drinks from the shallow stone saucers by lowering its wide, lipless mouth to the surface and drawing the water in so gently that the skin of it barely dimples, leaving the reflection of the curved ceiling unbroken. Once every few days, it uses a broad, flat tongue to lick the pale crust of precipitated lime from the rim of a saucer, or grazes the oldest, darkest ribbons of liverwort from the dampest stone—never stripping a patch bare, only taking what the plant has already finished with. The rest of its sustenance comes through its skin, absorbed directly from the warm, wet film of the back wall while it sleeps.

And it is the keeper of the hollow’s small, quiet geometries.

It was this creature, long ago, that pecked the four shallow scoops into the sloping limestone slab near the scree—back when it still climbed out into the night to feel the wind on the plain, before its joints grew too heavy and settled for the ascent. It was this creature that gathered the six flat stones and laid them in a ring around the dry depression in the center of the floor, though it no longer remembers what the ring was meant to hold; now it simply curls inside it from time to time, resting its chin on one of the lichen-furred stones because the curve of the circle fits the curve of its spine.

And beside the wettest seam at the back, it tends the three stacked stones. Whenever the slow creep of moisture or the imperceptible settling of the silt threatens to tip them, a broad, chalk-dusted hand reaches out, adjusts the top stone by a fraction of a finger’s width, and withdraws, leaving them balanced in that quiet equilibrium that looks almost accidental.

Then it settles back against the sweating rock, closes the pale seams of its eyes, and listens to the *tock* of the water. It is not waiting for anything to arrive, or for anything to end. It is simply keeping the hollow company, matching its temperature to the stone, and taking its time.
