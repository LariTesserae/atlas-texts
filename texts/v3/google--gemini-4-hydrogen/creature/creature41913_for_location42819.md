At first, even if you kneel at the lip and force your watering eyes open against the glare, you will not see them. There is too much white, and the sound is too thick to think through. Everything below you looks like what it is: dried earth, salt-crust, and dead weight.

You have to stay at the rim until the cold draft numbs your fingers, and your eyes begin to separate one shade of bone-grey from another. Only then do you notice that not all of the pale surfaces are clay.

Wedged deep inside the throats of the wider amphorae, and packed into the hexagonal cells of the honeycomb blocks, are things that match the vessels down to their velvet-matte finish. They are the color of old chalk and dried gypsum, sandblasted smooth by the same centuries of grit that wore down the thumb-presses. From above, what little of them shows looks like mineral plugs—flat, slightly cupped plates of salt-bleached crust sealing off a broken rim or a jammed chimney.

They do not crawl, and they do not cling like insects. They are shaped to the spaces they occupy, grown over decades to fit the exact curve of a clay throat or the corner of a pinched bell, locked in place by ribbed, papery flanks that press outward against the mineral walls. If you could lift one out—though there is no room for your fingers, and nothing down there yields without shattering—it would feel lighter than pumice, dry as a wasp's nest, and cold as the cellar air.

There is no moisture in them. They have no blood, no sap, no wet gut to dry out in the salt-laden draft. Instead, their bodies are hollow lattices of chalk-rich membrane and fine, brittle cartilage, chambered like tiny lungs. 

They live on the scream.

Because the pocket's chord never falters, every vessel in the hollow is in a state of constant, violent tremor. That vibration passes straight through the clay walls and into the creatures wedged inside them, setting the fine hairs and fluted passages of their interiors humming at the exact pitch of their host vessel. In that relentless shaking, the dry world inside them behaves like a liquid. Microscopic flecks of windblown lichen, salt crystals, and chalk dust—gathered from the air that skims their open upper vents—do not settle at the bottom of their chambers. The sound keeps the dust suspended, swirling it in steady, silent currents through dry sieves and mineral teeth, stripping out whatever trace energy the salt and spores hold before venting the spent powder back into the draft.

Without the note, they would clog and starve in their own dust. They are entirely blind—eyes would be useless in a bowl of shadowless glare—and they have no ears, because they live inside a single, unbroken tone. Yet they are acutely awake to the air.

You only see them move if you break the wind.

If you lean too far over the lip, or lower a hand toward the cool air rising from the hollow, your body interrupts the steady shear of the pressure gradient. For a second, the draft stutters. A cluster of chimneys directly beneath your palm drops a fraction of a tone, the tight whistle thinning into a dry, breathy rasp.

Down in the blinding white, the clay answers.

Inside the faltering chimneys, the pale plugs flex. Pleated, chalk-grey collars—until then indistinguishable from coiled clay—tighten or widen by the width of a hair, adjusting the apertures of the throats to catch the lost air. In the honeycomb blocks, papery membranes shiver and draw taut, compensating for the drop in pressure. Dozens of them, blind and silent inside the roar, shift together by fractions of an inch, fighting to pull the chord back into its exact, load-bearing pitch.

The moment you pull your hand back and step away from the lip, the draft smooths out. The collars relax into their grooves. The note locks flat again, dense as stone, and the glare erases them back into the vessels—leaving only the pale, screaming courtyard, and the sense that the earth beneath your feet just blinked.
