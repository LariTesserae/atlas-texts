At first, you do not see them, because they wear the patina of the basin as naturally as the brick wears its mineral salt. Against a retaining wall stained with rust and lime-runoff, a figure in an apron of stitched conveyor-belting and a wide-brimmed hat of woven wire looks like nothing more than a bundle of maintenance gear left beside a valve-wheel. Only when they shift—stepping with a slow, economical grace from a sun-baked parapet onto a moving cableway bucket—do you realize the mountain is inhabited.

They are built for the thin air and the kiln-heat. Barrel-chested and deep-ribbed, they breathe in long, unhurried draws that match the rhythm of the beam-engines. Centuries beneath the naked arc-lamp of the high sky have cured their skin to the color of oiled terracotta, dusted at the creases with pale lime-grit and the white zinc-paste they smear beneath their eyes to cut the glare off the concrete aprons. Their hands are broad, calloused, and permanently stained at the cuticles with copper verdigris, tallow-grease, and the milky sap of fig-stems. Most go barefoot on the warm masonry and the grated catwalks, their soles thickened into horn that can read the temperature of a steam-line or the faint, distant shudder of a failing bearing before an ear could ever catch it.

In a valley where the roar of flywheels, cicadas, and whistling relief-stacks never stops, shouting is a waste of precious breath. Close together, the basin-folk speak in a tight, low-pitched murmur that vibrates in the chest, pitched to sit beneath the metallic thrum of the high-voltage lines. Across the wide terraced gaps, they do not call out at all. They speak with their hands—sharp, angular gestures borrowed from the flight of the swifts—or they use the iron itself. Every worker carries a short brass rod or wears a heavy iron ring on the middle knuckle; a coded pattern of sharp taps against a handrail or a pressurized water-pipe travels half a mile through the basin’s metal skeleton in an instant, ringing out clearly beneath the din like a bell struck underwater.

They do not think of themselves as masters of the superstructure, any more than the starlings or the fig trees do. To them, there is no distinction between the built and the grown; the brick cliffs are simply the geology of the world, and the boilers deep below are its warm, beating heart. Their lives are spent in a kind of attentive grooming, keeping the great, slow machine from choking on its own abundance.

Up along the dizzying ridges, teams of fin-scrapers in canvas harnesses climb the vast condenser-sails during the dry morning hours, chipping away the crust of wind-blown grit and bird-lime from the galvanized mesh so that the afternoon clouds will still weep freely into the zinc gutters. Down in the middle tiers, orchard-tenders move through the vertical gardens with curved pruning knives, cutting back the aggressive tendrils of squash and thorny creepers before their roots can pry apart the vital mortar of the aqueducts. They are constantly negotiating with the basin’s other tenants. They leave bowls of bruised figs and pressed olive-mash on the warm boiler-casings to keep the pale-faced monkeys from stripping the porcelain insulators off the pylons, and in return, the grease-smudged tribes hunt the beetles that bore into the gourd-vines. When the bees swarm inside a disused junction box, the tenders do not drive them out; they simply hinge the iron door so they can harvest the dark, mineral-rich honey without disturbing the hive.

Nothing here is hurried, because the clock of the basin does not run on human impatience. Meals are eaten in the shifting bands of cool shadow cast by the groaning, pivoting sun-shields—thick flatbreads baked directly on the clean iron casings of the exhaust flues, eaten with roasted gourds, dried figs, and salt-cured dove. Water is drunk from earthenware flasks, always tasting faintly of zinc, copper, and wet stone. When the afternoon mist rolls through the terraces, soaking the air in a sudden, cool silver hush, work pauses for an hour. People sit along the dripping stone runnels with their hats off, letting the condensation gather in their hair and run down their necks, watching the kestrels ride the wet drafts.

Because there is no raw earth anywhere in the valley, every ounce of soil is an heirloom, and the dead are not wasted. When someone grows too old to climb the catwalks, or when a lung finally gives out to the thin air and the brick-dust, their body is not burned or sealed away. It is carried to the compost-drums—great, slow-turning cylinders of brick and riveted iron mounted near the middle-tier boilers, warmed by waste heat. Packed in with pruned fig-leaves, wood-ash, crushed mortar, and the rich guano scraped from beneath the dove-cotes, the dead are turned slowly over the course of a season until they become dark, sweet-smelling loam. The basin-folk carry that new earth out in baskets to pack around the roots of young olive trees and cane-brakes. A child grows up eating fruit from the terrace where their grandmother’s bones were compounded into soil; there is no grief in it, only the quiet satisfaction of staying inside the walls.

Yet there are those who never reach the compost-drums, or who delay it by descending.

Far below the sunlit orchards and the screaming swifts, down through the grated floors where the air turns blue, damp, and heavy with the smell of hot oil and ozone, lives a quieter sort of inhabitant. These are the elders whose eyes have been milky-blinded by decades of the high-altitude glare, along with the few young ones born with a strange, stillness-loving temperament that makes them uneasy under the open sky. They live in the dim vaults of the deep dynamos, where the sun has never touched a brick.

Down there, their skin loses its terracotta burn and turns the pale, cool gray of wet lime-mortar. They wear little more than greased loincloths and heavy leather ear-flaps to dull the endless, subterranean thunder of the water-lifts and the beam-engines. In the blue shadow, they have no need for sight. They move along the catwalks and vast flywheels by memory and vibration, their long, oil-slicked fingers trailing constantly over the cast-iron housings. They are the ones who pack the tallow into the ancient bearings, who tighten the weeping flange-bolts, who listen with their palms pressed flat against the masonry for the slightest hiccup in the mountain’s deep, mechanical breath.

Sometimes, a young orchard-tender leaning over a parapet in the bright afternoon will look down through the floor-gratings and see a pale, grease-darkened hand reach out from the shadow of a brick arch to test the drip of a pipe, or feel a slow, reassuring *tink-tink-tonk* rise up through the soles of their feet from three hundred feet below. It is the sound of the deep-tenders checking the pulse, reminding the sunlit tiers above that the machine is still turning, the heat is still rising, and the mountain’s skin of leaves, feathers, and people is safe for another long, unhurried hour.
