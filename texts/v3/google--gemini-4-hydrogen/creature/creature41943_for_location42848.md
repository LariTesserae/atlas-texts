If you cross the basin quickly, you will think it is empty of anything larger than a beetle. The light is too bright for casual looking, and the inhabitants do not announce themselves. They have no roads, no fires, no voices to carry across the thin air.

You only begin to see them when you stop moving and let your eyes adjust to the valley’s tempo.

Near a concrete plinth, or crouched beside the brushed-aluminum lip of a bunker, a pale, angular shape that you took for a transformer housing or a slab of sunlit quartzite will shift. Not with the sudden start of an animal, but with the same hydraulic creep that turns the flywheels inside the substations. If you hold your gaze on the edge of a shoulder or the curve of a forearm, you will see it clear the shadow of a strut over the course of half a minute.

They are tall, spare figures—humanoid in their proportions, though it is hard to tell where their bodies end and their casings begin. Every surface of them is built to refuse the sun and hoard the cold. They wear—or are sheathed in—articulated plates of pale, matte ceramic and silvery, waxed membrane that mimic the reflective cuticles of the shrubs and the mirrored backs of the valley’s beetles. Where a joint bends, dark, pleated insulation seals the gap against the biting air. Their heads are smooth, featureless domes of chalk-white composite, split by narrow, horizontal visors of dark, polarized quartz that turn the aching pewter sky into something bearable.

You will never see a plume of frost from their mouths. They have no visible mouths at all, only fine, louvered vents along the collar and ribs, packed with a damp, spongy mesh that traps the moisture of every shallow breath before it can escape into the dry air. In a place where water only exists for an hour after dawn, exhaling vapor is an extravagance no one can afford.

This is also why there are no tracks in the damp hollows.

To step off the rock and onto the springy mat of moss and lichen is to tear the seal that holds the valley’s water. The inhabitants never touch the undergrowth. Instead, they travel the basin along the thick, black armored conduits that snake beneath the canopies, placing narrow, grooved soles squarely on the cable sheaths and gliding from station to station without brushing a single waxy leaf. Where the conduits disappear beneath the moss, they step cleanly from one sharp shard of basalt to the next, their balance effortless, their limbs moving without swing or haste.

They are the tenders of the basin’s equilibrium, though it is difficult to tell whether they serve the machines or the machines serve them.

In the fierce, silent hours before noon, you might find one standing motionless beside a porcelain insulator, one gloved hand resting lightly against a copper busbar. They do not eat often—there are no crops here, no herds, only a dense, cold paste pressed from lichen and shrub-seed—so they draw what warmth they need directly from the plinths, letting a slow, low-amperage charge trickle into their cores to keep their blood from stiffening in the altitude chill. Around them, the static-saturated air lifts the glittering motes of pollen into a motionless halo.

When they work, they work at the pace of the valley. One might spend an entire morning clearing a single fallen shard of quartzite from the crown of a broad-leafed plant so the canopy can funnel its morning condensation undisturbed, or slowly wiping mineral dust from a parabolic mirror with a chamois dampened by dawn rime. Another might kneel by an electrolytic vat, waiting patiently for the weekly cycle to finish, watching the fluid levels shift by fractions of a millimeter.

Because the air is too thin and the silence too absolute for speech, they do not call to one another across the slopes. When two of them need to confer from opposite sides of the basin, they unclip small, hinged mirrors from their wrists and angle them into the white glare. A razor-edged flash of light leaps half a mile across the silver scrub—crisp as broken glass, utterly soundless, readable only to the dark quartz visor waiting on the far ridge. Up close, they do not even need the mirrors. They feel each other’s approach through the static prickle of the air, or lay their fingers together on the same black conduit to pass slow, faint electrical pulses along the buried line.

Even the rare animal life seems to fold into their quiet. The grey, long-winged gliders that drift from ridge to ridge will sometimes circle a lone figure standing on a pylon, using the slight thermal rising off a warmed ceramic plate, while the pale, mirrored beetles climb unmolested up their shins to glean mineral salt from the joints of their greaves.

Only when the sun drops behind the jagged western crest does the basin finally empty. As the temperature plummets and the thin rime of frost begins to gather on the sharp rock, the inhabitants retreat along the conduits to the low, windowless bunkers. Flush-sealed hatches open without a hiss and close again without a click. Inside, in the dark, pressurized warmth beside the slowing flywheels, they wait out the freezing night—conserving their heat, their moisture, and their charge until the sun clears the eastern ridge and wakes the valley’s dampness all at once.
