If you stand still in the grey-green twilight long enough for your eyes to adjust, you still will not see it at first. Everything in the hollow is rounded, wet, and glistening with the same unbroken film of water, and the thing that lives here has spent so long among the travertine columns that its silhouette obeys the same rules as the stone.

What looks like a low, bulbous shelf of flowstone tucked into the curve of a back basin is actually its flank. Only if you watch the reflection of the ceiling in the brim-full water beside it—watch it without blinking, for two or three minutes—do you notice a faint, rhythmic trembling in the glassy surface, as slow as the pulse in a salamander’s throat.

It is roughly the size of a crouched person, though it has no real posture of its own. Its body is long, supple, and slightly flattened, built for a place where nothing can stand upright and there is no room to swing a limb. Four long, double-jointed arms—or legs; it uses all four the same way—fold tight against its ribs when it rests. Its hands and feet end in broad, pale pads, soft and ridged like the undersides of mushrooms, able to grip the wax-smooth calcite without slipping. Where the stone is slickest, it does not push off; it simply presses its skin flat, letting the thin film of water hold it by suction while it shifts its weight an inch at a time.

Its skin is the color of wet pewter and pale cream, mottled along the belly and flanks with faint, permanent stains of rust-orange and sulfur-yellow where it has lain for months against the submerged biofilms. It has no hair, no scales, no sharp edges anywhere. Its head is a smooth, blunt oval set directly onto its shoulders, with two shallow, lidless hollows where eyes might once have been—skin stretched unbroken over the bone, sensitive only to the difference between the dim noon-gloom of the cleft and the total dark of night. Behind its jaw sit two small, recessed pits that catch the muffled *plip* of falling drops and the faint, glassy whisper of water creeping over a rimstone lip.

Because the chamber is warm and the air is thick, it barely needs to breathe. Its ribs rise once every few minutes, drawing in the smell of iron and wet lime, then settle back into the water.

When it moves, it does not break the silence. It uncurls from its basin with an unhurried, liquid patience, pouring itself sideways through the narrow gap between two fused columns—the same gap where someone long ago pecked footholds into the rock. Water sheets off its back in a single, soundless veil. Because it has lain still for so long in the lime-heavy seep, thin, brittle crusts of white mineral have formed along the ridges of its spine, its shoulder blades, and the backs of its knuckles, just like the salt rimming the clay bowls on the ledge. As it bends to squeeze between the pillars, those thin plates fracture with a tiny, dry *tick*—the sharpest sound the hollow ever hears—and flake off into the pools below, where the pale amphipods and translucent snails will gather to graze on them.

It feeds the way everything else here feeds: without hurry, and without waste. It glides over the submerged ledges, pressing its broad, flat mouth against the rock to rasp away thin ribbons of the orange and yellow biofilms, leaving pale, clean tracks in the slime that will take weeks to grow back. Once in a great while, when a beetle or a blind millipede tumbles down through the root-choked cleft above and hits the water, the creature is already turning toward the ripple before the insect has stopped struggling. Its long fingers close around it—not a strike, just a slow, inevitable folding—and the pool settles back into a mirror.

It does not bother the blind salamanders. Sometimes one will climb straight over its submerged shoulder, mistaking it for another shelf of calcite, and the creature will not even twitch.

It has no use for the dryish ledge high under the overhang, yet it knows the things left there. On the rare occasions when heavy rain on the unseen hillside above sends a cold trickle threading down through the tree-roots, chilling the nearest pools, the creature retreats into the deeper, warmer shadow beneath the overhang. There, it sometimes reaches up a single, dripping hand to rest its palm against the rim of the unbroken red clay bowls. It does not understand them as vessels, only as odd, rough-textured stones that never seem to grow slick with biofilm, and it lingers there for an hour or two, feeling the tiny, regular shock of the stalactite’s drip hitting the water trapped inside.

It remembers the tallow lamps, dimly—the sudden, violent orange glare that made the wet curtains of stone jump with black shadows, the harsh smell of burning fat, the heavy scraping of boots and chisels against the travertine. When the people came, it slid deep into the narrowest cleft behind the columns, flattening itself under the dark water until only its nostrils broke the surface, and waited. It waited until the lamps went out, until the voices faded up through the choked crack, until the soot on the ceiling cooled and the water settled back to its exact level.

It is still waiting, though it no longer remembers what for. Nestled back into its crescent basin, its skin slick with condensation and its back beginning to gather a fresh rime of pale lime, it has become part of the chamber’s balance—taking in only what seeps down from above, giving back only the faintest warmth to the water, as steady and unhurried as the next drop gathering at the tip of the stone above its head.
