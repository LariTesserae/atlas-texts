If you stand on the lowest steps long enough for your eyes to adjust to the slanted glare and your ears to go numb beneath the racket, you begin to notice that not every shadow in the scaffolding belongs to a wicker tray or a bundle of reed-matting. 

Something else moves through the middle tiers—slowly, almost without weight, testing each frayed hemp rope with a crooked toe before trusting it.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, though it is hard to count in a room where nothing sits still for long and every surface is broken into slats and rungs. At a glance, they look like they were pinched out of the same ochre plaster and damp clay as the walls. They smear their skin and cropped, matted hair with the slick grey mud from the lowest weep-holes to smother the bird-mites that drop from the rafters; as it dries, it flakes off in pale scales, leaving them streaked with lime and dusted in fine down. They wear little more than twisted belts of cedar bark and tattered aprons of knotted reed-matting, stiffened with dried droppings. 

They are thin—light-boned and sinewy, with long, flat-muscled backs and ribs that show clearly when they stretch between the beams. A heavier body could not live here. The old counterweight ropes are brittle with lime, and the cedar planks are split and weathered; to survive five stories above the dished brick floor, you have to learn how to spread your weight across three struts at once, never letting your whole mass settle in one place. Their feet are broad, calloused into thick, dark leather at the heel and ball, with toes splayed wide from gripping rounded timber. When they pause on a cross-beam, they crouch with their knees tucked up to their chins and their knuckles resting lightly on the wood beside their heels, looking less like people than like large, pale, featherless birds waiting out the heat.

The noise has remade them. Years—perhaps generations—of living inside a ceramic echo chamber have worn away their use for small, spoken sounds. Their eardrums are scarred and flat to the high chittering of the starlings, though they are never truly deaf. Instead, they listen with their bones. When the wind rises on the plain above and the whitewashed chimneys begin to hum, they press their palms or the flats of their skulls against the retaining walls, drinking the low, resonant thrum through their teeth and ribs. By the pitch of the masonry, they can tell a dry gale from a coming squall hours before a single drop of rain falls through the light-shafts.

Among themselves, they speak through the lattice. Because the whole volume of the pit is tied together by lashed timber and taut hemp, a message travels faster through wood than through air. Two sharp knuckle-raps on a cedar plank, a pause, and a measured pluck on a vertical hoist-rope will send a tremor three stories up, right into the soles of another’s feet. They have no names that could be shouted over the wingbeats; each one is known by a rhythm—a particular way of tapping the scaffolding when they approach a shared ledge, so as not to startle a sleeper into losing their grip.

They keep to the shadows between the bright columns of daylight, moving in the same slow, liquid patience as the rat-snakes. If you rush in the pit, you set off a panic of wings that blinds you in dust and chalky feathers. So they glide. They hang upside down beneath the catwalks, threading their long, lime-whitened fingers into the mouths of the terracotta pots from behind or above, feeling for the warm curve of an egg or the naked, pulsing weight of a squab. A quick twist inside the dark of the jar, and the bird is dead before its neighbor in the next niche even ruffles its throat. 

They eat almost everything the pit offers, and almost nothing that requires a fire—there is too much dry timber and hanging reed here to risk a spark. They crack eggs against their teeth, strip the bitter, dusty leaves from the stunted fig trees under the roof-rim, and snare the rat-snakes by pinning their heads behind the jaw with forked cedar twigs. When a rainstorm passes overhead, they scramble out onto the central walkways with hollowed gourds to catch the clean water falling straight down the shafts, before it strikes the floor pans and turns foul with guano. 

The only thing they take from the very bottom is the saltpeter. They descend the slick, fern-hung rungs only briefly, scraping the pale, bitter crusts from the evaporating pans with flat shards of broken pot, then climbing quickly back up out of the thick, ammonia-heavy damp. High up near the mouths of the light-shafts, where the warm thermal updrafts blow dry and steady, they hang thin strips of dove-breast and snake-meat on lengths of frayed twine, rubbing them with the saltpeter until they cure hard and dark.

They are not the ones who built this place. On the mid-level ledge where the hand-forged copper scrapers sit half-buried in feathers, they step right over the metal without looking down. To them, those heavy, flat-bladed tools are just another oddity of the masonry, like the carved brick channels or the glazed catch-basins—artifacts made by creatures with thick boots and loud voices who used to come down from the bright, terrible openness of the plain. 

When one of them grows too stiff to trust the catwalks, or too slow to dodge a sudden panic of wings, they do not carry the body down to the wet bottom, where there is no soil to dig. Instead, they carry the light, dry frame all the way to the top, tucking it into a deep crevice behind the shadow of the brick funnels, beside the motionless owls. There, the dry heat of the updrafts and the floor-beetles strip it clean in a matter of days, leaving only hollow, chalk-white bones that the swifts eventually weave into their nests.

By dusk, when the bats pour out of the deepest crevices and the air turns cool and thick with dust, you can barely tell they are there at all. They curl into the wide wicker trays or sling themselves in hammocks of knotted reed-matting beneath the beams, swaying slightly as the wind hums through the chimneys above—just another layer of the old, settled clutter, breathing in rhythm with the masonry.
