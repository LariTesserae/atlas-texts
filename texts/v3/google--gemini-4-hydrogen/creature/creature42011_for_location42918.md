At first, you might mistake them for the grazers. They move at the same low, hunched pitch through the waist-high sedges, broad-shouldered and dark, shedding sleet from greased, matted pelts. Only when one pauses to drive a hooked ash-pole into a peat bank to test its weight do you see the knees bending the wrong way for a beast, the blunt, gloved hands, the tight-bound wrappings of boiled alder-bark strapped around the shins to keep the mud from sucking their boots off.

They are human, or close enough to it that the shape is unmistakable, though generations of the valley’s cold and noise have pared them down to something denser and quieter. They are short-necked, barrel-chested, and scarred across the cheekbones by wind-driven grit. Their outer layers are stitched from the thick hides of the broad-hoofed grazers, worn fur-side in and rubbed raw-side out with rendered fat and the oily plumage-grease of the storm-birds until water beads off them almost as cleanly as it does off the metal pylons.

Nobody speaks out in the open. There is no point; a shout vanishes three inches from the mouth, eaten by the river and the wind. Instead, they plug their ears with tight wads of bog-cotton soaked in tallow to keep the pressure-snaps from bursting their drums, and they talk with their hands. It is a language of grip and rhythm—sharp, flat-palmed taps against a partner’s collarbone, a clenched fist pressed into the small of the back, a series of quick squeezes on the forearm to signal a shifting peat-bank or a ninety-degree shear coming off the ridge. They move in linked pairs or threes, tethered at the waist by braided sinew when crossing the braided channels, because a man who slips alone into the churning peat-water under a tangle of dwarf alder will drown before anyone hears him thrash.

Their homes are tucked into the lee of the matte-gray housings. 

They do not worship the seamless composite boxes wedged into the scree, nor do they wonder what they are regulating. To the people of the valley, a housing is simply a dry wall that never goes cold. They dig shallow, narrow warrens right up against the back sides of the metal, roofing the trenches with ribs of twisted alder and overlapping slabs of shattered slate, then packing live black-green moss into every crack until the roof disappears into the slope. 

Inside, the air is startlingly still. The roar of the valley drops to a muffled, rhythmic thudding through the stone, and the seamless gray wall radiates a steady, dry heat that smells faintly of hot dust and ozone. It is the only place in the basin where anything ever truly dries. Boots and greased hides hang from the low rafters; strips of dark grazer-meat cure on bone racks strung a few inches from the warm composite. The people sleep pressed together on beds of dried fern-fronds, bare-skinned and pale where the sleet never touches them, waking every few hours not to sunlight—which never comes—but to the change in the housing’s hum when the storm-load shifts outside.

Even the smallest niches around these warm circles are crowded. Because the housings keep a neat ring of moss steaming in the sleet, they have become tiny, isolated tropics for things that would freeze ten feet away. Thick, rubbery mats of pale orange fungus crust the edges of the composite, and fat, blind slugs the size of a thumb graze on it in sluggish herds. The people scrape the fungus off with slate knives to boil into a bitter, thickening paste for their stews, and they gather the slugs to render down for lamp-tallow.

Out in the marsh, they treat the pylons with the same blunt practicality. They know the pitch of the three needles better than they know their own pulses. When the air around the gravel bar begins to shimmer and the bone-rattling hum climbs toward that tearing, static shriek, anyone within a quarter-mile drops flat into the mud, pressing their palms over their ears and opening their mouths to equalize the pressure. 

The *whump* hits like a dropped boulder, flattening the sedges in a fifty-yard ring and sending a sheet of water skittering across the puddles. Before the reeds can even spring back, the people are on their feet and scrambling into the flattened circle. The shockwave stuns the amphibious scavengers in the shallow channels and tosses hundreds of the blind, pale crustaceans onto the mud. For three frantic minutes, the dwellers work with wicker scoops and hooked gaffs, stuffing sacks before the river surges back in and the sedges whip upright again.

They are not the only predators working the storm’s edges. In the deep, slow-moving backwaters where the peat-banks overhang the channels, something else waits—wide-bodied, flat-headed creatures with pale, milky eyes and jaws hinged like traps. They are neither fully fish nor fully beast, covered in loose, mottled skin the exact color of wet slate, and they lie motionless beneath the submerged alder roots until a heavy-shouldered grazer—or a careless dweller—steps onto a rotten bank. When the peat slumps, they lunge with a silent, muscular twist, dragging their prey down into the dark water before the surface even finishes rippling. The people carry heavy, iron-shod ash poles specifically for them, prodding every overhang before they step, and when they manage to haul one of the mire-beasts out onto the gravel, its tough, oily hide makes the best boots in the valley.

Above them all, the dark, heavy-winged birds wheel along the cliffs, and the people watch them the way sailors watch a barometer. When the birds stop fighting the updrafts and drop suddenly into the lee of the crags, shrieking at that sharp, metallic pitch that cuts through the water’s roar, the dwellers pack their scoops, tighten their waist-tethers, and head for the scree slopes. It means the katabatic squalls are about to tear down from the unseen ridges, and the pylons are about to answer at full, erratic load.

Beneath the lone cantilevered platform hanging out from the cliff, nobody goes at all. The sub-audible vibration dripping off its underside makes a person’s teeth ache in their gums and turns their stomach to water, and the continuous trickle of dancing shale would split a skull in seconds. Yet even there, life finds a use for the noise: a species of small, leathery-winged bat-thing—blind and clinging with hooked thumbs—roosts in the dry underside of the platform’s shadow, entirely unbothered by the hum, darting out into the sideways waterfalls to snatch the swarms of cold-midges that hatch in the moss.

Nothing in the valley waits for the weather to clear, because the weather is not an interruption; it is the medium everything swims in. Inside the slate-roofed warrens, a woman greases the seams of a child’s bark shin-guards while the matte-gray wall hums steadily behind her shoulders. Outside, the river tears another clump of ferns from the bank, a pylon snaps the air flat over the marsh, and two dark, hunched figures step out from the reeds, leaning forward into the sleet to check their traps.
