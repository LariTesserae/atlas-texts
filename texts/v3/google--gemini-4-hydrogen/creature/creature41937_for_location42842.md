At first, you would not see them at all. Against the iron-red curtains of giant kelp and the foot-deep pelts of sea-moss, the eye expects movement to mean life—a darting shadow, a scuttling claw—and here there is none. You could drift for a league over the sunken plazas and believe the plants had the basin entirely to themselves.

You would only begin to notice what lives here if you looked down, past the gnarled holdfasts, at the worn hollows in the flagstones.

Where bare feet and wooden sledges once polished the limestone smooth, pale threads lie pressed into the silt. They are the color of tallow or wet parchment, no thicker than twine, and at a glance they look like the bleached roots of some dead weed. They do not touch the living kelp. Instead, they gather around the fallen fronds—the heavy, dark-olive blades that have grown too old, torn loose in the glacial current, and slumped into the silt to rot. Wherever a frond settles, the pale threads thicken, creeping over the decaying margin like cold wax, sealing it from the water as they slowly draw its substance back along the stone.

Follow those threads, and you will see them braid together. As they leave the bruised slate-blue light of the plazas and enter the velvet-lined tunnels of the narrow alleys, they flatten into broad, pearlescent ribbons that hug the contours of the slumped lintels and the rounded, algae-upholstered stairs. They do not dart or coil. If you pressed a finger to the stone beside one and waited an hour, you would only just perceive that the ribbon’s leading edge had crept a hair’s breadth further into the dark.

Inside the drowned granaries and the deep, terraced cisterns, where the last stray photons of blue light fail, the ribbons end—and the dwellers themselves hang waiting.

Because the coralline algae has cemented the overlapping slate roofs into seamless, water-tight shells, the gases rising from centuries of decaying vegetation cannot escape. They pool beneath the apex of every corbelled vault, forming a flat, trembling meniscus that gleams like an inverted mirror of mercury in the near-total dark. Anchored to the stone walls by dozens of pale, taut guy-lines, each dweller hangs suspended directly beneath that trapped pocket of gas, buoyed up into the ceiling of the room like a lantern of wet, translucent silk.

They are neither plant nor animal, though they share the patience of the former and a strange, cold hungering that belongs to neither. A fully grown dweller is a broad, lung-like sac, as wide across as a hearth, its membrane thin enough to show the slow, internal weather of its body: a cloudy suspension of dissolved limestone, dark-red pigment salvaged from digested kelp, and tiny, bright pearls of gas pinched off from the ceiling above. It has no eyes, no mouth, no bone, no shell. It does not need them in a world where nothing hunts and nothing flees.

Instead, it listens with its whole skin to the heavy, still water.

In a silence so complete, every small event carries for hundreds of paces. The dweller feels the faint, wet *shush* of the kelp forests bowing outside, and knows from the rhythm of that lean that the vast continental current is shifting a degree warmer or colder leagues away. It feels the muffled *tock* of a pebble dropping from an ancient wall, and sends a slow pulse of pale ribbon out along the flagstones to see if the fall has uncovered fresh silt or brought down a tangle of edible weed. When its own trapped gas leaks—a soft *blup-blup* slipping out from under the arch—the whole hanging sac shudders gently, adjusting the tension of its guy-lines to hold its place in the dark.

Because their lives are measured in decades rather than seasons, the dwellers have grown into the shape of the architecture itself. Each major cistern, each sunken granary, each vaulted under-arch holds its own solitary, hanging presence, though their pale foraging-nets often meet and tangle along the causeways outside. Where their digestive secretions rest against the limestone year after year, they very faintly etch the stone, leaving branching, root-like grooves across the blank swells of erased boundary steles—a slow, unintentional script written over the lost labor of the builders.

They do not hurry even to multiply. Once in a generation—perhaps once in a century—when a dweller has grown heavy with salvaged red pigment and mineral silt, it pinches off a fist-sized bladder from its lower flank. Into that bladder it folds a single, measured bubble of gas from its ceiling, weighted with just enough limestone grit to keep it from shooting all the way to the unseen surface.

The bladder detaches. It slips quietly out from under the stone arch and drifts into the plaza, hovering at the height of the kelp eaves. Caught in the imperceptible current, it sails silently between the iron-red curtains for leagues, brushing past drowned roofs and terraced walls, until the cold water slowly dissolves the gas within it. Then, heavy at last, it sinks into some distant, empty courtyard, splits open in the silt, and begins to thread its pale twine into the hollows of the stone—waiting, with infinite patience, for the first dead frond to fall.
