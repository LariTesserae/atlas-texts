The people of the slate city are built like the masonry they inhabit: compact, thick-ribbed, and fitted tightly together against the cold.

Generations in the thin, biting air have broadened their chests and deepened their lungs. They are rarely tall, and they carry their weight low, moving with a slow, deliberate roll of the hips that keeps their balance on frost-slick roofs and narrow stairways. Nothing about them is hurried. To run here is to scorch the throat with freezing air and risk a fall down ten tiers of stone; even children learn early to walk with small, flat-footed steps, testing each slab before trusting it.

Their faces are dark and weathered—burnished to the color of oiled walnut by the fierce high-altitude sun and the constant, dry wind. Around their eyes, the skin is creased into deep, permanent squints against the glare of white light on black stone. Every morning, before stepping through the pelt-hung doorways, they rub tallow or lanolin into their cheeks, knuckles, and lips to keep the freeze from splitting the skin. As a result, the people smell faintly of woodsmoke, wet wool, and cured fat—a warm, heavy scent that hangs in the narrow alleys like a second layer of insulation.

Their hands are their clearest record. Broad-palmed and thick-knuckled, with fingernails worn flat and square from prying and fitting dry stone, their skin is permanently mapped with fine grey-black lines where slate dust has worked into the creases. They dress in layers that make them look wider and heavier than they are: soft, felted wool next to the skin, then woven tussock-grass tunics, and over all of it, stiff, grease-cured hides worn with the fur turned inward. Around their necks and lower faces they wrap thick woollen scarves that stiffen with hoarfrost within minutes of leaving the hearth. When two neighbors pass in an alley, they rarely waste breath on long greetings; they tap a gloved knuckle against their frozen collar to crack the ice free, dip their chins, and murmur a syllable or two in a low, gravelly register that does not carry beyond arm’s length.

Silence is not a rule here so much as a condition of the air. In the thin atmosphere, shouting leaves a person dizzy and breathless, and the dry-laid walls drink up sound. Conversations happen close together, face to face, often accompanied by small, economical gestures of the hand. Much of the city’s daily coordination relies on rhythm rather than voice: the steady *thud* of a wooden mallet from three tiers down tells a mason that a neighbor is driving wedges; the *clack-clack* of hooves on the roof overhead means a string of beasts is descending the switchbacks, and the alley below should be cleared.

Life is organized vertically around heat and water, and every household occupies a column rather than a flat floor. 

At the bottom, half-buried in the leeward talus or wedged into the deepest clefts, are the stables. These are dark, low-ceilinged chambers where the thick-fleeced, broad-horned beasts of burden stand shoulder to shoulder. They are massive, patient creatures with heavy, curving horns that they tilt sideways with practiced ease to navigate the narrow stone passages. Their hooves are wide and split, gripping the slate like iron, and their coats are so dense that frost clings to the outer tips of the wool without ever touching their skin. They do not merely work for the builders; they are the city’s furnace. Packed together in the lowest tiers, their collective body heat rises through the slotted stone and timber floorboards into the living rooms directly above, keeping the sleeping platforms just warm enough that water in a covered skin will not freeze overnight.

The people live in the middle tiers, in rooms barely tall enough to stand upright in, lit by small, grease-filled soapstone lamps and sunken hearths fed with dried dung and twisted scrub-roots. Above them, on the boulder-weighted roofs, life opens to the sky—and to the birds.

Immense flocks of dark-feathered alpine birds—glossy, sharp-beaked, and silent on the wing—share the upper strata of the city. They nest in the warm crevices around the chimney flues, lining their hollows with shed wool and wisps of dried tussock grass. The people neither cage them nor drive them away. The birds clean the roofs of insects and scraps, and they serve as the city’s living weather-vanes. When the flocks stop wheeling on the updrafts and tuck themselves deep into the leeward niches, the builders know a sharp drop in temperature or a heavy ridge-wind is coming, and they weight the roof-skins with extra stones before dusk.

In the spaces between—the millions of dark, unmortared chinks inside the walls—live the small, round-eared rodents. Plump and thick-furred, they dart along the baseboards and eaves, gleaning dropped grain and bits of tallow. Though they are trapped occasionally for their soft pelts to line mittens, they are mostly tolerated as quiet tenants. They are the first to feel the mountain move. When a retaining wall begins to groan under a fraction of an inch of geological shift, the rodents abandon that section of masonry days before a human eye can spot the hairline crack in a lintel.

The rhythm of the city is set entirely by the midday sun. 

Dawn brings only bitter cold and the slow, silent arrival of the cloud banks snagging on the upper crags. The city stays tucked indoors, tending the beasts, twisting hair into rope, or chipping slowly at new stone tools by lamplight. Then, as the sun climbs high enough to soften the white rime on the windward cliffs, the city wakes all at once.

People emerge onto the tiered roofs carrying antler picks, bone scrapers, and wide horn scoops. Along the hand-carved runnels, teams clear away old ice so the beading meltwater can run cleanly down into the deep basalt cisterns. Leather buckets swing along the hair-braided ropes spanning the chasms, hauled hand-over-hand from terrace to terrace. Every drop is measured and accounted for: first to the drinking jars and the cisterns, then to the steaming wooden troughs in the stables, and finally, drop by careful drop, to the south-facing stone boxes where stunted scrub and silvery tussock grass are coaxed into growing. By mid-afternoon, when the shadows turn ink-blue and the water begins to stiffen into needle-ice on the slate, the roofs empty again, and the heavy pelts drop back over the doorways.

Because wood is scarce—scrub-roots are twisted and small, and true timber must be salvaged from ancient, crooked pieces carried up from distant lowlands generations ago—wooden tools are treated like family heirlooms. Ladders and levers are spliced together from short, knotted lengths, bound tightly with rawhide, and rubbed with grease until they are as dark and smooth as horn. A broken ladder rung is a matter for careful repair, not replacement.

This same patience governs how the people understand time, and how they understand themselves.

In a city built without mortar, nothing can afford to be rigid. A mortared wall shatters when the mountain settles; a dry-laid wall shifts, groans, and settles with it. The people think of their own lives the same way. Few individuals expect to see the completion of the great retaining walls they spend their afternoons extending, or the deep cisterns they chip out of solid rock a handful of dust at a time. A grandfather begins a water-channel; his daughter widens it; her grandchildren seal the catch-basin with clay carried up from the lowest canyons.

When an elder dies, there is no timber to spare for a pyre and no soft earth to dig a grave. Instead, the body is wrapped tightly in woven grass mats and cured hide, carried to the highest, quietest ridges where the slate lies shattered and loose, and laid into a cairn built from the mountain’s own broken teeth—stacked dry, without mortar, so that as the range rises and shifts, the stone settles slowly around them, locking builder and mountain together at last.
