When the foxes slip behind the ridge and the wind settles back into a low hiss through the scree, the basin appears entirely empty again. Nothing stands upright. There is only the flat pewter light, the *drip-drip* of weeping stone, and the slick, oiled-iron gleam of wet basalt.

Then, a hundred yards out across the talus, a sharp-angled block of dark slate shifts—not sliding down the slope, not settling in the frost, but *re-angling* itself against the mist.

They are easy to miss because they share the geometry of the valley. Nothing about them has been rounded by time. Their limbs are long, spare, and jointed in severe, abrupt planes, so that when they fold themselves down into a hollow of scree, their knees and elbows match the cleavage of the newly fractured rock. Their hide is the color of wet slate, matte and slick with condensation, and in the creases of their shoulders and the hollows behind their necks, the evaporating mist leaves pale mineral crusts faintly stained with sulfur.

They do not walk across the basin so much as *place* themselves across it. Their feet are broad, leathery, and deeply cleft, capable of gripping the knife-edged faces of loose basalt without turning a stone or scraping a boot-scuff into the lichen. A fox leaves a faint trail of scent between the bogs; a human would have stacked a cairn or kicked a path into the scree. These leave nothing. Even where twenty of them have crossed a boggy shelf, the brittle fringes of ice along the sedge remain unbroken.

At the black-bottomed pools, you can see how thoroughly they belong to the world as it is now, rather than as it might once have been. When one kneels to drink, it does not cup its hands or lap at the water. It lowers its wide, flat jaw to the surface and draws its leathery lower lip across the meniscus, skimming the delicate, iridescent sheen of hydrocarbons inward before it takes the cold meltwater underneath. Its throat works once, twice, with a soft, wet *clack*—a sound almost indistinguishable from a pebble shifting in the frost.

At five thousand meters, the thin air would starve a warm-blooded runner, yet their chests barely seem to move. Their ribcages are deep, rigid, and slightly flattened, expanding only a fraction of an inch at a time. Instead of panting through the mouth, they draw the saturated mist through narrow, vertical slits tucked behind their jawbones, filtering the thin oxygen out of the cold fog. The coal smoke and ozone on the wind do not make them cough; the membranes inside those slits are dark and slick, trapping the soot and flushing it down their throats with the rainwater they drink.

Still, it is not the pools or the mist that keep them anchored to this high, hollow shelf. It is the floor itself.

During the long, quiet stretches between gusts, you can find them stretched flat along the bare granite outcrops, their long forearms and flat chins pressed hard against the stone. They are listening to the sub-audible thrumming rising through the bedrock. To them, the unseen turbines and deep-earth pumps churning miles below are not an intrusion; they are the valley’s pulse, the only steady warmth and rhythm in a place where the rock itself feels newly broken. They orient themselves to it the way cushion plants orient to the lee of a boulder.

When the low, muffled drone of a cargo plane passes invisible above the cloud ceiling, a few of them will slowly uncurl from the scree, tilting their narrow, angular heads upward. They do not look afraid, nor do they scatter like the pipits. Their rigid chests simply begin to hum in a low, wet register that matches the vibration in the air, holding the note until the plane fades back into the drip of the stone.

The rest of the basin’s life treats them as weather or geography. In the windless lulls, the slight, slow heat rising from their dark backs draws the midges upward in hovering ribbons, and the dark-winged swifts wheel inches past their motionless faces to snap the flies out of the air. Cold-tolerant salamanders shelter in the damp, soot-rimmed hollows behind their ankles. Even the pale-eyed harriers, quartering low beneath the pewter clouds, glide right over them without a flicker of interest.

Toward the south, where the cloud deck thins and the bruised, sodium-amber glow bleeds upward from the unseen valleys below, they often gather along the higher ridges of raw slate. They do not build fires, and they do not speak. They simply sit with their sharp knees drawn up to their chins, pale, unblinking eyes fixed on the amber haze—patient, mineral-skinned, and utterly at home in a world that is half-rock, half-smoke, and still trembling from whatever pushed it up out of the dark.
