If you stay long enough in the hut—long enough for your own breath to feel wet and intrusive in the thin air—you realize the floor is not merely swept. It is *maintained*. The dust has not settled anywhere, not even in the corners where the stone ribs meet the floor, because someone was here just before you arrived, or is here now, waiting just outside the angle of your vision.

You will not hear them return. You will only notice, when you step back out into the flat afternoon, that the bundle of silver-gray wire-scrub has been turned a quarter-inch, or that a figure is crouched beside the nearest cairn where there was only gneiss a moment ago.

They are shaped like people, though pared down to what can survive without moisture. 

At first glance, they look like they are carved from the same ash-colored, velvet-hided stuff as the tall grazers, or wrapped in it. Their skin is the color of unbleached linen left in a dry attic for a century—matte, tight over the bone, fine-grained as pumice. There is no sheen of oil on their foreheads, no dampness at the corners of their eyes. When they blink, it is slow and deliberate, like a shutter clicking shut to clear grit, though their eyes are clear and dark as the veins of black glass running through the cliffs. They wear garments of cured, paper-thin hide stitched with dry sinew, layered in overlapping plates that rustle no louder than a moth’s wing when they shift their weight.

Their feet are bare, or shod in soles of soft, felted hide worn smooth as soapstone. The callouses on their heels are thick, pale, and faintly striated, matching the polish of the concave footpath.

They do not speak. In a world with no wind to carry a voice and no water to lubricate a throat, speech would be a violent waste. Instead, they communicate—if there is more than one of them, and it is very hard to be sure—by posture, by the placement of stones, and by the slow, steady work of their hands.

Watch one of them walk the path. They do not push through the motionless herds of six-legged beasts; they thread between the ash-colored legs with an economy of motion that makes it seem as though the path and the walker were poured into the same mold. They never brush a flank. They never startle a cliff-clinger. Yet if you keep your eyes fixed on them as they pass behind a needle-thin arch, you may wait a full minute for them to emerge on the other side, only to realize they are already fifty yards down the ridge, kneeling beside a cairn, while their shadow is still lingering near the arch, slowly catching up.

It is difficult to tell whether there is a small, scattered tribe of them living across the endless mesas, or only one or two who have been folded by the basin’s geometry into dozens of overlapping moments. They never meet face-to-face on the path. Sometimes you see two of them on opposite sides of a bottomless chasm, both crouched in the exact same posture, both scraping at a slab of tar-black lichen with identical flint blades, lit by slightly different angles of the same unmoving sun.

Their work is slow, meticulous, and entirely concerned with keeping accounts.

They use the stone scrapers not to clean hides, for nothing rots here and nothing needs curing, but to peel back the papery, breathing mats of cliff-clingers where they grow too thick over the cairns, or to shave thin, waxy curls from the dry-shelled weavers’ membranes. They chew these waxy shavings slowly as they walk—not in hunger, for their ribs show no desperation, only a lean, permanent sufficiency—drawing from them whatever trace of silicate and salt keeps their dry tissues supple.

And they count. 

With the notched bone tally-sticks, they walk the knife-edge ridges and take inventory of a world that refuses to stay single. When a six-legged grazer steps behind a spire and emerges as two smaller ones, the walker stops, draws a thumbnail or a flake of flint across the bone, and makes a mark. When a drifter splits in the static air, or when a section of a mesa seems to drift a fraction closer across the chasm than it was before, they add a cobble to a cairn, or wedge a ring of hammered copper into a crevice, binding it tight with sinew as if to pin the stone to its proper place.

They are not trying to stop the folding. They seem to know, with the patience of the bedrock itself, that the basin is too old and too stubborn to be corrected. They are simply keeping a record of the surplus—making sure nothing slips away uncounted into the wrong hour of the afternoon.

Inside the beehive hut, there is no bed. When they rest, they sit with their backs against the curved stone wall, knees drawn up, hands resting palm-down on the swept floor. In that posture, their breathing slows until it is indistinguishable from the faint, rippling shiver of the cliff-clingers outside. Their temperature drops to match the cool, dry gneiss. For long stretches—hours, or whatever passes for hours here—they become part of the room’s furniture: dry, silent, and entirely unbothered by the weight of the sky.

If you stand in their doorway, blocking the flat slate light, they will look up at you with those black-glass eyes. There is no fear in them, and no welcome. You are damp, loud with your own pulse, smelling faintly of salt and distant rain—an impossible, leaking thing that does not belong to the end of the mountain’s history. 

They will watch you until you step aside. Then they will pick up the bundle of silver-gray wire-scrub, rise without a sound, and begin sweeping the place where your shadow fell.
