At first, the basin appears entirely automated, a clockwork terrarium left to run out its own slow winding. The cable-tram glides overhead without a driver; the brass needles descend into the swollen bladder-sedges on schedule; the streetlamps burn with a steady, low-wattage pallor against the overcast sky.

It takes a longer look to notice the small, deliberate interventions that machinery cannot manage on its own: a smear of fresh tallow on a steel cable, a numbered enamel plate wiped clean of encroaching lichen, a pair of dark, oiled-wool sleeves moving unhurriedly behind the ivy of the main press-house.

There are perhaps thirty or forty of them in all—never enough to crowd the cedar avenues, and rarely more than two visible in the same quadrant at once. They live in the basin as the pale slugs live on the retaining walls: quietly, continuously, and entirely within the bounds of the petrified root-rim.

They are human, though generations of cool, shadowless daylight and perpetual damp have given them a distinct, settled aspect. They are lean and slightly round-shouldered from a lifetime of leaning over electrolytic vats, low-slung cable conduits, and wooden gear-trains. Their skin has the even, matte pallor of wet chalk or the underside of a bracket fungus—not sickly, only wholly unacquainted with direct sun. Their hands are their most prominent feature: broad, long-fingered, and permanently stained at the knuckles with the green verdigris of tarnished brass and the dark, astringent brown of peat-tannin.

They dress for silence and insulation. Their boots are soled with thick, compressed sphagnum felt dipped in linseed oil, so that even a brisk walk along the elevated iron trestles produces nothing louder than a soft, muffled *pad*. Over shirts of coarse, pale linen—woven from the very plant-fibers dried in the northern kilns—they wear heavy, layered tunics of dark grey wool and leather aprons stiffened with machine oil. Nearly all of them carry a small, flat brass voltmeter tucked into a breast pocket like a pocket watch, and a coil of waxed twine or spare copper wire looped at the belt.

They do not shout across the three hundred paces of the basin. In a bowl this still, a raised voice feels less like communication and more like a mechanical failure—an unnecessary vibration that might startle a taut nerve-vine or prematurely rupture the delicate, swollen sacs of the morning sedge-harvest. Instead, they have cultivated a profound, tactile literacy.

When two of them meet on a narrow walkway, they rarely speak. One simply steps aside, resting a palm lightly on the live nerve-vine guy-wire beside the railing, and inclines their head. If a message must be sent between the drying-kilns and the press-house, it travels by the pneumatic tubes clamped beneath the planks—tightly rolled slips of damp-proof paper arriving with a soft, cushioned *thump* in a felt-lined basket. When they do speak, it is in a dry, unhurried murmur pitched no louder than the *shhh* of the leather drive-belts, their mouths close to one another’s ears, their breath visible as a faint, fleeting mist in the cool air.

Mostly, they read the basin through their feet and fingertips. Walking the oiled-cedar avenues, a tender can feel the health of the entire settlement as a continuous, low-frequency hum through their felt soles. They know when the peat-lined motor boxes in the press-house are running true, and they can feel the slightest stutter in a distant drainage pump before a single drop of condensation overflows a gutter.

Their work is an endless, patient choreography of maintenance and harvest. You will find them crouched along the moss-terraces in the cool morning, testing the conductivity of a nerve-vine by touching a bare, calloused thumb to a brass terminal—judging the amperage not by a dial, but by the familiar, sour taste of copper it raises at the back of their teeth. You will see them wading carefully into the shallow drainage channels in greased hip-boots to clear a tangle of creeping ash-roots from a sluice, moving slowly so as not to muddy the dark, standing water.

At the end of a shift—marked not by a bell or a whistle, which would shatter the basin’s peace, but by the subtle dimming of the streetlamps’ voltage and the halting of the cable-tram—they file back toward the western rim.

Their dwellings inside the living root-wall are cool, cavernous, and meticulously kept. The rooms are framed by the pleached willow-trunks, whose pale bark weeps a faint, sweet sap into the mortar and keeps the walls at a steady, cellar-like temperature. There are no open hearths here; smoke and crackling wood have no place in this damp equilibrium. Warmth comes instead from heavy ceramic radiators fed by the cooling-jackets of the northern kilns, or from small, peat-insulated electric coils that glow a dull, steady cherry-red behind thick mica windows.

They eat simply, from what the basin itself yields: dense, dark loaves baked in the residual heat of the kilns from ground lichen and roasted ash-root; pale, meaty mushrooms cultivated in the dark peat-cellars beneath the bunkhouses; and the crisp, slightly saline shoots of young bladder-sedges pickled in vinegar. They drink the pure, mineral-less condensation harvested at dawn, which tastes faintly of cool mist and brass cisterns.

In the long, grey afternoons, when the automated rollers have finished pressing the sedges and the vats are quietly bubbling, the tenders sit on the narrow, willow-arched balconies overlooking the moss. They mend their oiled canvas, carve replacement teeth for the wooden gear-trains out of seasoned ash, or simply sit with their hands folded, watching the solitary grey moths that cling to the warm housings of the transformers.

They have lived inside the oval rim for so long that the mist beyond the petrified roots holds no curiosity for them; it is simply the outer wall of the room. Over the decades, their own rhythms have slowed to match the settlement’s. They age quietly, their skin taking on the fine, papery grain of dried sedge-membrane, their joints stiffening in the perpetual damp until they move with the same deliberate, oil-bathed patience as the machinery at the center of the bowl.

When one of them finally stops, there is no bell, no stone marker, and no weeping loud enough to stir the water-pockets. They are wrapped in pale, kiln-dried linen and laid down beneath the blue-green sphagnum, deep into the gnarled, subterranean lattice of the ash-roots and compressed peat—returning quietly to the living mat that absorbs the footsteps of those who remain.
