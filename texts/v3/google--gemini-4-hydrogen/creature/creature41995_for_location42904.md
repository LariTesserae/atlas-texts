To stand among them—if there were room to stand—would be to feel, before anything else, the faint, shared heat of a hundred slow hearts.

In the bruised-indigo chill of the hollow, the rock itself is cold enough to ache, yet the air an inch above the moss-draped shelves holds a mild, damp warmth, like the inside of a closed fist. It comes from the dwellers. Packed shoulder to hip along every rounded ledge, their thick, silvery coats pressed together in continuous living rows, they are each other’s only shelter. The outer guard hairs of their pelts are always sleeked with condensation, silver-grey and heavy as wet lichen, while the dense underfur stays dry and close against the skin, trapping the meager heat of a metabolism that burns as slowly as a banked coal.

They are built for holding on and for waiting. Their limbs are neither long nor swift, but their wrists and ankles turn with a slow, oiled patience, allowing them to hang effortlessly from the braided roots of the stunted shrubs or wedge themselves into the bulbous flutes of the limestone without spending strength. Their hands are the most wakeful part of them: long-fingered, pale-palmed, with flat, blunt nails and pads wrinkled from lifelong damp. Even when a dweller is asleep—chin tucked into the damp fur of its own chest, huge dark eyes veiled by thick, slow-moving lids—its fingers remain curled around a root, a neighbor’s rib, or the smooth belly of a river pebble.

Those eyes are pools of light-starved stillness. In the pewter-grey dilution that filters through the roof-slot, they do not dart or squint; they simply stay open, drinking in the faintest shifts of wet sheen. Yet they rely less on sight than on the long, stiff whiskers fanning from their muzzles and brows. In the quiet dark, those pale bristles are always sweeping in tiny, deliberate arcs, reading the nearness of a neighbor’s shoulder, the exact curve of a stone lip, or the soft passage of a beetle across the moss.

With hundreds of bodies sharing a space no wider than a small nave, sound would be a kind of violence. A shriek or a bark would ricochet off the wet limestone and rattle the whole precarious equilibrium. So they have no voice at all—only a grammar of weight and pressure.

To ask for room on a crowded ledge, a dweller leans its shoulder steadily against the one beside it, waiting until the neighbor shifts a hip or tucks an elbow in answer. To offer comfort, or simply to check that a shelf-mate is still breathing, one lays a long, damp palm flat against the other’s spine and leaves it there. Grooming is their closest thing to speech. Sitting knee-to-knee on the wool-thick moss, they take turns parting each other’s wet coats with slender splinters of pale bone, working from crown to rump in unhurried rows. The bone tip teases out tiny pale mites, beads of lime-water, and stray fern spores, while the fingers behind it smooth the fur back down. Through that quiet parting and smoothing, they know one another completely—by the notched ear, the old healed ridge along a collarbone, the particular thickness of a pelt, the familiar tremor of a hand that has grown stiff with age.

Their tools are not possessions so much as permanent fixtures of the hollow, older than any living dweller. The palm-sized river pebbles used to crack snail shells have been held by so many generations of damp hands that the stone has worn into shallow, dark-stained hollows where thumbs and forefingers rest. The unhafted flakes of dark chert, kept for scraping fat-rich lichen from the weeping upper walls, have edges dulled and resharpened by use against the same mineral crusts for centuries.

And the bone splinters—lashed with single, tough strands of fern-fiber—are their own dead, returned to service. When an old dweller finally cools on the shelf, its neighbors do not cry out or turn away. They sit beside the body through the slow *plip, plip* of the ceiling until the warmth is entirely gone, then tuck it deep into the wettest fissures beneath the lowest shrub roots. There, the pale salamanders and velvety beetles and the patient acid of the moss take what is soft. Years later, a probing hand will find a clean, lime-whitened rib or forearm bone sunken in the bryophytes, lift it into the dimness, lash a fresh thread of fern-fiber around its base, and put it back to work parting a living coat. Nothing leaves the hollow; no one is ever truly discarded.

Around and between the dwellers, the lesser lives of the stone well move in the same unhurried trance. Pale, lungless salamanders, so translucent that the faint dark thread of their spines shows through their slick skins, wedge themselves into the warm creases between a dweller’s folded knees and the stone. The dwellers do not brush them away; a salamander’s chill skin against a wrist is simply part of the ledge’s texture, like the moss itself.

When hunger stirs—a slow, quiet tide rather than a sharp pang—a dweller reaches down into the hanging curtains of silvery fern. Long fingers feel along the undersides of the fronds, testing the ribbed shells of the clustered snails by touch alone. A shell too small is left undisturbed; one grown thumb-sized and heavy is plucked with a gentle twist, brought back to the shelf, and held against a pebble. *Tap-tap.* The soft body is drawn out and eaten without haste, and the broken shards of shell are pressed back into the damp moss, where their calcium will slowly dissolve into the seepage to feed the ferns and build the next generation of shells.

Down at the bottom of the basin, where the mirror-still pools hold the exact level of the mountain’s slow breath, the dwellers rarely disturb the water. They kneel on the mossy gravel and dip a single hand, licking the cold, mineral-rich drops from their knuckles so as not to stir the silt or wake the feathery-gilled amphibians suspended just beneath the surface. Those pale, soft-bodied sleepers hang motionless for hours, their external gills drifting like wisps of smoke in water that never ripples except when a fat drop lets go from the roof above.

Because the hollow is already full to its seams, birth is a rare, quiet event. A female carries a single pup once every few years, only when an old one has slipped beneath the roots and left a palm’s breadth of space empty on the shelves. The newborn is tiny, blind, and nearly bare, clinging to the warmth of its mother’s belly inside the parted curtain of her silver fur. For months it knows nothing but the slow rise and fall of her ribs, the *plip* of condensing mist, and the distant, muffled *tap-tap* of stone on shell. By the time it opens its huge dark eyes to the bruised-indigo gloom, it already knows the shape of the hollow by weight and vibration.

They have no names, no stories, and no memory of a world outside the cloud-wrapped crease. Yesterday and a hundred years ago are the same cool, crowded moment—a single, many-hearted stillness, balanced so finely between the drip of the ceiling and the hunger of the moss that time itself seems to have settled to the bottom of the pools, clear and motionless as the stone.
