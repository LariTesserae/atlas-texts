At first, you would mistake it for another cluster of the pale, fluid-swollen bladder-fronds, wedged deep in the center of the weed-tangle where three different directions of *down* meet and cancel one another out.

It is small—no longer than a human forearm from elbow to fingertip, though it is rarely stretched out straight enough to measure. It keeps itself folded tight, knees drawn up toward its ribs, spine curved to match the inward press of the surrounding vegetation. Its skin is the color of the chamber’s dimness: a cold, bruised slate-blue over the back and shoulders, paling to a cloudy, near-translucent grey along the belly and the insides of its limbs, so that in the faint light leaking from the stone, you can almost make out the dark, slow-beating knot of its viscera.

There are no sharp edges to it, even though it lives inches from newly cleaved basalt. Where its knuckles, elbows, and heels brace habitually against the rock, the skin has thickened into pale, chalky callouses, dusted with the same fine mineral grit that sheds from the walls. Elsewhere, it is slick and faintly velvety, coated in a thin, cold mucus that keeps the microscopic stone-shards from biting in and lets it slip without friction through the narrow veins of free water.

Its head is small and smooth, tapering slightly toward a blunt, lipless mouth that stays partly open, drinking the slow chemical weep of iron and sulfur from the water. It has no eyes—only two shallow, vertical hollows where eyes might have been, skinned over with the same pale membrane as its cheeks. Instead, it reads the chamber through a fringe of hair-thin, branching filaments growing along the sides of its neck and down the ridges of its forearms. At a glance, they are indistinguishable from the surrounding plant-growth; they drift with the same glacial apathy, tasting the faintest shift in mineral concentration or the hour-long turn of a thermal current.

Its hands are the most articulated part of it, and the most incongruous. Each ends in four long, slender fingers—narrower than a child’s, with broad, flattened pads at the tips. The pads are stained dark along the creases from pressing into the iron-rich clay, and the joints are slightly swollen, stiffened by cold and mineral crust.

It does not swim. There is nowhere here to swim, and no consensus on which way a stroke would carry it. Instead, it moves by *mooring*—reaching one pale hand out through the rubbery curtains of bladder-fronds to find the finger-grooves it pressed into the clay seam long ago, hooking its toes around a knotted loop of plant-fiber, and pulling itself, inch by agonizing inch, across the pocket.

It moves at the pace of the chamber. A single reach from one wall to the next may take an entire afternoon.

Because it is so slow, and because space here is tightly pleat-folded, the water does not quite let go of where it has been. Watch it shift its weight from one basalt spur to another, and you will see, hanging a few inches behind its shoulder, a second, fainter shoulder—pale, smeared at the edges, and not quite solid, like a bruise in the water that refuses to fade. When it uncurls an arm to touch the cairn of four river-pebbles, the ghost of its curled elbow lingers beside its ribs for hours afterward, slowly thinning into the slate-blue dimness until the next sluggish motion leaves a fresh echo in its place.

It spends most of its long, cold existence tending the geometry of its small room.

When a newly split edge of slate sheds a flake of stone, the creature feels the tiny *tick* through its gill-filaments and creeps over to wedge the shard into a crevice where it won’t sever the ribbon-weed. When a strand of the weed grows through a fold in the chamber’s smallness and emerges rooted at both ends, the creature gathers the slack in its four-fingered hands and patiently, over days, braids it into a cord, knotting it around a spur of basalt to keep the water-veins open. It does not seem bothered when a knot leaves a faint, unsolid twin hanging beside it in the dark; perhaps it uses both to steady itself.

Now and then, it visits the tight vertical cleft where the four flat river-pebbles are stacked. Those pebbles are older than the split rock around them—smooth, worn by some distant, sunlit current that this pocket has never known, carried here when the creature was smaller, or before the fracture sealed shut. It rests the flat of its palm against the topmost pebble, holding it steady against the local confusion of gravity, feeling the slow, rhythmic pulse of the gelatinous spawn clinging beneath. If one of the blind, fingernail-sized crustaceans rows past across its knuckles, the creature neither flinches nor snatches at it; it simply waits for the tiny pale legs to clear its skin before curling its fingers back in.

It has no voice, and makes no sound louder than the rubbery creak of the fronds it pushes aside. It does not rush, because there is nowhere to rush to, and nothing outside the basalt walls that it remembers wanting. Tucked into the ribs of the earth, half-lost in its own lingering afterimages, it simply keeps the knots tight, keeps the pebbles balanced, and repeats itself softly in the dark.
