If you stand very still on the woven floor, long enough for the bleached glare to flatten the edges of your vision, you begin to notice that the silence is not empty. Things live here, though nothing here breathes wet air, or sweats, or bleeds. 

Life in the basin is sparse, dry, and patient, built from the same cellulose, silica, and copper as the basin itself.

Along the older cedar aqueducts, where streams of fine white quartz sand flow like dry water, you can sometimes catch the flicker of *silt-ribbons*—pale, segmented creatures no thicker than a wood-shaving, made of translucent mica and flexible chitin. They swim against the sand-current, sifting it for microscopic flakes of lodestone. When an aqueduct reaches an ancient cypress trunk and splices straight through the solid heartwood without curving, the silt-ribbons do not stop; they ripple cleanly into the bark and emerge from the far side, undisturbed, as if the tree were only a thicker kind of air.

Higher up, in the rigging of the banyan-dynamos, live the *chaff-burrs*—fist-sized tangles of fine silver wire and dry milkweed-silk. When a cold downdraft rattles the canopy, they detach from the brass rivets and skitter across the catwalks like tumbleweeds, drawn to the faint hum of induction. A burr will pause between two live silver thorns, stretch out its filaments to bridge the gap, and stiffen with a brief, violet crackle as it drinks the static arc. A second later it drops away, slightly warm, smelling of ozone and toasted straw.

But the basin does not belong to the burrs or the herons. It belongs to the ones who keep the wafers moving.

At a distance, you will mistake them for part of the scaffolding—a surveyor’s tripod left standing on a truss, or a dead sapling lashed to a gantry with copper wire. They are tall, narrow, and starkly articulated, built on a geometry that only approximates the human. Their limbs are fluted ironwood and hollow, ribbed reed, joined at the elbows and knees by exposed spheres of dark, polished hornbeam that turn in sockets dusted with fine graphite. When they walk, they make no footfalls on the springy floor; there is only that faint, papery *shhh* of dry wood sliding against dry wood.

Their torsos are cages of woven bamboo-plate wrapped in overlapping strips of pale, cured parchment, like scroll-cases or the nests of paper-wasps. They have no mouths—there is nothing here to drink or chew—and no noses. Where a face should be sits a smooth, slightly concave oval of compressed plant-fiber, identical to the blank hexagonal wafers the refineries spit out, mounted on a swivel of tarnished brass. Set into the fiber are three tiny, faceted quartz lenses that click softly as they rotate to focus the blinding white light.

They are grown, not forged. Beneath the parchment-wrap, they have no gears or boilers, only a dense vascular web of copper capillaries carrying dry, pressurized air and static charge. If a limb cracks in a sudden gust, it does not leak oil or sap; only a pale, dry pith shows inside, hissing softly as air escapes, until the creature reaches into a pouch of resinous wax and seals the break itself.

It is impossible to tell whether these *tenders* are the distant, stripped-down descendants of whatever ancient intelligence first planted this forest, or merely a complex fungus of brass and reed that sprouted from the machinery long after its makers vanished. They carry no tools of conquest or invention—only long calipers of petrified bone-reed, coils of lodestone wire, and small brushes for clearing graphite dust from the bearings.

Because they are very old, and because they have never left the weave, they do not navigate the basin the way a stranger would. They do not walk miles across the shallow dish to reach a failing grove. They are creatures of the pleats.

Watch one at work in the dense thickets where the catwalks braid six layers deep. It will stand before a solid, opaque knot of burl-wood in a cypress pillar, tilt its wafer-face as if listening to the grain, and then plunge its long, multi-jointed arm straight into the bark up to the shoulder. Half a mile away, in a sunlit turbine-grove, that exact hand of brass wire and cured leather emerges from the hollow stem of a cooling-fern, delicately unslotting a brittle, graying wafer from a leaf and sliding a fresh one into its place. 

They carry the broken perspective of the world in their copper-coiled chests. They know which shadow belongs to a gantry three basins over, and which hollow pipe will spit a dropped rivet out a mile upwind. 

More than anything, they tend the places where the rules have worn thin. Where the blinding light casts a shadow that arrives a second too late—or where a stationary flywheel casts a shadow whose wooden teeth are still turning—you will often find two or three tenders kneeling on the hexagonal floor. They touch the tips of lodestone tuning forks to the edge of the lagging darkness, and the shadow *rings*, a dry, sub-audible vibration they read through the soles of their woven feet. Sometimes, when the drift between an object and its shadow grows too wide, they will carefully peel the uncoupled shadow up from the floor like a sheet of black carbon paper, roll it into a tight, crackling cylinder, and feed it into a pneumatic tube, sending it down into the cool, dustless dark beneath the weave.

Down there, in the deep root-grid where the bleached sky never reaches and the great flywheels turn in silence, live the only things in this world that never see the light: vast, blind, slow-moving *sweepers*, like segmented mats of lignified leather and stiff bristle-grass. They crawl endlessly along the undersides of the root-trusses, sweeping up the fallen wafer-dust, the shed copper scales, and the spilled graphite, compressing it all into neat, dry bricks that are fed back up the conveyor-vines toward the refineries.

Above, the tenders never speak. The dry air carries a sharp *tock* for miles, and there is no moisture for vocal cords. When two meet on a high catwalk, they pause, tilt their quartz lenses toward one another, and touch the silver-tipped knuckles of their left hands together. A crisp, solitary snap of static jumps the gap—a brief blue-white needle of light, a sudden scent of toasted chaff—and in that instant, whatever needs to be known is passed. Then they part, returning to the louvers and the burls.

They do not die so much as they dry out beyond the point of elasticity. When a tender grows too brittle, when its graphite wears thin and its parchment cracks in the cold wind, it climbs into the high canopy of photovoltaic fronds. It settles into a fork of copper-veined wood, lashes its limbs to the trellises with its own wire tendons, and goes still. 

Over the years, the wax-skinned leaves grow around it. Its quartz lenses cloud with dust; its wafer-face dries to powder and blows away in a pollen whirlwind. Eventually, there is nothing left of it that is not part of the weave—just another hollow junction-box, another dry, fluted strut humming quietly in the glare.
