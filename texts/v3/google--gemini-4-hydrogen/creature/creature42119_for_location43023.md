To know what lives in the basin, you first have to unlearn the habit of looking for movement. Here, life does not dart or scurry; it creeps at the pace of a cooling ember, and it is shaped less like an animal than like a geological fault.

Start at the dark slate floor of the avenues, which seems so impossibly, unnaturally bare. Even in a windless place, a city that sheds papery skins and talc-fine dust should be ankle-deep in its own debris. It isn’t, because of the **sweepers**. 

At first you would mistake one for a slight sheen on the rock, or a trick of the shadowless light. A sweeper is as flat as a spilled sheet of paper and nearly the exact matte charcoal of the basin floor—wide as a tabletop, with no head, no eyes, and no discernible edges, its perimeter fraying into a fringe of microscopic cilia. It glides across the plazas on a dry, static crawl, slower than the minute hand of a clock. As it passes over a fallen velum-membrane or a drift of salt-dust, its underside secretes a dry, alkaline enzyme that dissolves the debris on contact, drawing the mineral salts upward into its leathery body. Behind it, the slate is left wiped clean, faintly polished, and utterly dry. When a sweeper is gorged, it does not rest in the open; it slides itself into the hairline seam where a tower’s plinth meets the floor, flattens into the crack, and slowly transfers its gathered salts back into the porous foundation of the wall above.

Above the floor, clinging to the crisp, unweathered faces of the chalky towers, are the **masons**—the pale, segmented builders the size of a spread hand. They are rare, solitary things, and their bodies are marvels of austerity. Their shells are not chitin, but a dense, matte-white carbonate, indistinguishable from the stone they lay down. Beneath their flat bellies, soft electrostatic pads grip the vertical masonry without friction or sound. 

A mason has no eyes to admire the urban geometry it creates, and no voice to coordinate with its kin across the plaza. Instead, it reads the city through its feet. The chalky paste it extrudes from the glands behind its mandibles dries with a specific crystalline grain and a faint, lingering static charge; by pressing its pads against an older wall, a mason can sense the thickness of a buttress, the load bearing down on an arch, or the subtle draft rising through a hollow tower. It knows where a viaduct must span simply because the stone beneath it aches with an unbalanced charge.

And every millimeter of spire costs the mason dearly. The basin holds no open water, so the moisture required to mix its mineral paste comes from a single, precious reservoir of dense, briny fluid sealed inside the mason’s own abdomen. To build is to spend itself. A young mason is plump and slightly translucent along its seams; an old one is thin as a wafer, its shell chalky and brittle, moving slower and slower until it lays its final, dry course of mortar at the top of a cornice and locks its pads permanently into the wet paste. By the time the stone cures, the mason is part of the parapet.

That is, if it reaches the top alone.

In the upper galleries, where the dim light barely reaches beneath the lintels, hang the **ash-shards**. They are slender, blind, and folded tight against the ceiling like splinters of the outer basin rim—so dark and angular they look like fractures in the pale masonry. They can hang motionless for months, conserving every trace of energy, waiting for the faint, electrostatic hum of a mason climbing past below.

When one drops, it does not swoop; it simply lets go, falling through the still air without a whisper. It lands squarely on the mason’s flat back, its hooked, slate-like limbs snapping shut around the edges of the carapace. There is no struggle. The mason cannot reach its own back, and the shard does not kill it—not yet. Instead, the parasite drives a hair-thin, hollow needle through the dry seam between the mason’s segments and begins to siphon off that tiny, hoarded drop of internal brine.

What follows is a slow, feverish distortion. The parasitized mason keeps climbing, keeps extruding paste, driven by a scrambled hunger it cannot satisfy. Deprived of its fluid, its mortar comes out too dry, too dark, and slightly crooked. If you look closely at the skyline of the basin, you will see spires that suddenly veer a few degrees off plumb, or arches whose curves pinch inward like a grimace—the monuments of masons steered by a passenger. When the mason is finally drained hollow, it climbs to the highest, coolest ledge it can reach and freezes. Weeks later, the dry carapace splits open down the middle, and half a dozen pale, soft-bodied young shards unfold from the husk, darkening as they cure in the dim air before crawling under the nearest lintel to wait.

Deeper still, *inside* the buildings, is a world the light never touches at all. 

The **lattice-mites**—no larger than grains of salt, blind and soft-bodied—never see the wide avenues. They live entirely within the older, lower tiers of the city, chewing microscopic tunnels through the solid chalky pillars. They do not eat the stone for nourishment so much as they mine it for the trapped pockets of dried mineral salts and the microscopic spores of velum-fungus sealed inside centuries ago. Working in absolute, vibrationless silence, they hollow out thick load-bearing buttresses from the core outward, leaving behind a delicate, bone-white filigree that somehow still holds the weight of six stories above.

In those dark, porous honeycombs live the **seam-sleepers**—pale, segmented larvae and dormant cysts that belong to no single species, suspended in the cool, dry dark. Some are the resting stages of the masons; others are bloated, pearlescent sacs that hold the basin’s only true reserves of moisture, swelling imperceptibly over decades whenever a rare shift in the outer pewter haze brings a trace of dampness down into the depression. The mites never pierce them. They tunnel respectfully around the sleepers, turning the interior of every old pillar into a quiet, breathable lung.

And that is what the city’s grand architecture truly is: a lung. 

The wide, swept-clean avenues, the identical arched openings, the hollow six-story voids, and the tool-less ramps spiraling up the inner walls are not built for comfort or ceremony. They are a passive machine for moving motionless air. As the cool, heavy air settles on the dark slate floor, it warms ever so slightly against the stone and rises through the hollow towers in slow, laminar chimneys, drawing the faintest trace of vapor up toward the upper cornices—where the papery-white velum-growths spread their geometric fans to drink it.

Yet something *does* walk the boulevards. Something *does* climb those spiraling ramps.

They are the rarest inhabitants of the basin—perhaps only four or five in the entire town-sized depression—and you could walk the empty streets for hours without ever realizing one was watching you from twenty paces away. 

They are the **calipers**.

Tall—six or seven feet when fully unfolded—they look at first like discarded architectural struts or bundles of dry, pale reeds propped in the corner of a hollow tower. Their bodies are nothing more than a central, vertically ribbed thorax no thicker than a wrist, suspended between four impossibly long, multi-jointed limbs made of a material like unglazed porcelain. Each foot ends in a wide, flat, spongy pad of dry fibers that compresses when it touches the slate, absorbing every trace of impact so that a creature taller than a human can stride down a stone boulevard in total, eerie silence.

They have no eyes, and no mouths that open to speak. Instead, their narrow heads flare into broad, shallow, dish-like crests, thin as eggshells and angled slightly upward toward the pewter haze. These dishes are exquisite instruments for reading temperature, static charge, and the faintest whisper of humidity—allowing a caliper to feel the health of a tower’s air-draft from across a plaza.

For months at a time, a caliper remains folded inside the cool, dark void of a tower, limbs tucked tight against its ribs, indistinguishable from a pillar. When it finally stirs, it unfolds with an agonizing, deliberate grace, descending the spiraling inner ramp and stepping out onto the swept-clean avenue.

It walks the city like a gardener tending a dry, delicate mechanism. When a velum-growth on a high cornice grows too thick and begins to choke the airflow of an arched window, the caliper reaches up with its long, rake-like forelimbs, gently peels away the excess papery fans, and drops them into the center of the avenue for the sweepers to dissolve. When it senses the erratic, feverish static of a parasitized mason laying down a crooked spire, it climbs the facade, plucks the doomed builder and its ash-shard passenger cleanly off the wall with two delicate, tong-like digits, and carries them down to the slate floor, placing them gently in the path of a gliding sweeper before the crooked stone can ruin the balance of the tower.

It does this without anger, without haste, and without a single sound. 

Then, its slow circuit complete, the caliper steps back through an arched doorway, spirals up the empty, echoing-less ramp into the cool dark of a hollow tower, folds its porcelain limbs tight against its ribs, and goes back to sleep—leaving the avenues once more to the drifting membranes, the silent avalanches of talc, and the slow, relentless turning of the city’s dry chemistry.
