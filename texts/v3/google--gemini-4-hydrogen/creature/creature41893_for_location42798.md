Nothing lives here by fighting the air. To stand upright on the crowns is to be broken; to build high is to be erased. Whatever endures in this sunlit emptiness has learned either to wedge itself into the porous bone of the rock, or to surrender completely to the roar.

Even in the thickest mats of vegetation, life is sparse, secretive, and built for sudden flight. Buried deep within the meters-thick tangles of braided, rubbery roots live small, six-limbed creatures no larger than a closed fist. They are blind, pale as tallow, and armored along their backs with overlapping plates of tough, hooked keratin. For weeks they crawl slowly through the damp, sunless weave of the turf, chewing on decaying moss and the white, hungry tendrils of the epiphytes. But their true survival belongs to the gales. When a violent downdraft peels a mattress-thick carpet of turf off a windward cliff and hurls it out across a mile of open void, these burrowers do not flee. They sink their hooked dorsal plates into the moss and lock their jaws onto the thickest roots, curling into tight, armored knots. They spend minutes tumbling slowly through the blinding daylight, deafened by the wind, waiting for the bone-jarring *thump* against the crest of another spire. The impact shakes them loose; before the torn mat has even finished anchoring itself to the new stone, they are already crawling out to feast on whatever was crushed in the landing.

Out on the bare, pitted limestone—places too exposed even for the moss—live things that look like nothing more than calcified scabs. They cling in shallow wind-flutes, flat, gray, and dry as old mortar, indistinguishable from the ancient rock. For hours under the bright sun they are entirely inert. Then a moisture front collides with the spire, and a dense, warm cloud materializes against the stone. Within seconds of the first heavy condensation, the scabs split along hidden seams. Out unfurl vast, pleated curtains of spongy, pale-violet tissue, four or five times the size of their shells, trembling in the wet gale. They drink the mist directly out of the air, swelling heavy and slick with warm water in five frantic minutes—until a dry gust shreds the cloud into nothingness, and they snap shut with a wet, clicking sound lost in the roar, sealing the precious water inside until the next erratic front.

Deeper still, inside the lightless, vertical chimneys where the blind, chitinous scavengers cling to the wet walls, the landscape’s deafening voice becomes a hunting ground. Wedged into the narrowest stone throats—places where the wind shrieks past at brutal speeds—live long, boneless predators with pale, leathery hides. They have no eyes and no ears in the usual sense; instead, their flanks are lined with clusters of sensitive, fluid-filled blisters that read the shifting bass hum of the hollow rock. By inflating and deflating tough, internal air-sacs, these hunters can subtly choke the rushing draft inside a chimney, bending the pitch of the stone’s howl up or down by half a tone. They wait in the roaring dark, feeling the faint, rhythmic vibrations of scavengers scurrying along the walls below. When one wanders beneath a narrow vent, the hunter suddenly collapses its air-sacs. The abrupt release triggers a localized pressure-crack—a sharp, miniature thunderclap that stuns the scavenger off the rock and sucks it straight upward into a waiting, bristled maw.

Out in the mile-wide gulfs of open air, alongside the solitary, parchment-thin membrane-gliders, drift the *tangle-sails*. They are not single animals, but sprawling, colonial things—loose, shimmering nets of hollow, gas-filled bladders and trailing, sticky filaments, sometimes twenty feet across and nearly invisible against the glare. They have no muscles to fight a crosswind. Instead, they ride the corkscrewing whirlwinds, letting the turbulence spin them out into wide, glistening lassos. They harvest whatever the spires throw into the sky: detonated, tufted seed-pods, drifting insects, and occasionally a careless membrane-glider whose trailing stabilizer snags in the glue. When a dying thermal drops a tangle-sail against a cliff-face, its bladders cushion the blow; it simply drapes itself over the forty-foot silver-green moss curtains for a few hours, quietly digesting its catch while the wind whips it like a torn banner, waiting for the next updraft to peel it back into the void.

And then there are the ones who leave the copper pitons.

They do not belong to the spires, though they know them better than any creature born here. They arrive at dawn, when the rising basin air is steadier, gliding in across the abyss on wide, creaking kites lashed together from hardwood poles and cured, waxy leaves. Small, wiry, and sun-blackened, they come from somewhere beyond the heat-haze—some distant rim or deeper shelf where the tough, elastic fibers and pure amber resins of the spires are worth the terror of the crossing.

The moment their thick-soled sandals hit the springy turf of a leeward ledge, they are in a race against the weather. They do not speak; speech is a useless, throat-tearing vanity in a world this loud. Before stepping out of a sheltered notch, each harvester pulls down one of the heavy leather ear-hoods from its wooden peg—stiff with the salt and sweat of crews that came before—and buckles it tight beneath the chin, packing the ears with oiled moss so the rifle-cracks of collapsing pressure-pockets will not burst their eardrums.

Instead of voices, they use tension. Every crew carries coils of braided vine and heavy hammers. They drive copper pitons into the porous limestone and stretch taut guide-lines across the crowns, anchoring themselves and their woven windbreaks against the sudden shear of crosswinds. To warn a partner twenty feet away through a blinding five-minute sheet of warm mist, you do not shout—you pluck the line. Two sharp snaps mean *a downdraft is building*; a long, grating rasp with the back of a bone knife means *drop flat and hook in*. Deprived of hearing, they feel the world through the soles of their feet and the teeth clenched against their leather straps, reading the deep, chest-rattling bass hum of the hollow rock the way a sailor reads the swell of a deck.

They work crouched low beneath the hissing curtains of moss, slicing into the rubbery root-tangles with hooked knives to bleed the thick, fragrant resin into weighted, resin-sealed baskets. They strip the tough inner fibers from freshly landed turf-mats before the wind can tear them away again. All the while, they keep one eye on the bone wind-vanes spinning in blurs at the shelter’s edge. When a vane suddenly stalls, or reverses with a sharp *clack*, tools are dropped instantly. They throw themselves flat into the springy roots, hooking their elbows through the anchored vine-loops as a gale tears past, screaming through the stone throats above them.

They never stay long—only a few days, until the baskets are heavy and the bindings are tight. Before they leave, they stack dry kindling inside the resin-mortared shelters, weight down the stone cairns, and hang the salt-stiff ear-hoods back on their pegs for whoever comes next. Then, waiting for the morning updrafts to build out of the unseen depths below, they drag their leaf-winged kites to the cliff’s edge, unhook their safety lines, and step off into the bright, roaring emptiness—dwindling in seconds into tiny, dark specks against the sun-lit bones of the world.
