If you stand still in the warm, ordinary light long enough for your eyes to stop sorting the room into shelves and walls, you begin to notice the ones who built it.

At first, they are easy to mistake for the furniture. They are tall, narrow, and angular, with the dry, stacked proportions of the limestone plinths and the lashed reed-bundles overhead. Their stillness is complete—not the tense stillness of a hiding animal, but the settled quiet of a jar on a ledge. Then a sleeve shifts, crisp as parchment catching on a sharp corner, and a figure leans forward to scrape a fingernail along the rim of a clay bowl, testing its edge with a soft, chalky *shhhk*.

There is nothing wet in them. Where a human body would be soft with blood and water, theirs are built of the same dry, patient materials as the room itself: layered plates of pale horn and keratin, hollowed bone packed with fine ash, and joints bound tight with knotted cordage and hammered copper rivets. Their skin—where it shows beneath stiff, unbleached tunics of woven grass-cloth—has the matte, porous look of unglazed ceramic, dusted lightly with chalk from the walls. When they move, there is no sigh of breath or slap of soles on tile, only a dry, layered rustle like pages turning in a warm loft, and the faint *clack* of horn knuckles against stone.

They have no wet eyes to blink against the dust. Instead, their heads are narrow, tilted planes of smooth bone and pale shell, pierced along the brow and cheeks with small, dry vents and clusters of dark, faceted pits like those of the almond-sized beetles. They do not look at things so much as angle themselves toward them, listening to the faint *tick* of a cooling stone or the rasp of a crawler in the mortar.

It is impossible to say how many of them live here, because the room’s failed bookkeeping applies to them as much as to the tiles and the vines.

You may watch one kneeling on the east walkway, using a copper-tipped chisel to dress a limestone edge that is already razor-sharp, sending up small, dry puffs of white dust. Yet if you turn your head toward the west wall, there is the same notched shoulder-plate, the same chipped copper rivet at the elbow, patiently un-knotting a length of cordage from a timber peg. Sometimes two of them sit side by side on a low plinth, trimming the exact same adze-handle with identical strokes, their dry sleeves brushing without either one looking up. When the handle is smooth, one of them rises and walks away with it; the other simply ceases to be on the plinth, leaving behind only a faint crescent of bone-ash where their hip had rested.

They do not seem troubled by this. They move through the room’s broken geometry with the unhurried familiarity of people who have never known distance to be fixed. One will walk forty paces down the central aisle carrying a stack of amber-membrane rosettes, vanish behind a ceramic pillar, and step out from the far side two seconds before they arrived—pausing only to let their earlier self pass by before setting the stack down. Another reaches a long, cord-bound arm into a narrow crack between two dry-stone plinths—a gap barely wide enough for a beetle—and withdraws a freshly thrown bowl from the underside of the ceiling, three feet away, shaking a few clinging paper-winged gliders gently from its rim.

Their days—if you can call the slow, hour-long drift of warmth from east to west a day—are spent in quiet, circular maintenance. They sweep the bone-ash from the tiles with little brooms of silica-fiber, only for the ash to settle back down from the ceiling-slates a moment later. They gather the brittle husks shed by the six-legged crawlers and press them neatly into the chinks of the masonry. They tend the dry vegetation, trimming back a vine that is growing outward from a timber joint while leaving its twin, three feet away, to un-knit itself backward into a ceramic jar.

They do not eat or drink. Instead, when the warmth peaks, they pause near the thickest cushions of ash-gray and ochre moss, tilting their ventilated heads so the dry spores puff into the woven seams of their collars. Sometimes a pale beetle climbs onto a figure’s collarbone and grazes slowly along the horn plates for an hour before stepping off onto a shelf, and the figure does not brush it away.

And then there are the baskets.

Along the lower niches and beneath the reed rafters sit dozens of tight-woven grass baskets, some no larger than a melon, others big enough to hold a crouched child. Most are empty, or filled with stacked sherds and dried leaves. But when the mid-morning warmth settles heaviest against the west wall, a few of them begin to twitch—a dry, rhythmic flexing of the reeds, like a fist slowly tightening and relaxing.

If you watch one long enough, you see that the weaving is not quite finished, or is quietly un-making itself in reverse. A reed-bundle lengthens into a forearm; a tight knot at the rim loosens into a joint bound with copper; a seam parts to reveal the pale, chalky curve of a shoulder. Or, just as often, one of the figures grows still in a corner where the serrated leaves interlock, folding their long knees against their chest and drawing their stiff grass-cloth sleeves tight around their ribs, until the cordage at their joints blurs into the basketwork and they sit there among the bowls, indistinguishable from the craft, breathing in the dry, steady warmth.

When the heat shifts back toward the east, the room gives a single, soft sigh of settling leaves and cooling stone. A shadow finally detaches from a limestone wall and thins into the chalky air. Across the square, a figure stands up from a plinth, picks up a chisel that was already waiting in their hand, and taps once—a clean, dry *clink*—against an edge that has never been dull.
