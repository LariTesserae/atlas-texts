If you stood inside the bell-shaped hollow for an hour, letting your eyes adjust to the pewter twilight, you would still believe the room was empty. Even after you noticed the salamanders in their wet moss basins, and the spider-webs tucked deep into the cracks, you would look right past the thing that lives here. It has the same patience as the limestone, and in the gloom, it wears almost the exact same color.

It stays most often behind the crescent of cobbles along the leeward wall, folded into the curve where the pressure wave breaks and softens. At first glance it looks like a slump of dark, wet rock, or a bundle of salt-bleached driftwood wedged behind the stones. Then the tide outside shifts a fraction, the pitch of the cavern’s drone drops a quarter-tone, and the shape uncurls by an inch.

It is roughly the size of a crouched person, though spare and oddly proportioned—long in the forearm and shin, narrow at the hips, with a deep, barrel-curved ribcage that seems built to hold a great deal of air. Its skin is a matte, slate-gray, mottled with paler patches along the shoulders like weathered shale, and glistening faintly where the tidal mist settles on it. There is no fur, no scales, only tough, cool hide thickened into dark callous pads over the knees, elbows, and the heels of its long, blunt-fingered hands. Its face is quiet and broad-boned, with small, dark eyes set deep under heavy brows—eyes that stay half-veiled behind a milky inner lid to keep out the salt spray, because sight is of little use here anyway.

It lives by the sound, the way a marsh-bird lives by the wind.

In a chamber where speech vanishes a hand’s breadth from your mouth, ears are for nothing delicate. Instead, it reads the room through its sternum, its jawbone, and the flat soles of its feet. It knows the exact height of the sea outside by the frequency humming in its back teeth. When a drop of condensation swells on an overhang and finally lets go, it feels the tiny, wet slap against the floor through the rock beneath its palms. Even when a rock-wren drops silently through the high ceiling slit to roost in the dark, the creature knows it is there—not by the sound of wings, which is swallowed instantly, but by the brief, fluttering snag the bird’s body makes in the standing wave of air against its cheek.

For most of the tidal cycle, it barely stirs. While the sea is slack and the cavern’s voice drops to a low, ragged groan, it sleeps behind the cobble wall, its back pressed flush against the cool limestone, its ribs rising and falling in slow, heavy strokes.

When the tide turns and begins to push hard into the subterranean throat, the roar thickens. The mist rises, cold and sharp with salt. That is when it wakes. It unfolds itself slowly—never hurrying, because fast movement wastes warmth in the biting chill—and steps out from behind the baffle wall onto the slick, moss-blanketed boulders at the floor’s rim. There, where the hart’s-tongue ferns blur in their perpetual tremor, it sits with its knees drawn up and lets the mist coat its shoulders. From time to time, it draws the back of a forearm across its mouth to drink the cold condensation, or dips two fingers into one of the cup-sized hollows in the floor.

Sometimes, when the rising tide drives the chamber’s drone up to a pure, bone-shaking hum, the creature tilts its head back, opens its wide, lipless mouth, and answers. It does not shout—shouting is useless here. Instead, it drops a single, low note from deep in its barrel chest, pitched to match the exact resonance of the dome. For a few long breaths, its throat and the wet stone seem to lock together. The vibration in the floor steadies, thickening until the air itself feels solid, and the creature sits inside that doubled hum with its eyes closed, letting the sound pass straight through its ribs like a hand smoothing out creased cloth.

It does not belong entirely to the dark, though it rarely leaves it while the sun is up. Only on moonless nights, or when fog lies thick enough over the salt-marsh to hide the cleft, does it slip out. It folds its narrow shoulders sideways to squeeze past the briars and weathered rock, moving low through the bog-myrtle with the quiet, fluid heave of an otter. Out in the marsh, it hunts the mudflats for crabs, pries mussels from the tidal stones, and tears up handfuls of bitter glasswort, eating quickly and raw before sliding back into the seam of limestone ahead of the dawn.

Inside, it tends the small, quiet corner left behind by the people who used to come here. It has never lit a fire on the flat hearth-stone; the old charcoal is cold and meaningless to it, and it has no use for the chipped flint scraper tucked into the dry crack. Yet it cannot abide a rattle. Whenever the winter swells push harder through the lower fissure and one of the angular cobbles in the baffle wall begins to chatter—a sharp, dry *clack-clack* that cuts across the cavern’s deep voice like a broken tooth—the creature goes to it. It peels a clump of thick feather moss from the nearest boulder, squeezes the water from it, and wedges it firmly between the stones until the chattering stops and the room is smooth again.

Now and then, in the long hours between tides, it rests one hand against the weathered willow frame leaning against the wall, or traces the tip of a blunt finger along the notched tally-marks scratched into the soft limestone shelf. It does not count them. It only feels the small interruptions in the stone, the way it feels everything else in the dark.

Then it settles back behind the wall, draws its limbs in close, and waits for the sea to shift the pitch of the air once more.
