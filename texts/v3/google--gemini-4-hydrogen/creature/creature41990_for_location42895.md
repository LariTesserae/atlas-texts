To the eye—even an eye patient enough to watch the basin from dawn until the heat-haze blurs the horizon—nothing larger than a stalker lives here. The relay sheds stay locked. The power lines sag and tighten in the thermal shifts without a hand ever touching them.

Yet the plateau is not unpeopled. Its inhabitants simply do not stand on the tops of the ridges, and they do not spend their breath on the open air.

During the white-gold hours of midday, they are wedged into the deep, narrow fissures below the level of the travertine fins, stacked in the cool dark alongside the flat-bodied lizards. At rest, they are almost impossible to separate from the stone. Their backs and shoulders are broad, calloused, and pale as bleached rhyolite, crusted with a fine, chalky dust that reflects the glare; their bellies and inner limbs are dark, slate-gray, and laced with thick veins meant for dumping heat into damp rock. They fold their long, narrow-ribbed bodies sideways into the honeycombed pockets, knees drawn high, long-fingered hands tucked under their chins. Their chests rise and fall so slowly you might watch for a full minute before catching a single, silent breath drawn through slit-like nostrils that pinch shut between inhales to keep the moisture in.

They wait for the blue hour before dawn, when the cold air spills over the saw-toothed ridges and meets the radiating stone.

As the first dampness clings to the leeward cracks, they uncoil from the fissures. They are tall when they stand—spare, long-jointed, built for bridging gaps rather than walking flat ground. Their feet are wide, leathery pads with splayed, gripping toes that find purchase on knife-edged travertine without sending a single pebble clattering down the scree-slopes. Moving in twos or threes, or alone across wide stretches of the basin, they travel in absolute silence. Nothing with lungs wastes air on a voice up here.

Their first business is water. They kneel beside the tepid, mineral-heavy puddles under the overhangs, pressing their mouths to the water and filtering it slowly through narrow teeth, leaving faint ochre stains on their knuckles and white rings of purged salt crusted beneath their eyes and nostrils. They know every seam where the subterranean steam sweats into the rock, and they know the machines just as intimately.

To them, the scattered stations of the automated industry are neither sacred nor strange—they are simply a newer kind of geology, tall and predictable. At dawn, they climb the skeletal condenser-towers with effortless, spider-like precision, using scraps of dried woody scrub to brush salt-crusts and dead copper-shelled beetles from the fine copper mesh. They do not do this out of any loyalty to the unseen grid beyond the horizon; they do it because clean mesh condenses more mist, and more mist means a steadier trickle sweating down the outside of the insulated pipes and pooling around the concrete bases of the buried cisterns. They never pry open the windowless relay sheds, though they will sometimes press their dark, heat-starved bellies against the warm transformer boxes during the brief, bitter chill just before sunrise.

By the time the white-gold sun clears the outer ridges, they are already foraging on their way back to the shade. They prise swollen succulents from the rock-cracks with slivers of sharpened travertine, pluck sleeping lizards from the upper crevices, and tap open the warm, porous hives of the burrowing insects with probes of salvaged copper wire—always taking only a portion of the pale larvae inside before plastering the breach shut with wet, iron-rich mud so the colony survives to rebuild.

When they need to speak across the basin, they do not shout into the thin air. Instead, they go to the pylons.

A taut power line or a narrow metal conduit stretched between two distant stations carries a tremor for miles without costing a single lungful of breath. Placing a flat palm or the side of a jawbone against a metal guy-wire, they can feel the sub-audible hum of the geothermal cylinders, the distant *clack* of a pressure valve two ridges over, and the faint, deliberate rhythms tapped out by others of their kind. With a shard of ceramic insulator or the edge of a travertine knife, they strike and damp the taut wire in clipped, dry intervals—a language of vibrations that rides the outside of the automated grid, passing news of a cracked pipe, a rich seam of beetles, or an approaching cold front from station to station while the metering gauges click on, oblivious.

By mid-morning, the heat begins to rise off the basalt like the breath of an open kiln. The gliders catch the first thermals, spiraling upward on translucent wings. Down among the blistered mounds, the inhabitants slip back into the deep, wet seams of the plateau, tucking their salt-rimmed faces into the shadow of the stone, leaving the humming machines and the shimmering rock-fins to the sun.
