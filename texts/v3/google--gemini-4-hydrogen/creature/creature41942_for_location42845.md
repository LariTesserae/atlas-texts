If you descend to the wet basalt flags for only an hour, you will swear the shaft is empty of everything but the machines and the pale plants clinging to their joints. Eyes used to the surface are tuned to animal speed—to the dart of a wing around a bulb, the skitter of claws in a drain. Down here, in the amber haze, nothing moves faster than a cooling weld. You would have to stand still until the dry heat baked the hurry out of your joints, until your own breathing slowed to match the sigh of the hydraulic gates, before you noticed that the lagging on the overhead pipes is not all of a piece.

The first clues are small, and easily mistaken for the work of the machines themselves. Look at the massive brass flywheels: the mineral-rich steam leaves coral-like crusts of salt along their spokes, yet the gear-teeth where they mesh remain bright and slick. Look at the vertical guide-rails of the counterweights, greased with a pale, tallow-like wax that smells faintly of boiled fern-root and hot copper. Look at the undersides of the catwalks, where glassy stalactites of lime hang down like teeth. None of them ever grow long enough to foul the pulleys below. They are snapped off cleanly, and in the dry corners behind the brick kilns, you will find the broken tips stacked in neat, pale heaps, slowly baking back into dust.

If you watch the notch where a copper flue passes through a corner of brickwork—and if you have the patience to watch it for the length of three or four shifts of the hydraulic gates—you will see a bundle of tarred felt and plaster detach itself from the masonry. 

It does not startle or scurry. It moves with the agonizing, deliberate patience of a clock’s minute hand. A seam in its outer crust of lime parts with a dry, faint *tick*; a bead of clear, waxy sap wells up in the fracture, seals it, and turns opaque. 

At its core, it is kin to the stubborn, translucent ferns that drink from the condensers: a dense, knotted heart of pale rhizome, swollen with distilled water and threaded through with tough, fibrous vessels. Over decades, it has built a body around itself the way a root wraps a stone, gathering whatever the shaft sheds—strips of tarred felt, flakes of plaster, splinters of raw basalt, and the endless, creeping salt. It has no eyes, no mouth, no blood to boil in the oven-air. In a place where nothing flees and nothing hunts, sight is a useless expense.

Instead, it lives in the narrow seam between the shaft’s two extremes: the scorching dry heat of the lower manifolds and the weeping damp of the cold-return mains. It moves without muscles, driven by the same slow physics that governs the rest of the pit. When it leans into the cherry glow of a kiln’s draft-slit, the dry heat draws moisture from its outer casing; its salt-stiffened joints shrink and tighten, ratcheting its weight forward along the iron trusses. When it reaches the shaded underside of a cold pipe, it drinks the irregular drops through its porous plaster skin. Its waxy fibers swell, uncurling with a soft, fibrous creak, extending a long, jointed limb—half woody root, half calcified salt-bone—to turn a valve-wheel or smear a bead of its own tallow onto a dry rail.

They do not walk the switchback stairs the way the builders once did. There are perhaps a dozen of them—or perhaps they are all offshoots of a single, slow-creeping colony that long ago forgot where one body ends and the brickwork begins. They flow through the tightest clearances, hooking blunt, copper-stained extremities onto rivet-heads, draping themselves over pipe-bridges. Sometimes one will pause for half a day, pressed flat against a lagged steam-pipe, listening. It hears not with ears, but through the hum in its own mineral-heavy core, and through the brittle, hollow reeds that occasionally sprout from its upper crust—tall, colorless stalks identical to those rooted in the sulfur-silt, trembling in the rising thermal drafts. It knows the shaft by vibration: the healthy, visceral thrum of a clear flue; the thin, high *whine* of a bearing running dry; the stutter of a louver choked with lime.

They do not tend the boilers out of loyalty to whatever town built them, whose pale seam of gray light far overhead means nothing down here. They tend the shaft because the shaft is their body’s larger lung. If the kilns go cold, the thermal drafts die and the condensers stop weeping; if the flywheels seize, the brine-tanks stagnate and the salt-crusts choke the sumps. To them, the steep slate roofs are simply shelves that shed condensation; the caged filament bulbs are dry, amber fruits that never rot; the brass wheels are slow suns that keep the warm air turning.

When its work on a rail or a manifold is done, one of them will wedge itself back into the tight angle between a brick wall and a steel stanchion. It draws its waxy limbs inward, lets the heat bake its outer crust into a quiet, mineral shell, and waits for the next slow drip from the pipes above. In the dim light, as a drop hits the scorching iron below with a sharp *tst*, you could stare right at it and see only another lump of salt-crusted lagging, holding the town together in the dark.
