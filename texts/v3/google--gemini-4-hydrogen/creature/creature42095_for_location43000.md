If you stand still in the hazy, shadowless glare long enough, parts of the lattice begin to detach themselves and move against the grain of the wind.

At first, they look like nothing more than upright bundles of stripped reed-bark and weathered horn, pale with the same talc that coats the floor. They move with a clipped, braced economy, never trusting their weight to a single strut, always keeping one hand hooked into a tensioned cable or wedged behind a resin-rib. When a seed-bladder detonates nearby with its gunshot crack, they do not flinch; they only tilt their heads to judge the direction of the falling chaff, and keep climbing.

They are as dry as everything else in the basin.

There is no sweat on them—sweating here would be a kind of slow, fatal leak—and their skin has the tight, oiled sheen of cured parchment, rubbed daily with warm mineral sap and dusted with fine talc to keep it from splitting in the bone-dry updrafts. Around their eyes and mouths, where the skin works hardest, they smear thick daubs of pale plant-wax. Their hair, if they keep any, is cropped short and stiffened with resin so it cannot catch on the snapping tendrils or tangle with drifting pollen-ash. Most wear narrow slit-visors carved from the horn of the larger gliders, tied behind the skull with sinew to shut out the relentless, milky glare and the razor-edged salt dust.

They do not drink water, because there is none to drink. Instead, they live on the basin’s fierce, volatile chemistry. They tap the older, thicker resin-trunks with curved bone scrapers, catching the slow, amber-like sap in hollow reed-joints—a sharp, fiery liquid that tastes of toasted husks and warm turpentine, and leaves a faint mineral grit between the teeth. They crack open young seed-bladders before the gas builds to bursting, chewing the oily, nutty pith inside, and they roast the pale, blind borers over smoldering piles of chaff—not for moisture, which the borers scarcely have, but for the rich, waxy fat packed beneath their obsidian-hard heads.

Everything about them is built to endure the roar.

Outside the baffled huts, speech is useless. A shout dies two inches from the mouth, swallowed whole by the pipe-organ drone of the thermals and the metallic screech of the stridulators. So they pack their ears with rolled plugs of soft pith and wax, and they learn to listen with their bones instead. Their calloused feet, wrapped only in thin soles of glider-leather, read the basin through the soles up: the high, frantic thrumming of a reed-bridge about to snap; the heavy, dull thud of a glider slamming into an upper tower; the low, teeth-rattling rasp that means a pack of blind borers has chewed through the base of a nearby lattice.

When they need to talk across the tangle, they use the jungle itself. A tapper perched on a high shelf will hook a flint gaff into one of the long, taut fibrous cables that bind the towers together and pluck it—three sharp, dry twangs, a pause, a muffled scrape. Down the line, half a mile away in the hazy yellow light, another figure feels the vibration through a hand resting on the wood, and plucks a reply back into the roar. Up close, they speak with their hands in quick, jagged gestures—fingers stained bright yellow with sulfur-paste so the shapes stay legible through clouds of shed skins and wings—or they press their foreheads and knuckles together, humming low in their chests so the sound passes straight from skull to skull.

Their lives are paced to the frantic, jerky rhythm of the flora. Nothing in the basin belongs to anyone for long; a trail notched at dawn is swallowed by amber shoots by midday, or chewed to splinters by a passing stream of clicking grazers. So they travel light, carrying their whole lives strapped to their backs in sinew-stitched baskets: flint hooks, bone scrapers, spare wax, a bundle of sulfur-sticks, a coil of dried gut.

They are scavengers of opportunity and engineers of the temporary. When a great sector of cork-wood scaffolding groans, buckles, and crashes into the scree in a dry avalanche, the people of the basin converge on the wreckage before the dust has even settled. They swarm over the broken struts, prying loose the stunned, armored grazers, harvesting the exposed heart-wax from trunks that were unreachable an hour before, and dragging away the long, straight reeds before the new shoots can thread through them and lock them down.

They have no soft wood to burn, no clay to fire, no mud to pack. Their tools are horn, flint, hardened resin, and bone. The best sap-scrapers—the ones that keep an edge against the salt-crust without snapping—are carved from the femurs of their own dead, ground smooth against the sulfur-stained shale and polished with oil. When someone dies here, there is no wet earth to bury them in and no rot to take them. Instead, the body is wedged upright into the fork of a fast-growing resin-trunk; within days, the pale-amber shoots wrap around the ribs, the borers polish the joints clean, and the dead become part of the scaffolding, holding up the next generation’s blinds.

Only inside the huts—those low, crouching domes of woven bark and hardened wax wedged into the lee of the rock—does their pace slow.

Behind the stretched carapace-plates that serve as sound-baffles, the roaring wall of the basin drops to a dull, heavy hum, like distant surf heard through a stone wall. The floor still trembles underfoot, and fine dust still sifts down from the ceiling-mats in steady, dry curtains, but here, at last, a person can hear themselves think.

Here, they pull out their wax ear-plugs and unlace their horn visors. Their faces in the dim, filtered light are narrow and weathered, their eyes red-rimmed from the salt-glare, their lips cracked beneath coats of pale grease. They sit cross-legged on mats of flattened husks, passing a sealed reed-joint of warm sap from hand to hand, scraping the day’s resin from their knuckles with flint blades.

When they finally speak aloud in the muffled dark, their voices are dry, raspy, and pitched low—hoarse from disuse, almost indistinguishable from the faint, papery rustle of the husks outside. They do not talk about the past, because the basin has no memory; nothing here stays intact long enough to become history. They talk about which towers are swelling too fast, where the stridulator clouds are thickest, and which bridges are singing too high to trust tomorrow. Then they pack their ears with wax again, tie on their visors, and crawl back out into the bright, roaring, waterless day.
