If you stay long enough for your eyes to adjust to the silver glare, you realize the basin is not merely inhabited by the climbers in the cable trays and the mute, four-winged birds. Something larger lives here—something that has been standing three yards to your left the entire time, so still and matte-white that you mistook it for a ceramic insulator or the housing of a vertical switch.

You notice them first by their work. That chalk mark on the rim of the city-block flywheel did not put itself there.

When one of them finally shifts, it does not move like an animal. It moves like the pistons in the deep shafts: with a glacial, frictionless patience that makes it nearly invisible to an eye tuned to hurry. They are tall, narrow, and slab-sided, built for a world where clearance is measured in inches. Their outer surfaces are dry and unweathered—plates of pale, chalk-white plating that meet along hairline seams, chamfered at the edges like the steel I-beams so they never snag on a pipe flange. They have no faces to speak of, only a vertical recess of smoked, composite glass where a brow might be, angled slightly downward to keep the overexposed sky out of whatever passes for their sight.

Their limbs have an extra joint—not a deformity, just a quiet concession to the basin’s three-dimensional crowding—allowing their arms and legs to fold completely flat against their torsos. In this way, one of them can slip sideways through the four-inch gap between a transformer bank and a copper manifold without disturbing the silver-wire moss growing on the rivets. The fiberglass-furred climbers do not scatter when they pass; a cluster of climbers will hang upside down from one of their outstretched forearms as calmly as if it were a galvanized conduit, waiting out the noon glare.

They do not breathe the thin, sharp air, at least not in any way that plumes or makes a sound. Instead, they seem to sustain themselves on the basin’s stray discrepancies.

Watch one cross a maintenance terrace and stop beside the cluster of thick glass pipes. From where you stand, the pipes are full of that slow, luminous grey fluid. From where it stands—three paces to the left—the pipes are empty, and the fluid is pooling instead across the dry aluminum decking. The tall figure folds itself down with a faint, dry whisper of plate against plate, lowers its smoked-glass face to the seemingly dry metal, and drinks. There is no slurp, no ripple. The luminous grey sheen simply thins and vanishes from the aluminum, drawn upward into the seams of its jaw, while your own boots, standing inches away in the same pool, remain completely dry.

They are as fluent in the fractured geometry of the valley as the four-winged birds are in its updrafts. A chalk-white figure will walk along a third-tier catwalk, pass behind a three-foot cooling duct, and emerge on the far side no taller than your knee—shrunk to the exact proportions it needs to crawl inside a condenser housing and scrape clean a clogged gutter. Minutes later, it will step out from behind a narrow structural pylon restored to its full, spindly height, carrying a pinch of waxy lichen in two flat fingers. Where the missing inches went while it was small, and where it kept them, is a question the basin does not answer.

In the same way, they do not walk around the seamless walls of vitrified brick where the empty conveyor belts vanish. They simply press a flat palm against the masonry and step into it, continuing uninterrupted on the far side as if the brick were only a change in the light.

They are not alone in using the seams. If you linger near the steel drainage channels, you will see them kneeling at the edges of the inch-deep puddles, reaching their long, jointed arms down past the surface—past the inch-thick steel plate beneath—all the way to the shoulder into the reflection of the inverted sky. Down there, in the deeper, colder basin that only exists in standing water, something else lives.

You never see those inverted things whole, only what the keepers bring up—or what slips up on its own. Sometimes a four-winged bird that dove into a puddle hours ago will burst back upward out of a completely dry puddle-stain, its translucent feathers crusted with a frost that does not melt in the sun. Sometimes a keeper will draw its arm back out of the reflection holding a pale, ribbon-like creature that has no thickness at all, only width and length, undulating through the thin air like a strip of bright tin before the keeper tucks it neatly into a slot in its own chest-plating.

And then there are the shadows.

When a visitor’s shadow detaches from their heels and slides up onto the underside of the overhead grating, it does not always return on its own. Left alone, those loose shadows begin to behave like flat, dark lichens—spreading along the busbars, pooling in the bolt-holes, feeding on the dry static prickle until the air around them smells faintly of ozone and cold iron. The chalk-white inhabitants spend hours tending to them. They carry long, flat-bladed scrapers of brushed aluminum, peeling the stray shadows off the underside of the catwalks with quiet, rhythmic strokes, rolling them up like bolts of black cloth and tucking them away into the deep, narrow shafts where the light never reaches. They themselves cast no shadows at all; whatever darkness belongs to them has been folded inward long ago.

Even inside the air’s smaller fractures, something quick and bright has taken root.

When a drop of condensation detaches from a brass fitting and hangs motionless at eye level for three full minutes—trembling slightly, holding the reflection of that impossible interior room full of spinning gears—you can see movement inside the bead of water if you lean close enough. Between the teeth of the phantom brass wheels, tiny, needle-limbed things dart and cling. They are no larger than specks of solder, bright as freshly cut copper, moving at a frantic, chittering speed that has nothing to do with the glacial patience of the valley outside. They seem to live their entire lives in the three-minute pause before gravity remembers the drop, scurrying across the spinning gears that do not exist anywhere in the basin, until the drop snaps upward back into the pipe and takes their whole world with it.

The tall, chalk-white keepers ignore the hanging drops, just as they ignore the staircases that loop back onto their own landings. They have their own work to see to, slow and silent as the turning of the flywheel. They walk the lattice with sticks of dry white chalk tucked into the joints of their wrists, pausing beside piston sleeves, transformer housings, and the rims of vast, silent wheels to make small, crisp hash marks on the unweathered metal.

They are keeping track of what moves, and how fast, and whether anything has begun to slip.

If you stand still long enough in the blinding silver glare—long enough for your breathing to slow and the sound of your own pulse to arrive a half-second before your heart beats—one of them may step silently out of the clearance behind you. It will not speak, and it will not touch your skin. It will only lean its narrow, smoked-glass brow close to your shoulder, wait a patient minute to see if you shift, and then draw a single, neat line of white chalk across the sleeve of your coat—just to be sure, when it looks back an hour from now, whether you are part of the machinery or merely passing through.
