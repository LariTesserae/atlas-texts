If you only stand at the rim, or hurry across the floor to inspect the buried disks, you will never see it. Your eyes are too busy sorting the glare of the plateau from the emerald shock of the succulents, and your ears are too full of the cicadas’ saw-toothed static and the wet, percussive *tock-tock-tock* of the frogs. In a room this loud, anything that does not buzz, snap, or leap effectively ceases to exist.

It takes twenty minutes of sitting still on a warm ledge before the pattern of the bare rock begins to bother you.

The hollow is packed with life, yet the wide lanes of smooth gneiss between the moss cushions are weirdly immaculate. There are no wind-drifted piles of dried cicada husks in the corners, no shed mantis skins caught against the base of the ferns, no dead beetles baking on the sunlit stone. Even the palm-sized cups worn beneath the dripping fern fronds are clean of grit, their rims worn smooth as soapstone.

Then a shadow near the pale, slotted ceramic ring detaches itself from the wall—except the shadow does not match the sun, and it is moving uphill.

At first glance it looks like a slab of the iron-stained gneiss that has simply decided to slide across the floor. It is roughly the length of a human arm, wide-bellied and flat-backed, its upper hide the exact mottled slate-gray and rust of the bedrock, dusted with a dry rime of quartz grit. It has no shell, no scales, no fur—only thick, pebbled skin that pleats loosely along its flanks like heavy wet canvas. Four short, muscular limbs splay outward from its sides, ending in broad, paddle-flat hands with four blunt, calloused fingers apiece.

It moves with a slow, sweeping gait, swinging each forelimb in a wide, deliberate arc before setting the palm down. As it goes, the pale, fringed skin along its lower flanks drags lightly over the rock, gathering every speck of fallen chaff—a broken copper wing, a dried frog-slough, a pinch of plateau dust—and rolling it inward toward the soft, translucent pouch beneath its jaw.

Nothing in the hollow flees from it. A stick-thin mantid holds its ground as the broad gray flank glides two inches past its forelegs; a jewel-skinned frog barely blinks as the creature’s blunt snout passes over its crevice. It moves too slowly to trigger their panic, too low and heavy to register as anything other than shifting stone.

It is deaf to the roar of the bowl. There are no ear-openings behind its wide, bony head—only smooth, fused plates of mineral-hardened bone. The screaming wall of cicadas and the rifle-crack of diving swifts do not exist for it as sound, only as a faint, meaningless tickle when a bird’s wingtip grazes a fern. Instead, it listens with its chest. Beneath the pebbled gray hide of its back, its sternum is a broad, flat keel of resonant cartilage pressed flush against the warm gneiss. Through its palms and its belly, it reads the hollow in vibrations: the tiny, rhythmic *plip* of water falling into the floor-cups, the scramble of beetle claws across a fissure, and, deeper than all of them, the slow, mechanical pulse of the buried artifacts.

Its eyes are almost useless in the high-altitude glare. Set beneath heavy, overhanging ridges of bone, they open only as narrow horizontal slits of milky silver—built not for sharp edges, but for catching the subtle shimmer of damp air and the faint, oily distortion that hangs above the seven or eight machines.

Watch it long enough, and you see where its circuits lead.

While the cicadas stop dead at the invisible boundary around each artifact, leaving neat, bare halos of stone, the sweeper crosses straight through them without hesitation. It pauses beside the matte-black dinner-plate disk, lowering its chin until its throat-pouch rests on the warm rock. The dust motes caught in slow, lazy orbit around the metal drift against its dusty shoulders and cling there, breaking free of their invisible tracks.

Where the structured dew beads on the nearby stone in precise hexagonal lattices, the creature presses one broad, pale palm flat over the pattern. The water does not scatter; drawn by the fine, sponge-like pores of its underside, the whole lattice collapses inward and flows up into its skin, leaving the rock dry behind it. When it reaches one of the palm-sized cups beneath the ferns, it does not lap like a dog. It simply lays its face sideways into the shallow hollow and drinks through the soft, pale membrane of its cheek, its milky eyes half-closing in the bath-warm shade.

It never eats the living things in the bowl, though a frog would be an easy mouthful and a resting cicada easier still. Instead, when its throat-pouch is full of the dead husks and wind-blown grit it has swept from the open stone, it crawls to the edge of a copper-colored sphagnum cushion, parts the damp, velvety moss with two blunt fingers, and spits the compacted pellet of chitin and dust deep into the root-mat. Then it pats the moss back down and moves on. Whether by instinct or ancient habit, it tends the only soil in the world it knows, keeping the bare lanes clear and the green islands fed, wasting nothing in a basin forty paces wide.

It is only when the hollow *thrums* that you realize there is more than one.

Without warning, the deep, sub-audible tremor ripples out from the buried machines. The air does not change, and the cicadas do not miss a beat, but every hanging droplet on the maidenhair ferns shivers and hops upward a fraction of an inch before gravity catches it again.

Instantly, the sweeper freezes. It flattens its chest hard against the gneiss, and the loose, pleated skin along its neck and flanks tightens and swells slightly—not with breath, but with water drawn taut beneath the hide, turning its whole body into a living drumhead tuned to the standing wave in the stone.

Across the hollow, thirty feet away, a second shape—smaller, paler, clinging vertically to the curved wall just inside the impossible, inward-leaning shadow of the slotted ceramic ring—freezes in the exact same posture. Until that moment, you would have sworn it was a shelf of weathered mineral crust. Both of them hold perfectly still for the three or four seconds it takes the vibration to die out of the rock, drinking the deep *thrum* through their bones while the frantic, deafening jungle roars unnoticed around them.

Then the tremor fades. The droplets settle back onto the ferns. The smaller one unhooks its broad fingers from the wall and begins its slow, sweeping descent toward the floor, while the larger one turns back toward the sunlit gneiss, gathering a single fallen beetle wing into its palm as it goes.
