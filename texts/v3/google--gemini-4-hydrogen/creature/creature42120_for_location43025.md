At first glance, looking out across the terraced ridges and sunken flats, you would not know anything lived here at all beyond the occasional beetle and the high, drifting glider. Whatever inhabits the valley floor wears the valley’s own colors—the sodden grey-green of micro-moss, the dark umber of wet peat, the dull pewter sheen of settled metallic dust—and moves at a pace so deliberate that it vanishes against the frantic, silent churning of the flora.

Only when one of the low, humped shapes uncurls from the base of a silver-barked reed does it separate itself from the landscape.

They are broad, heavy-shouldered things, roughly the height of a crouching bear, built low to keep beneath the flight of flung, iron-hard seeds and out of the path of the tallest static arcs. They have no true fur; instead, their backs and flanks are shingled in thick, overlapping plates of dark, non-conductive horn, fused over years with the valley’s own crystalline resin. Sodden micro-moss has taken root in the seams between these plates, turning each inhabitant into a walking mound of insulated turf. Beneath the moss, their hides are slick with a pale, sulfur-scented oil that sheds the freezing vapor and keeps the wet from reaching their skin.

To walk here is to walk on acid, pressure, and live charge. Their feet are wide and flat, shod in thick, pale pads of calloused wax that hiss faintly when they step across an acid-secreting mat, sacrificing a thin layer of dead tissue with every stride and regrowing it from the inside out. Their forelimbs are shorter, ending in blunt, spatulate claws wrapped in leathery sheaths—hands made not for tearing, but for prying, wedging, and holding fast when a thirty-foot vascular tower buckles nearby and sends a shudder rolling through the peat.

In a valley wrapped so tightly in sound-deadening fleece, they have no use for external ears. Their heads are wedge-shaped and smooth, with small, deep-set eyes protected by cloudy second lids that flick shut whenever a reed-stand discharges or a puff of metallic dust blows off the press-plants. Instead of listening to the air, they read the ground and the charge. Through the waxy pads of their feet, they feel the slow peristaltic pulse of the braided slurry-vines, the tightening groan of an osmotic tendril winding toward its snap, the distant, muffled thud of a collapsing grove. Along their jaws lie a pair of thick, pale filaments, usually tucked neatly into grooves in the horn; when they approach a cluster of swollen spore-boilers or a thicket of silver reeds, those filaments unfurl, tasting the static buildup in the damp air. A sudden prickle along the filament tells them to flatten themselves into the moss seconds before a blue-white arc jumps or a tendril whips loose.

They are creatures of the brief, violent intervals between assembly and dissolution.

They do not graze on the living machinery—the reeds are too acidic, the vines too tough and pressurized. Instead, they shadow the valley’s breakdowns. An inhabitant will sit motionless for an hour beside a towering, resin-heavy stalk, waiting as it leans and groans. The moment it collapses into the turf, the creature moves in with surprising, heavy grace, working in the narrow window before the acid mats beneath can claim the wreckage. With its blunt claws, it cracks open the still-warm vascular chambers, scooping out the dark, mineral-rich paste and the soft, uncured resin at the core. When a pressurized spore-boiler swells until its waxy seams begin to split, the creature will sometimes lean in and slit the seam itself, holding its wide, slit-like nostrils over the vent to drink in the brief rush of warm, damp gas before it freezes into rime.

They have even learned the patience of the broad-leaved press-plants. Now and then, one will gather a handful of the iron-hard seeds scattered in the moss—seeds too dense for its own jaws—and drop them neatly onto the open leaf-bed of a press, then wait quietly beside the dark condensation pool as the plant slowly folds inward, crushing the shells and squeezing out the rich, sulfurous pulp for the creature to lap up once the leaf relaxes.

They are almost never seen in groups. The valley’s thin air and cold, specialized bounty cannot support a herd, and two of them meeting along a ridge will usually pass with only a slow, heavy nod of their armored heads. Yet they are not entirely solitary. In the crevices between their back-plates, they carry passengers: two or three of the valley’s heavy-shelled, pale beetles. Over days, mineral crust and crystallized sap build up along the creatures’ joints, threatening to lock their armor stiff; the beetles inch quietly across their backs, scraping the crust clean, undisturbed by the slow, steady gait beneath them.

When they do speak to one another across the muffled miles—to mark a freshly collapsed grove, or to warn of a sudden frost short-circuiting a basin—they do not use their throats. They raise one heavy heel and drive it down into the compacted peat, once, then twice, sending a low, dull vibration rippling through the ancient strata of petrified bark. To anything watching from above, it is indistinguishable from the valley’s own endless thudding.

When the cold deepens or a creature has eaten its fill and needs to shed the static charge building in its horn plates, it does not sleep in the open, where the acid mats would slowly test its pads and the mist would ice its joints. Instead, it searches out the hollowed, emptied husks of dead slurry-vines—wide as tree trunks, dry and insulated within—and slips headfirst down into the dark. Down there, winding through the braided roots beneath the peat, it finds the faint, steady warmth radiating from the unseen root-furnaces, and curls up in the dry dark to wait for the next collapse.
