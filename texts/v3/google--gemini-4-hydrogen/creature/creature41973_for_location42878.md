At first, it seems that nothing could live here except the plants—that anything with a pulse or a purpose would be ground to paste by the rolling cobbles or swept over the rim of the plateau into the abyss. In the bruised, near-black water, the eye finds only bare stone, roaring silt, and the violent swaying of the groves.

Yet in the lee of the low dry-stone walls, and inside the caved-in mouths of the single-room shelters, there are heaps of cobbles and tangles of grey-olive fiber that do not wash away when the current turns.

To look closely at one is to mistake it for debris: a snarl of drowned reed-roots, perhaps, or a bundle of old, waterlogged rope snagged on a fallen lintel. It has no eyes, no mouth, no symmetry of limb or shell. It is as bare of flesh as the slate beneath it. Only if you wait—through the long, grinding roar of a tidal surge, through the distant *thump* of a bursting bellows-kelp—do you notice that the tangle is under tension.

Fine, tough filaments, no thicker than twine and faintly crusted with limestone silt, stretch taut from the heap to the surrounding stones. Where a doorway has collapsed, dozens of these threads are strung across the gap like the warp of a loom, anchored to the slate walls and weighted at their lower ends with river-worn pebbles or old, notched flint sinkers. When a thirty-foot organ-reed groans half a mile away, the threads hum in sympathy, vibrating so cleanly that they shed the silt settling on them.

These are the valley’s true inhabitants: neither plant nor animal, but slow, stone-hearted knots of braided cellulose and cold-water fungus that have grown into the exact physics of the drowning shelf.

They have no skeletons, so they borrow the valley’s weight. At the center of each fibrous mass lies a dense, leathery pouch packed tightly with ingested gravel, limestone grit, and fist-sized cobbles. This ballast is what keeps them on the plateau when the subterranean river pours across the terraces. Anchored by their own heavy bellies, they can rest in the open troughs without being tumbled into the dark beyond the rim.

Because they have no muscles to swim and no legs to scuttle, they cross the barren expanses between the groves with the patience of creeping roots—or else they borrow the current itself. A heavy, silt-crusted mound will slowly draw in water and build a small pocket of metabolic gas inside its fibrous core, just enough to lighten its stone-packed belly by a fraction of an ounce. For a heartbeat, the surge lifts it off the slate, nudging it a few inches downstream; then a pore opens, the gas vents in a single cold bubble, and the creature drops back into the gravel with a dull, stony *clack*. In this way, over the course of weeks, they inch across the terraces, leaving behind only faint, scoured tracks where their rough undersides have rubbed the glowing moss from the stone.

They gather where the current slows. Inside the ruined shelters, the roaring water breaks into quiet, swirling eddies, and here the pale spores of the bellows-kelp and the shattered, calcified shards of glass-ferns settle like snow. The stone-hearted knots congregate in these pockets, unspooling sticky, pale-grey hyphae across the silt to draw the fallen detritus inward.

They do not digest their food with acid or teeth. Instead, they grind. As the constant surge of the valley rocks their heavy bodies back and forth—or as their tough, fibrous bands rhythmically tighten and slacken—the cobbles inside their cores rub together, milling the tough fern-shards and spore-husks into a fine, mineral-rich paste. If you lay an ear against a dry-stone wall in the dark, beneath the thunder of the outer current, you can sometimes hear them at work: a slow, muffled, rhythmic crunching, like dozens of tiny mortars turning in the silt.

In the freezing, oxygen-poor water, they live on a timescale closer to the erosion of the slate than to the brief, darting lives of the upper seas. They are creatures of vibration and chemistry. They have no ears, yet their taut listening-threads catch every pressure wave that crosses the plateau—the shifting chord of a reed-grove as the tide turns, the hiss of a mineral cataract spilling over a terrace lip, the far-off boom of a spore-pod bursting in the murk. When a strong chord strikes them, a pale green-white luminescence—the same dead-starlight shimmer that clings to the moss—ripples slowly along their fungal veins, a silent, chemical echo of the valley’s noise.

Over decades, they become part of the architecture that shelters them. They do not remember the people who stacked the slate walls or carved the stone mortars, yet they tend the ruins with a blind, practical fidelity. When a stone slips from a dry-stone wall, widening a gap and letting the roaring current scour out a quiet eddy, a nearby knot will slowly creep up the breach, wrap its tough, mineral-secreting fibers around the fallen slate, and wedge it back into place. Others coil around the forgotten artifacts surfacing in the gravel—a hammered-copper awl slowly leaching trace ions into the dark, or a smooth stone bowl wedged between two roots—using them as anchors, as ballast, as the mineral hearts around which new braids of fiber form.

When one grows too old and heavy to lift its own stones, it stops creeping altogether. It settles deep into the silt of a doorway or the corner of a collapsed wall, draws its listening-threads tight, and lets the limestone grit in the water slowly petrify its outer skin. Within a few years, it is indistinguishable from the river-worn slate around it—another rounded boulder in the wall, holding back the current for the younger, humming knots that string their threads across its back.

And so the valley keeps its own company. Above, the cold sea presses down in bruised, unbroken darkness; below, the abyss waits beyond the rim. Between them, the reeds groan, the kelp-bladders boom, and in the quiet lees of the sunken stones, the heavy, eyeless things tighten their threads against the surge and listen to the grinding of the world.
