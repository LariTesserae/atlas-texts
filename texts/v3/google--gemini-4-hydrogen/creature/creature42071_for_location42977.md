At first, you would not know they are there. 

In the dim twilight and the crushing wall of sound, anything that does not flutter, shriek, or stridulate disappears into the geometry of the ruins. A shape hunched on a stepped terrace looks like a slump of dry-laid limestone; a figure standing beneath a gallery arch blends into the hanging curtains of pale-green creepers. They add nothing to the roar. They do not shout over it, and they do not flinch from it.

They are human in outline, or were once, though millennia in the sunken bowl have pared them down to match its austerity. They are lean and dry-fleshed, their skin the dull, chalky buff of weathered bone, dusted so thoroughly with limestone grit and powdered guano that they seem carved from the same bedrock as the plazas. They wear loose, layered wrappings woven from the shredded inner bark of salt-cedars and bound with the tough, leathery vines of the creepers, patched here and there with the cured pelts of bats. Nothing on them jingles or flaps; there is no metal here to clink, and no loose cloth to catch a draft.

Their relationship to the noise is the first thing that sets them apart from any outsider. An unshielded visitor is battered into numbness by the relentless acoustic pressure, but the people of the basin sealed themselves against the air long ago. From childhood, their ear canals are plugged with smooth pellets of hardened fig-resin mixed with chalk dust, leaving only thick, weathered outer rims. They do not listen to the air. Instead, they listen to the stone.

They go barefoot on broad, flat soles hardened to the texture of horn, and when they pause in the wide, empty avenues, they stand with their weight settled evenly into their heels, or crouch with their palms and chins pressed lightly to the scoured bedrock. The roar of the millions of bats, swiftlets, and insects does not stop at the surface of the plazas; it vibrates through the dry-laid masonry, turning the entire basin into a single, humming drumhead. Through the bones of their feet, their shins, and their heavy, squared jaws, they read the world. A falling stone three terraces away, a sudden scattering of rock-doves in an upper tower, the slow drag of a visitor’s boots across the grit—all of it arrives as a shift in the hum, a brief, localized shadow in the pressure against their teeth and sternums.

Because they cannot speak into the roaring air, they carry their voices inside their skulls. When two of them meet on the undulating, grit-worn ramps, they step close and press their foreheads together, or lay a flat palm against the other’s collarbone and throat. With their mouths closed, they click their molars or hum deep in the back of the palate—dry, percussive little rhythms that travel straight through bone and cartilage, clear and private inside the head, completely untouched by the deafening chaos an inch away.

Across the broad plazas, where they cannot touch, they speak in slowness. There are no storms here, no rain, no sudden gusts of wind to scatter the dust. A line drawn with a finger in the fine limestone grit of a square will stay sharp for years. They leave messages for one another in small, deliberate arrangements: a sprig of salt-cedar wedged into a dry-laid joint, three chalk pebbles balanced on a slumped lintel, a shallow spiral traced around the base of a gnarled fig. A conversation across a quarter of the city might unfold over the course of a decade, one person shifting a stone by a hand’s breadth, another returning seasons later to answer it.

Their lives are governed by the same patience as the plants. With no surface water anywhere in the basin, they survive by tapping the unseen aquifers through the massive, pale taproots that snake down through hairline fractures in the rock. Each family or cluster knows the great dryland figs of its quarter the way other peoples know wells. Carrying awls of shaped bone and wedges of knapped flint, they climb down into the shadowy crevices beneath the terraces and pierce the swollen roots—never deep enough to kill, only enough to draw a slow, steady bead of cool, chalk-bitter sap. They drink through hollow swiftlet-bones, waiting motionless in the twilight for each drop to gather, and when they have taken enough, they seal the wound with a paste of chewed fig-leaf and limestone dust so not a breath of moisture is lost to the bone-dry air.

There is no fire in the basin. The air is choked with desiccated leaves, papery vines, and dry guano; a single spark would turn the sunken bowl into a furnace. Everything they eat is cold and cured by the arid air. They move through the vaulted ceilings of the squat towers with a slow, liquid economy that barely disturbs the roosting colonies, plucking torpid bats by the handful during the dim hours, gathering fallen swiftlet eggs, and sweeping baskets through the dry thickets for crickets and cicadas. They press the crushed insects with salt-cedar seeds and bitter figs into dense, gray cakes that keep for years without spoiling.

They do not see themselves as masters of the city, nor as squatters in someone else’s ruin. If their ancestors built these stepped terraces and wide avenues, the memory of it has long since been ground away like the sharp edges of the staircases. What remains is a quiet stewardship. Moving silently through the dimness, they wedge flat stones beneath slumping lintels to keep the towers from collapsing; they prune back the heavy tapestries of creepers when they threaten to choke an avenue; they guide the thick, thirsty roots away from fragile joints in the masonry.

They are simply another part of the basin’s final equilibrium—dry, unhurried, and enduring, holding their place in the deafening twilight with the same quiet stubbornness as the figs and the stone.
