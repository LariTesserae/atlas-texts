To live on the plateau is to live on the tight-stretched membrane of a drum, and everything that survives here has shaped itself to the beat.

### Beneath the Skin

Down in the dark, wind-scoured voids between the bedrock and the root-mat, life is pale, flat, and acutely tuned to vibration. No sun reaches these chambers, only the steady, organ-pipe boom of the gale blowing across the plateau’s hollow ribs and the faint, rhythmic *thud-thud* of footsteps worlds away above. 

Here, clinging to the fibrous underside of the root-ceiling, live the **gleaners**—blind, segmented creatures as wide as a human palm and thin as a leather strap. They move like slow ripples across the woven roots, chewing away the oldest, deadest fibers with scissor-like mandibles and leaving behind a fine, dry sawdust that the underground drafts swirl into drifts against the limestone. Their shed husks, translucent and papery, hang from the root-pillars like forgotten lanterns.

Moving through those same root-pillars are the **under-packs**. They are lean, long-bodied predators, furless except for a sparse ridge of stiff bristles along the spine, their skin the color of chalk and wet clay. They have no need for eyes; their narrow skulls are dominated by flaring, petal-like nostrils and arrays of yard-long whiskers that sprout from their cheeks, wrists, and flanks. As they lope silently across the glass-polished stone floor, those whiskers brush the root-ceiling overhead, reading the world above as a shifting map of pressure and weight. 

When a long-eared grazer pauses on the turf above to nibble a cushion of moss, the pack freezes. They gather beneath the faint depression where the moss sags, brace their broad, clawed hind feet against the stone, and strike upward in unison—driving their wedge-shaped snouts straight through the living weave to snap at ankles and bellies before dropping back into the dark. A moment later, the springy turf closes over the tear, leaving only a tuft of disturbed sedge and a fading tremor.

### In the Light and the Roar

Above, in the blinding, unfiltered sun, the plateau belongs to things that can endure the wind and the noise.

The **copper-shelled locusts** are the true masters of the mat. Heavy as plums and armored in burnished, metallic bronze, they do not merely hop; they launch themselves into the gale like sling-stones, letting the wind carry them in rattling, shimmering clouds before dropping back into the sedge. Their wing-cases are so thick that when they swarm, the sound is like hail on a tin roof. They chew relentlessly at the silica-ribbed sedge, their jaws grinding with a dry, metallic rasp that forms the bassline of the plateau’s endless roar.

Hunted by the harriers and the rust-colored stalkers, the **grazers** live lives of perpetual, twitching readiness. They are small, no larger than hares, with hind legs coiled like springs and ears nearly as long as their bodies—translucent, veined with blood, and constantly swiveling. They do not dig burrows; instead, they know every natural vent and thin spot in the root-mat, diving headfirst into the hollow dark at the first shadow of a hawk, though it means risking the pale packs waiting below.

The **rust-colored predators** that hunt them are marvels of focus. Built like lean, long-strided hounds or tall-shouldered cats, their coats match the dried, iron-rich moss of late summer. Because the wind and the locusts drown out nearly all subtle sound, they hunt with their feet and their eyes. A stalker will stand motionless on a stone dome for an hour, watching the silver-green turf for the telltale, unnatural shiver of a grazer moving beneath the blades, or pressing its chin flat to the moss to feel the difference between the erratic pattering of prey and the steady, dangerous *thrum* of a human stride.

Even the solitary **giant reeds**—rising thirty feet into the empty air like bleached masts—harbor their own isolated worlds. Inside their dark, hollow throats, where morning mist condenses and trickles down the pale walls, live colonies of **reed-skinks**: tiny, slick-skinned lizards with suction-padded toes that never touch the ground. They feed on the midges drawn to the dampness, drinking directly from the sweating walls of their vertical caves. High above them, strung across the fractured, whistling crowns of the reeds, **wind-weavers**—heavy, yellow-legged spiders—spin dense, elastic funnels of silk that hum like harp-strings in the gale, catching the locusts that ride the upper air.

### The People of the Drum

And then there are the people.

They are few, scattered across the twenty-mile swell in small, shifting bands, and they have learned to be as light and tough as the sedge itself. You would know them first by their posture: a slight, perpetual crouch into the wind, knees soft, weight balanced over the balls of their feet. To walk here with a heavy heel is to risk punching through an old, rotted patch of turf into the dark below, or—worse—drawing the attention of an under-pack.

They are lean, sun-blackened, and wind-leathered. Their hair is cropped close or braided tight with sedge-twine and weighted with small beads of hammered copper so it does not whip into their eyes. Because the silica-ribbed sedge cuts bare skin like glass, they wrap their shins and forearms in flexible greaves woven from cured grazer-hide and overlapping plates of locust-chitin. Their feet, however, are bare in the warm hours, or shod only in thin, supple moccasins. Over generations, their soles have grown broad, splayed, and thick as horn, yet they remain exquisitely sensitive—capable of feeling the difference between solid bedrock beneath a shallow mat and a hollow chamber yawning twenty feet deep.

Out on the open plain, where the wind and the insects swallow the human voice, they rarely speak aloud. Shouting shreds the throat and carries no farther than an arm’s length. Instead, they converse in **blade-talk**: a crisp, wide-armed sign language of sharp angles and flattened palms, easily read against the bright sky from half a mile away. For closer, subtler talk—or when mist rolls over the rim and hides them from one another—they speak through the ground. A person will kneel, press the tip of a hollow reed-cane or their own chin against the turf, and listen as a companion a hundred yards off taps out a rhythm with a weighted walking-staff: *Safe footing. Water in the dome. Pack beneath.*

Only inside their low-slung, boat-shaped shelters, where the thick walls of split reed and cured hide muffle the gale to a dull, comforting rumble, do they use their voices—speaking in low, gravelly tones that rarely rise above a murmur.

Their lives are bound to the rhythms of the mat and the rare stone domes:
*   **Water:** At dawn, before the sun drinks the mist, they walk the reed-stands with long, slender bone spiles, tapping the hollow stems just above the root-line to drain the sweet, mineral-tinged condensation trapped inside. After an afternoon rain, they hurry to the glass-polished stone domes to ladle tea-colored water from the natural catch-basins into cured-bladder skins before the wind evaporates it.
*   **Food:** They sweep wide-mouthed nets of woven fiber through the sedge at first light, when the copper-shelled locusts are still heavy and sluggish with morning dew. The insects are roasted over small, smokeless fires of dried root-peat—carefully tended on flat stone slabs so the living turf doesn't smolder—or split and hung on racks to dry in the ceaseless wind. They trap the long-eared grazers with nooses set at the root-vents, and occasionally, when a band grows bold and hungry, they will lure an under-pack to a thin patch of moss and spear it from above.
*   **Metal:** They do not mine, for they cannot dig. Instead, they scour the pitted limestone of the rare, exposed domes, prying out small nuggets of native copper left behind by eons of wind-erosion. They hammer these cold between river-worn stones into thin, bright clappers to hang from their path-poles, or into curved awls for sewing hide.

Because the living skin of the plateau is both their floor and their furnace—warming them from below as they sleep pressed close to the moss—they treat it with a quiet, practical reverence. You do not cut the turf deeper than a finger’s length without pressing your palm to the wound to feel its pulse; you do not spill blood on it if you can catch it in a bowl, lest the pale things below learn your scent.

And when someone dies, they are never buried. To put a body into the dark below would be an insult to the skin, and a gift to the packs. Instead, the dead are carried to the highest, smoothest stone domes and laid out beneath the open sky for the swifts and the harrier-hawks. Once the wind and the birds have finished their work, the band returns for the skull. They rub it smooth with grazer-tallow, bore a hole through the crown, and hang it from one of the tall path-poles beside the hammered-copper clappers—so that even after the breath is gone, the bone can keep clattering in the roar, marking the way for those who still walk the drum.
