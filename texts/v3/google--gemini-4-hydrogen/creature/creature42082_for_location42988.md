You recognize them first by their hands and by the way they listen.

The hands are broad, flat-nailed, and pale along the knuckles, the skin toughened by a lifetime of wet slate and pickled slightly at the cuticles by the mineral water. Even when dry, a faint, chalky bloom lingers in the creases of their palms and under their fingernails—the residue of the same pale crust they spend their days scraping from the settling-pans. They do not wear rings or bracelets; anything loose catches on dry-stacked stone or snags in the wicker filter-baskets. Instead, they bind their wrists and ankles with oiled rawhide strips to keep the cold seepage from wicking up their sleeves.

They dress for water rather than warmth, in short, sleeveless tunics of tightly felted marsh-wool and aprons of greased hide that shed the constant mist. Their footwear is always soft-soled—raw hide cured with tallow and stitched with sinew—because hard leather or wooden clogs would slip on the glazed slate and turn every roof-walkway into a drumhead. When they move through the narrow, stepped alleys, they walk with a slight, habitual crouch at the knees, weight kept forward over the balls of their feet, one hand always hovering near a wall or lintel. It is the posture of people who have spent their lives on wet stairs where a single misstep drops you into a drainage channel or onto a neighbor’s shingle.

Because there are no open spaces in the cleft, there is no privacy of sight, so they have built a culture of extreme privacy of sound. Conversations are conducted at the volume of a breath, pitched below the soft, continuous hiss of the sheeting water. Children learn before they can walk steadily not to shout; a raised voice in the lower tiers bounces off the damp, four-story faces of slate and turns into a confusing clutter of echoes that masks the sound of the machinery.

And the machinery must be heard. Every household knows the exact cadence of the tipping-troughs in its tier—the slow, wooden *clack* of a counterweight dropping, the brief rush of released water, the dry knock as the empty trough swings back. If a trough misses its beat, or if the hiss of a roof-gutter deepens into a choked gurgle, doors open along the stairways before anyone has even called out. Someone is already emerging with a bone pick or a copper-edged paddle to clear a jam of mineral needles before the water can crest the curb and flood a neighbor’s sleeping shelf.

Inside the dwellings, the air smells of wet peat, woodsmoke, and crushed charcoal. There is very little furniture—only what can be lashed together from scarce driftwood and marsh-reed, or wedged directly into the mortarless joints of the slate. They burn little wood, hoarding the salt-bleached driftwood for lintels and trough-frames; for heat and cooking, they rely on slow, smoldering bricks of dried marsh-peat packed into slate braziers that fill the low rooms with a dense, earthy haze. They sleep on raised platforms of woven reed, slung a foot or two above the floorboards so the cold runnels beneath can carry the spent springwater toward the marsh without soaking their bedding.

Their days are governed not by the sun—which only reaches the bottom of the cleft as a brief, milky diffusion at noon—but by the growth of the glaze. Each morning, teams of scrapers move up and down the tiers with reed hampers strapped to their backs, kneeling beside the settling-pans to harvest the night’s accumulation of brittle mineral needles. It is patient, rhythmic work: the *shhhk-shhhk* of copper-edged paddles lifting the frosted crust, the soft rustle of it falling into the hampers.

They do not grow their own food; there is not a square yard of soil inside the ring of stone. Instead, the cleft lives in quiet exchange with the lowlands beyond the rim. At dawn and dusk, small parties climb the single notched trail out to the marsh-edge, carrying hampers of the harvested mineral salt—prized by the lowland villages for glazing clay, curing hides, and sealing the hulls of flat-bottomed boats against the rot of the swamp. They return carrying bundles of dried sedge, smoked eel, baskets of marsh-tubers, and the occasional bar of raw copper or coil of sinew. Yet they rarely linger above the rim. Out on the open flats, under a wide sky where the wind blows unobstructed, they grow visibly uneasy, squinting against the glare and speaking even more softly, as if the lack of enclosing walls leaves their voices dangerously exposed.

Below the living tiers, in the dark, covered cisterns where the filtered water rests before its final release, live the only creatures the townspeople actively tend. When a cistern-keeper lifts a wooden hatch to inspect the water level, they drop in a pinch of crushed reed-seed or a scrap of dried fish-skin for the blind, translucent shrimp that turn in the cold dark. The people call them *clear-backs* and regard them as essential citizens of the basin; without the tiny, ceaseless grazing of the shrimp along the submerged slate, a slick bacterial film would foul the cisterns and taint the outflow. A household with healthy, numerous shrimp beneath its floorboards is considered fortunate, its water sweet and its walls sound.

As the inhabitants age, the relentless dampness settles into their joints. Their knees stiffen until the narrow, steep staircases become impossible, and their breathing takes on a wet, rattling weight. When an elder can no longer safely navigate the glazed roofs, they do not leave the cleft; instead, the community shifts them upward, trading a lower, larger room for one of the tiny, dry attic-boxes wedged directly beneath the upper rim, where the mist thins and the wire-grass catches the wind. There, wrapped in oiled hides and listening to the whole hydraulic chorus of the town rising from the hollow below, they spend their last years braiding reed hampers, knotting rawhide lashings, and carving bone scrapers for the younger hands still working in the wet dark beneath them.

When they die, they are not buried in the cleft—there is nowhere to dig—nor are they burned, for driftwood is too precious to waste on pyres. Instead, at low water, they are carried out through the lowest runnel-gate into the sedge-flats and weighted into the deep, cold peat of the marsh, leaving their dry-stacked rooms to be re-chinked with fresh moss and claimed by the next generation, while the springs continue to weep and the pale mineral skin thickens over the threshold, sealing the stone a fraction of a millimeter more each day.
