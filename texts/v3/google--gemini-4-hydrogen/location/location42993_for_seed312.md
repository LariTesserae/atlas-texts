From a distance, it is hard to tell where the mountains end and the city begins. Both are made of the same dark, newly broken slate, and both share the same sharp, unweathered geometry. 

The range is vast enough to make its own weather—a continental spine of jagged, freshly uplifted rock rising into a thin, brilliant sky. Nothing here has been rounded by time. The peaks are shattered teeth, the ridges are knife-blades of grey and iron-black stone, and the valleys below are choked with angular talus that clinks like pottery when it shifts. The light is crisp and white, pouring down through the high, dry air to cast shadows as sharp and blue as ink.

It is bitterly cold, the kind of permanent, dry freeze that turns breath to hoarfrost on a wool collar and makes exposed skin ache within minutes. There are no rivers here, no lakes, no sheets of snow. Water arrives only as a grudging, intermittent dampness. Each morning, slow-moving banks of cloud drift up from the distant lowlands and snag on the highest crags, coating the windward faces of the rock in a fur of white rime. Around midday, under the direct, high-altitude sun, the frost softens just enough to weep. Moisture beads on the cold stone, slicks the slate roofs, and trickles down hand-carved runnels into deep, stone-lined cisterns before freezing solid again by mid-afternoon.

Clinging to these sharp ridges, and spilling down into the leeward canyons for leagues in every direction, is an immense, densely packed urban landscape. It is built entirely without mortar, machines, or wheeled transport. Generations of builders using antler picks, stone mauls, wooden levers, and bone-tipped drills have split the mountain’s own slate into flat, heavy slabs and stacked them into a continuous, honeycombed crust of habitation. 

The city is layered like geological strata. Because flat ground is almost nonexistent, structures climb the cliff faces ten or twelve tiers high, the slate roof of one dwelling serving as the courtyard and thoroughfare for the one above it. Every cubic meter of sheltered space is claimed. Narrow, wind-baffled alleys twist between windowless stone walls; wooden ladders and steep, worn flights of stone steps connect the levels; heavy ropes braided from animal hair span the chasms, hung with leather buckets and bundled hides. The roofs are weighted down with boulders against the high-altitude gusts, and the doorways are low, narrow slots hung with thick, grease-cured pelts to keep the heat inside.

Despite the density of the settlement—tens of thousands of lives pressed shoulder-to-shoulder into the rock—the place is remarkably quiet. The thin air does not carry sound well, and the thick, dry-stone masonry swallows whatever noise is made indoors. There is no ring of iron on anvils, no rumble of carts. Instead, the soundscape is small and dry: the steady *clack-clack* of hooves on frozen slate, the muffled *thud* of a wooden mallet driving a wedge, the soft *rustle* of woven grass mats being shaken free of frost, and the low, constant breath of wind threading through ten thousand stacked stone chinks.

Everywhere, the city is shared with animals. The fauna here is abundant, pressed into the same tight quarters as the builders. The lowest, darkest tiers of the stone terraces are warm, crowded stables where thick-fleeced, broad-horned beasts of burden stand shoulder to shoulder, their body heat rising through the slotted floorboards to warm the living quarters above. In the eaves and the gaps between the dry-laid stones, thousands of small, round-eared rodents nest, darting out to glean dropped seeds and scraps of dried fat. Above the roofs, immense flocks of dark-feathered alpine birds wheel silently on the updrafts, nesting by the hundreds in the cliff-face niches and the warm crevices around chimney flues.

Vegetation is sparse, clinging only to the pockets where midday condensation pools and wind cannot reach. Crustose lichens—bright orange and pale chartreuse—stain the older masonry like slow-moving rust. In sheltered, south-facing stone boxes built along the terraces, people tend stunted, wind-pruned scrub and tough, silvery tussock grasses, irrigating them drop by drop with meltwater from the cisterns. Dried dung and twisted scrub-roots burn slowly in sunken stone hearths, sending thin, straight plumes of pale smoke up into the still, freezing air.

Nothing here happens quickly. A retaining wall takes three lifetimes to extend across a ravine; a cistern is chipped out of solid basalt a handful of dust at a time over decades. The mountain itself is still settling, shifting a fraction of an inch a year, cracking a lintel here or opening a new seep of moisture there. In the cold, bright silence, stone, beast, and builder endure together, locked into a slow, patient geometry against the vast sky.
