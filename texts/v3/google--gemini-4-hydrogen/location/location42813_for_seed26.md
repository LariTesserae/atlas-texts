The ceiling is not rock; it is a ribbed vault of cast concrete and blackened steel trusses, hung so high in the damp gloom that the upper girders disappear into a permanent, drifting layer of cold mist. Below it lies a sunken basin roughly four miles wide and twice as long, sealed off from whatever world exists above. There is no raw earth here, no exposed stone or natural bed. Every surface—the terraced floors, the retaining walls, the sweeping drainage aprons—has been poured, bolted, or tiled into place.

It is cold enough that breath hangs in the air and the metal railings sting bare skin, hovering just above freezing. Because the basin is sealed and the machinery within it runs constantly, the air is saturated. Moisture beads on the concrete ribs overhead, gathers along the rivets of the I-beams, and falls in a steady, scattered patter onto the broad, empty plazas below. Nothing here is ever quite dry. The poured-basalt tiles of the floor are slick with a permanent film of black water, and in the lower tiers, shallow sheets of runoff stand ankle-deep, rippling whenever a distant pump kicks on.

The scale of the place is vast, yet it feels oddly empty. The structures are set far apart, separated by wide expanses of wet grating, flooded sumps, and broad, unrailed causeways. A solitary transformer tower—three stories of humming copper coils and porcelain insulators—stands in the middle of a half-acre puddle. A quarter-mile away, a row of six massive, cylindrical heat-exchangers rises from a sunken trench like gray silos, connected to the far wall only by an overhead bundle of insulated pipes. Between them, there is nothing but space, dimness, and the long, unbroken lines of drainage channels.

Light is scarce and strictly functional. Tall, slender stanchions, spaced too far apart to overlap, hold caged mercury-vapor lamps that cast small, bruised-violet pools onto the wet floor. Elsewhere, the only illumination comes from the dull, steady amber of pilot lights on control cabinets, or the occasional brief, sizzling blue flash of a high-voltage relay snapping shut in the damp. In the spaces between the lamps, the basin falls into deep, wet shadow, where the outlines of gantries and catwalks are known mostly by the sound of water dripping from them.

And the place is loud. The sound has no single source; it is an overlapping, industrial weather. A low, foundational *thrum* vibrates up through the soles of the boots—the work of deep-set turbine shafts and subterranean intake fans. Over that sits the rhythmic, metallic *clack-hiss, clack-hiss* of pneumatic valves cycling along the main manifolds, and the heavy, hollow booming of water falling down vertical concrete chutes into unseen cisterns below the floor. Because the surfaces are hard, smooth, and newly cast, the echoes do not soften. A dropped wrench or the slam of a distant sluice-gate rings out sharp and clear, bouncing off the tiled walls for seconds before dissolving back into the roar of the blowers.

Everything looks remarkably young, as if the basin were poured and bolted together only a few seasons ago, or else scrubbed clean by some relentless cycle. The concrete seams are crisp, showing the sharp, unweathered ridges of the wooden forms they were cast against. The welds along the iron catwalks are bright and jagged; the paint on the yellow safety bollards hasn't had time to peel, only to darken with damp. Even the mineral formations—thick, pale crusts of salt and lime that bleed from the pipe joints—have sharp, brittle, crystalline edges, freshly extruded and not yet worn down by the flow.

The basin runs on an active, multi-layered cycle, like a machine imitating a tide. Every few hours, the pressure in the upper headers shifts. Warning klaxons—low, flat, two-tone horns—groan across the empty flats, and moments later the massive sluice-gates along the eastern wall grind upward. Cold, gray brine surges out across the upper terraces in a wide, foaming sheet, sweeping over the tiles, rattling the loose floor grates, and flushing the mineral-slicks down into the central sumps. For twenty minutes, the basin is a rushing, roaring shallow river; then the gates slam shut, the water drains away through the slotted floors, and the vast, wet silence—filled only by the engines and the dripping—returns. During the high-flow cycles, cold mist rises off the churning water in thick banks, blinding the lamps until the exhaust fans pull the air clear again.

Life here is sparse, pale, and entirely opportunistic, clinging to the margins of the machinery. There is no soil, so nothing with roots can take hold. Instead, a sparse, strange vegetation occupies the wettest, dimmest niches. Thick, rubbery ribbons of translucent, colorless kelp-like growth hang from the undersides of the condensation trays, swaying in the draft of the cooling fans and feeding on the trace nutrients washed down from the upper levels. Along the concrete pilings, just above the waterline of the periodic floods, grows a low, velvety mat of bluish-white lichen that glows faintly when the violet lamps hit it. Inside the warm, grease-slicked housings of the electric motors, brittle, orange-brown clusters of iron-eating bacterial growths—shaped like tiny, sharp-edged corals—sprout overnight and snap off under their own weight.

Fauna is even rarer, glimpsed mostly as movement at the edge of a lamp's reach. Slow, flat-shelled sump-crabs, the color of wet concrete and no larger than a palm, clatter across the iron grates after a flood cycle, scavenging the dead bacterial mats and the pale, blind amphipods washed out of the pipes. High up in the dark trusses, tucked into the dry, warm pockets behind the transformer baffles, small, leathery-winged creatures roost in silence, dropping down only during the quietest intervals between the sluice-cycles to skim the surface of the settling ponds.

Nothing in this valley is meant to be looked at, and nothing is built for comfort. It is a cold, echoing, fully-constructed gut—a place of sharp edges, dripping steel, and relentless, rhythmic work, sitting quietly beneath the surface of the world and breathing out only mist and hum.
