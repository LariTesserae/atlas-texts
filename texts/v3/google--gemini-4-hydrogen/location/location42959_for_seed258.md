High above the lower valleys, under a clear, unremarkable daylight, lies a broad, shallow-rimmed plateau where there is no stone, no soil, and no dead wood. The ground itself is a continuous, living floor—a taut, seamless expanse of pale-jade and bone-ivory plant skin, stretched flat across a basin wide enough to hold a town.

It is warm here, and everything is wet. A thin sheet of tepid, clear water sits over much of the plateau, held in by low, gently curving ribs of living tissue that divide the floor into vast, shallow pans. Where the surface rises slightly, the plant-paving is bare and glossy, slick with a thin film of moisture that never quite evaporates and never quite drains away. The air hangs heavy with condensing mist, rising in faint, lazy wisps off the warm water, though the sky above remains bright and open.

Despite the richness of the living mass beneath your feet, the landscape feels remarkably empty. Across the mile-wide stretches of glistening floor, structures rise only at wide, deliberate intervals, leaving huge expanses of flat water and pale membrane between them. And yet, nothing here is wild. Every feature on the plateau is unmistakably built—not carved, not stacked, not lashed together with dead fiber, but *grown* into exact, load-bearing geometry.

Long, straight causeways of braided, flattened stem-flesh cut across the shallow pans, linking solitary, roofless rotundas of arched xylem ribs. Farther out, tapering siphon-spires stand like distant mile-markers, their walls built of thick, turgid, slightly translucent leaf-tissue fused at the seams. Where one structural rib meets another, there are no joints or bindings—only smooth, pale biological welds, as if two wet shoots had been pressed together until their skins forgot they were separate. There is not a single mark of a blade, a chisel, or a peg anywhere.

The strangest quality of the place is its unfinishedness. Every spire, arch, and stepped basin looks as though it emerged from a bud-scale only an hour ago. The surfaces are unscarred, soft-edged, and faintly glossy with embryonic dew. There is no rot, no fallen leaf-litter, no lichen creeping up the shaded sides of the walls, no silt settling in the corners of the water-pans. Yet nothing is growing, and nothing is decaying. The plateau is locked in a warm, wet equilibrium—permanently poised at the moment of just having come into being. The water beads on the waxy, unweathered floors without ever carving a channel; the pale sutures between the floor-plates neither split nor thicken.

And it is deafening. 

With no wind to speak of and almost no animal life, the noise comes entirely from the architecture itself. The plateau is a single, high-altitude hydraulic engine, and the sound of its circulation fills the open air. Deep beneath the taut membranes of the floor, unseen vascular bladders pulse with a slow, heavy, rhythmic *thrum* that travels up through the soles of your feet. The hollow xylem arches and tall siphon-spires act as vast, damp resonators, amplifying the rush of water being drawn upward through millions of invisible pores. From the flared mouths of the distant spires, thick, steady curtains of warm water pour down into stepped, translucent basins, raising a continuous, booming roar that carries uninterrupted across the sparse, flat plain.

In the quietest corners—if any corner can be called quiet beneath that constant, organ-pipe hum—a rare creature occasionally appears. A long-legged, nearly transparent strider might skate across a warm pan between the causeways, or a pale, blind grazer might cling to the weeping underside of an arch, drinking the condensation. They leave no tracks on the self-smoothing skin of the world, and they make no sound of their own. They are merely small, passing specks against a vast, unchanging machine of green flesh and running water, humming endlessly to itself under the open sky.
