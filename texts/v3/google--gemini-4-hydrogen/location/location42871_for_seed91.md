The first thing you register is not the cold, or the dimness, or even the drop—it is the sound. It hits as a physical weight against the sternum, a continuous, deafening roar that fills the entire sunken basin from floor to rim. It has no rhythm and no pause: the low, turbine-driven thunder of massive industrial pumps and air-compressors, bounced and multiplied off sheer, unweathered rock walls until the air itself seems to vibrate. You cannot hear your own boots sink into the ground. You cannot hear the wind, if there is any, or the water dripping from the overhangs.

You are a hundred meters or more below the level of the surrounding plain, standing in a vast, open-topped rift—a collapsed limestone and basalt valley roughly a mile across, wide enough that the far wall is a dark, indistinct smudge in the gloom. Above, the sky is a narrow, overcast band of flat gray, letting down just enough dim, bluish light to pick out the shapes of the floor without warming anything. The air is right at freezing. Every exhale hangs white for a second before the draft from the machinery shreds it.

Beneath your feet, there is no bare stone and no soil. The entire floor of the valley is a continuous, springy mattress of cold-adapted plant life—meters of layered sphagnum, feather-moss, dark liverworts, and compacted, waterlogged peat. Because the basin is geologically young, the rock underneath is all razor-sharp fault-scarps, angular talus blocks, and fresh collapse-rubble, but the moss has colonized every edge. It drapes over jagged boulders like wet, heavy felt, bridges narrow crevices in treacherous, yielding mats, and sleeves every fallen beam or boulder in thick, olive-and-russet velvet. Where the walls rise steep and fractured, curtains of damp beard-lichen and pale, shade-tolerant ferns hang from every ledge, fed by the constant chill.

Yet for all that living green-and-brown carpet, the space feels starkly empty. There are no trees, no tall canopies, no crowded thickets. Across the wide, undulating expanse of the moss-floor, presences are few and far between: a solitary, stunted willow twisted flat against a rock shoulder; a cluster of frost-rimmed boulders; wide stretches of unbroken, undulating peat where nothing breaks the horizon for hundreds of yards.

Out of that emptiness rise the machines. They are scattered sparsely across the valley floor—small, utilitarian pump-houses of corrugated steel and poured concrete, isolated transformer sheds, and heavy-gauge, insulated pipelines snaking across the peat on rusted steel stanchions. High-voltage lines drop down from the rim on skeletal pylons, humming invisibly above the roar. Every half-mile or so, a massive, electrified dewatering station or rotary ventilation housing squats in a shallow depression, its intake grilles shuddering as it draws and drives air or groundwater out of the deep strata. Only a few harsh, cold-white work-lamps burn on their exteriors, casting small, sharp pools of light onto the wet moss and leaving the miles between them in deep, gray twilight.

Everything here is damp, never quite flooded and never dry. Condensation beads on the cold steel housings and drips steadily from the tips of hanging lichens. Shallow, dark puddles of groundwater sit in the low peat hollows, edged with fragile needles of rime-ice where the air settles coldest. A thin, ground-hugging mist drifts slowly through the depressions, smelling of wet peat, ozone, and warm machine oil.

Life, aside from the mosses and lichens, is rare and keeps close to the margins. Pale springtails and cold-sluggish beetles work through the upper layers of damp peat. Now and then, a lone raven or dipper drops down from the rim to perch on a pipe-clamp, tilting its head against the noise before lifting back out into the brighter world above. Near the transformer boxes, where the steel casings give off a faint, dry warmth and a steady sixty-cycle hum, small voles sometimes tunnel into the moss-mat, taking advantage of the only heat in the valley.

Nothing here hurries. The engines run at a fixed, monotonous pitch day and night, month after month, keeping the water table down and the shafts clear. Around their concrete footings, the moss creeps upward by millimeters a year, slowly swallowing bolts, cable-trays, and warning signs. Frost forms along a pipe flange, melts slightly at midday when the dim sky brightens, and refreezes by dusk. Under the deafening, unchanging wall of industrial sound, the valley settles into a patient, cold equilibrium—rock, wet plant, and iron locked together in the dim light below the world.
