Three thousand meters above the distant lowlands, the plateau stretches so wide it makes its own weather. From the edge of any terrace, the horizon does not end in peaks—those were ground down millions of years ago into broad, flat-topped mesas and smooth-shouldered ridges—but in a pale, curving haze where the high stone meets its own towering anvil clouds.

The air is warm and heavy with water. Moisture rises from the lower continents, strikes the high, sun-baked limestone, and condenses in a perpetual, patient cycle. There are no violent storms here, only a slow, warm weeping. Every cliff face glistens with a thin sheet of runoff. Water beads on iron railings, pools in the worn hollows of stone steps, and collects in wide, milky-mineral basins that steam faintly in the bright, normal daylight. When the afternoon clouds thicken, a warm, fine rain settles over the plateau for hours, scarcely disturbing the surface of the canals, simply adding another sheen to a world that never quite dries.

There are no trees. There is no grass, no moss, no fern clinging to the mortar. Where plants might have taken root, there is only bare, ancient rock—dark basalt and pale karst—slicked with mineral efflorescence and thin, velvety films of grey and rust-colored slime-fungi. These slicks are the closest thing the plateau has to greenery: slow-growing, wet, and faintly iridescent in the sun, feeding on the dissolved minerals in the weeping stone.

Across these vast, stepped terraces spreads the city.

It is an industrial metropolis built on a continental scale, layered into the ancient erosion of the plateau like strata of its own. Retained by massive, soot-darkened masonry walls, the city steps down from the mesa tops into the wide, shallow gorges. Brick foundries with towering chimneys stand shoulder-to-shoulder with terraced tenements, their slate roofs slick and gleaming. Heavy iron viaducts, riveted and rust-streaked, carry narrow-gauge rail lines and bundled copper power cables across the milky canals. Steam turbines hum inside low, arched powerhouse halls, drawing water from the high basins and venting great, slow plumes of white vapor into the humid air.

Everything here is dense, built close and stacked high, yet nothing moves in a hurry. The machines run with a heavy, settled rhythm—the slow *thump-hiss* of reciprocating pumps, the steady turning of waterwheels, the low, continuous thrum of electrical transformers. The brick is weathered smooth; the iron is pitted and patched with decades of careful, unhurried maintenance. The city has been running like this for so long that its industrial heat and runoff have become part of the plateau’s climate.

And everywhere—covering the stone, crowding the air, crusting the brick and iron—is the fauna.

Without plants to anchor the food web, life here has built its own towering architecture out of bone, shell, chitin, and mud. The sheer volume of it is overwhelming. Along the wet stone plazas and the broad, sloped retaining walls, herds of massive, slow-moving grazers creep at a glacial pace. Shaped like meter-wide, flattened chitons with overlapping plates of calcified grey shell, they spend weeks crossing a single courtyard, their rasping mouthparts scraping the mineral-fungi off the rock with a dry, rhythmic *scritch-scritch-scritch* that carries through the soles of one’s boots. Smaller, barnacle-like creatures encrust their shells, and pale, blind crustaceans scuttle in the wet shadows beneath their skirts.

Look up, and the brick facades of the factories are barely visible beneath dense, honeycombed cities of mud-daubed nests and calcified tubes. Every iron girder under the viaducts hangs heavy with papery, communal hives and long, glistening curtains of silk-spun cocoons. The warm runoff canals are thick with pale, flat-bodied amphibians and slow, whiskered bottom-feeders that crowd around the warm discharge pipes of the powerhouses, churning the milky water into a sluggish froth.

Even the air is crowded. Vast, shimmering gyres of winged insects—midges, heavy-bodied beetles, and translucent-winged flies that breed by the billion in the settling ponds—drift between the smokestacks in clouds so dense they cast moving, dappled shadows across the wet cobblestones. Through these clouds wheel endless flocks of long-winged, pale-feathered aerialists and gliding, membrane-ribbed reptiles, their bodies sleek with oil to shed the constant mist. They swoop through the ironwork of the bridges, snatching insects from the rising thermals of factory steam, never quite colliding with the power lines or the slow-moving cable cars.

The sound of the plateau is a solid, physical presence. It is loud, layered, and ceaseless. Beneath is the industrial bass: the groan of iron cranes, the rush of water over terraced spillways, the rhythmic pounding of distant drop-forges. Above and through it all rings the deafening, vibrating chorus of a billion living things—the high, metallic whine of insect swarms, the guttural, resonant croaking rising from every drainage culvert, the dry clatter of shells jostling on the wet stone, and the sharp, whistling cries of the flocks wheeling in the bright, steam-thick air.

Yet for all the noise and the crowding, the tempo of the place is deeply, stubbornly slow. The clouds drift across the high mesas without urgency. The warm water seeps down from terrace to terrace, carrying dissolved lime and iron filings. The great shelled grazers inch across the plazas, untouched by the workers stepping around them on the way to the foundries. The engines turn, the nests thicken by a millimeter a year, and the vast, wet, teeming city hums on in its warm, high light, settled into an ancient balance that neither stone, machine, nor creature seems inclined to break.
