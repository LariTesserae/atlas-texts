The light here is ordinary, high-altitude daylight—clear, sharp, and merciless—falling across a plateau so high and dry that the sky above it is a thin, bleached indigo, empty of a single cloud. There is no water anywhere. Not in the air, which parches the throat on the first breath; not in the ground, which is a vast, sun-glazed pan of vitrified salt, cracked red clay, and wind-scoured mica-schist. The heat is radiant and relentless, rising off the flat mineral crust in sheets that turn the distant mesa rims into trembling, liquid ink.

It is an ancient, settled landscape. The wind has spent millions of years planing the ridges into smooth, aerodynamic blades and sweeping the basins into wide, shallow bowls, miles across. There are no plants—not a thorn, not a crust of lichen, not even the memory of a root. Nothing green has ever anchored here. And nothing has ever been built here: there are no walls, no roads, no towers, no wires, no bricks laid upon bricks. 

Yet the place smells unmistakably of an overheated foundry.

Ozone hangs thick in the dry thermals, sharp as a welding arc, layered over the scent of scorched copper, hot stone, and dry, mineral grease. Beneath your boots, the ground does not feel solid. It rings with a faint, taut resonance, like a drumhead stretched over an abyss. The plateau is largely hollow—not carved by rivers, for no river ever ran here, but *evacuated*, as if the matter beneath the crust simply thinned out eons ago, leaving a thin, vitrified shell arched over miles of empty, superheated dark.

And though the air is thin and the space between things is vast—emptiness stretching for miles in every direction—the place is deafening.

A low, chest-rattling hum saturates the basin, steady and unhurried, like a turbine running at the bottom of a well. Every twenty or thirty seconds, a sharp, dry *crack* splits the air from somewhere across the salt-flat, followed by a long, metallic groan that seems to rise from directly beneath your heels even when you can see its source three miles away. 

The fauna is everywhere, and none of it makes biological sense.

They are sparse, spaced out across the immense distances like ships on an empty sea, yet they are so vast and so loud that they fill the world entirely. A mile out on the white salt-pan stands a creature that looks like a cathedral-sized ribcage of bleached, porcelain-hard bone, balanced on four spindly, jointed stilts of black iron-wood and calcified tendon. It has no head, no skin, no organs—just the hollow, sun-baked lattice of its frame. Suspended inside that empty ribcage, touching nothing, hung in mid-air without axle or bearing, is a jagged, thirty-foot wheel of raw quartz and magnetite. 

It spins with agonizing slowness—perhaps three revolutions a minute—held in place by a localized, humming warp in the air itself. As it turns, blue-white arcs of static leap from its stone teeth to the inner ribs of the creature, making a sound like tearing sheet metal. It takes one step every minute. When its foot touches the salt, the hollow ground below booms like a struck boiler.

Further out, drifting lazily along the rim of the mesa, are others: hollow, translucent bladders of heat-cured membrane the size of barges, tethered to nothing, floating fifty yards apart in the thin, hot air. They are bristling with natural, chitinous exhaust pipes—brass-colored, ribbed, and encrusted with salt. They do not flap or swim. They simply drift on the slow thermal currents, and every few minutes, in a staggered, patient chorus across the whole valley, they vent superheated gas through their pipes with a deafening, rhythmic *HISS-THUMP* that shakes the dust from the rocks.

There are smaller things, too, though still spaced far apart, keeping the air between them empty. Things like six-legged coils of braided copper-gristle that inch across the mica flats, dragging their bellies and leaving faint, smoking tracks of fused glass where their natural electrical charge grounds into the mineral crust. When two of them pass within a hundred yards of each other, a thick, slow ribbon of violet lightning stretches between them, hanging in the air for seconds at a time, buzzing like a transformer, before snapping out.

Nothing here was engineered, yet everything operates on industrial principles: induction, pressure, torque, heat-exchange. Evolution here didn't find flesh and blood; it found ceramics, heavy metals, salt-electrolytes, and raw mechanical force. Life here is a feral factory, running without walls or workers.

And reality itself has frayed around the edges of the machinery.

Look closely at the spinning quartz wheel inside the distant stilt-beast, and you realize its shadow on the salt-flat is spinning in the opposite direction—and it is three times too large, detached from the creature's actual silhouette, sweeping across the ground like the shadow of an invisible flywheel turning in the empty sky. Sound refuses to obey distance: the *clank* of a joint three miles north arrives in your ears before the leg actually moves, or echoes up from the hollow earth beneath you as if the whole plateau were a single, badly wired speaker. 

Walk toward a mesa that looks five miles away, and after an hour of trudging across the ringing, blistering salt, it is still five miles away, though the landmarks you passed have vanished behind you—not obscured by haze, just folded out of the line of sight. The air between presences feels curiously *thin*, as if space itself has been hollowed out to match the ground, leaving vast, empty intervals where geometry doesn't quite stitch together.

Yet for all the broken physics and the overwhelming, mechanized din, nothing happens quickly.

The sun crawls across the bleached sky. The massive, hollow walkers take their patient, minute-long strides. The quartz rotors turn with heavy, unshakeable inertia. Deep beneath the thin crust, something unimaginably large—a subterranean, blind tangle of living slag and magnetic ore—grinds slowly against the underside of the basin, sending a single, continuous, teeth-aching vibration up through the salt for hours at a time without ever changing pitch.

It is an ancient, blisteringly hot, bone-dry engine of a world, running in slow motion, loud enough to drown out thought, with miles of empty, shimmering nothingness between every moving part.
