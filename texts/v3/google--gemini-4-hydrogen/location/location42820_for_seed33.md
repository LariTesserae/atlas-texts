At this altitude, the air has almost given up the habit of carrying sound. You can drop a fist-sized piece of dry pumice from a parapet and watch it fall for six seconds into the dimness below, and the only thing you hear is the faint, papery *shh* of your own sleeve shifting against your ribs.

The world here is cool, bone-dry, and suspended in a perpetual, bruised twilight. There is no sun—only a flat, slate-grey luminescence that seems to seep from the high, thin haze itself, leaving no sharp shadows, only soft gradations of ash and charcoal. The temperature never quite reaches freezing, hovering instead in that crisp, cellar-cold range where stone feels faintly damp even when there isn’t a drop of moisture within a hundred miles.

Below and around you stretches a city that no one ever built.

It spans a series of jagged, extreme-elevation ridges and the bottomless chasms between them, laid out on the scale of a great metropolis. There are tiered plazas, broad avenues, stepped amphitheaters, viaducts, and clustered towers rising like bleached organ pipes. Yet if you kneel and press your hand to a curbstone or a lintel, there are no chisel marks, no mortar lines, no brick seams. There is no metal, no glass, no wood, no trace of a tool ever having touched the place. Here, at the top of the world, matter simply *habitually* accretes into architecture. Dust and mineral vapor, carried up from the unseen depths on dry thermal currents, settle into right angles, arches, and colonnades the way salt crystals form cubes. The city is a geological habit—an urban crystal.

And it is almost entirely empty.

Despite the organized density of its streets and the layered complexity of its districts, the place is hollow. Vast, miles-wide gulfs of thin air separate one cluster of towers from the next, bridged only by single, razor-thin walkways that sag slightly in the middle like dried sinew. Inside the buildings, there are no rooms, no furniture, no floors—only towering, windowless shafts of still air, or empty courtyards where a lone, weathered obelisk stands half a mile from the nearest wall. You can walk along a grand, colonnaded promenade for an hour without encountering a doorway that leads anywhere except back out onto the same sheer drop.

Time has been working on this place for so long that even the impossible parts of it look tired. The edges of the terraces are rounded and sandblasted by centuries of dry, silent wind. Fine, chalky dust pools in the corners of staircases like drifted snow, though it never melts and never packs.

Because reality here does not hold its shape—and hasn’t for an age.

The city is not static; it is in a state of slow, silent, continuous breakdown. Across the chasm, an entire district—a layered knot of stepped plazas and blind arches—is tilted at a forty-degree angle, its avenues now functioning as sheer cliff faces. A mile further on, a stone bridge ends abruptly in mid-air, its missing central span hanging seventy feet above it, floating upside down in a pocket of inverted gravity. These aren't ruins of a catastrophe; they are the weather. Slowly, over seasons and years, sections of the city unhook themselves from the local rules of weight and direction, drifting laterally across the abyss or rotating in place with a motion too gradual to see, though you can feel it as a faint, sub-audible tremor through the soles of your boots.

Space itself frays in the open squares. Looking down a straight, mile-long avenue, the far archway may suddenly appear ten paces away, looming huge and clear, while the pavement beneath your feet still stretches out for the full, exhausting mile it takes to actually walk there. Sometimes, instead of rain, a district experiences a dry squall of detached shadows—flat, matte-black shapes with no casters, fluttering down through the dim light like burnt paper, pooling in the sunken amphitheaters before thinning out and vanishing into the stone.

Nothing green grows here. The closest thing to vegetation is a dry, brittle crust—half-fungus, half-mineral—that clings to the leeward undersides of arches in pale, rusted-orange patches, feeding on trace salts and the faint static charge generated by shifting rock.

Yet the city is not entirely dead. It has an ecology, sparse and quiet as dust.

In the sheltered crevices of the weathered colonnades, tiny, blind, chalk-shelled grazers—no larger than fingernails—scrape slowly across the mineral-crusts, leaving faint, silvery trails of dry powder behind them. Above them, riding the silent updrafts between the hollow towers, are the gliders: paper-thin, translucent things made of stretched silica-membrane and hollow, calcified ribs. They have no wings to beat; they simply unfurl like kites, catching the dry, rising currents, drifting for miles across the empty gulfs without making a sound.

And occasionally, if you watch the broken viaducts long enough, you will see one of the city’s rare predators. It looks at first like a tangle of dark, jointed scaffolding—too thin, too angular, moving with a slow, deliberate grace. It does not leap across the miles-wide gaps where reality has torn away; instead, it steps into the frayed edge of a shadow-pool or a fold in the air, and a moment later, without having crossed the space between, its long, dry limbs unfold silently from the stonework on the far side of the chasm.

Then it is still again, blending into the sharp, weathered angles of a street that leads nowhere, under a dim sky that holds nothing, in a silence so complete you can hear the dust settling on your own shoulders.
