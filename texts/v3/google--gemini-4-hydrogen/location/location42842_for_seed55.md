The light here is not an absence, only a memory of light—a cold, bruised slate-blue that filters down from a surface too far above to see. It is just enough to give edges to things, to turn the water from ink to pewter, and to feed the slow, dark-blooded plants that have claimed every surface.

Everything is submerged. The water is near-freezing, heavy, and still, carrying the faint mineral tang of dissolved limestone and old silt. It presses evenly from all sides, muffling distance so that a wall twenty paces away dissolves into the grey-blue murk, and a wall a hundred paces away might as well not exist. Yet the place goes on—vast enough to hold its own slow, continental currents, a drowned lowland stretching for hundreds of leagues without a single rise of raw mountain or untouched shore.

There is no natural ground left to see. Every inch of the basin is made.

Beneath the silt and the thick, velvet carpets of algae lies a world of dressed stone, stacked slate, and rammed earth long since petrified. Before the waters came—or perhaps as they rose, inch by patient inch over generations—hands with simple tools shaped the entire plain. You can read the primitive labor in the grain of it: the shallow, rhythmic scars of bronze and copper chisels on cyclopean blocks; the uneven, hand-fitted joints of dry-stone retaining walls; the worn hollows in flagstones where bare feet or wooden sledges once passed. There is no iron, no glass, no poured concrete. Only stone set upon stone, corbelled vaults, low-pitched roofs of overlapping slate, miles of sunken causeways, terraced basins, and endless, interlocking courtyards.

And all of it is ancient. Time and the slow, relentless chemistry of cold water have rounded every sharp corner into something soft and bulbous. Sharp lintels have slumped into gentle curves; carved inscriptions on boundary steles have been erased into smooth, blank swells. Where mortar once held, it has leached away, leaving stones that hold together solely by their own immense weight and the thick, calcified crusts of coralline algae that have cemented them back into a single, seamless mass.

Because there are no animals here—no fish to dart between the pillars, no urchins to scrape the stone, no crabs to scuttle through the doorways—the vegetation has grown without check for centuries.

It is a lushness built for the cold and the dark. Great, heavy forests of deep-water kelp rise from the sunken plazas, their holdfasts gripping the paving stones like gnarled knuckles. Their fronds are not bright green, but the color of wet iron, dried blood, and dark olive—pigments tuned to catch the last stray photons of blue light drifting down from the upper world. Without grazers to trim them, individual blades grow to the length of ships, hanging in dense, motionless curtains that brush against the eaves of drowned granaries and drape over the rims of old cisterns.

Closer to the stone, every horizontal surface is buried under a foot-deep pelt of sea-mosses and filamentous red algae. They grow so thickly that the architecture beneath looks upholstered, its staircases turned into soft, undulating ramps, its narrow alleys choked into dim, velvet-lined tunnels. In the shelter of doorways and under the arches of aqueducts, pale, branching sponges and calcified fan-weeds crowd together, near-touching, filling the volume of the rooms with a quiet, tangled density.

Nothing here hurries. In this cold, dimly lit water, growth is a matter of decades rather than seasons. A kelp blade lengthens by a finger’s width in a year; a mat of coralline crust thickens by the breadth of a hair. Even the water itself moves with an almost imperceptible patience. A vast, cold current—driven by distant temperature shifts leagues away—creeps across the sunken terraces at the speed of a walking man, or slower. As it passes, the entire forest of giant fronds leans together in unison, a slow, silent bow that takes minutes to complete before the stems settle back toward the vertical.

It is almost entirely quiet. There are no breaking waves at this depth, no wind, no chatter of shells or snapping claws. The loudest sound is the faint, wet *shush* of heavy kelp blades sliding past one another in the drift, or the occasional, muffled *tock* of a small pebble—freed at last from its ancient mortar—dropping onto a bed of thick silt. Now and then, a pocket of trapped gas from decaying plant matter slips loose from under a stone arch and rises toward the unseen surface, its soft *blup-blup* swallowed almost instantly by the heavy water.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute: cold preserves, darkness slows, and water levels all things. What was built to keep the world out—walls, dikes, granaries, roads—now serves only as a trellis for the dark-red gardens of the deep. The architecture has outlasted its builders, its purpose, and the very air it stood in, settling at last into a final, quiet equilibrium with the plants that have quietly swallowed it whole.
