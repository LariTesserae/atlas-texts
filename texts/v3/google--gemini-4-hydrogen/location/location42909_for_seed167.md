The water here is heavy, mineral-rich, and as warm as blood. It sits in a broad, low-lying basin miles below any wave or weather, trapped beneath an immense weight of dark ocean. There is no sunlight—none at all—only a vast, enveloping blackness that presses gently against the skin. What little there is to see comes from the faintest, accidental glimmers: a stray spark of bacterial luminescence clinging to a rough wall, or the brief, pale-blue pulse of a drifting jellyfish-kin startled by a current, illuminating a few feet of chalky stone before winking out.

Yet the darkness is not empty. Reach out in any direction and your hand meets architecture.

Spread across the sunken valley floor is a city, dense and tightly packed, built entirely without hands, tools, or fire. Over millennia, colonies of blind, shell-secreting organisms—massive, slow-moving creatures resembling a cross between turret-snails and colonial tube-worms—have extruded a metropolis of aragonite, cemented silt, and discarded carapace. Because the valley is low and geothermally active, warm water seeps upward through cracks in the basaltic bed, carrying dissolved lime and sulfur. The builders have organized their entire world around these invisible, rising plumes.

Broad avenues run straight and deliberate between the thermal fissures, acting as channels that guide the warm updrafts through the heart of the settlement. Flanking these thoroughfares are towering, multi-tiered warrens stacked ten or twelve stories high, their walls ribbed like the undersides of giant mushrooms and honeycombed with thousands of oval alcoves. Where the towers lean toward one another across the narrow alleys, sweeping arches and buttresses of calcified mucus bridge the gap, locking the structures together against the slow, steady tug of the deep currents. High above the street level, thick, fluted chimneys rise into the black water, venting spent, nutrient-stripped warmth back into the abyss.

Everything here is old and settled. The lower tiers of the city, buried knee-deep in pale, flocculent silt, have been worn smooth by centuries of passing bodies and mineral-heavy water. Sharp edges have softened into rounded, velvety limestone ledges; abandoned chambers have slumped and fused into solid foundations. Higher up, where the builders are still at work, the rims of the towers are chalky and sharp, smelling faintly—if one could smell underwater—of sulfur and wet lime.

Despite the city’s density, it is mostly quiet. There is no roar of surf, no clatter of machinery. The soundscape is an intimate, muffled murmur: the rhythmic, velvety *thrum* of water being pumped through countless biological siphons; the dry, faint scraping of thousands of rasping tongues harvesting mineral paste from the outer walls; the occasional sharp *tick* of a crab’s claw snapping in an alleyway. Every few minutes, a low, sub-audible pressure wave rolls through the avenues—the wake of something large gliding silently above the rooftops.

Life here is packed into every crevice, layered like sediment. The streets are never empty, even in the pitch dark. Clouds of translucent, eyeless shrimp hang suspended in the warm updrafts of the chimneys, filtering microscopic detritus. Flat, pale crustaceans patrol the compacted silt of the plazas, sweeping up fallen scraps, while ribbon-bodied eels weave through the porous mortar-joints of the walls, their heads poking from tiny holes to test the current. Vegetation is scarce—there is no sun to feed it—consisting only of sparse, ghostly tufts of sulfur-feeding filaments that cling like pale fur to the warmest chimney rims, and the occasional stunted, rubbery sea-fan anchored to an exposed ridge at the valley’s edge.

The city runs not on day and night, but on a slow thermal tide. Every few hours, the deep geological pressure shifts, and the warm seeps beneath the avenues surge. A fresh, rich plume of minerals and heat blooms upward through the streets. Instantly, the city quickens. The builders extend their fleshy mantles from their alcoves to drink in the calcium and lay down fresh, wet layers of shell-cement. The shrimp clouds thicken; the crabs scuttle faster. Drawn by the sudden vibration of a thousand feeding bodies, sleek, blind predators—pale cephalopods and lateral-line hunters—drop down from the dark ceiling of the water column. They slip effortlessly through the arched bridges and narrow alleys, feeling their way by the tiny eddies displaced by their prey, striking in total silence before vanishing back into the black.

Then the thermal surge ebbs. The water cools by a fraction of a degree, the silt settles back onto the smoothed ledges, and the crowded, blind metropolis returns to its quiet, tireless work in the dark.
