The land sits so high that the sky has ceased to be an overhead thing and has become the medium you walk through.

It is a broad, shallow basin—the worn-down saddle of a mountain range so ancient that every jagged tooth has been ground away over epochs into whale-backed ridges of dark, soap-smooth basalt and pale granite. There are no cliffs here to mark the peaks, only long, undulating swells of stone that slope gently inward toward a town-sized bowl, and, at the outer rim of the plateau, drop away into an unseen abyss of cloud. The air is thin and cool, hovering just above freezing, carrying the crisp, metallic bite of high altitude and a persistent dampness that never quite resolves into rain.

Across the basin, the light is a permanent, diffused pewter. The cloud deck rests directly on the ridges, pressing the ceiling of the world down to a few hundred feet above the ground and turning the sun into a pale, wandering bruise behind the stratus. Only in the sheltered hollows and along the terraced streets do small, warm pools of amber appear—tungsten work-lamps hung from low iron brackets, glowing through the mist like embers.

Nothing in the basin is bare. Even though the air is cold, the ceaseless saturation of the fog has bred a dense, slow-motion jungle of miniature things. Every boulder, every slate roof, and every brick retaining wall is upholstered in thick, overlapping pelts of cushion-moss, liverwort, and waxy-leafed heather. Gnarled, flat-topped cloud-trees—no taller than streetlamps, their trunks twisted like wrung cloth—crowd into the gullies and lean out over the alleys, their branches draped in heavy curtains of silver-green beard-lichen. Ferns unfurl from the mortar between paving stones. It is lush in a compressed, near-touching way: root, rock, leaf, and rusting iron packed tightly together, competing for every square inch of damp surface.

Into this upholstered bowl, a town has been wedged. It is an old industrial settlement built in tight, terraced tiers that follow the natural contours of the eroded stone. Narrow cobbled lanes wind between two- and three-story workshops of soot-darkened brick and corrugated iron, linked by stone-arched viaducts and catwalks that bridge directly over choked tangles of dwarf-pine. There is no neat separation between the wild and the built; overhead, heavy black power cables and insulated steam-pipes are threaded straight through the forks of ancient trees, and the stone foundations of the factories sit flush against primordial rock bowls worn smooth by ten million years of wind.

Despite the factories, the cableways, and the hum of an active grid, the basin is remarkably quiet. The thin air at this altitude refuses to carry sound far, and the thick carpets of moss swallow footsteps and engine vibrations alike. You hear the place in small, wet details: the steady *shhh* of mist combing through wire mesh, the rhythmic *plip-plop* of rainwater gutters emptying into slate cisterns, the faint, dry *tick* of cooling transformer fins, and, now and then, the distant, muffled groan of a low-RPM flywheel turning deep inside a stone powerhouse.

The town’s industry is built around the mist itself. Along the upper ridges, towering rows of slatted wooden frames and galvanized-mesh sails stand like the rigging of grounded ships, harvesting water directly from the passing cloud-tides. Twice a day, on a schedule dictated by the warming and cooling of the invisible valleys miles below, a dense bank of fog rolls up over the western lip of the plateau, carrying a faint, sharp scent of ozone and wet slate. As it sweeps through the town, the collector sails begin to weep. Water runs down into copper troughs, feeds into gravity-driven sluices, and drops through a terraced cascade of mineral-leaching vats, waterwheels, and pressurized condenser-tanks before pooling in dark, mirror-still sumps at the bottom of the basin.

Everything here is in slow, constant motion. Overhead, iron ore-buckets glide silently along greased aerial cables, emerging from the grey veil and vanishing back into it. Relay switches inside glass-fronted street boxes click over with tiny blue sparks as the voltage fluctuates with the water-head. In the biological layer, the pace is just as relentless: spores drift like dust motes through the lamp-beams, condensation beads and falls from a million fern-tips, and a strange, velvet-textured emerald moss—known locally as wire-pelt—creeps steadily along the electrical conduits. Drawn by trace heavy metals and the faint warmth of stray current, the wire-pelt wraps itself around porcelain insulators and junction boxes in thick, bulbous sleeves, acting as a living, damp-proof casing that the town’s maintenance crews trim back only when it threatens to choke the switches.

Animals are rare in this cool, quiet saturation. You might walk the entire length of the terraced basin and see nothing with a spine. Occasionally, a single pale, long-winged glider-bird will slip silently through the gap between two smokestacks, riding an updraft from the plateau's edge without a beat of its wings. Closer to the ground, in the lukewarm runoff troughs beside the boiler houses, small, translucent salamanders cling to the submerged bricks, motionless in the dimness, waiting for the next tide of mist to turn the wheels and stir the water.
