High in a limestone fold where the mountain air is thin enough to taste of slate, a single bowl-shaped tarn holds the snowmelt of three peaks. From above, the water looks like a disc of dark jade set into gray scree; below the surface, it opens into a bright, cool, cathedral-sized world, clear as blown glass and roughly four hundred paces from wall to wall.

Everything here is submerged, and everything is green. No bare stone shows on the basin floor or its steep, curving walls. Centuries of slow, cold-water growth have upholstered the entire bowl in a dense, living pelt: cushions of iron-green moss thick as mattresses, curtains of silver-veined water-fern, and the colossal, pale-amber trunks of bladder-cedars that rise from the silt floor like the pillars of a flooded hall. Their roots do not merely anchor in the mud; they braid together into a springy, continuous mat—a soft, yielding floor that gives slightly underfoot and never stirs up silt. Above, the canopy of flat, translucent fronds spreads just beneath the silvered ceiling of the water’s surface, breaking the high-altitude sunlight into broad, steady shafts of pale gold and turquoise.

The water is cold enough to slow the blood—just above freezing in the deep hollows, slightly milder near the sunlit canopy—and it moves with an almost imperceptible drift. A broad, steady sheet of snowmelt spills over a mossy lip at the northern rim, feeding the tarn, while an unseen seepage through the southern root-mat drains it at the exact same pace. Nothing rushes. The plants add only a finger’s breadth of length in a year; the mineral crust on the older stems thickens over lifetimes.

Yet for all its slowness, the basin is remarkably loud. Underwater, the northern inflow does not splash—it *thrums*, a deep, continuous pressure-note that vibrates in the chest and seems to come from the walls themselves. Against that bassline, the hollow, gas-filled trunks of the bladder-cedars knock and groan softly as the slow drift nudges them, turning the whole grove into a sparse, resonant organ. Closer in, the place crackles with life: thousands of armoured, thumb-sized crayfish click their claws in the moss-beds; broad, silver-bellied trout-kin snap at drifting larvae; and fat, mottled salamanders the length of a forearm bark and pop their throat-sacs from the hollows of submerged stumps.

Suspended in the wide, empty spaces between the great plant-pillars hangs a town.

It is small—no larger than a mountain hamlet—yet laid out with the deliberate, layered geometry of an old city. There are no streets of stone, only taut, braided-fiber swim-lines and narrow catwalks of lashed, waterlogged larch planks, weighted with river-cobbles so they sit steady at three different depths. The buildings are inverted domes and tiered baskets, woven from split willow and peeled cedar-bark, caulked tight on the outside with dark pine-pitch and rendered fat. Each dome traps a pocket of clean mountain air brought down in weighted bladders from the surface.

The clusters of domes are spaced well apart, leaving broad, sunlit avenues of open water between them—a quiet, airy sparseness that lets a swimmer see from the central plaza-platform all the way to the outer terraced ledges in a single glance. At the heart of the basin, four massive air-bells are lashed around the trunk of the oldest bladder-cedar, forming a communal hall, a dry-store, a curing-room where fish are packed in coarse mountain salt, and a workshop. Inside, the air smells of wet wool, resin, and brine. No fires are ever lit beneath the water; the air is too precious to foul with smoke. Instead, tools are shaped cold—flint scrapers, antler needles, bone awls, and pins of soft, hammered copper—all bound with sinew and soaked plant-fiber.

Infrastructure runs through the town like veins through a leaf. Wooden sluice-baffles, anchored to the basin floor with stone-filled wicker cages, gently steer the slow current past the doorways to carry away waste. Woven mesh fish-weirs hang like curtains between the lower trunks, tended with long-handled bone gaffs. Up near the surface canopy, where the light is sharpest, flat, buoyant rafts of lashed reeds hold shallow beds of emergent sedge and bitter-cress, harvested from below.

The inhabitants move through the cold water with unhurried, economical strokes, clad in oiled sealskin or thick, greased wool that traps a layer of warmth against their ribs. When they work inside the domes, the muffled thud of stone hammers on copper and the low rumble of their voices drum outward through the pitch-caulked walls, adding a human cadence to the clicking of the crayfish and the endless, steady organ-note of the snowmelt pouring down from the peaks.

At dusk, the high mountain shadow falls suddenly across the tarn. The golden shafts vanish all at once, leaving the basin in a deep, clear slate-blue. The fish settle into the moss-velvet; the salamanders tuck into the root-knots; the town’s wicker lanterns—filled not with flame, but with jars of cold, pale-glowing marsh-fungus—are unhooded along the plank-walks. Nothing hurries, nothing shifts, and the whole drowned bowl settles into its long, loud, patient night.
