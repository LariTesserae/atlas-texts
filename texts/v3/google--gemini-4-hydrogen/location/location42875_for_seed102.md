Beneath the fractured crust of the surface world—down past the last dampness of soil, past the roots that still remember rain—there is a sealed pocket no larger than a sunken hall. You do not walk into it so much as slip through a tight, jagged seam in young basalt, where the rock has split cleanly and never been smoothed by a single drop of water.

The first thing that strikes you is the heat: a dry, fever-warm stillness that sits against the skin like the air inside a kiln hours after the fire has died. There is no draft. There is no gradient from floor to ceiling. Every corner of the chamber holds the exact same heavy warmth, smelling of hot slate, dry amber, and a faint, sharp tang of sulfur.

The second thing is the sound.

It is deafening, yet it has no urgency. It is a continuous, flat-lined roar—halfway between the groan of a massive organ pipe and the hiss of dry sand pouring through a funnel—that never swells, never falters, and never stops. Hairline fissures in the sharp, unweathered floor exhale warm, non-aqueous gas at an unvarying pressure, forcing it through natural reeds of needle-sharp anhydrite and fractured glass-stone. Because the chamber is small and enclosed, the sound folds back on itself, locking into a permanent standing wave.

In the dim, bruised-amber light, you can see the shape of that noise on the ground. The bare basalt floor is dusted with pale, bone-dry mineral powder, and the vibration has sorted it into crisp, geometric ridges and rings—Chladni patterns etched into dust, undisturbed for as long as the vents have been singing.

Light here is scarce and strange. There is no sun to filter down, no phosphorescent water to reflect. Instead, a dull, steady glow bleeds from the stressed veins of the young rock—piezoelectric sparks trapped in a perpetual hum—and from the undersides of the plants, which oxidize trace gases in a slow, flameless burn. It is just bright enough to pick out the razor-sharp edges of the ledges and the deep, empty shadows between them.

Despite the total absence of water, the pocket is lush.

Clumps of thick, tangled vegetation erupt around the gas vents and spill in heavy curtains from the ceiling crevices, leaving wide stretches of bare, angular stone between them. Nothing here is green with sap; if you crush a frond between your fingers, it does not wet your skin. Instead, it crackles like oiled parchment and puffs a dry cloud of ochre spores, or bleeds a single, thick bead of warm resin that hardens almost instantly in the arid air.

Great *bladder-ferns* dominate the clusters—tall, feathery plumes made of translucent, silica-stiffened tissue. They have no water to give them turgor; their hollow stems and veined fronds are inflated with buoyant, warm gas drawn from the floor, trembling visibly in the roar of the room. Beside them, *pitch-creepers* hang in matte-black and rust-red ropes, coated in a dry, powdery bloom, while velvet-dry mounds of *spore-cushion* wedge themselves into the sharp angles of the rock, pulsing faintly in sympathy with the chamber’s acoustic bass.

And the place is full of life.

The fauna here does not fight the noise; it lives inside the architecture of the sound. Broad, flat *parchment-drifters*—translucent-winged things no larger than a hand—hang suspended in midair without beating their wings. They have found the quiet nodes of the standing wave, the invisible pockets where opposing pressures cancel out, and they hover there motionless, surfing the geometry of the roar.

On the bare rock walls and among the dry thickets, *drum-backed husks*—six-legged, blind creatures with fluted, paper-thin carapaces—anchor themselves to the vibrating stone. They let the constant thrumming pass straight through their hollow bodies, using the resonance to sift drifting spores out of the dry air. Along the resin-vines, segmented *spine-crawlers* graze slowly, leaving behind neat, dry pellets of mineral ash that roll into the geometric dust-lines on the floor and become part of the pattern.

Scattered sparsely among the stone and the dry thickets are the marks of hands.

They are small, primitive things, crafted without metal—nothing more than flat splinters of basalt wedged into gas-slits with hardened resin, or tripods of dried vine-wood and bone holding scraped-out, hollow seed-pods aimed at the ceiling. In a few places, chimes of thin anhydrite shards hang from dry sinew. There is no wind to swing them, yet they buzz continuously against one another, shaken by the sheer volume of the air, adding a thin, glassy shimmer to the room’s low roar.

On a broad, unweathered slab near the center, someone has scratched rows of sharp, shallow tally marks—the edges of the grooves as crisp and white as if the stone were cut an hour ago.

That is the true strangeness of the pocket: nothing here changes. Without water, nothing rots, nothing rusts, nothing washes away. Without seasons, without day or night, without a single degree of cooling or warming, the chamber has settled into a fierce, loud equilibrium. A frond snapped a century ago lies beside a living thicket, indistinguishable in its dry, resinous perfection. The dust-patterns do not drift. The drifters do not leave their nodes. The chorus of rock and insect holds the exact same note forever—a bustling, warm, deafening little world, frozen in the middle of its own breath.
