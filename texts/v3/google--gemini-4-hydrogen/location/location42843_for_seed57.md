It is no larger than a cistern, or the hollow beneath a fallen hearthstone, though its boundaries do not quite agree on where they meet.

Cold, black water fills it to the absolute brim—there is no air pocket, no meniscus, no surface to break toward. The temperature sits just above the point where water stiffens into ice, a brisk, numbing chill that holds everything in suspension. You are low in the earth here, wedged inside a newly split fracture of dark basalt and wet slate. The stone is young: its edges are razor-sharp, freshly cleaved, unblunted by centuries of current. Run a bare knuckle along the wall and the rock bites cleanly, still shedding microscopic grit from the break.

Yet there is almost no open water left to swim or drift through. The chamber is packed—saturated from wall to wall with cold-water growth that has no business thriving in such near-total darkness.

Thick, velvety mats of black-green bryophyte carpet every sharp plane of the stone, softening the angles only slightly before giving way to dense, tangled curtains of translucent ribbon-weed and hair-thin, branching filaments. Bladder-fronds, swollen with clear fluid rather than gas, crowd the center of the pocket like a cluster of pale grapes. They press against one another with a gentle, rubbery insistence, leaving only narrow, crooked veins of free water between them. Nothing here reaches upward, because there is no light above to reach for; instead, the vegetation grows outward along chemical gradients, feeding on the iron and sulfur weeping slowly from the fresh rock wounds.

The darkness is nearly complete. What little illumination exists does not come from a source—it seems to leak from the geometry itself, a bruised, slate-blue dimness that lets you see the silhouette of a frond only when it is inches from your face, and even then, the edges of things smear slightly, as if the water is thicker than water ought to be.

Here, the rules of the outside world have gone slack.

Gravity has no consensus inside the pocket. A shred of dead moss detaches from a stem and drifts sideways, sliding lazily toward the western wall until it stops dead in mid-water, flattening itself against an invisible pane. Upward and downward are local opinions, changing every few feet. If you follow a single long strand of ribbon-weed from its root to its tip, you may find the tip emerging from the opposite wall, rooted a second time, with no clear middle—the plant has grown through a fold in the chamber's smallness. Space is tightly pleat-folded here. Reaching a hand through a dense curtain of pale filaments, your fingertips brush the back of your own wrist from behind.

It is mostly quiet, the kind of quiet that presses against the eardrums. The only sounds are glacial and intimate: the faint, dry *tick* of a mineral grain detaching from the newly split slate; the slow, wet creak of two swollen bladder-fronds shifting past one another as a weak thermal current turns over once every long hour. 

Scattered among the packed greenery are faint, pre-tool marks—remains so modest they almost pass for geology. Wedged into a tight vertical cleft is a small cairn of four flat river-pebbles, balanced carefully in a place where no current could have stacked them, and where balance itself shouldn't hold. Nearby, a loop of braided plant-fiber, stiff with mineral crust, is knotted around a sharp spur of basalt. A few inches to the left of the knot hangs a second, fainter knot—not a reflection, and not quite solid, like an afterimage left behind in the water from when the cord was tied, or a second attempt that never quite faded. There is no metal, no glass, no worked timber; only finger-grooves pressed into a seam of soft grey clay before it hardened, and those three or four deliberate arrangements of stone and stem.

Fauna is rare, and moves at the pace of the chamber. Deep in the center of the weed-tangle, a single pale crustacean—blind, flattened, no larger than a fingernail—hangs upside down from a frond, its translucent legs rowing with agonizing slowness, taking minutes to cross an inch of dark water. Elsewhere, a ribbon of gelatinous spawn clings to the underside of the stacked pebbles, pulsing faintly every few minutes, though whether it is breathing or simply distorting with the local warp of the water is impossible to say.

Nothing rushes here. The cycle is long and weak: mineral seepage, slow cellular division, the imperceptible creep of a stone edge dissolving at a rate too gradual to measure. It is a tiny, drowned room tucked into the ribs of the world, stuffed full of cold life and quiet fractures, repeating itself softly in the dark.
