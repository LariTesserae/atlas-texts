At this altitude, the air outside the lattice ought to be thin and biting, but inside the frame it is thick, warm, and smells of wet iron, crushed fern, and fresh mortar.

The whole place is no larger than a railway cathedral—a single bounded volume, roughly two hundred paces long and half as wide, perched on a sheer, newly-driven pier of steel and poured concrete high above the cloud-deck. There is no rock beneath your feet, no dirt, no weathered stone. Everything you can touch was made, and made recently. The rivets along the main stanchions are still bright and un-pitted; the edges of the extruded terracotta tiles are sharp enough to nick a thumb; the dark, oiled iron of the overhead trusses hasn't yet had time to flake or gather a proper skin of rust. Even the concrete walkways still carry the pale, chalky bloom of recent curing, though they are already slick with water.

Water is the engine here, and the place is built to catch it out of the bright, thin sky.

High up, where the iron ribs arch into a vaulted clerestory, great slanted baffles of ribbed glass and copper mesh face into the prevailing wind. Outside, the light is clear and high-altitude crisp—normal daylight, sharpened by elevation—and it pours through the upper glazing in clean, bright shafts. As the cold mountain air strikes the warm, damp interior surfaces of the baffles, it weeps. Not a rain, exactly, but a steady, heavy condensation that gathers in silver beads along the copper wires, merges, and runs down in silent, glistening threads into lead-lined gutters.

From there, the water drops into the works.

The industry of this place is almost eerily quiet. There are no stamping presses, no roaring furnaces. Instead, the space is dense with slow, heavy, hydraulic patience. Massive cast-iron counterweights hang on greased chains in the corners, descending an inch every few minutes as overhead cisterns fill and trip. Below the catwalks, enclosed in riveted housings that sweat with heat, low-pressure boilers and heat-exchangers keep the ambient temperature at a steady, enveloping warmth—twenty-eight degrees, perhaps, thick with vapor. You hear the machinery mostly as a series of small, polite, rhythmic events: the *tock* of a brass float-valve seating itself; the soft, wet sigh of a felt-baffled steam vent releasing pressure; the barely audible hum of insulated copper busbars carrying current from a wind-turbine somewhere out on the upper spar to the immersion heaters in the lower vats.

In the spaces between the pipes, the catwalks, and the dripping gutters, the vegetation has exploded.

Because there is no natural soil, the plants grow out of what the builders gave them: long, tiered troughs of perforated ceramic, slings of woven fiberglass mat, and beds of crushed, porous slag kept perpetually saturated by weeping drip-lines. And because the place is young—still in its first, furious flush of colonization—nothing has reached a settled equilibrium yet. It is all elbows and ambition. Broad, waxy, elephant-eared leaves unfurl visibly over the course of an afternoon, positioning themselves to catch the stray drips from overhead manifolds. Thick, pale roots—some green with chlorophyll where the light hits them—spill out of the ceramic trays and wrap tightly around the warm water-pipes, using the plumbing as both trellis and radiator.

The density is claustrophobic in the best way. You walk along narrow, grated iron catwalks with lush, wet foliage brushing your shoulders on both sides, layered three and four tiers deep from the flooded sump-floor up to the mist-hung rafters. Many of the plants look subtly wrong, or at least over-specialized for this life of iron and steam. There are pale, fleshy vines that produce hollow, bladder-like nodes; when they fill with condensed water, they sag until they touch a lower tray, burst softly, and seed the level below. Ferns with black, wire-stiff stems have learned to anchor themselves directly into the greasy packing around the valve-stems, thriving on the mineral runoff.

The air moves constantly, though you rarely feel a draft. It is a slow, vertical circulation: cool air sinking along the outer glass, warming as it passes the lower boiler-casings, rising through the dense, wet jungle of leaves, carrying spores and warm mist back up to the condenser-baffles. Everything is active, locked into a tight, biological-mechanical loop. Minerals dissolve out of the fresh slag-beds, travel through the roots, get exhaled as vapor or dropped as dead leaf-litter into the grated sumps, where slow-turning Archimedes screws lift the rich, dark slurry back up to the top troughs.

Fauna is scarce, as if the animals haven't quite found this high, newly-assembled island yet. If you stand still on the upper gantry for ten minutes, letting the warmth soak into your clothes, you might spot one of the few things that live here: a tiny, nearly translucent frog clinging to the underside of a brass pressure-gauge, its throat pulsing; or a pair of large, slow-moving moths with wings the color of wet newsprint, resting on a warm conduit. Once in a while, a small, dull-plumaged bird—something like a wren, though longer-legged—darts through a gap in the upper louvers, picks a drowned beetle out of a gutter, and vanishes back out into the bright, thin air.

Mostly, though, it is just the plants, the water, and the quiet, unweathered iron—a small, warm, furious room suspended in the cold sky, still smelling of the factory that made it, already half-swallowed by the garden it was built to feed.
