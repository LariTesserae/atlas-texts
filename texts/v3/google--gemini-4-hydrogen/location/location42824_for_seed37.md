The first thing that strikes you is the glare. A pocket this far beneath the surface has no business being bright, yet the chamber is flooded with an even, shadowless white light—cool as refrigerator air and sharp enough to make your eyes water. There is no lamp, no crack to the sky. The light seems to be what the room is made of before the stone finishes arriving.

It is a small, intimate space, no larger than a root cellar, sealed away inside raw bedrock. Yet the edges of it refuse to settle. Where two walls ought to meet at a corner, the angle folds slightly too far inward, so your gaze slides off it without finding a seam. The floor has none of the worn smoothness of a cave; erosion hasn't touched it. Instead, the mineral looks newly extruded, like pale plaster or wet bone caught mid-set—sharp-edged, unweathered, and faintly greasy to the touch. In a few places, the stone hasn't decided to be solid at all: it flickers silently between damp limestone, something like frosted glass, and sheer white absence.

Despite the relentless brightness, the air is cool and smells of wet chalk. Every few minutes, a quiet weather cycle moves through the pocket. The light sharpens into a cold shimmer, and condensation abruptly sweats from the raw walls in fat, slow beads. They drop into shallow dimples in the floor—not carved by water, just leftover depressions in the unfinished rock—creating thin, temporary mirrors. If you look into one of those puddles, the reflection is slightly out of step with the room: you see a pale creature skittering across the ceiling a full second before its shadow actually passes over your hand. Then, just as quickly, the floor drinks the moisture back in, hardening slightly where the water touched it, and the dampness retreats into the seams.

Between the wide stretches of bare, un-eroded stone, life is sparse in its roots and astonishingly crowded in its motion.

A few tough, bleached-looking mosses cling to the wettest crevices like damp eyelashes, alongside a single stunted fern-frond that curls inward to cup the falling drops. Scattered across the empty floor are small, deliberate structures—no tools were used to make them, only mandibles and persistence. There are teacup-sized rings of sharp, un-chipped pebbles stacked to catch condensation, and faint, rhythmic tally-marks scratched into a patch of soft, un-set clay by something testing whether the wall was real. Half-buried near the center, the calcified ribcage of a larger animal—something that wandered in before the pocket closed—fuses seamlessly into the emerging floor, its hollow vertebrae cradling the only lush tuft of liverwort in the room.

And everywhere, in the air and across the stone, the fauna is abundant.

Because the space is so quiet, it takes a moment to realize how frantic the ecology actually is. There is no hum, no rustle, no cry of prey—only the faintest *shhh* of dry carapaces and the tiny, swallowed *tock* of a water drop. Through the bright, cool air drift dozens of ribbon-thin, translucent swimmers, flat as mica shavings, undulating without wings. When they pass through one of the room’s fractured corners, they briefly split into three or four overlapping echoes of themselves before snapping back into a single body.

Along the damp seams, herds of palm-sized grazers—pale, many-legged, with feathery external gills that drink the mist—crop silently at the moss. They are hunted constantly by things that do not bother with distance. Resting motionless on the far wall, a predator with long, jointed, porcelain-white limbs waits until a grazer drifts near a spatial crease; then the limb simply reaches *through* the bent air, emerging inches from the prey to pluck it off the stone. There is no sound—only a sudden twitch, and a bead of pale ichor that freezes into a tiny salt-crystal before it even touches the floor.

The shed skins of the grazers don't rot; they calcify within hours, stiffening into raw mineral ridges that become part of the chamber's growing floor. Everything here is in motion—sweating, grazing, folding through the light, turning slowly into stone—held inside a blinding, refrigerated quiet that feels as though the world forgot to turn off the work-lights before it left the room unfinished.
