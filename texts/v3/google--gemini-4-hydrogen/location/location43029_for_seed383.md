Far below the level of the outer plains, the sinkhole opens into a sunken valley of pale, water-worn stone. From the rim—a jagged lip of ancient limestone so high it looks like a chalk cliff—the floor lies in a perpetual, shadowless glare. The sky above is a flat, blinding white, and the basin below is carved from rock so bleached and smooth that it throws the light back up from every angle. You have to squint to make out the edges of things, even though the air down here is cool and clammy.

The bedrock is unimaginably old. Whatever sharp ridges or jagged faults once existed have been rounded off by eons of slow, patient dissolution. The floor of the basin undulates in broad, slick ribs and shallow, saucer-like depressions, polished like soapstone. There is no soil, only a thin, damp paste of ground limestone and mineral grit that collects in the lowest seams. Nothing green grows here. In the wettest crevices, where cold condensation beads on the rock and drips in slow, irregular intervals, a velvety grey-black film of fungus clings to the stone, looking more like soot or tarnish than anything alive. Elsewhere, the rock is bare, streaked with salt-crusts and faint, rust-colored stains where iron-rich water once seeped and dried.

Yet this sunken, barren bowl is a city.

Across the vast, open floor, layered terraces of dry-stone masonry rise in stepped tiers, hugging the gentle curves of the ancient bedrock. Long, straight causeways—built of fitted limestone slabs—cross the empty expanses between clusters of towers, sunken cisterns, and low, flat-roofed compounds. There is an unmistakable urban order to it: retaining walls, drainage runnels carved into the stone, broad plazas, and narrow, winding alleys stacked three or four levels deep against the basin’s lower walls. Still, the city feels strangely hollow. Wide stretches of polished, empty stone separate the built quarters, as if the settlement were a net cast over a space far too large for it, with most of its knots pulled tight in scattered corners and long, bare cords stretching between.

Nothing here is made with anything more complex than bone levers, wooden wedges, braided fiber, and stone. The walls are stacked without mortar, held together by weight and careful wedging; the roofs are flat slabs of split gypsum; the tools left leaning against doorways are chipped flint, hammered copper pins, and heavy mallets wrapped in thick, dampened hide.

And the whole place is in frantic, silent motion.

From a distance, the scattered quarters look like disturbed anthills. Hundreds of figures—and hundreds more pale, damp-skinned creatures—are constantly moving along the causeways and swarming over the stepped terraces. Yet if you stand on a high terrace and close your eyes, you hear almost nothing. No shouting, no clatter of carts, no ringing of hammers. Only the faint, occasional *plink* of a water drop hitting a shallow cistern, a dry, papery rustle, or a muffled *thud* swallowed instantly by the vast, open air.

The silence is deliberate, or perhaps simply bred into everything that lives here. The builders—lean, pale-eyed, wrapped in layered tunics of felted fungus-fiber—wear thick, sodden pads bound to their feet. They move at a rapid, hunched trot, carrying heavy blocks of chalky stone on woven slings, communicating with quick, sharp flicks of their fingers and chin-jerks. Their hide-wrapped mallets strike wooden wedges with a dull, wet *tock* that barely carries ten paces.

They are never finished. They are never even maintaining a steady shape. Across the basin, entire quarters are being torn down and rebuilt at the same time. A crew of twenty will spend an hour dismantling a perfectly sound retaining wall, passing the stones hand-to-hand along a human chain, only to stack them into a new baffle thirty yards away to catch a shifting trickle of condensation. Another group frantically shores up a sagging causeway where the ancient silt-bed beneath has suddenly slumped, tilting the flagstones at a steep angle.

The ground itself is restless, even if the rock is ancient. Pockets of trapped moisture and cold subterranean air shift beneath the thin crust of the basin floor, causing whole sections of the damp grit to heave, sink, or vent sudden, silent plumes of cold mist. Whenever a new damp seam opens—glistening darkly on the white stone—the city convulses toward it. Builders race to trench it, wall it, and roof it over before the water can evaporate in the bright glare; others abandon dry cisterns, prying up the paving stones to carry them to the new site.

Through all of this weave the creatures that share the pit. They are abundant, pale as the limestone, and just as quiet. Long-necked, soft-footed grazers with damp, pebbled hides wade through the shallow, intermittent runnels, scraping the black fungal film from the stone with broad, flat teeth. Swarms of segmented, chitinous scavengers—the size of small dogs, with pale, translucent shells—scurry in tight, erratic streams along the bases of the walls, cleaning up dropped scraps of fiber and mineral paste. They make no vocal sounds; when startled, they only scatter in a faint, dry patter of legs against damp stone, vanishing into the gaps between the unmortared blocks.

Even when a terrace wall gives way—sliding down in a cascade of heavy, pale slabs—the collapse is strangely muted. A low, grinding rumble, a puff of white chalk-dust that hangs briefly in the bright, cool air, and then the dust settles. Before it has even cleared, dozens of figures are already scrambling over the rubble, silent and urgent, lifting the fallen stones to build something else.
