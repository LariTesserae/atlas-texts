Just below the surface of the lagoon, hidden beneath the overhang of a flat limestone quay, sits a sunken room no larger than a cistern. You enter it by dropping through a square timber-framed hatch in the stone deck above, or by swimming under a low lintel on the seaward side. Once inside, there is no natural rock anywhere: floor, walls, baffles, and ceiling beams are all hand-cut, fitted, and mortared.

The chamber is young. The limestone blocks—pale cream and chalk-white—still hold the crisp, parallel ridges of a mason’s toothed chisel along their faces, and the corners where blocks meet are sharp enough to catch a fingernail. Between them, the hydraulic mortar is a warm, dusty rose from crushed brick mixed into the lime, pressed flush with a trowel and barely touched by salt. A few paces across, the room is laid out with quiet, practical geometry: stepped ledges along two walls, a recessed channel cut into the flagstones of the floor, and a row of three terracotta pipes set low into the far bulkhead.

Above, the roof is not sealed. A wide rectangular opening, crossed by heavy cedar beams spaced like a grate, opens straight to the sky through less than a meter of clear water. At midday, the sun drops directly through that aperture and turns the whole flooded room blindingly bright. There are no dim corners. Light bounces off the pale flagstones and whitewashed mortar seams until the water itself seems to glow, shot through with fast, restless nets of caustic gold that whip across the walls and shudder over the ceiling beams.

The water is warm—sun-heated in the shallows outside and held in the stone box like bathwater, thick and silky against the skin. And it is loud. Because the chamber is small, rigid, and entirely enclosed in dressed stone, every movement of the sea outside arrives magnified. Each swell that rolls against the quay sends a low, chest-rattling boom through the walls. A moment later, a jet of aerated water blasts through the narrow bronze-grated intake high on the seaward wall, filling the upper half of the room with a roaring white cloud of bubbles that hisses as it rises toward the beams.

The current drives across the bare floor in a steady, pulsing rhythm. Near the bottom of the opposite wall, a hinged cedarwood flap-valve—weighted with a smooth river cobble lashed in hemp cord—lifts with a dull wooden *clack*, lets a surge of water spill into the terracotta drainpipes, and drops shut again as the swell recedes. Between surges, the terracotta mouths hum with a hollow, resonant drone, like wind blown across the lip of a jar.

Despite all this motion, the room feels remarkably empty. Between the intake plume and the drain, there are wide, uncluttered expanses of pale stone and clear water. Nothing darts across the light; no crabs wedge themselves into the mortar joints, and no barnacles have yet crusted the bronze grate. The chamber is too new, too warm, and too violently scoured for animals to settle. Only in the quietest angles—behind the stepped ledges, or in the recessed mortar grooves where the current eddies slowly—does life appear at all: sparse, isolated tufts of bright green hair-algae and a few translucent, paper-thin ribbons of sea lettuce, anchored to the rough stone and streaming out like flags each time the swell pushes through.

Fine pale grit, washed in from the lagoon, skates across the flagstones in brief, glittering sheets, gathers in the floor channel, and vanishes down the pipes. Everything here is in its first season—sharp-edged, sun-drenched, and roaring with warm water, slowly being broken in by the tide.
