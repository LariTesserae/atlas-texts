High in the throat of a mountain that has not finished rising, there is a flooded blister no wider than a courtyard—a single, hidden hollow sealed inside raw stone, miles above the timberline.

From the outside, the peak is all fresh fracture: jagged, unweathered plates of pale slate and black basalt thrust upward so recently that the wind has not yet rounded a single edge. Somewhere behind a sheer wall of translucent quartz and wind-packed ice, the mountain has split open into an internal bell, and that bell is drowned.

It is cold, clear, and almost completely empty. 

The water is not the heavy, salt-thickened dark of an ocean. It is alpine meltwater and storm-catchment, thin and bitingly cool, bright with crisp high-altitude daylight that pours straight down through the jagged skylights of ice and glassy mineral above. Because the pocket is small and its walls are pale, unstained stone, the light bounces endlessly through the hollow, turning the whole volume into a blinding, pale-blue lens. You can see from one wall to the other—eighty paces of sheer, vacant water, broken only by ribbons of silver-white micro-bubbles torn loose from the ceiling.

And yet, for all its emptiness, the place is never still, and it is never quiet.

The mountain is still climbing. Deep in the floor and along the razor-sharp ribs of the walls, the rock groans, snaps, and shifts with a slow, tectonic violence. Fresh fissures open without warning, venting icy, pressurized jets from higher snowfields, while old cracks pinch shut. The water inside the blister is caught in a perpetual, roaring churn—a trapped maelstrom with nowhere to spill. Currents slam against the raw, angular stone, rebound off the ceiling, and collide in the center of the hollow, creating sudden, invisible shear-zones where water moving in opposite directions tears at anything caught between them.

The sound is deafening. Underwater, there is no air to soften it; the noise enters directly through the skull and the sternum. It is a continuous, bone-rattling thunder made of cavitation—millions of tiny vacuum-bubbles forming and violently collapsing in the eddies—layered over the low, metallic shriek of fresh basalt grinding against slate. Every few minutes, a pocket of trapped air compresses in a ceiling fissure and detonates like a struck drum, sending a shockwave through the water that makes the teeth ache.

Because the rock is so young—barely emerged from the crust, younger even than the storm-water that fills it—there is no silt, no mud, no soft sediment on the floor. Every surface is wickedly sharp, glassy, and bare. 

Yet someone has been here, or keeps returning. 

Scattered across the leeward sides of the sharpest rock-fins, wedged into the few stable notches where the current splits, are small, stubborn works of hand-craft. There are cairns of flat, unshaped slate, bound together with thick, braided sinew and weighted down with chunks of raw copper ore so the turbulence cannot scatter them. Here and there, heavy bone hooks have been driven into soft seams in the stone, trailing long, knotted cords of twisted root-fiber that whip horizontally in the racing current like taut bowstrings. In one quiet corner—a dead-zone behind a jutting blade of quartz—a cluster of hollowed-out stone bells hangs on leather thongs, clacking furiously against one another in the wash, adding their small, frantic percussive rattle to the mountain’s roar.

There is no sign of who tied them, or what they were measuring, or whether they came down from the ice-cracks above to listen to the mountain grow.

Life here is sparse, clinging only to the margins of the violence. Along the shadowed undersides of the slate cairns and inside the tightest fractures, a tough, pale-green moss grows in flat, rubbery crusts that refuse to tear. Occasionally, a cluster of translucent, gas-filled bladder-weeds breaks loose from the rock and goes spinning wildly across the empty center of the chamber like lost lanterns, caught in the cross-currents until they are shredded against the quartz walls.

Once in a great while, something moves in the clear void that is not water or weed. A pale, flattened creature—half-armored in flexible plates like a blind, foot-long trilobite—detaches itself from the trembling wall, rides the violent updraft for three heartbeats with its hooked legs tucked tight, and clamps itself back onto a distant rib of stone. It feeds on the microscopic mineral dust shaken loose by the grinding rock, and on the stray fibers shed by the knotted cords.

Mostly, though, there is only the bright, freezing volume of the hollow, the razor-edged walls still rising into the sky, and the deafening, endless collision of water that cannot escape.
