The heat hits first—a heavy, wet, radiant warmth that rises off the floor as much as it presses down from above. There is no sky to speak of, only a blinding, milk-white glare that fills the upper half of the world with a uniform, shadowless brilliance. You have to squint to see across the basin, and even then, the distance wavers in the rising steam.

Underfoot, there is no dirt, no bedrock, no wild stone. The entire valley floor—stretching out for a mile or more in every direction—is hand-troweled lime plaster laid over interlocking, low-fired terracotta tiles. You can see the shallow, rhythmic arcs of wooden floats in the wet white surface, and here and there, where a tile sits exposed, the crisp impressions of human thumbs along its bevelled edge. Everything here is made, and made with simple tools: bone scrapers, wooden molds, twisted reed cordage, hand-knapped chert. Yet nothing is worn down. Despite the standing water and the thick, warm humidity, every corner of every brick is razor-sharp, as fresh as if it were pulled from a kiln an hour ago.

The expanse is vast and mostly empty. Between the structures lie wide, glaring flats of wet plaster, slick with a thin film of warm water that never quite drains and never quite evaporates. Shallow, hand-dug runnels cross the flats in straight, careful lines, their clay lips sculpted to hold the water at an exact, trembling brim.

Scattered sparsely across this bright emptiness are the gardens—each one a self-contained island of impossible, suffocating lushness. They grow out of massive, waist-high vessels: wide rings of mortared chalk-blocks, or great, sagging cauldrons woven from split cane and sealed on the inside with dark pitch. Inside these widely spaced beds, the vegetation erupts. Broad, waxy leaves the size of shields crowd over one another, dripping with condensation. Thick mats of emerald moss spill over the woven rims, studded with fleshy, crimson-throated blossoms and heavy, leather-petaled lotuses. The vines trail downward toward the wet plaster floor, but they stop a finger’s breadth above it, curling back upon themselves in tight, dry spirals as if repelled by the surface.

It is almost entirely quiet. There is no wind to stir the giant leaves. The air feels thick and padded, swallowing sound before it can travel. Every few seconds, a heavy bead of condensed water detaches from a leaf-tip and drops into a clay basin with a single, muted *tock*, and then the stillness closes back in.

Yet the place is crowded with life. You only notice how much of it there is when your eyes adjust to the glare. Dozens of pale, translucent tree frogs cling motionless to the undersides of a single broad leaf, their throats pulsing slowly, out of rhythm with their breathing. In the shallow rectangular cisterns, stark-white wading birds stand knee-deep in the warm water—four or five to a basin, completely silent, never preening, never blinking. If you look closely at the mirror-flat water around their legs, their reflections are looking the wrong way, facing the opposite rim of the pool.

Along the sharp, unweathered ridges of the mortar joints, thumb-sized beetles with iridescent, oil-slick shells crawl in slow, deliberate files, leaving no wet tracks behind them. Below the surface of the runnels, thick braids of pale, blind eels twist endlessly around one another in water so clear it barely seems to be there.

Nothing here decays, and nothing progresses. Condensation drips steadily from the hot air into the brimming basins, yet the water never spills over the low clay lips. A fallen petal rests on the wet plaster—still turgid, still bright scarlet, refusing to rot or brown. Across the blinding flats, the distant, reed-thatched drying sheds and clay mounds sit in the exact same white-hot afternoon they have always occupied, locked in a wet, breathless equilibrium where the work of hands has outlasted time itself.
