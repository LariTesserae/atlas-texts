If you hear the basin before you see it, you will spend a long time looking for the smokestacks.

The sound carries across the low rim of the valley as a steady, heavy rhythm: a low *thump—hiss*, followed by a deep, vibrating drone like a bank of transformers under load, punctuated every few minutes by the sharp, wet *crack* of an electrical arc. It is loud enough to feel in your molars. Yet when you crest the worn, rounded ridge of dark slate and look down into the lowland, there isn’t a single wall, pipe, wire, or roof anywhere in sight.

The basin is wide and open, stretching a few miles from one slumped, ancient rim to the other under a plain, bright sky. The light is ordinary daylight, clear and flat, slightly softened near the ground by a thin veil of warm mist that never quite burns off and never thickens into fog. Everything down here is low, settled, and old. The surrounding hills have long since finished slumping into smooth, tired curves; the valley floor itself is a broad, nearly level expanse of wet grey shale, rust-stained clay, and pale salt-crusts, worn down to its final grade.

There is no green anywhere. Not a tuft of grass in the cracks, not a cushion of moss on the damp stones. The only things clinging to the rock are thin, slick films of sulfur-yellow and copper-oxide, and occasional greasy patches of mineral-eating slime along the waterlines.

The air down in the basin is warm and heavy with moisture, like the air inside a laundry or a boiler room. Condensation beads on the leeward sides of boulders and runs down in slow, permanent threads. Shallow, lukewarm pools of murky brine sit in the lowest depressions, linked by sluggish trickles that barely seem to move, though they never dry up and never overflow. Every puddle is ringed by a crisp, pale crust of precipitated salt and iron, marking a waterline that has not risen or fallen by an inch in living memory.

Scattered sparsely across the wide flats—with long stretches of empty, damp mud and broken shale between them—are the engines.

They are not built; they are simply how the rock here has arranged itself over millions of years. At intervals of a quarter-mile or so, clusters of hollow basalt chimneys rise a few feet above the mud. Inside each chimney, subterranean heat and steady groundwater pressure build until they lift a massive, naturally rounded plug of dense ironstone seated in a smooth, water-worn socket. With a deafening *THUMP*, the stone lifts, vents a violent plume of white steam that hisses across the wet ground, and drops back into its seat with a dull, metallic *clack*. Then it waits three seconds, and does it again. And again.

Because the rock beneath the valley floor is laced with thick, exposed veins of quartz and native copper, the constant, pounding vibration of the stone valves does something else: it charges the ground.

Walking across the sparse, wet flats, you can hear the hum rising straight through the soles of your boots—a loud, continuous, sixty-cycle-like buzz vibrating through the damp, salty mud and the crystalline veins. Where two heavy nodules of raw copper sit close together across a wet crack in the shale, blue-white sparks occasionally snap and hiss, ozone mixing briefly with the smell of wet rust and warm sulfur. In the shallow channels between the vents, the continuous shaking of the earth and the steady trickle of warm, acidic water act like natural sluice-boxes, sorting the grit by weight—washing pale silica downstream while leaving neat, gleaming pockets of separated iron sand and copper granules in the hollows.

It feels, in every sensory detail, like an open-air foundry or a hydroelectric yard, except that nothing has been welded, poured, or intended. There are no paths between the vents, no scrap heaps, no soot—only the clean, ancient geometry of erosion and mineral equilibrium.

Life here is scarce, and what little exists has adapted to the noise and the current. Now and then, if you watch the warm, rust-rimmed puddles closely, you will see a palm-sized crustacean with a thick, dull, bronze-colored shell creeping slowly over the wet slate, grazing on the mineral films. They have no eyes and no ears; they navigate by the steady, unchanging thrum of the rock, keeping just clear of the copper veins where the current bites. Once in a long while, a pale, leathery-winged thing—blind and heavy-bodied—flutters silently from one warm basalt chimney to another, avoiding the steam blasts with the ease of a creature whose ancestors have timed the exact same three-second pause for ten thousand generations.

Nothing here changes. There is no wind to speak of, no weather rolling over the low rims, no shift in the light except the slow arc of the day. The mist hangs at the same knee-high level over the brine pools. The stone valves lift and drop at the exact same tempo, hour after hour, year after year, in a loud, warm, damp equilibrium that neither wears out nor winds down.
