The light hits first—thin, high-altitude glare that turns every wet surface into a mirror. At this elevation, the sky is a pale, washed-out blue, so bright it hurts to look near the horizon, and the sun pours down unfiltered onto a vast, open basin miles across.

There is no soil here, and almost no bare ground. The entire floor of the plateau is buried under a continuous, living mattress of highland vegetation—feet-deep cushions of saturated sphagnum moss, matted sedges, copper-colored bog-moss, and tough, creeping clubmoss. It is a world made of wet green and ochre sponge. Every step sinks ankle-deep, and water wells up immediately around the boot, clear and cold, before the springy mat closes back over the print. Even the scattered boulders and the sharp, angular ledges of dark basalt are wrapped in thick, dripping pelts of lichen and liverwort, as if the plants are slowly swallowing the stone whole.

Beneath that living blanket, the geology is young and unfinished. The basin sits high on a newly uplifted ridge, its edges defined by raw, unweathered fault-scarps and jagged talus slopes that haven’t had time to round off. The rock breaks in clean, brutal planes. Water doesn’t carve gentle meandering channels here; it pools in the mossy hollows until the mat can’t hold another drop, then sheets across the surface in wide, gleaming films and drops straight over the sharp-edged ledges in sudden, white-water falls. The air is cool—cold enough to redden your knuckles and keep the mist clinging to the shaded faces of the cliffs, though the sun warms the top layer of the moss until it steams faintly in the glare.

Despite the total cover of vegetation, the landscape feels remarkably empty. There are no trees, no shrubs taller than a knee, nothing to break the sweep of the basin. You can see for miles in every direction, across undulating swells of bright wet moss and shallow, sky-reflecting pools. Life with legs or wings is scarce up here; now and then a lone, pale-breasted pipit darts low over the sedges, or a beetle crawls across a slick rock, and then there is nothing moving at all.

The stillness of the view makes the noise hit even harder.

The basin is deafening. It isn’t the sharp, erratic clatter of a city, but a massive, continuous wall of sound that presses against your eardrums from every side. High-altitude wind pours over the sharp rim of the plateau, booming through the hollows. Below that sits the relentless, chest-vibrating roar of water—millions of gallons plunging off the un-eroded scarps and being funneled into the scattered industrial works that dot the expanse.

Built things are sparse here, spaced half a mile or more apart, sitting like solitary ships on a bright green sea. A single poured-concrete turbine house squats at the base of a raw cliff, fed by two thick, riveted steel penstocks that drop straight down the rock face like black veins. Half a mile away, a cluster of giant aluminum cooling towers and high-voltage transformers hums at a furious, teeth-rattling pitch, wrapped in a permanent halo of condensing steam. A line of galvanized lattice pylons marches in a straight, indifferent line across the bog, their ceramic insulators sizzling faintly in the damp air.

Everything mechanical runs at a steady, automated, unbroken clip. Massive intake gates stand half-open, screaming as water forces past their steel lips; pressure-relief valves vent white plumes of mist with a hissing roar that carries across the entire valley. Because the basin is so open and the surrounding rock walls so flat and unweathered, the sound doesn’t dissipate—it bounces, layers, and settles into a permanent, grinding thunder.

Yet for all the noise and the glare, nothing here is in a hurry. The turbines turn at the exact same speed hour after hour, month after month. The water seeps through the deep peat at an imperceptible crawl before it ever reaches the steel intakes. The moss creeps millimeter by millimeter up the concrete foundations of the pump-houses, staining the gray walls green with damp, patient, unhurried, and completely indifferent to the roar.
