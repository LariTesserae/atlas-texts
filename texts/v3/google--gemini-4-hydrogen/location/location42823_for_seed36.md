The air here does not move, and the light has nowhere to hide.

The entire landscape sits in a vast, sunken basin—miles across, dropped deep below the surrounding crust of the world. From above, there would only be a jagged, raw-edged scar in the earth, as if a slab of the planet’s skin had cracked and settled downward overnight. Down here on the floor, the surrounding cliffs rise like steep, unweathered walls of pale, glassy stone and milky quartz, catching the overhead glare and bouncing it back and forth across the valley until even the undersides of leaves are bright. There is no shade that isn’t luminous. The light is white, relentless, and heavy with heat.

Nothing has eroded. The rock beneath the surface growth has no soil on it, no sand, no rounded gravel—only freshly fractured planes, razor-sharp ledges, and stepped shelves of raw mineral that look as though they were cleaved apart an hour ago and left untouched. Water has never carved a channel here; wind has never smoothed an edge. Because the basin lies so far below the surface, the winds of the upper world pass straight over the rim without dipping down. The heat settles into the pit and stays, baking the air into a thick, motionless equilibrium.

Over this raw, sharp geology, vegetation has taken total hold, serving as both skin and architecture. You do not walk on stone; you walk on a dense, multi-layered mat of living mass—several feet deep in places—woven from fibrous, cork-barked creepers, overlapping rosettes of waxy, pale-green succulents, and thick, lattice-like canes that lock together in a springy, near-solid floor. The plants grow directly out of the bare fractures in the stone, anchoring into mineral seams with pale, grasping roots. Above the mat, taller growths rise in crowded, near-touching tiers: broad, translucent paddles and fluted, hollow stems that filter the blinding overhead glare into a glowing, chartreusely-yellow haze. Everything is packed tight, layered, and saturated with life, yet nothing sways. Without wind, the dense canopy stands as still as carved jade.

Water is present, though you will never see it flow or fall. The air is hot and faintly muggy, holding just enough moisture to bead on the cooler, shaded undersides of the thick, waxy leaves. Deep inside the cupped bases of the giant rosettes, and in the narrow, un-eroded crevices where the stone meets the root-mat, small pockets of warm, still water collect—dark, mineral-heavy puddles fed entirely by condensation. If you press your hand into the dense vegetation, it comes away damp and warm, smelling of crushed pith, hot resin, and wet chalk.

The silence is almost complete, broken only by the smallest mechanical sounds of heat and moisture: the faint, glassy *tick* of a taut leaf expanding in the glare, or the single, heavy *plink* of a condensation drop falling from a paddle-tip into a cupped stem below.

Animals are rare and unhurried. Every few hundred paces, a pale, flat-bodied lizard—the color of bleached bone—clings motionless to the underside of a frond, waiting out the glare, or a slow, papery insect with translucent wings creeps along a damp seam in the bark. There are no swarms, no birds, no calls. Whatever lives here is small, quiet, and tuned to a world that never changes its temperature or its season.

Scattered across the valley, half-swallowed by the dense, static flora, are faint marks of something that once lingered here. There is no metal, no carved stone, no sign of any tool harder than a fingernail or a broken shard of rock. Instead, there are small, low domes formed by weaving living, pliable creepers together and daubing the gaps with hardened plant-resin; small cairns of sharp, unweathered crystal shards stacked carefully on flat rock-shelves; and shallow, hand-pressed bowls of dried mud and sap wedged beneath the heaviest, wettest leaves to catch the slow drip of condensation. They look less like ruins and more like nests left behind by something that needed shelter from the light, took what the plants offered without cutting them, and then vanished—leaving the basin to its hot, bright, unchanging stillness.
