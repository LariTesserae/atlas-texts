From a distance, the world looks like the stripped ribs of something dead for a billion years: a vast, sun-lit emptiness of sky, broken only by widely scattered monoliths of pale, pitted limestone rising out of a heat-blurred abyss. There is no horizon in the usual sense—only a distant, shimmering haze where the floor of the basin ought to be, thousands of feet below, and mile after mile of open air between one spire and the next.

The rock itself is ancient beyond reckoning, worn past all sharp angles into smooth, bulbous crowns, sweeping concave flanks, and colossal natural arches. Time and wind have bored the stone hollow. Every pillar is honeycombed with vertical chimneys, collapsed caverns, and wind-scoured flutes, so that from up close the solid ground feels less like a mountain and more like a sponge of calcified bone.

And it is never quiet. The emptiness is a roaring, violent engine.

Warm air rises relentlessly out of the unseen depths below, colliding with cooler high-altitude currents that sweep across the open expanse. The result is a state of permanent atmospheric turbulence. Sudden updrafts slam against the undersides of overhangs; crosswinds shear past the smooth stone at gale force; localized whirlwinds spin up out of nowhere, corkscrewing across the void before tearing themselves apart. Because the ancient stone is hollow, the wind turns the entire landscape into a deafening instrument. A deep, chest-rattling bass hum vibrates perpetually through the rock, impossible to escape even in a sheltered crevice. Above it rides a chaotic, shifting wall of noise: the shriek of air forced through narrow stone throats, the sharp rifle-crack of pressure-pockets collapsing inside caverns, and the constant, tearing hiss of foliage whipped to its limits.

Under the bright, normal daylight, the air looks deceptively clear—until a moisture front hits. There are no rivers here, no lakes, and no steady rain. Instead, the violent mixing of warm basin air and cold upper drafts wrings out sudden, erratic sheets of warm mist. A dense, damp cloud will materialize against the windward face of a spire, drenching the stone in heavy condensation for five minutes, and then shred into nothingness as a dry gust sweeps it away.

Yet wherever stone meets air, life is impossibly lush. The flat, weathered crowns of the monoliths and the deep leeward ledges are buried under thick, springy mats of vegetation. There is no soil to speak of—only meters-deep tangles of braided, rubbery roots, broad-leafed epiphytes that curl their waxy blades shut when a gale strikes, and forty-foot curtains of silver-green moss that trail off the cliff edges and whip horizontally in the wind like torn banners. Cupped leaves and hollow knots in the wood hold tiny, precious pockets of condensed water, warm to the touch.

Because the wind is so brutal and erratic, the greenery does not grow quietly; it lives in a state of frantic, turbulent turnover. Whole carpets of root-bound turf—thick as mattresses and heavy with moss—are regularly peeled off the windward cliffs by sudden downdrafts. They sail across a mile of empty air, spinning slowly in the sunlight, until they snag on the jagged crest of another spire and immediately send out white, hungry tendrils to anchor into the damp pores of the rock. Seed-pods the size of fists swell in the heat and detonate like gunshots, hurling sticky, tufted spores into the updrafts. On any given afternoon, a spire might be stripped half-bare on its western face while its eastern ledges are choked with freshly landed, rapidly rooting debris.

Animal life is rare, discouraged by the noise and the sheer drops. Occasionally, a solitary membrane-glider—a flat, wide-winged creature no thicker than parchment, with long trailing stabilizers—will ride the chaotic thermals in the open spaces between pillars, banking sharply to snatch drifting insects or pausing for a few seconds to hook onto a swaying moss-curtain and drink from a leaf-cup. Deeper inside the deafening, lightless chimneys of the stone, blind, flat-bodied scavengers with segmented, chitinous shells cling to the wet walls, navigating entirely by the changing pitch of the rock’s vibration and feeding on whatever organic matter the wind sucks down the vents.

Signs of anything built are scarce and easily missed against the scale of the spires. Wedged into the deepest leeward notches—the few places where a curve of ancient stone deflects the worst of the crosswinds—are small, weathered shelters. They are made of dry-stacked limestone fragments mortared with hardened plant-resin, roofed with lashed hardwood poles and cured, waxy leaves. Near the cliff edges, thick cables of braided vine are anchored to the rock with hammered copper pitons, green with verdigris, dangling down toward lower ledges or stretched taut to hold down heavy woven windbreaks.

Around the thresholds of these tiny outposts sit simple, functional remnants: bone wind-vanes that spin in blurs, stone cairns weighted down with resin-sealed baskets, and heavy leather ear-hoods hung on wooden pegs, stiff with salt and sweat. Whoever comes here—perhaps to harvest the tough, fast-growing fibers or the rich resins that the plants bleed in the heat—does not stay long, and does not build high. Everything here is temporary, lashed down, or waiting to be torn loose, clinging to the ancient, booming bones of a world that is mostly sky.
