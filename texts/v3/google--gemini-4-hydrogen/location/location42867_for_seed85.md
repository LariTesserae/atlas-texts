High above an unseen abyssal floor, the spire rises to a sharp, unweathered crest, still completely drowned. There is no surface here—only kilometers of cool, heavy water pressing down from above, and kilometers more falling away below into a deeper dark. The place itself is small, bounded by the jagged rim of the peak’s summit, no wider than a modest hamlet or a sunken cathedral precinct. Beyond the rim, the rock drops away in sheer, freshly fractured planes.

The stone is young. It has the raw, conchoidal edges of basalt and volcanic glass split open yesterday, not a single ridge rounded by current or sediment. Where a terrace has been cut or a sheer wall left standing, the edges are razor-clean, catching no silt because there is no silt to catch. Nothing grows on it. Not a thread of algae, not a film of bacteria, not even the pale crust of mineral-eating slime that usually colonizes deep stone. The rock is as sterile and sharp as a newly broken blade.

Into this fractured crown, a sparse city has been built—or precipitated. It is unmistakably urban in its organization: layered tiers, terraced plazas, vertical shafts, and slender causeways linking one side of the peak to the other. Yet it is mostly empty space. Wide volumes of black water separate the structures, so that a lone tower or a cantilevered platform hangs in its own pocket of dark, balanced against the emptiness around it.

The architecture belongs to an engineering that leaves no seams, no fasteners, and no moving parts. Paper-thin planes of matte, light-drinking material extend out over the drop-offs for dozens of meters without thickness enough to bear their own weight, yet they do not flex. Hollow polyhedra and open, skeletal rings float moored to the basalt by filaments finer than hair, taut and motionless. There are no airlocks, no pressurized domes, no bubbles of trapped gas; whatever built this did not fight the water, it simply arranged it. Near the structures, the water is held in a state of quiet obedience. Currents that might drift across the outer ridge die out the moment they cross the perimeter. Temperature holds at a steady, cool equilibrium, neither warming near the walls nor chilling in the shadows.

Light is almost absent. If you look straight up, the water above is a solid, impenetrable black. The only illumination comes from the structures themselves, and even that is stingy: faint, cold pinpricks of bruised violet or pale slate-blue tucked into the joints of the masonry, glowing without warmth, barely bright enough to outline the sharp silhouettes of the tiers against the surrounding dark. Because the water carries no suspended dust, those few glimmers do not scatter into halos; they sit like tiny, cold needles in black glass.

The silence is near-total. There is no rush of water through the causeways, no creak of metal adjusting to pressure, no hum of machinery. If you wave a hand or kick a fin, the wake does not roll outward; it seems to lose its energy within a meter, damped into stillness by the same unseen geometry that keeps the towers upright. Only rarely does a sound break the quiet—a single, impossibly crisp *tick* from deep within a structural joint, or a low, sub-audible vibration felt in the sternum rather than the ears, gone before you can place its direction.

Life here is scarce and unhurried. Once in a long while, a pale, translucent ribbon—half-animal, half-geometric—drifts along the edge of a plaza, propelled by a ripple so slow it looks frozen. A few blind, jointed things with shells like frosted quartz cling to the sharpest basalt overhangs, motionless for days at a time, facing the faint violet nodes of the buildings as if drinking some invisible leak of energy rather than food.

Nothing shifts. No sediment settles on the terraces; no rust blooms on the filaments; no tide tugs at the hanging rings. The spire and its sparse, dark city sit in a permanent, cool pause—a sharp-edged monument locked into the water like an insect in clear, cold amber.
