From the rim of the basin, the valley looks less like a city and more like a vast, flooded switchyard left to weather in the sun. It sits high above the surrounding lowlands, a broad, stepped tableland of pale, porous stone that has been worn smooth and terraced over millennia. The air is warm, heavy, and perpetually damp—not with rain, though clouds occasionally snag on the upper ridges, but with the slow, steady weeping of the rock itself. Warm, mineral-rich water seeps from horizontal seams in the limestone shelves, slicking every flat surface, sheeting down stepped aprons in thin, gleaming films, and pooling in wide, shallow pans before draining away into subterranean fissures.

The light is clear, high-altitude daylight, bright and unhurried, glinting off acres of wet stone and catching the thin, lukewarm mist that rises from the basins. Across this wide, open expanse, the city is scattered in massive, deliberate clusters. It is unmistakably urban—built to a municipal, industrial scale, with numbered sectors, layered masonry, elevated viaducts, and a dense web of overhead power lines—yet the structures stand far apart, separated by broad stretches of empty, glistening rock and shallow settling flats.

What fills the space between the buildings is sound.

It is not the sharp clatter of a busy street or the frantic hammering of a forge, for nothing here moves quickly. Instead, the valley is saturated with a deafening, continuous roar—a physical weight that presses against the ribs and vibrates through the soles of the boots. Deep beneath the stone aprons, colossal hydraulic rams and slow-stroke turbines turn at two or three revolutions a minute, driven by the immense, patient pressure of the warm aquifers. Above ground, towering stacks of riveted iron and glazed ceramic bleed off excess vapor in steady, hours-long exhalations that hiss like tearing canvas.

Scattered among the pump-houses and settling tanks are the city’s strangest structures: vast, open-mouthed diffusers of cast concrete and perforated copper, angled toward the shallow pools. The industry here relies on vibration as much as heat or electricity. The diffusers project low, relentless standing waves across the wet flats—frequencies tuned to keep suspended minerals from caking inside the underground conduits, or to coax fine metallic silts out of the warm brine. Where the sound is loudest, the ankle-deep water in the basins does not ripple randomly; it locks into slow, geometric lattices of tiny, trembling ridges that hold their shape for hours at a time, shifting only as the sun warms the stone or a sluice-gate inches open a fraction of a turn.

Everything built here shows the weight of long use. The oldest layers are cyclopean blocks of travertine, pitted by centuries of warm mist and encrusted with pale, shelflike deposits of lime and ochre. Bolted directly into this ancient stonework are the additions of an electrified, industrial age: heavy cast-iron gear housings, verdigris-stained copper manifolds, steel gantry frames, and rows of thick porcelain insulators humming faintly beneath the roar of the pipes. Nothing looks newly finished, nor does anything look abandoned. The rust is oiled, the gaskets are tight, and the catwalks—strung high above the wet flats on slender iron piers—are swept clear of mineral crust, even though a person might walk a kilometer along them without meeting another soul.

Life here is sparse, beaten back by the mineral water and the punishing volume of the air. Only the toughest vegetation takes hold: leathery, olive-black mats of moss clinging to the leeward faces of the aqueducts, and pale, rubbery lichens spreading across the drier upper masonry. In the quietest corners, tucked behind the massive stone buttresses where the acoustic waves break and weaken, thin tufts of salt-hardy sedge manage to root in the silt. Animals are rarer still. Pale, flat-bodied amphibians and small, vibration-blind crustaceans shelter in the dark undersides of the warm settling pans, while far above, high-flying birds occasionally cross the bright sky along the basin’s rim, never descending into the roar below.

Down on the floor of the valley, change happens at the pace of sedimentation and pressure gauges. A counterweight descends the length of a brick tower over the course of an afternoon. A trickle of warm water slowly bridges a limestone lip, widening by a finger’s breadth over a season. The machines grind on in their slow, deafening equilibrium, and the vast, wet, half-empty city holds its breath and hums.
