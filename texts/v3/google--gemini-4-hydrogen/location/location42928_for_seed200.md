The basin lies low and wide, a flat expanse of newly cooled geology that has never known a river or a frost. Beneath the dim, motionless lid of the sky, the ground is all sharp-edged basalt, blistered slag, and broad plates of dark, vitrified stone—raw and unrounded, as if the crust finished hardening only days ago and was immediately claimed by everything that could crawl, root, or cling.

There is no soil here. Not even mud, in the usual sense. Where the stone dips into shallow, valley-wide depressions, warm mineral water seeps quietly from hairline fractures and sits in mirror-still, knee-deep sheets. Elsewhere, the rock rises in low, jagged shelves and collapsed bubbles, their ridges still razor-crisp. Yet the stone is barely visible. It is buried under a thick, suffocating pelt of life that sits directly on top of the glass without ever grinding it down.

The air is fiercely hot—a heavy, inescapable, oven-like heat that rises from the thin crust underfoot and presses down from the cloud-ceiling above. Nothing moves the air. There is no wind, no draft, no shift in pressure. The light is a permanent, bruised twilight: a dull, coppery-grey dimness filtered through miles of unbroken, low-hanging vapor. It never brightens into noon and never drops into true night. The heat, the dimness, and the wet are locked in a flat, windless equilibrium.

Because the air is saturated and the stone is cool only in the faintest relative sense, everything weeps. Water beads along the undersides of leaves, gathers on the backs of shells, and runs in thin, oily films down the unweathered faces of the rock. A slow, steady rain of condensation drips from the upper fronds into the warm shallows below, even though no storm ever forms.

The vegetation is lush, fleshy, and built for the gloom. Thick, waxy-stemmed plants with broad, dark-emerald and bruised-violet fronds anchor themselves into the microscopic pits of the raw stone with pale, grasping aerial roots. They do not grow tall into the dimness; instead, they spread outward in dense, overlapping shelves and low-ceilinged mats, forming tangled, waist-high jungles over the dry ridges and buoyant, root-woven carpets across the water. Fine, translucent hairs coat their stems, combing moisture straight out of the heavy air. Beneath their canopy, the light drops further into a murky, tea-colored dusk, where pale fungi and slick, gelatinous films coat every damp surface.

And everywhere, packed into every cubic foot of space, is the fauna.

With no winter, no dry season, and no storm to thin their numbers, animals have filled the basin to saturation. They are stacked in layers, near-touching, indifferent to one another except as food or perch. In the warm, root-choked shallows, broad, flat-headed amphibians and gill-frilled reptiles lie half-submerged, their mottled skin slick with mucus, their throats pulsing rhythmically as they gulp the thin, hot water. Over the exposed, glassy ridges, dense carpets of armored, many-legged scavengers click and jostle over one another, their pale undersides scraping against the sharp basalt.

The air between the fronds is thick with heavy-bodied beetles, wet-winged moths, and motionless columns of midges that hang like smoke above the pools. Every broad leaf sags under the weight of clinging snails, coiled tree-snakes, and clusters of motionless, slit-eyed lizards all facing the same direction along a warm seep. Nothing is wasted here, and nothing lingers to rot into dirt: the moment anything dies, the scavengers strip it clean before it can cool, leaving the bare, unweathered stone beneath as sterile and sharp as the day it formed.

The sound is a constant, heavy wall. It is not the roar of wind or rushing water, for neither exists here; it is purely biological and utterly unbroken. A deafening, layered chorus of stridulating wings, hollow, booming throat-sacs, wet slithering, and the dry, brittle percussion of thousands of carapaces tapping against volcanic glass. Beneath it all sits the soft, rhythmic *plip-plip-plip* of condensation falling into warm water, and the occasional, guttural sigh of something large shifting in the weeds.

Looking out across the basin, the landscape stretches flat and open for miles—a vast, steaming patchwork of dark green mats, glinting shallows, and jagged, unweathered rims barely visible through the grey-amber haze. Not a single frond stirs on its own. The light does not shift. The heat does not break. The whole teeming, deafening expanse hangs suspended in a single, endless, sweltering moment, endlessly repeating itself on top of stone that has barely begun to age.
