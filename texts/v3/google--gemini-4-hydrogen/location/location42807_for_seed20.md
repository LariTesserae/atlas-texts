High above the timberline, where the air ought to be thin and freezing, the mountain's broken crown holds a warm, drowned room.

It is not a sea, though you are completely submerged in it. The basin is no larger than the nave of a sunken cathedral, walled in by jagged, newly fractured cliffs of black obsidian and pale, glassy rhyolite. The rock is young—razor-edged, unweathered, still sharp enough to slice flesh at a brush—rising from a floor of raw tectonic rubble into a low, turbulent ceiling where storm-cloud and water churn together without a clean surface between them.

The water is bath-warm, heated from below by narrow, unseen fissures in the crust, and it never stills. Currents shear past one another in violent, conflicting sheets: a column of scalding, silt-thick water surges upward from a vent, colliding head-on with a cold, heavy downdraft of rain-fed brine pouring through the fractured roof. Between them, pockets of trapped air—the size of fists, the size of boulders—scream upward, flattening and tearing apart before they can reach the top.

Light barely enters here. What little filters down through the bruised ceiling is a dim, murky amber, quickly swallowed by suspended ash and swirling sediment. In the deeper corners of the basin, the only illumination is a dull, intermittent pulse of heat-glow from the cracks in the floor, turning the sharp edges of the basalt briefly copper-colored before the murk closes back in.

And the sound is absolute. Underwater, there is no distance to soften it. The deafening, continuous roar of boiling vents, grinding stone, and collapsing air-pockets fills the basin until the water itself feels thick with vibration. It is not something heard through the ears; it hammers against the sternum, rattles the teeth, and blurs vision. Because the obsidian walls are so close and so freshly cleaved, the noise cannot escape. Echoes pile atop echoes, folding back on themselves in a permanent, concussive thunder that makes it impossible to tell whether a crash came from directly behind you or from the far side of the chamber.

Within this tight, roaring space, the rules of matter have begun to fray.

Gravity here is hesitant and local. A cluster of sharp gravel, knocked loose from a ledge, will sink halfway to the floor, pause in a trembling knot, and then drift sideways as if pulled by a wall. Shadows detach from the jagged spires and swim slowly against the current, lagging a second behind the rocks that cast them. Now and then, the fracture that split this peak open seems to stutter and repeat: a massive, needle-thin slab of black glass shears away from the cliff face, tumbles in slow, silent-looking collapse through the warm murk, dissolves into a cloud of pale silt—and snaps back onto the cliff, whole and vibrating, as its delayed shockwave finally slams into your ribs.

Almost nothing can take root in a place this restless. Only in the narrow, sheltered crevices behind boulders—where the current splits and leaves a thin seam of relative calm—does anything grow. Sparse ribbons of pale, rubbery kelp cling to the raw stone, along with tufts of translucent, hair-like lichen that sway violently in the backwash. They are torn loose constantly, shredded by flying grit, and regrow in pale, stubby knots.

Fauna is rarer still, limited to a few blind, heavy-shelled drifters—pale things shaped like flattened nautiluses or armored eels—that hug the bottom rubble. They have no eyes to be tricked by the failing light or the stuttering reflections; they navigate entirely by the deafening pressure waves, riding the violent updrafts like birds in a gale, vanishing into the silt whenever a thermal vent blows open.

Scattered among the raw talus are faint, inexplicable traces of habitation—not ruins, and nothing made with metal or tools, but small, deliberate arrangements pressed into the stone by bare hands. In the lee of a jagged overhang, someone has stacked a cairn of razor-edged basalt shards; by all rights the shearing currents should scatter it in seconds, yet it stands, trembling slightly, while pebbles orbit its peak. Elsewhere, pale, calcified bones and thick shells have been wedged tightly into cracks in the obsidian to form crude, hand-sized grips, as if something once clung there to keep from being swept into the boiling dark. Half-moon hollows, worn smooth by persistent scraping, mark a few of the lower ledges—the only dull, rounded surfaces in a room of fresh blades.

Nothing here lasts long enough to erode, yet nothing ever quite finishes happening. The basin remains locked in its warm, deafening, dim upheaval: a small, high-altitude wound in the world, full of water and noise, endlessly breaking itself open.
