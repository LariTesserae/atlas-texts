From the outside, there is nothing to mark it—only a low, sun-blistered shelf of pale gypsum and salt-crust, indistinguishable from the surrounding flats. You only find it if you feel the ground humming through the soles of your sandals, or notice the narrow, jagged lip where the crust has buckled upward like a healed scar.

Step down through that lip, and the world drops away into a single, hidden room.

It is a vast, hollow blister in the earth—perhaps three hundred paces across, domed over by a thin, warped ceiling of translucent selenite. The desert sun strikes that crystalline roof and scatters downward in a white, shadowless glare. The light is fierce and dry, bouncing off pale mineral walls until the air itself seems to glow like heated milk. There is no water here. No pools, no seep, no dampness in the crevices, not even the memory of mud. Everything you touch is chalk-dry, powdery, or polished to a slick, waxy sheen by centuries of wind-borne grit.

And it is deafening.

The sound hits before your eyes can adjust to the brightness—a physical, chest-rattling roar that fills the entire hollow. The chamber is an accidental instrument. High in the northern rim, a series of eroded wind-throats catch the fierce exterior gales and funnel them into the dome, turning the whole pocket into a giant’s whistle. It is not a single tone, but a thick, layered chord of pressure: a low, tectonic groan that vibrates in your molars, overlaid by a screaming, metallic hiss where the air shears past sharp gypsum fins. You cannot hear yourself shout. You cannot hear your own footsteps on the stone.

Yet the central space of this roaring vault is astonishingly empty. Between the far-flung walls, the air is a vast, hollow column of light and noise, unbroken by pillars or hanging bridges. Nothing lingers in the middle; the standing waves of sound are so violent there that dust devils twist apart before they can form.

All the life, all the weight, all the intricate clutter of the place clings to the perimeter.

Along the curved, eroded ribs of the walls, a city has been built—layered six, sometimes seven tiers deep into the chalky stone. It is an ancient, settled architecture, made entirely by hand with bone chisels, flint scrapers, and soft copper pins. There are no cranes, no mortar-mixers, no iron. Instead, there is a staggering density of small, patient craft: narrow terraces cut directly into the gypsum, retaining walls of dry-stacked salt-brick, and hundreds of tiny, rounded chambers plastered with a pale paste of crushed shell and plant-wax. Stairways no wider than a shoulder zig-zag up the cliff-face, linked by tightrope catwalks of braided fiber.

And everywhere, spilling over the ledges, crowding the balconies, and choking the narrow alleys between dwellings, grows the flora.

Without a drop of water, the walls are lush. The plants here have abandoned liquid altogether, running on a slow, dry chemistry that drinks mineral dust, morning heat, and the volatile oils sweating from the deep rock. Thick mats of papery, amber-colored lichen velvet every horizontal surface, soft and dry as old parchment. From the cracks burst towering clusters of hollow, honeycombed stalks—bone-white and brittle as porcelain—topped with wide, translucent leaves like stretched bladder-skin. When the sunlight hits them, they glow green-gold, veining the white walls with shadow.

Because the air never stops vibrating, the vegetation is part of the noise. Millions of dry seed-pods rattle in continuous, frantic unison. The hollow stalks catch the chamber’s drone and whistle back in shifting harmonies, while broad, taut membranes of resin-leaf buzz like snare drums. The city doesn't just sit inside the sound; it is overgrown with instruments that play it louder.

In the rare quiet pockets—tiny, windowless alcoves carved deep behind double walls of packed pith and woven matting—the roaring drops to a dull, bearable thrum. That is where the inhabitants sleep and work their copper wire. Outside, in the blinding, shrieking alleys, no one speaks. They communicate with sharp, practiced hand-signs, or by tugging taut sinew-lines strung along the handrails like bell-pulls, sending sharp physical pulses through the railings to warn of a climber coming down.

Animals are rare here, and strange. You might go days without seeing one, until you spot a papery, translucent glider—flat as a dried leaf and blind as a worm—riding the upward drafts along the wall, navigating entirely by the vibration of its own stiff wings against the standing waves. Deep in the dry, fibrous roots of the amber-lichen, armored salt-ticks the size of thumbnails click slowly through the dust, grazing on shed plant-wax.

Nothing rots in this warmth; nothing rusts. A dropped basket or a snapped reed simply dries, bleaches, and slowly frays into fine white powder, worn down by the endless, invisible friction of the sound.

The place has its own fierce, daily pulse. As the sun climbs and bakes the selenite roof, the dome expands with sharp, pistol-crack snaps that cut even through the wind-roar. The warming air shifts the pitch of the chamber upward—from dawn’s hollow, belly-deep bass to noon’s blinding, white-hot shriek. In that peak heat, the bladder-plants pop open their dry husks all at once, showering the empty central void with millions of weightless, glittering chaff-scales that spin and dance in the acoustic currents like snow that never melts, before settling softly onto the terraces below.

Then, as evening cools the crust outside, the wind-throats slacken. The pitch drops back down into the bones of the rock, the rattling pods slow to a dry rustle, and the hidden city—bright with the last amber light filtering through the ceiling—breathes out a long, low, deafening hum into the dark.
