The basin is no wider than a small town’s market square, walled in by sheer, dressed basalt and poured-concrete retaining bulkheads, and roofed over by a dark, vaulted ceiling of collapsed brickwork and bedrock fifty meters below the surface. Every cubic meter of it is full. There is no open water here—only a dense, three-dimensional tangle of submerged brick workshops, iron gantries, bundled copper conduits, and warm-water vegetation, all suspended in bath-warm, mineral-heavy stillness.

You enter through a flooded masonry culvert, and the first thing that meets you is the temperature. The water is thirty-two degrees Celsius, thick with dissolved salts and fine iron-oxide, carrying the faint, sweetish tang of steeped peat and hot transformer oil. It does not swirl or surge. It hangs in lazy thermal layers—cooler near the dark vault above, fever-warm along the floor grates where submerged geothermal manifolds bleed heat into the basin.

Because the place is small and bounded, the buildings were stacked tight before the water ever rose—or perhaps they were always meant to be drowned, built with thick pressure-glass windows, bolted cast-iron hatches, and external catwalks that now serve only as anchor points. Narrow alleys, barely wide enough for two people to pass abreast, wind between four-story brick foundries and corrugated-iron pump-houses. Across these alleys, overhead pipes, braided power cables, and iron-link conveyor chains are strung so thickly that you cannot swim straight up without threading your way through a lattice of metal and stem.

Nothing here is rushing, and nothing is breaking. The rust has long since stopped flaking; it has settled into a hard, matte-black crust of magnetite that seals the iron beams against the water. The mortar between the bricks is recessed and velveted with pale lime deposits. Everything has reached an old, heavy equilibrium.

What light exists is dim and amber-tinted, bruised by the murky water. There is no sun. A few street-level indicator lamps still burn inside thick, cage-protected glass globes, their filaments reduced to dull orange threads by voltage drop and age. Through the grimy portholes of the larger factory halls, you can see the slow, rhythmic pulse of submerged electrolytic vats—faint, sickly-green arcs of electricity passing between lead plates in sealed oil-baths, illuminating clouds of suspended silt that hang motionless in the glow. Lower down, where the vegetation thins around the intake grates, pale bacterial films cast a weak, chalky luminescence over the brass valves.

The plants have taken every surface the machines do not actively scrape clean. This is not the sparse, brittle flora of the cold deep, but a lush, soft-tissued jungle adapted to perpetual warmth and dimness. Thick, olive-drab mats of water-moss upholster the roofs of the workshops. Long, fibrous vines with translucent, gas-filled bladders trail from the overhead busbars like beaded curtains, swaying only when a slow thermal plume rises from below. Broad, leathery fronds of submerged fern root directly into the silt-filled gutters and the cracks of brick chimneys, their leaves coated in a fine dusting of precipitated calcium. In the alleys, the vegetation and the infrastructure have grown so tightly together that it is hard to tell where a wrapped cable ends and a creeping rhizome begins.

And the machines are still working. Not with the clatter of dry-land industry, but with an eerie, hydraulic patience.

In the center of the basin stands a three-story stamping house, its walls bristling with copper cooling fins. Inside, visible through a cracked observation slit, a massive iron flywheel turns at a rate of one revolution every four minutes, driven by the slow pressure differential of the warm vents below. Beside it, an automated winch-chain drags a line of perforated brass baskets along a silt-choked track—moving a millimeter every few seconds, pausing, clicking softly, then moving again. Nobody collects what the baskets carry. At the end of the line, neat, heavy ingots of extruded mineral wax and grey, rubbery resin pile up in the mud, half-buried by silt and already colonized by pale water-ferns.

The silence is almost complete, broken only by sounds that travel better through bone than through water. If you press your palm against a heavy conduit, you feel a low, steady sixty-hertz thrum—the quiet, tireless heartbeat of the submerged grid. Every few minutes, a distant thermal valve lets out a single, muffled *tink* as metal expands, or a pocket of marsh gas detaches itself from a rotting mat of moss and rises with a soft, hollow *blup* toward the dark ceiling.

Animal life is scarce enough that any movement feels like an intrusion. A few blind, chalk-white crayfish, each as long as a forearm, cling to the warm porcelain insulators on the transformer shacks, their antennae sweeping the sluggish current with agonizing slowness. Once in a long while, a pale, ribbon-like eel threads out of a flooded sluice-pipe, lingers near the orange glow of an induction coil, and vanishes back into the dense, warm thicket of iron and kelp.

Everything here operates on a clock measured in hours and decades rather than seconds. Silt settles at the pace of falling snow in a windless room. Electricity leaks quietly into the brine, warming the roots of the bladder-vines. The factories go on pressing, plating, and extruding into the dim, crowded water, in a town with no sky, no dry ground, and no one left to turn the switches off.
