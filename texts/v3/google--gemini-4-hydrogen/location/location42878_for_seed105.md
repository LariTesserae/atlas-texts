The valley sits high above the true abyss, a broad, drowned shelf of dark slate and pale limestone submerged beneath a cold, heavy sea. To look out across it—as far as the bruised, near-black water allows—is to see a wide, open basin where the mountain ridges have been worn down into smooth, rounded shoulders, and the floor stretches away in long, gently sloping terraces of gravel and silt.

It is very cold here, the kind of cold that feels thick and deliberate, as if the water itself is close to stiffening into ice. Light barely reaches this depth. Above, whatever sky exists is lost behind fathoms of liquid ink; down on the plateau, the world holds only a bruised, indigo-grey darkness, pierced here and there by the faintest, ghostly sheen of pale green-white luminescence clinging to the undersides of leaves. You cannot see the far walls of the valley, only the nearest swells of stone and the looming silhouettes of things rising from the silt before they dissolve back into the murk.

Yet for all its darkness, the place is loud.

Water moves across this high plateau with the force of a subterranean river. Deep, frigid currents sweep in from the outer ocean, funneling through the ancient notches of the drowned ridges and roaring across the open flats. Where the current hits the lip of a terrace, it tumbles over in a heavy, invisible cataract, churning up curtains of fine mineral grit that hiss and rattle against the stone. Closer to the ground, the surge rolls fist-sized cobbles back and forth in the troughs, a constant, low, grinding thunder that vibrates through the soles of anything resting on the bottom.

Nothing swims through this noise. There are no fish darting in the eddies, no crabs picking at the gravel, no barnacles crusting the rocks, not even a worm threading the silt. Animal life simply does not exist here. The rocks are bare of shells; the water is empty of eyes and darting shadows.

Instead, the valley belongs to a strange, restless vegetation that has grown lush and opulent in the absence of anything to graze it. These plants do not carpet the ground evenly; they gather in towering, tangled islands separated by wide, barren expanses of swept stone and pale mud. In the dark, a grove appears suddenly—a dense, cathedral-like thicket of pale-olive, ink-black, and bruised-violet growth, swaying violently in the current.

Because there are no animals to carry their seeds or stir the water around them, the plants have become loud and mechanical. Great, hollow-stemmed organ-reeds rise thirty feet from the silt, rooted in knots of tough, fibrous tubers; as the cold current pours across their notched mouths, they groan and hum in deep, resonant chords that carry for miles through the dense water. Beside them grow the bellows-kelp—massive, leathery fronds anchored to swollen, bladder-like chambers. Slowly, over the course of an hour, a chamber draws in water and builds internal metabolic gas until its skin stretches tight as a drum; then, with a sudden, percussive *thump* that shakes the surrounding silt, it vents a towering plume of cold bubbles and pale spores into the roaring current. Smaller, brittle-leafed glass-ferns grow in the lee of the larger stems, their calcified edges clattering together like dry bones whenever a surge passes through.

Between these loud, pulsing groves, the open terrain holds the scattered, weathered marks of a people who built here long before the water swallowed the basin—or perhaps during some forgotten, brief era when the shelf stood dry. There are no cities or grand monuments, only small, practical things left to the slow work of the currents. Low dry-stone walls of stacked slate snake across the bare gravel, their sharp edges long since smoothed into rounded, river-worn forms. Here and there stands a single-room shelter, its corbelled stone roof caved inward, its doorway choked with drifting silt and a single, massive cluster of humming reeds.

In the lee of a half-buried boundary stone, an occasional artifact surfaces as the gravel shifts: a heavy, notched flint weight still bound with a petrified collar of braided plant-fiber; a small, hammered-copper awl, blackened and pitted by centuries of cold water; the smooth, carved bowl of a stone mortar wedged firmly between two roots. Everything is in balance with the erosion now. The stone does not crumble; the metal does not flake away quickly in the frigid, oxygen-poor dark. They simply sit, slowly being draped in thin, velvet mats of cold-water moss that glow with a faint, dead-starlight shimmer.

The rhythm of the valley is entirely its own—a cycle of cold tides, grinding stone, and botanical hydraulics. A spore-pod detonates in the dark with a muffled boom; a distant reed-grove answers with a long, shifting chord as the current turns; a cloud of pale, luminous dust drifts across a sunken slate threshold and vanishes over the rim of the plateau, falling toward the silent, deeper abyss below.
