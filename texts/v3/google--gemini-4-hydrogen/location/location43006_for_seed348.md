The basin sits low in the world, a flat-bottomed depression no wider than a small town, ringed by sheer, stepped walls of pale grey slate. Above it hangs an even, shadowless daylight—not bright enough to dazzle, not dim enough to soften—filtered through a permanent, featureless ceiling of high cloud. The air is cool and clammy, holding the steady, brisk chill of an unheated cellar, and it smells sharply of wet iron, ozone, and boiled stone.

There is no green here. Not a blade of grass, not a thread of moss in the mortar, not even a stain of lichen on the slate. Everything that is not mineral or metal is flesh, shell, or chitin.

The floor of the basin is a vast, sparse yard of worn flagstones and compacted dark slag, laid out with the deliberate geometry of an old municipal district. Wide, empty plazas stretch between the structures, their surfaces slick with a thin film of condensation. Shallow, hand-cut drainage channels cross the pavement in straight lines, carrying trickles of lukewarm, mineral-heavy water toward iron grates that never overflow and never run dry. Centuries of dampness have rounded the edges of the curbstones and pitted the concrete into smooth, settled contours; the whole place has the look of something that finished weathering a long time ago and now simply persists.

Set far apart across this open expanse stand the buildings—seven or eight massive, windowless blocks of poured concrete and riveted iron, connected high overhead by a sparse web of steel catwalks and insulated power conduits. They are unmistakably urban, stamped with faded stencil numbers and lined with dead-end access stairs and humming arc-lamps, yet they feel like monuments left behind in an empty courtyard. Inside and along their flanks, heavy machinery runs without pause. Huge copper-wound dynamos whine behind slatted vents; exposed flywheels turn at a steady, hypnotic pace; tall ceramic insulators crackle with blue-white static; and reciprocating pistons drive plumes of cool, damp vapor out through corroded brass manifolds. The vapor drifts a few yards, chills in the air, and settles back onto the flagstones as a fine mist, completing a circuit that requires nothing from outside the slate walls.

Between these sparse, roaring blocks, the emptiness is entirely full of animals.

They are everywhere, stacked in strata from the wet stone to the tops of the smokestacks, yet they have no plants to eat and no soil to burrow into. Instead, the fauna has locked itself into the machinery's metabolism.

The air in the wide plazas is thick with flyers—palm-sized, translucent creatures with four stiff, parchment-like wings and long, trailing tails like silvery filaments. They never seem to land. Caught in the steady thermal updrafts rising from the transformer vents, thousands of them wheel in tight, predictable figure-eight currents, passing close enough to brush a person’s face with a dry, papery snap. As they fly, air rushes through rows of tiny spiracles along their ribs, producing a high, reedy whistling that layers over the bass drone of the turbines.

Below them, the concrete walls and the undersides of the overhead pipe-bridges are carpeted with dense, overlapping clusters of pale, fist-sized shell-bearers. They look like a cross between limpets and squat, segmented crabs, clamped fast to the damp masonry by broad muscular feet. They graze endlessly on the chalky white mineral scale precipitated by the condensing steam, rasping at the concrete with toothed tongues. Every few seconds, as if triggered by a tremor in the pipes, whole patches of them snap their hinged shells shut in unison—a dry, percussive clatter that ripples across a building face like hail on tin.

On the flagstones themselves, the open ground crawls with scavengers: long-legged, blind, porcelain-white things with articulated, needle-thin limbs and flat, shovel-shaped heads. They move in wide, orderly lanes around the bases of the pylons, keeping clear of the reciprocating rods and the hot exhaust grates. They sweep the wet stone for fallen flyers, crushed shells, and oily grit, their mandibles clicking softly as they sift the damp film. When one dies, the others strip it clean within minutes, leaving only a hollow carapace that the condensation gradually softens and washes into the nearest drain.

The noise is immense and relentless. The deep, chest-rattling thrum of the electrified engines, the sharp hiss of the steam manifolds, the high whistling of the wheeling swarms, and the constant, rattling click of a million shells against concrete blend into a single, wall-to-wall roar that bounces off the surrounding slate cliffs. Yet for all its fury, the sound never swells or fades. There is no morning chorus, no evening quiet, no sudden panic scattering the flocks.

Nothing here changes. The dynamos do not wear down, because the oil and water cycle through them in a closed, self-balancing loop; the concrete does not crumble further, because the mineral scale left by the steam replaces what the limpets scrape away. The populations of flyers, grazers, and scavengers neither boom nor crash, held in an exact, unnatural equilibrium by the fixed output of heat and dampness from the machines. Even the wind is merely the breath of the vents, blowing in the same direction at the same speed forever—a loud, crowded, damp machine-yard frozen at the exact point of its own balance.
