You drop down into the basin from a rim of raw, unweathered limestone—or what looks like limestone until you press a heel into it and feel the dry, fibrous give of calcified pith. There is no soil here. Below the rim, stretching across a shallow valley perhaps four miles wide, lies a city with no builders, no tools, and no inhabitants.

It is entirely green, bone-white, and bruised violet, and it is screaming.

The sound hits first, rising out of the low basin like heat off asphalt: a continuous, deafening roar of hydraulic pressure, splintering timber, and wet snapping. With no animals to rustle in the brush and no machinery to hum, the noise is purely vegetative. Thousands of hollow, reed-like towers draw sap upward at such violent pressures that their walls whistle like organ pipes. Every few seconds, somewhere in the dense tangle below, a load-bearing arch of braided fig-wood fails under its own rapid growth, cracking with the report of a felled mast, followed by the wet, tearing hiss of a hundred vines instantly surging to bridge the gap.

From the rim, the valley looks unmistakably urban. There are avenues—straight, sharp-edged thoroughfares thirty feet wide, paved in interlocking plates of dry, corky bark. There are districts: a low, sprawling grid of boxy, windowless chambers formed from fused agave-flesh; a tighter cluster of spires where pale, waxy trunks spiral around one another into buttressed minarets; a sunken plaza ringed by tiered galleries of shelf-fungus. Nothing here has been carved, mortared, or tied. No hand has ever touched it. The vegetation simply *grows* in right angles, lintels, staircases, and drainage channels, as though the flora itself has contracted urbanism like a fever.

The air down in the streets is warm and thick with the smell of crushed celery, hot resin, and ozone. Despite the lushness—every surface, every wall, every overhead span is living or freshly dead plant matter—the place is surprisingly dry. Water is scarce, hoarded inside the architecture. The bark-pavements are dusty in the bright, steady daylight, cracked into sharp, young polygons that haven't had time to wear smooth. Moisture appears only in frantic, localized pockets: condensation beading along the undersides of broad, translucent leaf-awnings; shallow, sharp-rimmed cisterns grown from cupped lily-pads where tepid water collects and immediately steams; the sudden, brief misting when an over-pressurized tuber bursts in a wall, drenching a ten-foot radius before the neighboring mosses drink it dry in seconds.

Walk a hundred yards down one of the avenues, and the geometry begins to slip.

You pass through a colonnade of fluted, celery-green pillars, each spaced with rigid mathematical precision. You count twelve pillars on your left, twelve on your right. When you glance back, there are nineteen, and the avenue behind you has pinched shut into a narrow, dead-end alley choked with thorny latticework. It didn't grow slowly; it *shifted* while your eyes were forward, the whole block sliding sideways on lubricated root-skids with a low, seismic groan that vibrates through the soles of your boots.

This is not growth as a slow, seasonal patience. It is a turbulent, hyperactive churn of creation and demolition. A wall beside you ripples—not in the wind, for the air in the tight alleys is dead calm, but from the inside. Pale, knuckle-thick shoots punch through the dry outer bark like fingers through paper, thicken to the size of forearms in the span of three breaths, fuse into a lintel, and immediately begin flaking into dry, papery scales at their outer edges. Twenty feet away, an entire three-story pavilion of woven bamboo-grasses sags, turns black with sudden rot, and collapses inward into a heap of dry chaff, which is instantly punctured and lifted by a fresh grid of rigid, square-stemmed shoots.

Because the place is so young—barely emerged, its edges still razor-sharp and unrounded—nothing has settled into equilibrium. The reality of the valley feels thin, as if the space itself is struggling to contain the sheer density of what is happening inside it. Light falls normally from a clear sky, yet the shadows cast by the leaf-towers sometimes detach slightly at the base, pooling darker than they should, or trembling a fraction of a second before the branch above them moves. In the denser quarters, where layered bridges of vine cross and recross six stories above the street, distance stops behaving cleanly. A courtyard you can see clearly through an archway—bright, dusty, empty—takes twenty minutes of brisk walking down a straight corridor to reach, and when you step into it, the archway you entered through is high above you, looking down like a balcony.

There are no birds in the rafters. No beetles in the bark-cracks. Not a single ant patrols the sticky, sap-glazed joints of the balustrades. The total absence of animal life makes the frantic motion of the city deeply unsettling—a bazaar with no merchants, an anthill made entirely of the hill itself. Without insects to pollinate or herbivores to prune, the plants are locked in a closed, cannibalistic loop of competition and mimicry. Some structures seem to be imitating things that have never existed in this valley: you find a row of tall, hollow gourds perforated with neat, thumb-sized holes arranged in rows of four, humming a discordant, deafening chord as hot air rises through them. You round a corner and confront a vast, tiered amphitheater grown from overlapping cabbage-thick leaves, its steps steep and sharp-edged, facing an empty pit where three thick white roots knot and unknot in the dust like wrestling limbs.

By midday, the heat and the noise peak together. The sun beats down into the low basin, baking the outer crusts of the plant-towers until they pop and crackle like cooling iron. Moisture sucked up from deep beneath the valley floor is pumped furiously through the living infrastructure, misting out through microscopic pores in the upper canopies to form a thin, shimmering haze just above the rooftops, which condenses and drips down the exterior walls in sparse, warm trickles.

Nothing stays put long enough to map. By evening, the avenue you walked in on will have been digested into a terrace of dry, pale moss, and a new district of sharp-cornered, whistling towers will have unfolded in its place—loud, warm, bone-dry on the skin, and endlessly busy with the business of being a city for no one.
