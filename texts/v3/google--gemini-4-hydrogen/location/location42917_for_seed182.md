The whole place fits inside a single fractured pocket of rock, no wider than a city square and roofed over by a jagged ceiling of newly split basalt. Because the cavity is young—opened by some recent subterranean shift—the stone faces are still razor-edged and raw, unsoftened by millennia of seepage. Into this bounded, sweltering hollow, a dense tiered settlement of brick, cast iron, and living root has been packed until there is scarcely an inch of empty air left.

It is oppressively hot. The air hangs thick and motionless, carrying the smell of wet mortar, warm copper, and crushed, resinous leaves. There are no streams here and no rain, yet everything is slick with dampness. Moisture rises from deep thermal fissures beneath the lowest floor, condenses against the cool, high stone of the ceiling, and gathers in beads along the undersides of overhead pipes. Every few seconds, a single heavy drop falls—tinkling into a ceramic catch-basin, or slapping softly against a broad, waxy leaf.

Light is scarce and amber-tinted. Low-wattage filament bulbs, caged in brass wire, hang at irregular intervals along the catwalks, casting pools of dim orange that barely penetrate the tangles of foliage and the stacked ironwork. Further down, behind thick mica windows in the boiler-casings, heat-exchangers glow a dull, steady cherry-red. Everything else is warm shadow.

The architecture is urban and layered, built vertically in five or six cramped tiers of narrow walkways, riveted gantries, and brick-archedalcoves. You cannot walk three paces without ducking under a lagged steam-main or squeezing past a row of glass-fronted pressure gauges. Yet the machinery here does not roar. It is an industry of immense patience. Massive, grease-darkened flywheels turn at the speed of clock-hands; hydraulic presses advance a fraction of an inch an hour, slowly squeezing dark mineral-cakes or pulping thick-stemmed tubers; electrolytic vats hum with a low, muffled vibration, their copper busbars furred with verdigris and damp moss. The only sharp sounds are the occasional *tick* of expanding iron and the soft, brief sigh of a relief valve bleeding a wisp of steam into the leaves.

And the leaves are everywhere, braided into the infrastructure so tightly that it is hard to tell where the factory ends and the conservatory begins. Lush, heat-drunk vegetation spills from brick planter-troughs, hangs in wire baskets from the catwalks, and roots directly into the slag-wool insulation around the boilers. These are strange, subterranean varieties—broad-fronded ferns the color of bruised plums, pale succulents with translucent skins that drink the condensation off the iron railings, and thick, fibrous vines that seem drawn to the electric conduits, wrapping their tendrils around ceramic insulators as if feeding on the faint warmth of the current. Some of the broad leaves curl slowly toward the amber bulbs over the course of hours, tracking the dim light with glacial deliberation.

Animals are almost absent. Now and then, a blind, chalk-white millipede threads its way along the rim of a condensation pan, or a small, heat-tolerant beetle sits motionless on a warm transformer casing, its carapace beaded with sweat. Otherwise, nothing scurries or calls.

The place operates on the timescale of rising sap and slow thermal drift. Pipes weep into troughs; troughs feed roots; roots split mortar and brace the old ironwork; and deep in the dim, packed heat, the quiet engines keep turning, millimeter by millimeter, holding the whole tangled pocket in a state of heavy, breathless equilibrium.
