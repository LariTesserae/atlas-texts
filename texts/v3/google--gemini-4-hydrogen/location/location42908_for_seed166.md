Fifteen feet below the surface, the water holds a steady, uniform chill. It is clear enough that normal daylight reaches the bottom without much loss, though the blue-green shift of the water flattens the reds and leaves everything—stone, iron, copper, and weed—in cool, muted tones. There is no surface chop down here, no visible current, and no sign of the sky above except for a faint, pale brightness overhead that stays nearly constant through the day.

The place is a bounded, natural limestone basin, no larger than a small village square or the floor of a modest church. Its walls are stepped shelves of pale gray rock, worn smooth over centuries by water that no longer moves. The floor is flat, dusted with a thin, settled layer of fine mineral silt that has not been stirred in years. Nothing here is built in the architectural sense—no walls, no pilings, no concrete pads, no walkways. The rock is simply itself, and whatever else is here rests directly on the stone.

Scattered across the basin floor, with wide stretches of bare limestone between them, are heavy, un-housed pieces of industrial machinery. They sit on bolted steel skids anchored straight into the bedrock: cylindrical cast-iron pump housings, banks of finned transformer radiators, thick armored power cables snaking across the silt like black roots, and wide racks of dull copper busbars and sacrificial zinc plates. None of it is enclosed. The machinery is bare to the water, built to operate submerged, cooled directly by the cold lake around it. 

Most of the equipment is long-settled. A thin, even crust of pale calcium carbonate has grown over the bolt heads and along the seams of the iron castings, and the copper has darkened to a flat, velvety brown. Yet the array is clearly live. Heavy-gauge conduits lead out over the northern rock lip toward some unseen shore station, and a few glass-faced pressure gauges mounted on the manifolds still show steady, unmoving needles. If you press an ear close to one of the transformer housings, you can hear—or feel in the bones of the skull—a faint, continuous sixty-hertz hum, completely steady, never rising or falling. Otherwise, the basin is quiet. Water absorbs almost everything; there is no clank of valves, no rush of intake, no bubbling exhaust. Whatever work these machines are doing—electrolysis, steady-state filtration, silent pumping—happens at an unvarying, invisible pace.

Life here is minimal and entirely plant. No fish pass through the basin; there are no snails on the limestone, no insect larvae in the silt, no crustaceans under the steel skids. The water is too cold, too still, and perhaps too faintly charged with dissolved metals from the anode racks to support them. Only sparse tufts of dark-green aquatic moss cling to the rougher edges of the iron housings, and thin, pale stalks of stonewort grow in scattered patches where silt has collected in the rock fractures. They do not sway, because the water does not move.

Everything in the basin has reached a quiet, permanent balance. The silt lies level around the base of the cables. The light shifts only slightly from morning to afternoon before fading, and the temperature never varies by more than a degree. The machinery hums at its fixed load, the stonewort holds its place in the cracks, and the cold, clear water holds them both in the same suspended stillness day after day.
