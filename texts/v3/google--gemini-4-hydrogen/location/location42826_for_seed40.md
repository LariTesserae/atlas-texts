The basin is high enough that the air feels thin and sharp in the lungs, cold enough that your breath would plume if the light weren't so blindingly bright it seems to bleach the moisture right out of it. There is no dirt here, no bedrock, no grass-covered slope. From rim to rim, across a valley-sized expanse, the entire terrain is fabricated—a dense, multi-tiered tangle of brushed aluminum plates, white vitrified ceramic, and dark composite decking, all locked together with edges so crisp they look as if they were milled this morning.

Nothing has had time to weather. The chamfers on the steel I-beams are razor-sharp; the stamped serial numbers along the pipe-flanges are unblurred by rust or grit; the weld-seams gleam like fresh solder. Even the vast cooling towers along the northern ridge—hundreds of them, packed shoulder to shoulder—show no water-staining, only a pristine, chalk-white glare under a sky that is a flat, overexposed silver.

Because the basin is both open and intensely crowded, your eye never rests on an empty horizon. Instead, you look *through* layers. Catwalks cross above conveyor troughs, which thread beneath banks of oiled transformers, which sit perched on gantries straddling deep, narrow shafts of other machinery. Everything is near-touching, separated by inches of clearance, forming a three-dimensional lattice of industrial intent. Yet for all this mechanical density, the place is almost entirely quiet.

There is no roar of combustion, no clatter of stamp-mills. The engines here work on a different patience. A flywheel the size of a city block hangs suspended between two ceramic pylons, turning so slowly you only know it moves because a chalk mark on its rim has shifted a hand's breadth since you looked away. Pistons the thickness of tree trunks ease down into their sleeves over the course of an hour, without a hiss of steam. The only steady sound is a faint, dry static prickle from the overhead busbars, punctuated now and then by the sharp *tick* of cooling metal or the solitary *plink* of a water drop hitting a dry tray.

Water is scarce and strictly localized. In the cool shade beneath the heat exchangers and inside the slotted expansion joints of the decking, moisture condenses out of the thin air, beading along the undersides of copper manifolds and trickling into narrow, machine-cut gutters. Those damp seams are the only places where plants take hold. Sparse tufts of silver-wire moss and flat, waxy-leaved lichens wedge themselves into the bolt-holes and the wet mortar of the ceramic piers, drinking the metallic runoff. A few stunted, pale-stemmed shrubs grow in the condensation traps at the base of the substations, their leaves angled upward like tiny dishes to catch the noon glare.

The animals, by contrast, are everywhere. Despite the cold and the lack of soil, the basin is thick with life, adapted to the tight clearances and the high-altitude brightness. Pale, long-toed climbers with fur like spun fiberglass crowd the undersides of the overhead cable trays, hanging in silent clusters to stay out of the direct glare. Flocks of slender, four-winged things—translucent-feathered and completely mute—roost along the porcelain insulator strings, darting down in quick, synchronized drops to sip from the condensation gutters before flitting back up into the scaffolding. Down in the deeper, dimmer troughs between the generator housings, armored, multi-legged grazers scrape the silver moss from the rivets with quiet, rhythmic precision. None of them make a sound; they move through the dense ironwork with the practiced ease of things born inside a watch.

Spend more than a few minutes watching them, though, and the architecture begins to betray its own geometry.

A flock of the four-winged birds drops toward a puddle in a steel drainage channel, and half of them vanish *into* the reflection—not splashing, simply continuing downward into an inverted sky that seems deeper than the inch-thick plate beneath it. Up on the third-tier catwalk, a gantry crane glides silently along its rail, passes behind a vertical cooling duct, and emerges on the other side *smaller* than it went in, though the duct itself is only three feet wide. If you walk the length of that same catwalk, your shadow falls ahead of you, then beside you, then detaches entirely to slide along the underside of the grating overhead, pointing straight toward the sun.

The machinery itself seems to be obeying a fractured set of physical laws. A conveyor belt carrying nothing at all runs smoothly into a solid, seamless wall of vitrified brick—not through a slot, not into a chute, just *into* the masonry, the rubber belt continuing uninterrupted on the far side as if the brick were merely a suggestion. At the center of the basin, a cluster of thick glass pipes carries a slow, luminous grey fluid upward; when you step three paces to the left, the pipes are empty, and the fluid is pooling instead on the dry aluminum floor beside your boots, though your boots do not get wet.

Even the air has seams. Cross from one maintenance terrace to the next and the temperature doesn't change, the light doesn't dim, but the sound of your own footsteps suddenly arrives a half-second before your heel touches the plate. A drop of condensation detaches from a pipe overhead, hangs motionless at eye level for three full minutes—trembling slightly, reflecting an interior room full of spinning gears that does not exist anywhere in the basin—and then snaps upward back into the brass fitting it fell from.

The fauna seem entirely untroubled by this. The fiberglass-furred climbers step cleanly around the places where the air folds, and the armored grazers chew their moss along the edges of staircases that descend four flights only to arrive back on the exact landing where they began. The whole bright, cool, razor-edged valley hums at the edge of audibility—an immense, freshly built factory running at a glacial pace, manufacturing something silent out of space that no longer quite adds up.
