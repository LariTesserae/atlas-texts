The ground here has not finished being made. It rises in colossal, razor-edged steps and sheer, unweathered slabs of pale silicate and dark glass-basalt, lifted to an altitude where the air is thin, bone-dry, and steadily cool. There are no rounded boulders, no scree slopes softened by rain, no gullies carved by runoff. Water does not exist here—not as ice, not as mist, not even as the memory of dampness in a crack. Every ridge is an embryonic fracture, fresh and sharp as newly broken ceramic, jutting out across a vast, open basin of jagged terraces and bottomless drops.

Above it all hangs a dim, sunless sky the color of bruised pewter. Light seems to seep from nowhere in particular, diffused and weak, barely enough to distinguish the pale ash-gray of the raw stone from the soot-black of the crevices.

And the noise is absolute. 

It is not wind—there is scarcely a breeze in this cool, stagnant air—yet the entire valley is filled with a deafening, physical roar. It sounds like two continents of dry slate grinding together in slow motion, layered beneath a high, metallic whine that vibrates in the teeth and behind the eyes. The sound has no single source; it radiates from the un-eroded cliffs themselves, from the thin air, from the sheer friction of a world whose physical laws are failing. 

Because nothing here quite holds together. At the edges of the terraces, the stone does not always end in a cliff; sometimes it simply thins out into a flat, gray smear where depth ceases to function, like a drawing left unfinished. A spire that looms a hundred yards away may take an hour of walking to reach, its proportions shifting subtly with every step, while a pebble kicked over a ledge might fall straight down for ten feet, hang motionless in the dim air for a long minute, and then drift sideways into a cleft. Shadows detach themselves from the rocks that cast them, pooling in hollows like dry, black dust, lingering hours after whatever brief angle of light produced them has shifted. Time itself feels slightly unglued: the sharp *crack* of a splitting stone will echo across the basin minutes before the fracture actually opens in the rock face.

Yet, across these immense, empty expanses of raw mineral and broken geometry, life has found a strange, extravagant foothold. 

Widely separated by miles of barren slate, dense thickets of dry vegetation erupt from the fissures. They are lush, sprawling, and fiercely alive, though they contain not a drop of moisture. Instead of sap, their stems hold dry, pressurized gas and fine mineral powder; instead of leaves, they unfurl towering, feathery plumes of spun silica, velvet-black moss, and ash-silver fronds that feel like dry silk and brittle charcoal. They are cymatic plants. They feed on the deafening sound. In the crushing acoustic pressure of the valley, their dense, labyrinthine fronds tremble violently, trapping the sound waves and converting the endless vibration into slow, patient growth. Stepping into one of these scattered thickets brings an eerie, sudden drop in volume—the plants drink the roar so greedily that the air inside a clump of ferns feels thick and muffled, heavy with the dry, peppery scent of pulverized stone.

Nothing moves among them. There are no insects humming in the dry plumes, no birds riding the thermals over the abyss, no burrows beneath the mineral roots. The silence of animal absence sits oddly inside the deafening roar of the world.

Only occasional, scattered marks suggest that something with hands once passed through—or perhaps still does, moving too rarely to be seen. Perched on the leeward sides of the sharpest ridges, tucked inside the sound-muffled hollows of the black fern-thickets, are small, crude shelters stacked from flat shards of unweathered slate. There is no mortar, no metal nail, no sign of advanced craft. Beside the low doorways hang bundles of dry plant-fiber lashed together with twisted silica-grass, hung like primitive baffles to dull the relentless noise. Inside, one finds only simple, hand-knapped stone tools: slate scrapers, dull obsidian wedges, and rough, notched rods of compressed mineral-wood, left resting on flat stones beside heaps of dry, powdery ash.

Everything here changes at the speed of cooling glass. The cliffs extrude imperceptibly upward; the dry thickets widen by a fraction of an inch each decade; the broken geometry of the horizon drifts and settles like dust in a sealed room, while the terrible, unceasing roar of the unfinished earth rings on without end.
