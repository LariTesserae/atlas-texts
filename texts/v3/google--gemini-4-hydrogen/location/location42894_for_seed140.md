High above a flattened, ancient world, inside the worn-down crown of a mountain that lost its peaks eons ago, there is a hollow.

From the outside, there is almost nothing to mark it—only a bald, weathered dome of stone, smoothed by wind and time until it looks like the back of a sleeping whale, sitting miles above the surrounding plains. Through hairline fissures in that ancient cap, thin, cold mountain air is drawn inward, pulled by something patient and unseen.

Inside, the world changes completely.

The hollow is bounded, roughly the size of a great cathedral or a tight-knit hamlet—a few hundred paces from one curved stone wall to the other, vaulted high overhead. Yet it feels vast, because almost the entire interior volume is empty air. And it is dark. Not the soft dark of a moonless night, nor the dimness of a deep forest, but an absolute, unbroken absence of light. Nothing glows here. No fungus sparks, no eyes reflect, no machine bleeds a pilot-light. Vision ceases to be a category of experience.

In that total dark, the first thing you feel is the warmth. The air is heavy, tropically mild, and wet—saturated to the point of condensation, carrying the thick, sweet-mineral smell of damp rot, warm stone, and crushed stalks. Water is everywhere, though there are no rivers. The ancient ceiling weeps continuously, fat drops forming on rounded stone ribs and falling through the empty dark to strike the floor with hollow *ploks* and *splashes*. The walls are slick with a permanent film of moisture that trickles down into shallow, warm marsh-pools nestled in the eroded basins of the floor.

Because the rock is unimaginably old—settled into smooth, bulbous curves and soft, rounded shelves with every sharp edge long since dissolved—the whole chamber acts as a resonant acoustic drum. And it is loud.

In a world without light, everything speaks to see. The empty air is criss-crossed by an overlapping, relentless chorus of sound. Blind, leathery fliers—things with wide, papery ears and pleated snouts—sweep through the vast central void, firing rapid salvos of sharp clicks and high, whistling chirps that bounce off the wet stone walls. Below them, along the dripping ledges and marsh-edges, thousands of smaller creatures answer: low, throbbing croaks from inflated throat-sacs; the dry, rhythmic rasping of hard-shelled grazers; the wet slap of amphibious things slipping into warm puddles. Every wingbeat, every falling drop, every shift of a limb is amplified and braided together by the curved stone into a continuous, loud, living roar.

Yet beneath the noise, the actual pace of this place is glacial. There is no day or night here, no seasons, no storms. The water drips at the same measured rate it did a century ago.

The life here clings densely to the boundaries of the void. Wherever there is a horizontal surface—the uneven floor, the broad stone shelves, the sunken marsh-rims—the vegetation is lush and tangled. Without sunlight, it grows not toward the sky, but toward the warmth and the dripping mineral water. Thick, spongy mats of pale, rubbery moss-analogues blanket the stone, ankle-deep and squelching underfoot. Out of them rise clusters of tall, hollow, reed-like stalks, waxy and cool to the touch, their broad, cupped leaves arranged to catch falling condensation and funnel it down to their roots. Curtains of fibrous, wet tendrils hang from the upper ledges like heavy tapestries, swaying slightly in the slow, rising currents of warm air. They feed on the minerals leached from the ancient rock, the constant humidity, and the subtle, steady heat that radiates from the chamber’s few artificial presences.

Those presences are scattered and sparse—no more than five or six structures in the entire hollow, separated by wide stretches of empty, echoing air. They are older than the erosion of the mountain itself, left behind by an engineering that current physics has no language for.

Near the center of the void, suspended thirty feet above the marshy floor with no cables, struts, or visible fields holding it up, hangs a seamless polyhedron the size of a small cottage. Its surface is matte, slightly warm, and impossibly smooth—water neither beads on it nor wets it, simply sliding off as if the material refuses contact. If you throw a stone toward it, the stone slows as it nears, curves lazily around the hull as though the air had thickened into syrup, and drops straight down. Further back, half-embedded in the weeping stone of the eastern wall, stands a cluster of three slender, curved ribs of dark, non-metallic matter that hum at a frequency just below hearing—a deep, bone-felt vibration that makes the shallow water in nearby puddles shiver in concentric, geometric ripples.

No one knows what these artifacts are still doing. Perhaps they are stabilizing a fault line, or running an ultra-slow calculation, or simply idling at the end of a billion-year task. Whatever their purpose, their waste-energy is the engine of this place: they pull trace moisture from the dry, high-altitude air outside through microscopic pores in the mountain, bleed gentle heat into the stone, and keep the hollow warm, wet, and alive.

Every few decades, one of the suspended objects shifts its position by the width of a finger, accompanied by a brief, deep chime that shakes loose a shower of droplets from the ceiling. The blind things in the dark scatter for a moment, clicking wildly in alarm, and then settle back into their slow, loud, endless routines.
