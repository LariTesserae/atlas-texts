The heat hits first, not as a dry blast from a sun you can see, but as a wet, heavy weight pressed down from a sky the color of bruised tin. You are high up—the air has that thin, slightly metallic edge of altitude—yet the plateau breathes out a stifling, feverish warmth. Above, a permanent ceiling of low, steam-bellied cloud traps the dimness in an eternal late-afternoon gloom. Light filters through in a flat, pewter wash, just bright enough to pick out the sheen of moisture on dark stone.

Everywhere, the world is sweating. The basaltic plateau has been here long enough for its sharp edges to slump into broad, weathered terraces and shallow, dish-like basins. There is no soil to speak of, and no grass, no moss, no roots cracking the pavement. Instead, the rock is slick with warm condensation and stained in wide, mineral aprons of ochre, salt-white, and pale verdigris where thin, heat-loving biofilms cling to the wettest seams. Water doesn't fall here so much as it *appears*—beading on the undersides of ledges, weeping from hairline fissures in the cliff faces, pooling in warm, shallow sheets across the flat stone before sliding lazily into carved drainage channels.

Across this wide, steaming basin stretches the city, though *city* feels like a word borrowed from somewhere more crowded. It is vast, organized, and unmistakably urban—mile after mile of terraced stone embankments, tiered brick-and-iron viaducts, sunken plazas, and towering, lattice-ribbed cooling structures—yet it is almost entirely hollow. The buildings are mostly shells: cavernous, multi-story frames of riveted iron and blackened masonry with no interior walls, no floors, no glass in the tall arched openings. You can stand on a wet causeway and look straight through three consecutive blocks of monumental architecture to the misty horizon beyond, seeing only empty volume, dripping girders, and the occasional spiral stair climbing into nothing.

Between these skeletal districts lie immense stretches of vacant, paved plain. The scale is open and deliberate, as if whatever laid out the grid needed vast buffers of empty air between one structure and the next. A lonely row of ceramic-insulated pylons marches across half a mile of slick flagstones to meet a single, windowless substation. A broad avenue, wide enough for twenty carriages abreast, runs arrow-straight for leagues without a single doorway opening onto it, flanked only by low, mossy-looking mineral crusts and iron lamp standards whose amber bulbs glow faintly, pointlessly, in the dim muggy air.

Yet the place is not dead. It is running—slowly, quietly, on a vast and patient rhythm.

Listen closely, and the near-silence breaks into small, industrial pulses. Somewhere off in the haze, a heavy counterweight drops with a muffled *thud*, followed by the long, wet sigh of a steam-relief valve. Warm water, collected from a hundred acres of tilted slate roofs and copper-lined eaves, funnels down into tiered stone sluices, turning massive, slow-moving paddle wheels that have no belts attached to them—or perhaps the belts are underground, driving deep-rock pumps you can only feel as a faint, rhythmic tremor through the soles of your boots. High overhead, the thick copper cables slung between the pylons give off a low, damp, sixty-cycle hum, and occasionally a fat drop of condensed water rolls down a wire, hits a hot transformer housing, and vanishes with a sharp *tiss*.

The strangeness of the place settles in the longer you watch how it works. The water here doesn't just run downhill; in the intense heat rising from the vents beneath the city, mist spirals upward in tight, greasy ribbons that cling to the ironwork and condense *inward*, forming thick, crystalline stalactites of pale mineral salt that grow horizontally toward the electrified pylons, humming faintly when the current surges. The architecture, too, follows a logic that feels slightly tilted away from human use: miles of catwalks suspended at awkward, waist-high intervals; monumental cisterns built *above* the rooftops on slender iron legs, forever brimming with lukewarm, rust-colored water that spills over their rims in continuous, shimmering curtains.

Life is scarce, tucked into the margins of this slow, hot machine. In the dimmest corners under the viaduct arches, where the iron stays perpetually damp and warm, pale, long-legged things—neither quite insect nor crustacean, with translucent carapaces and slow, deliberate movements—pick their way along the rivets, grazing on the sulfur-yellow crusts. Once in a great while, something larger shifts in the upper rafters of a hollow foundry: a leathery, bladder-winged glider, catching a rising thermal from a geothermal grate, drifting silently from one empty tower to the next before folding itself back into the shadows of the eaves.

Then the sound fades back to the steady *drip-drip* of warm water on hot stone, the distant groan of expanding iron, and the vast, dim, steaming emptiness holding the city together.
