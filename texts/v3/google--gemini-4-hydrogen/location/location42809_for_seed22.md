The first thing that hits you is not the sight of it, because there is almost nothing to see, and not the ground, because the ground is mostly absence. It is the sound.

The air here is a physical weight, vibrating at frequencies that rattle the fluid in your inner ear and hum in the marrow of your shins. It is not a single roar, like a waterfall or an engine, but an architecture of noise—layers of sub-bass thrumming so low you feel it as a pressure behind your sternum, braided with razor-thin harmonics that shriek when the warm thermal drafts shift. If you try to shout, your voice doesn’t carry; it is sheared off a foot from your mouth and folded into the ambient din.

Yet when your eyes adjust to the dimness—a flat, slate-colored twilight that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere at once—you realize the space is almost completely empty.

You are standing in a low, basin-wide valley that stretches for miles in every direction, unspooling into a bruised-indigo haze. The ground beneath your boots is raw and young: fresh, unweathered slabs of dark mineral, fractured into jagged, glassy edges that haven’t seen enough rain or time to round off. Step carelessly and the rock will slice through leather. There are no soil beds, no soft loam, no worn paths—only sharp, newly-emerged terraces stepping down toward the center of the basin, slick in the creases with a thin film of condensation.

Rising out of this sharp stone is the city, if you can call something so hollow a city.

It is organized at a metropolitan scale—districts, transit corridors, tiered platforms, towering spires—yet it occupies almost none of the volume it claims. There is no concrete here, no brick, no solid walls. Instead, the urban grid is built of tension and negative space. Hair-thin filaments of some matte-black metamaterial stretch for kilometers between impossibly slender pylons, anchored into the jagged rock not by heavy foundations, but by localized pockets of altered gravity that hold the bases pin-still without touching the stone around them. Suspended along these filaments are vast, paper-thin rings and hollow geometric shells—some the size of cathedrals, some no larger than a room—hanging in mid-air like the skeletons of soap bubbles.

The engineering doesn’t obey the rules of load and compression. A ribbon of translucent alloy, no thicker than a fingernail and half a mile long, curves gracefully across the dim sky with nothing beneath it, carrying the faint, rippling shimmer of an active transit current. Every surface is tuned. As the warm air rises off the dark, damp stone of the basin floor, it passes through these taut urban lattices and turns the entire valley into a wind-harp the size of a county. The city *is* the noise. Its structural fields hum as they constantly micro-adjust to the shifting air currents, and the hollow shells act as acoustic chambers, amplifying the faintest draft into a booming, resonant chord.

Because the air is warm and the light is scarce, water here doesn’t fall as rain; it breathes. Every few hours, a slow, lukewarm mist creeps across the low terraces, clinging to the razor-edges of the rock and beading along the city’s tension-cables. That brief dampness is enough to feed a bizarre, weightless kind of jungle.

The vegetation is lush, draping the valley in sprawling curtains of green-black and pale chartreuse, yet it has almost no mass. Nothing grows thick trunks or woody stems. Instead, aero-flora—vast, gossamer nets of velvety moss, hollow bladder-vines, and translucent condensation-sails—hangs from the urban filaments and drapes over the sharp basalt ridges like wet lace. Some of the plants don’t root in rock at all; they anchor themselves to the *sound*, growing along the standing-wave nodes where the city’s deafening hum creates pockets of still, vibrating air. Their broad, tissue-thin fronds tremble constantly, shaking droplets of collected dew down onto the lower terraces, where tiny, mirror-flat pools gather in the sharpest creases of the stone before evaporating back into the warm gloom.

In this vast, dim, roaring emptiness, the wildlife is everywhere—and almost all of it is built to survive inside a sonic storm.

You see them first as flickers against the slate sky: kite-like manta-creatures with wingspans of twenty feet and bodies no thicker than a reed, riding the thermal updrafts between the hollow towers. Their skins are perforated with rows of whistling vents; by opening and closing them, they steer through the turbulent air and broadcast deafening, metallic clicks that echo off the suspended rings. Down along the cables, long-legged, hollow-boned striders—pale, spindly things with too many joints—scurry rapidly across the hair-thin metamaterial lines, their feet gripping the invisible field-sheaths that surround the tech.

They are hunting. Swarms of tiny, gas-buoyant jellies drift up from the damp rock-crevices in shimmering, bioluminescent clouds, glowing a faint, sickly amber in the twilight. As they rise into the acoustic corridors of the city, the striders drop from the cables on silk-like tethers, snatching the glowing spheres out of the air, while larger, silent-gliding predators—sleek, eyeless things with massive, dish-shaped ears occupying half their skulls—dive down through the miles of empty air, homing in on the exact frequency of a strider’s footsteps on a vibrating wire.

Even the silence, when a brief lull passes through a sheltered pocket behind a jagged ridge, feels unnatural and active—like the intake of a breath. Within seconds, the warm mist rolls back in, the condensation-sails swell and drip, the city’s invisible fields whine up a pitch to compensate for the added weight of the water, and the vast, hollow valley swells once more with a wall of sound that shakes the teeth in your jaw.
