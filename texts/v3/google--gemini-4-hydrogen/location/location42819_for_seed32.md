From twenty paces away, there is nothing to see—only a low, flat shelf of pale, sun-bleached marl, cool under a high, white sky. The place does not announce itself until you are standing at its lip, looking down into a sunken pocket no wider than a small courtyard. 

Even then, the eye struggles to read it, because the drop is filled to the brim with glare. Every surface inside the hollow is white, bone-grey, or chalk-yellow, and the light pouring down from above bounces endlessly off thousands of pale faces until shadow is nearly erased. It is a bright, cold, eye-watering white, like looking into a bowl of crushed salt. Yet when you lower a hand toward the rim, the air rising from it is surprisingly cool—dry as a cellar, carrying no warmth at all.

Nothing natural shows through the floor or walls. The entire pocket is built, packed solid from its sunken base to just below the lip with hand-formed mineral vessels: narrow, unglazed amphorae, coiled clay chimneys, pinched gypsum bells, and honeycomb blocks of pressed chalk-paste. None of it was shaped on a wheel or fired in a kiln; you can still trace the ghosts of thumb-presses and palm-ridges in the dried paste, though centuries of dry grit and steady air have worn the edges down to a smooth, velvet-matte finish. The pieces are wedged together with no mortar and no mercy—leaning, nesting, fused at their contact points by dry mineral crust. There is nowhere to step without crushing a rim, and nowhere to sit. Every cubic inch is claimed by another hollow throat or curved flank.

And the whole packed mass is screaming.

A steady, cool draft—part of a permanent, featureless pressure gradient across the low shelf—skims endlessly over the sunken lip. As it shears across the thousands of open mouths, throats, and narrow slots, the pocket answers with a single, deafening, unchanging chord. It is not the howl of a storm, which rises and falls; it is a flat, solid wall of sound, dense as stone. A low, dry roar from the wider chimneys underpins a tight, shivering cluster of high whistles from the narrowest reeds of clay. Because the air never falters and the vessels never shift, the note does not breathe. It simply exists there, filling the small room as completely as the clay does.

Inside the seams—wedged into the hairline cracks where windblown dust settles out of the main current—a sparse handful of plants survive. They are so dry and pale they look like flaking paint: brittle, papery lichens in shades of ash and silver, and tiny, woody tufts of salt-moss with roots like dry thread. They do not sway in the wind, because they are tucked too deep and are too stiff to bend. They grow at the speed of mineral crust, taking decades to claim a fingernail's width of chalk.

No insects crawl between the vessels. No birds nest in the chimneys; the sound is too violent, the glare too raw, and there is not a drop of water to be found—not even a trace of morning condensation on the coolest stones. The air tastes only of chalk dust and dry salt.

Nothing here decays, and nothing is being built. The sharp edges were rounded long ago; the loose pieces settled into their final wedges ages past. The light stays flat, the clay stays cold, the sparse lichens hold their grip, and the sunken room holds its single, endless note—a small, hidden engine of wind and dried earth, locked into a silence of its own making.
