The basin is no wider than a courtyard, pinched into a saddle between three converging ridges of pale, frost-split gneiss. Above it, the sky is a dark, bruised violet even at noon; below the lip of the saddle, the lower world is buried under a flat sea of cloud that never rises quite high enough to swallow the hollow. 

It is a place of fierce, unblinking light. At this altitude, the sun has no softness—it strikes the rock like a hammer, bleaching the stone to chalk and turning every shadow into a sharp, ink-black edge. Yet the air remains thin and bitingly cold. Even in the direct glare, warmth never settles; it only agitates the surface of things before the cold reclaims them.

The floor of the hollow is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with ice-blades. Thousands of them, standing waist-high to twice the height of a person, lean at the same steep angle toward the south. They are not smooth glaciers or soft snowdrifts, but dense, serrated fins of compacted hoarfrost and old melt, crowded so tightly together that there is barely room to slip a hand between their bases. Because the rock beneath them is rich in iron and pale, chalky salts, the blades are banded in muted rust, slate-grey, and bone-white. Their windward faces are pitted and hollowed into honeycombs by the sun; their leeward sides are razor-sharp and glassy.

Nothing grows here. There is no moss in the cracks, no lichen on the boulders, no bird circling the ridges, not even a beetle under the scree. The only movement belongs to water, stone, and light—and they are in constant, quiet negotiation.

By mid-morning, the sun reaches the angles of the ice-blades. They do not melt into slush; the air is too dry and cold for that. Instead, they sublimate, thinning into invisible vapor, while thin beads of briny moisture form on the shaded undersides and creep downward. Where this dampness meets the dark bedrock, it pools in shallow, frost-heaved pockets and immediately begins to crust over with white, feathery blooms of mineral salt. By late afternoon, as the sun drops behind the western ridge, the temperature plummets. Mist spills up over the lip of the cloud-sea in cold, damp tongues, coating the honeycombed ice and the bare stone in a fresh skin of rime. By nightfall, the basin is sealed again, waiting for the next day’s light to pare it back.

The silence here is vast and slightly deadened by the thin air, yet the basin is never entirely still. Throughout the midday glare, the crowd of ice-blades gives off a faint, continuous *ticking* and *sighing*—the sound of internal stresses snapping inside the crystal lattices as the sun warms one face while the other stays frozen. Occasionally, a frost-loosened shard of gneiss detaches from the rim and clatters down the scree, its echo cut short by the dense labyrinth of ice. When the wind rises, it does not howl; it hisses thinly across a thousand sharp edges at once, like sand pouring over glass.

Scattered along the higher, ice-free rim of the hollow are the spare, weathered marks of hands. They are old—half-sunk into the settled gravel and furred with white salt-crusts, as much a part of the basin’s geology as the scree itself.

Near the northern ridge, someone long ago stacked flat slabs of gneiss into three low, curving windbreaks, no higher than a crouching shoulder. Between the stones, wedged tightly against the frost-heave, are small cairns topped with fist-sized chunks of milky quartz. Beside one windbreak, a shallow groove has been pecked directly into the bedrock with a stone maul—a narrow channel leading a trickle of the bitter, mineral-heavy midday drip into a hand-hollowed basin no bigger than a bowl. The bowl is dry now, its bottom caked with a thick, bitter ring of pale yellow salt.

A few paces away, hanging from a crooked staff of bleached mountain-wood jammed into a fissure, a strip of hammered copper wire holds three flat discs of slate, each bored through the center with a bow-drill. They hang motionless in the still air, catching the blinding sun on their worn edges, waiting for the afternoon mist to coat them in frost and weight them down until morning.
