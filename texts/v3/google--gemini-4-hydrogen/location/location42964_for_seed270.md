You only find it by nearly stepping into it.

For miles in every direction, the plain is a study in erasure: ancient, pale-grey stone planed almost flat by wind and time, without a sharp edge or a shadow taller than a pebble. No grass, no lichen, no puddle in any hollow. Just cool, dry air moving over stone worn smooth as old bone.

Then the ground drops away—a hidden, steep-walled pocket no wider than a courtyard, sunk twenty meters below the level of the plain. Daylight pours cleanly into the bowl, illuminating every inch of it, and from the lip you can hear it before you can properly take it in: a dense, roaring wall of sound. Not wind, and not water, but a ceaseless, dry clatter—millions of shells and claws scraping against stone and sheet iron, layered over the heavy, rhythmic *thunk-hiss* of ancient machinery still turning at the bottom.

The basin is packed to the brim.

Down in the cool, shaded low of the sink, scattered industrial remnants jut from the worn bedrock like half-buried ribs. There are no grand buildings, only a few squat, riveted iron housings, a tangle of thick-walled ducts, and an exposed gear-train the size of a cartwheel, its outer casing pitted and thinned by eons of dry grit while its inner teeth still gleam, polished bright by relentless motion. Something deep underground still drives them. A row of stubby intake cowls shudders every few seconds, coughing out cold, dry, metallic-smelling drafts that swirl the dust into miniature, short-lived cyclones.

There is not a drop of water anywhere in the pit, nor a single strand of green. The floor is a shifting, ankle-deep carpet of powdered mineral, salt-flour, rust-flakes, and cast-off husks—layers upon layers of pale chitin and shed scales. Yet the place is suffocatingly alive.

Every surface is coated, near-touching, moving.

In the dry silt of the floor, flat-bodied, stone-colored things—armored beetles and long, segmented burrowers—churn endlessly through the mineral dust, sifting out flakes of dried tallow and oxidized grease that weep from the ancient pipe joints. Above them, the smooth rock ledges and the cool iron ducts are shingled with overlapping layers of dry-skinned lizards and spiny, crab-like climbers, packed flank to flank. They jostle constantly for purchase, their claws ticking like rain on tin, snapping at smaller scuttlers that pour from the pitted holes in the metal.

The air column inside the pocket is a swirling, crowded funnel. Pale, leathery-winged gliders and sharp-beaked, stiff-bristled flyers wheel in tight, colliding spirals, riding the cold updrafts from the vents. They dive straight into the writhing mats on the pipes, snatching climbers off the metal, only to be ambushed mid-bank by heavy-jawed, long-limbed predators wedged invisibly into the dark elbows of the machinery. Nothing falls to the floor without being torn apart and carried off before it settles.

There is an uncanny, practiced rhythm to the chaos. The creatures here have grown alongside the engines for so long they seem half-mineral, half-clockwork themselves—beetles whose carapaces have taken on the dull, coppery sheen of the iron they graze on; burrowers that emerge in synchronized waves only during the three-second pause between the compressor's strokes; flyers that time their dives to the loud *clack* of an ancient relief valve, using the burst of noise to mask their approach.

Cool, dry, and deafening, the little pit churns on in broad daylight—a self-contained engine of tooth, shell, and iron, tucked invisibly beneath the quiet, dead plain.
