The first thing that hits you is not the dark, though the dark is thick enough to press against your eyelids. It is the noise—a wall of sound so dense and layered that it feels less like hearing and more like standing in a warm, vibrating surf.

The valley lies low and wide, a freshly torn basin of jagged basalt and fractured slate that has not yet had time to learn softness. Rain rarely falls here, and no rivers cut through to round the stones. Every ridge, scree-slope, and fault-crack still keeps the razor-edge of whatever tectonic heave split the ground open. Yet the air is warm—thick with trapped geothermal heat rising from deep, unseen vents—and heavy with a slow, clinging dampness. Moisture does not run; it sweats. It beads on the sharp black rock, pools in shallow, mineral-rimmed hollows, and drips in a slow, irregular patter from the underside of leaves the size of shields.

Above, the sky is either absent or choked out entirely by a permanent, low-bellied ceiling of ash-cloud and soot-dark mist, leaving the whole expanse in a bruised, nearly total darkness. Only here and there does a smear of pale, greenish bioluminescence cling to a rotting stalk, or a faint amber seam glow briefly where warm vapor seeps from a fissure—just enough light to prove how crowded the dark actually is.

Because the valley is not empty. It is choked.

Vegetation erupts from every fissure where a spoonful of dampness and grit can collect. Without sun to chase, the plants grow toward heat and each other, forming a tangled, multi-storied jungle of black-barked trunks, swollen tuberous bases, and broad, waxy fronds veined in dull copper and bruised violet. They are lush, fast-growing, and restless. In the warm murk, you can almost hear them drink—sap surging through fibrous stems, roots wedging deeper into the young stone until a slab of slate cracks with a sharp *tock* and slides down the slope. Nothing here has the patience of old trees. A vine shoots up, flowers in a pale, foul-smelling burst that lasts an hour, drops a heavy pod, and collapses into wet pulp before the week is out.

And through this churning, dripping thicket moves an overwhelming tide of animal life.

They are packed into every layer: burrowing beneath the sharp scree, swarming through the root-knots, clinging to the undersides of the giant fronds, and beating through the warm air in blinding, colliding clouds. Because there is nothing to see by, the valley runs on sound, vibration, and touch. Millions of chitinous legs rasp against leathery leaves. Throat-sacs inflate and boom like struck hides, sending low pressure-waves through the damp air that rattle your ribs. High above the audible roar, shrill echolocation clicks rain down like hail on tin, overlapping into a continuous, metallic shriek. Every few seconds, a whole sector of the canopy erupts into a frantic clatter of wings and snapping stems as some pack-predator lunges, or a swarm shifts course, moving through the valley like a single, agitated organism.

It is a place of constant, anthill-energy—turbulent and unstable. Nothing stays still long enough to settle. The ground itself seems to crawl with pale, segmented scavengers that strip a fallen carcass in minutes; small rockslides hiss down the unweathered slopes as burrowers undermine the scree; warm mist-plumes flare from ground-cracks and scatter whatever is nesting nearby, sending waves of screeching, clicking bodies surging into the next thicket.

There are no walls here, no cleared paths, no roofs raised against the dark. The valley will not hold still for architecture. Yet if you crouch in the lee of a sharp basalt outcrop, running your fingers along the damp stone, you find the quiet traces of hands.

Tucked into a dryish crevice are the fresh, crescent-shaped flakes of a knapped obsidian blade—easy to make when the whole valley is made of sharp, young stone—lying beside a handful of cracked carapace shells and a length of braided plant-fiber weighted with a grooved pebble. Further on, a hollow seed-pod has been scraped clean and notched to serve as a rattle or a water-flask, abandoned when its owner had to move fast. Whoever comes through here does not build; they travel light, carrying bone gaffs, simple copper-tipped skewers, and resin-daubed torches that they rarely dare to light. They read the valley by its deafening roar, moving along the edges of the chaos, taking what they can pry from the rock or snare from the air before the tide of teeth and wings shifts back toward them.

Stand still for too long in the warm, sweating dark, and the sound closes over you completely—a roaring, clicking, dripping bazaar where everything is hungry, everything is growing, and nothing waits for morning.
