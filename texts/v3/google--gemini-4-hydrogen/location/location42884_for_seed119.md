The place is a single, sunken rotunda—cathedral-sized, yet closed in on itself, roughly eighty paces from wall to wall and half as high at the crown of its corbeled dome. Every surface is made. There is no raw earth, no exposed bedrock, no wild gravel beneath the water. Even the floor is a deliberate geometry of hand-cut river-slate and worn terracotta tiles, laid in shallow, concentric steps that descend toward a central drain-grate of notched stone and lashed bone.

The walls curve inward and upward in spiraling courses of thumb-pressed, low-fired brick, pale ochre darkened to the color of wet liver. Crushed-shell lime mortar binds them, and no metal appears anywhere in the construction—only clay, stone, bone, and the blackened, spongy remnants of wooden pegs wedged into the joints. Centuries of seepage have brought the structure into a quiet, weathered equilibrium. Mineral salts have bled through the mortar in long, chalky tears, hardening into brittle ribs along the brick. The lower steps of the floor are worn smooth as river-glass, their sharp edges rounded away by water and the passage of small bodies.

Above, the dome narrows to a small, slatted oculus of overlapping stone slabs, supplemented by a ring of angled weep-slits near the springline. Through these, a dim, slate-grey light filters down—never enough to warm the air, only enough to turn the dampness visible. The temperature stays cool and steady, held down by the surrounding water table. Moisture presses inward through the porous brick rather than falling as rain; the walls sweat continuously, and fine mist hangs in the upper vault, condensing on the mineral crusts and dripping in steady, irregular curtains to the floor.

Despite the size of the chamber, the volume inside feels almost entirely empty. There are no pillars, no hanging walkways, no clutter in the center. Between the curved walls and the flooded steps lies a vast, hollow bowl of cool, echoing air.

At the floor, the weeping water gathers in ankle-deep, mirror-black sheets, spilling silently over each concentric step toward the central bone-grate. Vegetation is barely present: only thin, velvety smears of dark-olive liverwort clinging to the mortar lines where the dim light reaches, and a few translucent, hair-thin fungi sprouting from the rotting pegs.

Yet the chamber is deafeningly loud, and its surfaces are crowded with life.

Built into the curving brick walls are thousands of small, recessed niches—like amphora-rests or dovecotes—originally fitted with unglazed, narrow-necked clay jars. Most of the jars remain, cemented in place by lime scale. Wedged into their mouths are pale, blind-eyed amphibians the size of a cupped hand, their slick skins trailing wispy, silver-white strands of a mist-drinking water mold. They use the hollow clay vessels as resonators. Triggered by slight shifts in air pressure or the rhythm of falling drops from the oculus, they inflate their translucent throat-sacs in rolling, staggered waves.

The sound they produce is not a croak, but a deep, wet, booming percussion—like hollow wooden drums struck underwater—amplified by the jars and flung across the empty dome. The acoustics turn the chamber into an instrument; the booming vibrates through the air with enough force to raise tiny, geometric standing ripples across the black water on the floor.

Beneath those trembling ripples, the stepped basins teem with finger-long, milky-white eels and pale, segmented crustaceans that swirl in slow, continuous spirals around the central drain, grazing on the biofilm and drowned midges washed down from the walls. Every few minutes, a different sound cuts through the low booming: a rapid, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of echolocation. Small, slate-grey swifts with long, sickle-shaped wings drop from their dry crevices near the oculus, plummeting straight through the hollow center of the room. They skim inches above the rippling water to snatch an eel or pluck a midge from the air, then bank sharply upward into the dimness, their wingtips brushing the mist.

Nothing here is still for long, yet nothing changes its shape. The water weeps in, circles the steps, and vanishes down the bone-grate; the swifts dive through the empty air; the jars boom in pulsing cycles, and the old bricks hold it all in a cool, wet, echoing balance.
