The basin is small enough to cross in five minutes, a sunken pocket of black basalt pinched between sharp, unweathered cliff walls just a few feet above sea level. The rock looks freshly broken—crisp fracture planes, angular talus at the base of the walls, and jagged ledges that have not yet been rounded by wind or time. Overhead, a thick, low lid of coastal cloud and industrial steam seals the sky off at the rim of the cliffs, holding the light down to a flat, murky twilight.

It is relentlessly hot. The heat rises from the ground itself, where geothermal brine sits inches below the surface, and radiates off the machinery planted in the rock. Because the air is already saturated, sweat does not evaporate; it simply joins the film of moisture coating every surface. The basalt walls weep continuously, dark streaks of mineral water sliding down the sharp faces to pool in shallow, ankle-deep pans across the basin floor. From the corrugated eaves, the overhead pipe-racks, and the undersides of the steel catwalks, condensation gathers and falls in a steady, warm patter.

Within this tight boundary, the built environment is dense and layered, though arranged in distinct clusters with wide, empty aprons of wet stone and shallow water between them. Three multi-story blocks of concrete, riveted steel, and brick housing—a brine-pumping station, a condenser bank, and a stacked complex of control rooms and workshops—rise from the basalt. They are linked by elevated walkways, cable trays, and thick insulated conduits that span the open yards. Between these humming islands of infrastructure, there is mostly space: broad, glistening stretches of dark pavement, rectangular settling pans, and open drainage channels where warm, brackish water slides toward the sump.

The machinery runs at a fixed, unvarying pitch. The place is loud—a thick, steady wall of sound made of electric motors, the rhythmic thud of piston pumps, the hiss of pressure-relief valves, and the low, metallic thrum of transformers. Cooling fans mounted in the upper walls churn the heavy air without ever cooling it. Because the steam lid never lifts and the plant never powers down, the basin exists in a state of flat equilibrium. Morning and evening are indistinguishable; the caged sodium and tungsten lamps bolted to the steel frames stay lit around the clock, throwing small, greasy halos of amber light onto the wet concrete and the drifting vapor.

Very little grows here. The rock is too young, the water too warm and mineral-heavy, and the light too dim for trees or thick brush. Only thin tufts of salt-tolerant sedge manage to wedge themselves into the cracks along the drainage channels, alongside dark mats of algae in the warm runnels and pale, rubbery moss clinging to the cooler joints of the concrete retaining walls.

Animal life, by contrast, thrives in the warm, sheltered seams of the plant. Clouds of midges and brine-flies orbit every caged work-lamp, thick enough to coat the glass globes. Pale geckos and small, dark skinks flatten themselves against the warm brick and pipe-casings, darting out to pick insects off the illuminated steel. In the shallow puddles and sedge-lined drains, heat-tolerant toads call continuously beneath the roar of the fans, while small crabs scuttle along the submerged edges of the concrete sluices. High up in the dry, sheltered rafters of the condenser trusses, colonies of swifts and pigeons have built dense rows of nests, dropping down through the dim, steam-thick air to hunt over the warm water pans before returning to the ironwork above.
