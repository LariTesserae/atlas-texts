The plateau is so high and so old that its original stone has been forgotten beneath a continent of compressed turf. There is no bare earth anywhere. What feels like ground is a dense, springy felt of pale-sage lichen, millennia of matted root-fiber, and slow-growing highland moss, worn down by wind and time into vast, gentle swells that stretch to a crisp, flat horizon. Even the deepest gullies are only shallow troughs in the living mat, floored with dark, waterlogged peat that squelches softly under weight.

The air is cool and thin, carrying the sharp, clean scent of crushed pine-needles and wet iron. There are no rivers or lakes here—only dampness. Mist drifts across the open expanse in low, ragged sheets, beading on everything it touches. Dew gathers in the hollows of broad, leathery leaves and drips steadily into cisterns, keeping the surface of the world perpetually slick without ever quite flooding it.

Across this immense, open tableland, the city is scattered in wide, lonely intervals. It is unmistakably urban—organized, layered, built at the scale of heavy industry—yet its districts stand miles apart, separated by empty expanses of undulating moss. From a rise, you can see half a dozen of them at once under the clear, pale daylight: massive, solitary complexes of living timber and riveted copper, rising out of the turf like stranded ships or colossal, squared-off tree stumps.

Every structure is grown as much as it is built. The factories and refineries are vast, hollowed boles of ironwood and petrified fern-trunk, reinforced with bolted steel bands and terraced with walkways of braided vine. Copper smokestacks sprout from the upper canopies, trailing thin plumes of white steam and sweet, roasted-sap vapor into the cool air. Between the distant compounds run long, straight causeways and elevated trestles—arteries of woven root-cable and humming power lines that carry brass tram-cars and pulsing xylem-pipes across the emptiness.

Even from a mile away, the place is loud. Sound travels effortlessly over the flattened terrain, and the city never stops working. The wind drives towering slatted-wood turbines mounted on the roofs of the mill-trees, filling the air with a rhythmic, clattering roar. Beneath that is the deep, wet thrum of hydraulic sap-pumps pulling moisture from miles of subterranean root-networks, the hiss of condensation valves, and the periodic, ear-splitting shriek of pressure-whistles as boilers vent steam into the mist. The turf itself vibrates faintly with the bass frequency of buried dynamos.

Yet for all the noise and industry, almost nothing moves on its own. Animal life is rare—an occasional pale, slow-moving beetle clinging to the warm flank of a condenser-pipe, or a handful of translucent moths fluttering around the bare electric bulbs strung along the tram-trestles. No birds cross the sky. The work of the city seems to run on clockwork, wind-catchers, and automated botanical cycles, tending to itself long after whatever built it has thinned out or vanished.

Spend enough time walking the long, damp causeways between the districts, and the world begins to slip its seams. The plateau is too old, its root-grids and copper circuits tangled too deeply for too long, and reality here has grown frayed at the edges.

You hear the steam-whistle from the western refinery three seconds *before* the white plume bursts from its stack. Shadows cast by the tall turbine-towers sometimes lag behind the movement of the clouds, lingering as dark, rectangular stains on the bright moss after the sun has already been covered. Along the elevated tram-lines, distance refuses to stay honest: a stretch of trestle that takes twenty minutes to walk under a clear sky may fold into three hours of featureless, repeating ironwood pylons if a mist-bank rolls across it, only to spit you out at the far station with your boots dry and your pocket-watch ticking backward.

In the older districts, the breakdown is architectural. Inside the cavernous, echoing halls of the textile-mills, where copper looms clatter in the damp gloom, rooms occasionally fail to match their exteriors. A staircase climbs four stories inside a two-story trunk; a window looking out onto the empty, sunlit plateau shows instead the interior of a flooded cellar, complete with submerged gears turning slowly in dark, motionless water. Sometimes a bolt shears off a vibrating press and, instead of falling to the moss floor, hangs in the cool air, humming softly as it slowly sprouts pale, fibrous roots into nothing at all.

The machinery keeps running through all of it. The wind-turbines clatter, the sap-pumps throb, and the mist settles on the copper and the lichen alike, holding the vast, quiet-and-deafening plateau in a state of ancient, humming, unravelling equilibrium.
