It is cold here—not the brittle, freezing cold of ice, but the heavy, numbing chill of deep mountain water that has never seen the sun.

You are miles above the true floor of the world, yet entirely submerged. This is a drowned highland, a vast, open basin and ridge-system perched at the top of a submerged cordillera, where the ledges drop away into mile-deep blackness on every side. Above, there is only more water, thick enough to swallow the sky down to the faintest, bruised memory of indigo—not light, really, just the barest difference between looking up and looking down.

And it is loud. Before your eyes make out a single shape, the sound presses against your ribs: a constant, cavernous roar woven out of a million smaller rhythms. There is a low, organ-pipe booming that vibrates through your teeth, layered beneath an endless, dry-sounding *clack-clack-clack* like hailstones striking hollow wood, and a wet, collective scraping that seems to come from the ground itself.

If you reach out in the dark to steady yourself against that ground, you will not find rock. You will not find sand or mud.

Everything here is plant, and everything here is made.

The floor beneath you is a dense, springy, tightly felted mat of dark-olive and bruised-plum turf, woven so thickly from ancient rootlets and cold-water moss that it feels like wet, heavy leather. Follow it outward across the wide, open terraces and you will find that the terraces themselves—the sweeping retaining walls, the broad causeways, the monumental, open-air plazas, the sheer buttresses clinging to the mountain-drop—are all grown and braided from living, lignified vegetation. Massive, slow-growing kelp-timbers, thick as old oaks and hardened by millennia of mineral-rich cold, have been bent, knotted, and grafted into sweeping parabolic arches and vast, hollow viaducts. There is no mortar, no cut stone, no mark of a blade or a chisel. Whoever or whatever shaped this high valley did it with bare limbs, patience, and biology alone—training holdfasts to grip the invisible mountain spine beneath, coaxing fronds into vaulted roofs, weaving living curtains of eelgrass into wide, porous walls.

Now, it is ancient. Whatever impulse built these wide, open geometries is long finished, and the architecture has settled into its final, unhurried form. Millennia of slow, cold currents have worn the braided kelp-beams smooth as driftwood, rounding the sharp corners of the stepped terraces and softening the great root-pillars until they feel less like buildings and more like the natural bones of the plateau. Nothing is actively rotting, and nothing is visibly sprouting; the cold water holds the whole vast, vegetal city in a kind of pickled, living stasis.

Despite the scale—a sprawling, town-sized expanse of stepped basins and high ridges—the place feels strangely empty, balanced toward open water. The structures are sparse and widely spaced. You can swim for minutes across a broad, level meadow of short, velvety plant-floor without touching a wall, before the dark silhouette of a lone, woven spire or a sweeping colonnade of root-arches looms out of the gloom. Between them hangs only cold, clear, black water, stretching out toward the unseen drop-offs.

Yet that open water is crowded with life. You feel the fauna before you see them: the sudden, heavy displacement of water as something large, slow, and blind glides past ten feet away; the tickle of thousands of tiny, translucent antennae brushing your skin.

In the near-total dark, the animals are the architecture’s voice. Countless pale, hard-shelled grazers, no bigger than fists, blanket the distant walls in slow-moving herds, their rasping mandibles producing that ceaseless, rhythmic *shhhk-shhhk* as they crop the microscopic film off the ancient kelp-masonry. Nestled inside the porous, braided cavities of the arches live millions of snapping, segmented things whose synchronized clicking turns every hollow root-beam into a chattering resonance chamber. And out in the wide, open spaces between the ruins, great, bellows-bodied swimmers—pale, finless, and sluggish in the cold—pulse steadily from terrace to terrace, inflating and emptying vast acoustic bladders that send low, booming bass notes rolling across the valley floor.

Every few seconds, a scatter of faint, cold bioluminescence—a sickly, pale-green flicker from a disturbed grazer, or a drifting string of dim blue sparks from a passing swimmer—briefly illuminates a fraction of a massive, eroded root-arch or the edge of a stepped plaza. Then it fades, leaving only the dark, the chill, and the thunderous, unchanging hum.

Nothing here hurries. No storms reach this depth; no seasons shift the temperature of the water; no new shoots break the ancient, smoothed lines of the woven terraces. The herds graze the same paths, the currents turn the same slow circles through the viaducts, and the drowned highland booms in the dark, locked in a loud, cold, finished peace.
