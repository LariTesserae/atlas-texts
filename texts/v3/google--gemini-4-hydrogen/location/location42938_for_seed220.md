The first thing you notice is not the dark, though the dark is nearly absolute. It is the weight of the sound—a warm, dry-throated roar that lives inside your ribs before it reaches your ears.

You are standing on a cantilevered lip of sintered basalt and dull, heavy alloy, looking out into a space the size of a cathedral, though its proportions are wrong for worship. It is a bounded subterranean pocket, sealed at its far ends by fused, glassy bulkheads where the rock above was melted into place long ago. Between those walls, the air is mostly empty—a vast, hollow volume of rising heat and drifting mist, crossed only by a sparse web of slender, load-bearing filaments that hum like plucked strings.

The architecture clings to the perimeter. Around the hollowed-out core, the walls are built up in dense, layered tiers: narrow galleries, stacked service-crypts, recessed conduits, and stepped terraces that mimic the logic of a town, though there are no doors sized for walking through, only maintenance hatches, intake louvers, and smooth, seamless housings. Everything is weathered into a state of quiet truce with the earth. The non-ferrous plating—some kind of layered, phase-locked ceramic-metal that absorbs vibration without cracking—has been pitted by centuries of mineral-heavy air. Lime and pale gypsum have crusted along the seams like dried foam, softening the sharp angles of the construction until the oldest infrastructure looks half-grown from the bedrock.

Light is scarce, barely enough to separate an edge from a drop. Here and there, deep within the recessed galleries, a stressed conductor bleeds a bruised, ember-violet glow through a hairline fracture in its casing. Closer to the floor, thermal vents exhale a faint, dirty-orange shimmer where the heat from deeper strata meets the machine-housings. You learn the shape of the vault not by seeing it, but by listening to how the roar bounces back.

The sound comes from everywhere at once, layered and mechanical, yet rhythmic enough to feel like weather. Deep beneath the floor-plates, something vast and post-industrial is still cycling—not turning on bearings, because there is no rhythmic clatter of metal on metal, but shifting its mass through invisible fields. It produces a low, chest-rattling thrum that rises in pitch over twelve-minute intervals, peaks in a breathless, high-frequency whine that makes your teeth ache, and then drops back down with a heavy, pneumatic *whuff* that sends a gust of warm air swirling up through the hollow center of the room.

Because of that cycle, the air is always moving. It is uncomfortably warm, thick with the smell of hot dust, ozone, and damp stone. High up near the vaulted ceiling, where the rock is marginally cooler, moisture condenses out of the rising currents. It gathers on the undersides of the upper walkways and along the sagging catenary curves of old tension-cables, then lets go in intermittent, heavy curtains of tepid rain. 

Most of it never reaches the bottom. Droplets fall into the rising thermal plumes and scatter into fine, stinging mist; others strike the hot, ribbed casings of the wall-mounted manifolds and flash instantly into steam with a sharp, percussive *hiss* that cuts right through the bass hum. Only in the sheltered corners, behind the thicker buttresses of the lower tiers, does the water actually collect—shallow, black basins of standing condensation, warm to the touch and filmed with iridescent mineral oil.

In those damp, sheltered pockets, a sparse ecology has taken hold. Pale, fibrous mats of something between moss and fungus cling to the undersides of the ledges, just out of reach of the hottest updrafts. They are colorless, toughened by the heat, and rooted directly into the calcified crust on the metal. Near the dripping seams, thin, hair-like tendrils hang down in curtains, swaying in the periodic gusts of the twelve-minute cycle, drinking the mist before it evaporates.

Nothing here moves fast, and almost nothing moves in the open. The hollow center of the vault belongs to the air and the sound. But along the damp terraces and inside the dark, hollowed-out conduits, rare, cautious things make their living. Small, flat-bodied grazers with matte-grey carapaces creep along the wet stone, their shells vibration-damped so they can tolerate the constant trembling of the floor. They have no eyes—there is nothing worth seeing—and they navigate by the temperature gradients radiating from the walls, following the cool, wet seams where the pale moss grows.

Once in a long while, if you stand very still near the edge of a gallery during the quietest part of the machine's cycle, you might feel the brief, leathery displacement of air as something larger glides past in the dark—blind, wing-webbed, riding the warm updrafts between the tension-filaments, hunting the grazers by the faint, wet click of their shells against the mineral crust.

Then the deep thrum begins to climb again, the air thickens with heat, another curtain of condensation lets go from the ceiling, and the vault goes back to its own tireless, indifferent work.
