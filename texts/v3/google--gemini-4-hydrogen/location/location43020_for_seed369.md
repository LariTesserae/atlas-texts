High on the shoulder of a warm massif, tucked just below the level where afternoon clouds usually form, there is a stone amphitheater no larger than a cathedral nave. On three sides, dark, pitted walls of ancient limestone curve inward and overhang the floor; on the fourth, the lip of the terrace drops sheer into a bright, misty gulf, looking out over nothing but air and the pale tops of distant ridges.

It is a loud place, though nothing here is in a hurry.

Warm, damp air rises steadily up the cliff face from the lowlands. When it strikes the cooler, shaded stone of the back wall, it condenses—not in a rush, but in a patient, perpetual sweat. Millions of beads gather along the dark rock, join, and slide down runnels worn smooth over centuries into deep, bowl-like basins carved into the floor. Beneath the terrace, the limestone is honeycombed with flooded cavities and narrow, vertical chimneys that open at the cliff’s edge. As the warm updrafts blow across those wet mouths, the whole stone shelf hums. It is a deep, continuous, organ-like bass note that you feel in your ribs before you hear it with your ears. Every heavy drop that falls forty feet from the overhang into a full basin rings out like a struck bronze bowl, amplified by the curved rock behind it.

Sunlight pours cleanly into the open mouth of the hollow, bright and unfiltered at this altitude, turning the rising mist at the cliff’s lip into pale, shifting halos. The stone floor is warm underfoot where the sun reaches it, slick and dark where the shade lingers, gleaming like wet slate.

Despite the moisture and warmth, the terrace is mostly open, bare rock. There is no turf, no tangle of undergrowth, no carpet of fallen leaves—only wide, echoing expanses of wet stone and shallow mirror-puddles that hold the sky. Standing far apart across this emptiness, rooted in the few deep, silt-choked fissures in the floor, are seven or eight colossal trees. Each one is an extravagant, self-contained jungle: thick, spongy trunks wrapped in emerald moss, broad waxy leaves the size of shields, and heavy curtains of silver-green lichen and epiphytic orchids trailing from every bough. They do not crowd each other. Between their dripping canopies, there is only space, light, and the booming hum of the rock.

The water here is thick with dissolved stone. Over decades, the slow mineral drip has coated the lower trunks and exposed roots in a pale, pearlescent crust, so that the bases of the trees look half-wood, half-travertine, fused into the terrace floor. Hanging from the lower branches are the trees’ strangest growths: translucent, melon-sized leaf-bladders that cup the condensation trickling down the stems. They fill with agonizing slowness—over days, then weeks—swelling heavy and taut in the warm shade. When a bladder finally reaches its tipping point, the stem flexes all at once and dumps a gallon of water onto the hollow stone below. The impact sends a single, thunderous boom rolling around the amphitheater, vibrating in the chest, before the empty sac curls slowly back upright to begin another month of filling.

Almost nothing with blood or wings stays here. The constant vibration of the floor and the lack of cover keep birds and insects away; only the occasional shadow of a high-soaring hawk crosses the sunlit lip without pausing. In the deepest, darkest of the stone basins, a single pale salamander may hang motionless near the bottom, its skin nearly as translucent as the water-bladders above it, waiting out the slow rhythms of the pools.

There are no paths leading up to the terrace, no cairns, no walls, no shelters built against the rock. Yet the place is not entirely unknown. Tucked into a dry crevice behind the roots of the oldest tree rests a single, unhafted wedge of knapped flint, left behind by someone who climbed up from below. On the pale, calcified bark nearby, a neat, healed notch shows where a bone awl once tapped the mineral-sweet sap—caught in a gourd, carried back down the cliff, leaving the stone bowl to its booming, patient equilibrium.
