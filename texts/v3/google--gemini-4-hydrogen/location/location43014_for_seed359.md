The air is warm—a steady, dry, baker’s-loft warmth—and the light is entirely ordinary, the clean, unremarkable brightness of a mid-morning courtyard. Nothing flickers or glows. Yet there is no sky above, only a low, hand-laid ceiling of dry-fitted slate and lashed reed-bundles, and no earth below, only a floor of interlocking, unglazed clay tiles and packed bone-ash. 

The whole place is small enough to take in at a glance, roughly the span of a modest nave or a sunken market-square, bounded on all sides by terraced walls of freshly chiseled limestone, stacked ceramic sherds, and tight-knotted cordage. Every edge is sharp. The chisel marks on the lintels have not softened; the corners of the dry-stone plinths are crisp enough to catch a sleeve. Nothing here has been worn down by wind or rain, because there is no water—not a drop of moisture in the mortar, not a dampness in the air, not a stain on the pale, chalky stone. Everything is made, layer upon layer, by hand and simple tool: adze-trimmed timber pegs, woven grass screens, hammered copper rivets, clay bowls stacked three deep in niches. If you pry up a floor tile, there is only another floor tile beneath it, and beneath that, packed dry-brick, all the way down.

Out of the seams between these hand-built surfaces grows a thick, rustling abundance of dry vegetation. It is lush in the way a full granary or a well-stuffed mattress is lush—dense, layered, near-touching—yet wholly non-aqueous. Broad, papery rosettes of translucent amber membrane crowd the low ledges; trailing vines of brittle, hair-thin silica-fiber drape from the reed rafters like dry cobwebs spun from glass. Mosses here are not soft and wet, but crisp, velvety cushions of ash-gray and ochre that drink the warmth and the steady light directly, puffing out tiny, dry spores when a breeze—or a passing creature—brushes them. Where two walls meet, the plants grow thickest, their dry, serrated leaves interlocking like pages of a book left open in a warm room.

Because the space is small and crowded with shelves, partitions, low walkways, and foliage, it feels dense, almost intimate. And it is mostly quiet. The only regular sounds are the faint, dry *tick* of a cooling stone, the papery sigh of leaves settling against each other, and the soft, sand-on-ceramic rasp of the creatures that live here in great numbers.

The fauna are small, dry-skinned, and numerous: pale, flat-bodied beetles the size of almonds with carapaces like carved horn; slow, six-legged crawlers wrapped in overlapping plates of keratin; and clusters of moth-like gliders whose wings are indistinguishable from the papery leaves they rest against. They do not chirp or buzz. They move with a patient, unhurried deliberation through the chinks in the masonry and across the woven mats, grazing on the dry spore-crusts and shedding skins that dry almost instantly into brittle, translucent husks. It is hard, sometimes, to tell where the fauna end and the craft begins—some of the smaller crawlers glue their molts into the mortar-gaps, and some of the woven baskets seem to twitch slightly when the warmth peaks.

And then, if you stay still long enough to watch the place breathe, you notice that the rules holding it together have quietly given way.

Nothing announces the rupture. The light stays even and calm, the temperature steady. Yet a beetle crawls behind a stacked-clay pillar and emerges from the far side two seconds *before* it went in, so that for a brief, quiet moment there are two of it, walking in parallel before the earlier one simply ceases to be. A shadow cast by a hanging reed screen does not move when the screen sways; instead, the shadow lingers on the limestone wall like dry soot, drifting an inch to the left every ten minutes, long after the screen has stilled.

Distance here is not a fixed quantity, only a polite suggestion. Two identical clay bowls sitting on opposite ledges—both thrown by the same hand, both chipped on the same rim—share a single interior: drop a dry seed into one, and it rattles softly at the bottom of the other. If you walk the length of the central walkway, it takes forty paces; if you turn immediately and walk back, it takes twenty-seven, though no wall has moved and the chisel-marks on the pillars are in the exact same order. Looking down into a narrow gap between two dry-stone plinths, you do not see the floor beneath; you see the underside of the ceiling, three feet away, with a cluster of paper-winged gliders clinging to it upside down.

Even the vegetation obeys this slow, unbothered breakdown. A vine of silica-fiber grows outward from a joint in the timber, its tip uncurling at a glacial pace, while three feet away the exact same vine is growing *backward* into a ceramic jar, neatly un-knitting its own leaves into a tight bud. Neither process disturbs the other. There is no violence in it, no tearing or heat—just a landscape whose bookkeeping has quietly failed, leaving all its hand-made pieces, its dry plants, and its silent creatures to overlap and repeat in the warm, ordinary light.

Change arrives here across long, patient durations. Over hours, the warmth shifts slightly from the east wall to the west, and the dry flora turns a fraction of a degree to follow it, rustling once like turning parchment. A crawler sheds its shell; a shadow finally detaches from its wall and fades into the chalky air; a newly carved stone at the edge of a terrace silently trades places with an identical stone ten paces away. Everything remains dry, bounded, crowded, and strangely serene—a small, hand-built world sitting calmly inside its own impossibility.
