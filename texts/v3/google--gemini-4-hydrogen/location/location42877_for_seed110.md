There is nothing to see here. Light does not enter, and nothing within generates it; the darkness is not a shadow cast by something else, nor an absence waiting for a dawn, but a permanent, physical condition, thick as wet wool pressed against the eyes.

The place reveals itself first through the lungs. The air is thin—the unmistakable, hollow thinness of high altitude, where every breath feels slightly unfinished—yet it is also blood-warm and saturated with water. It clings to the skin like a damp sleeve. To stand still is to feel condensation gathering on the brow and forearms, drawn out of a stillness that has no breeze to stir it.

This is a pocket-scale chamber wedged high inside the ribs of a young mountain, a sealed blister of stone no wider than a small room, though it is impossible to measure it with a glance. Everything here is crowded together. The ceiling hangs low enough to graze a raised hand, and the space between wall and wall is choked with raw, unweathered geology: jagged fins of newly cooled basalt, overlapping shelves of travertine, and dense clusters of mineral needles that jut inward from every surface like the teeth of a closed jaw. Nothing has been smoothed by wind or tumbled by a river. Every edge is crisp, freshly fractured, still carrying the sharp geometry of its making. Yet the making itself has paused. The rock sits in a strange, humid equilibrium—embryonic in form, frozen in its infancy, neither crumbling nor growing.

Water is everywhere, though it never rushes. It weeps steadily from the seams of the ceiling and beads along the sharp ridges of the stone, thick with dissolved lime and iron. It coats the crowded pillars in a warm, slightly slick film, pooling in shallow, irregular cups where the rock folds in on itself. The floor—if a surface broken into countless tiered ledges and narrow crevices can be called a floor—is perpetually awash in an inch or two of tepid, mineral-heavy water that tastes faintly of copper and wet chalk.

In the pitch dark, the density of the place is overwhelming. You cannot take two steps without brushing against a wet stone rib or ducking beneath a hanging curtain of mineral crust. To move through it is to thread a needle by touch alone.

Life here is sparse and indifferent to the sun. Clinging to the wettest, warmest seams of the stone are thin, colorless mats of growth—neither quite moss nor fungus, but something pale and rubbery that feeds on the sulfur and iron bleeding from the rock. Here and there, a few hair-thin roots hang down from the ceiling like damp thread, having forced their way through meters of solid peak from some unseen surface far above. They sway slightly when a drop of water swells and falls from their tips, then return to stillness.

Fauna is rarer still. Occasionally, a palm-sized creature—blind, soft-shelled, and sluggish—clings to the underside of a warm shelf, its body nearly the same temperature as the wet stone. It moves only when the water shifts, sliding a fraction of an inch over the slick mineral skin before settling back into its long, unhurried wait.

Yet the chamber is not entirely untouched. Scattered among the raw stone teeth are small, deliberate interventions—the work of hands that must have navigated this place entirely by feel. A low lip of unmortared stone has been wedged across a natural hollow to deepen a catching-basin for the weeping water. On a prominent rib of basalt near the center of the pocket, someone has ground a series of shallow, horizontal notches with a harder stone—a tactile marker, meant to be read by a passing thumb. In two places, short lengths of braided plant-fiber cord, stiffened and calcified by the mineral mist, remain knotted around narrow rock pillars, stretched taut to guide a traveler between the sharpest drops. The tools that made these marks were simple—chipped stone, bone, twisted grass—and the artifacts are so few and so encrusted with fresh lime that they seem half-swallowed by the geology.

The silence is almost complete. There is no wind to moan through the cracks, no distant roar of subterranean rivers. There is only the rare, heavy *plock* of a drop falling from a stone needle into a shallow pool, the soft, wet sigh of air shifting as someone breathes, and the faint, steady hum of heat rising through the mountain's core—a quiet, blood-warm stillness held intact in the dark, high above a world it never touches.
