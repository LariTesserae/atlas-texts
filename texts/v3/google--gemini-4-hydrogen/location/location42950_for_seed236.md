High above the lower plains, resting on a narrow shelf of newly uplifted basalt, sits a city no larger than a walled orchard. The rock beneath it is geologically young—black, glassy, and fractured along razor-sharp planes that the wind has not yet had time to round. Beyond the perimeter of the shelf, the edges drop away in sheer, unweathered cliffs, isolating this small plateau in clear, thin, sun-warmed air.

There is no water here. No springs well up through the tight seams of the stone, no rain clouds gather at this height, and the air itself is dry enough to parch a tongue in minutes. Yet the shelf is choked with a lush, crowded greenery that rises in thick tiers alongside the architecture. The plants are built for hoarding rather than spending: broad, waxy, copper-green paddles; swollen, segmented trunks wrapped in papery, silver bark; and dense curtains of rigid, articulated vines that grow in straight, angular segments like dry coral. Their roots do not seek soil—there is scarcely any—but wedge themselves into the hairline cracks of the fresh basalt, tapping deep pockets of mineral dampness far below the surface.

The settlement is packed tightly into this small space, its walls and walkways braided so thoroughly into the vegetation that it is hard to tell where the grown world ends and the built world begins. There are no cut timbers, no metal fasteners, no signs of chisels or saws. Instead, the structures are raised through patient, bare-handed labor: unshaped shards of black stone stacked like dry-masonry, locked together with thick, amber-colored plant resin and plastered smooth with a pale, chalky paste of ground gypsum and saliva. Narrow alleys twist between three- and four-story towers, spanned overhead by living bridges of woven, calcified vine. Every surface is close to every other surface. Eaves nearly touch across the lanes; balconies overhang stairways; alcoves are carved into the bases of giant, woody succulents.

Despite this urban density—and despite the astonishing abundance of life—the place is wrapped in a profound, heavy silence.

The silence is partly an accident of materials. The dry gypsum plaster is porous and matte, drinking up echoes; the thick, felt-like mosses that cloak the shaded walls act as baffles; the narrow, labyrinthine streets break the high-altitude breeze into dead, warm pockets of stillness. Nothing rattles, nothing drips, nothing flaps in the wind.

Even the fauna—which teems in every crevice, on every ledge, and along every sunlit parapet—is mute. Thousands of creatures share these close quarters, yet they produce no calls, no songs, no clicking of claws on stone. Broad, flat-bodied lizards with skin the color of oxidized copper bask in dense clusters along the upper terraces, their feet padded with soft, gripping lamellae that make no sound on the plaster. Huge, velvet-winged moths, dusty white and silent as falling ash, drift slowly from one resin-coated eaves-trough to another, probing the waxy joints of the vines for sweet, thick sap. Long-limbed, soft-furred climbers—the builders and tenders of the gypsum towers—move along the narrow bridges with deliberate, unhurried grace, communicating not with their throats, which are smooth and voiceless, but through slow, rhythmic shifts in posture and the flaring of pale throat-pouches.

Everything here operates on a stretched, patient clock. In the warmth of the afternoon, the city seems almost frozen, like a diorama carved from bone and jade. A climber may take a full minute to cross a three-pace bridge, testing each dry vine with a gentle, soundless press of a leathery palm. The lizards turn their heads by degrees to follow the sun. Even the plants move, though too slowly for the eye to catch: over the course of the day, their stiff, jointed leaves pivot a few millimeters on fibrous hinges, angling their waxy faces away from the sharpest glare to keep their internal moisture from cooking away.

When the sun begins to dip toward the sharp horizon of the plateau, the shadows lengthen with crisp, unblurred edges across the pale plaster. The heat radiates gently back out of the black foundation stone. A few of the velvet moths settle onto the warm walls, folding their wings flat; the climbers retreat into the shaded, resin-scented chambers behind the woven screens; and the dense, waterless city settles into the dusk, as quiet as the dry stone beneath it.
