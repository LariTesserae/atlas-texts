The air hits first: thick, wet, and hot enough to make every breath feel like swallowing warm broth. 

This is a sunken basin no larger than a walled hamlet, carved deep into low, waterlogged ground and roofed over by a sagging canopy of rusted iron trusses and thick, grime-fouled glass. Far above, the outside sky is only a rumor—a bruised, olive-amber dimness that filters down through mineral-crusted panes and narrow gaps in the surrounding basalt walls. Nothing here receives direct sun. Everything sits in a permanent, steaming twilight.

Within the hollow, the floor is mostly water. A broad, shallow lagoon of dark, mirror-slick water covers the sunken stone, broken up by wide, submerged causeways and widely spaced islands of layered brickwork. The architecture is distinctly urban in its ambition—multi-tiered arches, octagonal pump-towers, elevated iron catwalks, and brick-vaulted boiler houses—yet the structures stand far apart, separated by generous, echoing expanses of open water. You can see across the whole basin in a single sweep, watching the vertical silhouettes of smokestacks and gantry cranes dissolve into the low-hanging mist.

The place is deafening. Even before your eyes adjust to the gloom, the sound fills every corner of the enclosure: a layered, relentless industrial roar. Heavy hydraulic rams thud in slow, staggered rhythms beneath the waterline, sending visible concentric ripples across the black lagoons. High above, sluice gates groan open on rusted chains, dumping sheets of scalding, mineral-heavy water down tiered brick spillways. Steam vents shriek at irregular intervals, releasing white plumes that flatten against the glass ceiling, condense into heavy droplets, and rain back down in a continuous, warm drizzle.

Everything is weathered into an old, settled balance. The red brick of the pump-houses is softened by decades of salt-bloom and verdigris; the brass pressure gauges are clouded green; the iron walkways are pitted and slick. Yet the machinery does not feel abandoned so much as autonomous—an enclosed, self-perpetuating mechanical organ that has long outlasted whatever crews originally built it. Gears turn, flywheels spin behind iron grates, and automated scraper-blades sweep back and forth across the intake screens with patient, blind regularity.

Clinging to this wet, vibrating skeleton is a lush, strange flora. Without bright sunlight, the plants have abandoned greens for deeper, oilier shades—bruised violet, dark copper, and translucent, waxy olive. Great curtains of thick moss hang from the undersides of the catwalks, heavy with condensation and swaying in the updrafts from the boilers. Fleshy, broad-leaved ferns burst from every mortar joint in the brickwork. 

Strangest of all are the pale, thick-stemmed creepers that wrap directly around the hottest steam pipes and engine casings. Drawn to the warmth and the constant vibration, their root-tendrils grip the scorching iron, pulsing slightly in time with the pistons beneath. Because the air is so rich with warmth and dissolved minerals, the vegetation grows at an unsettling pace—uncurling new fronds within hours, swelling with water, and periodically dropping heavy, fluid-filled seed pods into the lagoons with loud *plops*. The machines fight back on a fixed schedule: every few minutes, a rotating scythe clears a clogged spillway, or a sudden burst of superheated steam blasts a manifold clean, stripping the encroaching vines back to wet pulp before the cycle begins again.

Despite the lushness and the constant, churning motion, there is not a single animal here. No birds nest in the high trusses; no insects hover over the warm pools; no rats scurry along the brick ledges. The water is too hot and too rich in dissolved copper, and the endless sub-bass shudder of the engines makes the stone itself inhospitable to anything with nerves. There is only the wet heat, the dim amber gloom, the deafening pulse of water and iron, and the silent, relentless greening of the walls.
