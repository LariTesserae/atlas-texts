The light arrives here without anything to soften it. At this elevation, the sky is a hard, pale violet-white, so bright it bleaches the color out of the stone until the whole valley reads in shades of chalk, bone, and warm ash. The air is thin, yet the basin holds the heat like a shallow dish—sunlight baking the broad, wind-planed limestone and basalt flats, radiating back upward in shimmering, silent thermals.

You can see from one rim of the plateau to the other, a stretch of fifteen or twenty miles of worn, settled terrain. Everything here has been in its place for an immense stretch of time. The ridges are rounded down to smooth, sleeping backs; the dry washes are wide and gently sloped, their cobbles polished to a dull satin by centuries of grit and occasional flash-runoff. There are no sharp, newly broken cliffs. Even the boulders look as though they have sunk halfway into the earth and made peace with it.

Water is present, though you rarely see it flowing. It exists as a stubborn dampness tucked into the architecture of the stone. In the lee of the larger outcrops, dark, velvety cushions of moss and waxy, salt-crusted scrub cling to fissures that stay cool enough to trap the night’s moisture. Scattered across the central flats are shallow mineral pans—wide, crusted saucers of pale clay that hold an inch or two of brackish water for a few hours after dawn before the sun draws it back up into the thin air.

Set miles apart across this open expanse are the structures. There are no roads between them, no foundations or scaffolding—just solitary, small-scale artifacts that seem to have weathered alongside the mountain. One might be a slender, matte-black needle, thirty feet tall, driven at a slight angle into a dry wash; another, three miles away, is a low, curving ribbon of pearlescent material that arches out of the scree and disappears back into it without leaving a seam. 

The engineering behind them belongs to no recognizable lineage of metal or stone. They do not heat up in the fierce sun; touch the black needle at noon and it feels neutrally cool, almost absent, as if your hand were resting against a patch of shade rather than a solid object. Near the pearlescent arches, the air behaves oddly. Light bends a fraction of a degree too sharply around their edges, and the shadows they cast sometimes lag behind the movement of the sun, or split into faint, double-edged silhouettes. Their purpose—if they still have one—has settled into the rhythm of the landscape. Every afternoon, as the heat peaks, the arches draw moisture directly out of the dry, cloudless air, condensing it along their undersides in beads that drift *upward* for a few inches before losing their strange buoyancy and pattering down onto the rocks below, feeding small, permanent pools of impossibly pure water.

Despite the sparsity of the scrub and the wide, empty miles between landmarks, the plateau is thick with life. It is an eerie, crowded place, because almost all of it is silent.

The thin atmosphere carries sound poorly, and the creatures here have evolved to ignore it altogether. Great herds of long-limbed, soft-padded grazers move across the limestone flats, cropping the tough lichens and drinking from the condensation pools beneath the arches. Their hooves are split and cushioned with thick, spongy keratin; fifty of them can trot past at a hundred yards and produce nothing louder than a faint, papery rustle. Instead of calling to one another, they communicate with the bright light: broad, iridescent flaps of skin along their necks and flanks snap open and shut in rapid, rhythmic flashes—silver, cobalt, copper—signaling alarm or direction across miles of glare.

Overhead, riding the warm updrafts without a single wingbeat, are dozens of wide, membranous gliders. They look like tattered kites of translucent parchment, drifting in slow, intersecting spirals as they watch the herds below. When one dives, it folds its ribs and drops like a stone, making no whistle in the thin air, striking a straggler or a basking rock-lizard in complete quiet. 

Around the damp bases of the artifacts, smaller life swarms. Pale, segmented burrowers with shells that glint like mica tunnel through the moist clay, harvesting the mineral crusts. Tiny, wingless insects—bright crimson against the chalky stone—cluster by the thousands on the shaded sides of the arches, drinking the upward-falling mist. 

Nothing here is still for long, yet the stillness of the air remains unbroken. The wind moves across the open basin in broad, steady sheets, too smooth to whistle, carrying only the dry scent of hot stone, ozone from the needles, and the faint, sharp tang of wet clay. By late afternoon, the thermals thicken, the gliders rise until they vanish into the glare, and the herds begin their slow, flashing trek toward the higher ridges, leaving the scattered artifacts to hum—just below the threshold of hearing, a faint vibration you feel only in your teeth—as they pull tomorrow’s water out of the empty sky.
