At the roof of the range, miles above the timberline, the mountain opens into a broad, shallow basin of bleached stone. It is an ancient, settled place—a high-altitude saddle several miles across, ringed by worn, round-shouldered ridges that have long since stopped shedding scree. The air is thin, cool, and fiercely bright. Under a sky the color of bruised indigo, the unfiltered sun pours down without mercy, turning the pale quartzite and limestone terraces blindingly white and sharpening every shadow to a hard, black edge.

The first thing that strikes you—before the glare, before the thinness of the air—is the sound. It is not a noise that rises and falls; it is a permanent, physical wall of pressure. A steady, laminar river of upper-atmosphere wind pours ceaselessly over the western rim of the basin, and the basin itself is shaped to receive it. Millennia of erosion have carved the exposed rock into vast concave dishes, undercut overhangs, and honeycombed ribs. Together, they act as a single, miles-wide instrument. A deep, sub-bass hum vibrates up through the soles of your feet and settles in your sternum, overlaid by a relentless, glassy shriek from the thinner stone flutes along the ridges. You cannot hear your own voice, nor the crunch of gravel under your boots. The wind does not gust, and the tone never wavers. It is an equilibrium so complete that the roar feels less like weather and more like a property of the rock itself.

Across the wide, open floor of the basin, there is almost nothing. Great empty stretches of scoured stone and pale grit separate the weathered outcrops, giving the whole valley a hollow, swept-clean quality. Yet the place is dry only at first glance. There are no streams, no snowfields, and no rainstorms, but the cool air sheds its moisture wherever the sun cannot reach. Deep inside the undercut stone ribs and along the shaded undersides of the ledges, condensation gathers in a slow, constant sweat. It drips into saucer-shaped depressions in the rock, forming isolated pockets of clear, ice-cold water scattered across the bone-dry flats.

Because the bedrock never stops humming, the water in these basins is never still. Every pool is locked in a pattern of permanent standing waves—tight, geometric lattices of tiny ripples that catch the fierce sunlight like cut crystal, trembling in place without ever spilling over the rim. 

Life clings tightly to these damp margins and to the air itself, leaving the wide stone terraces bare. Vegetation is sparse and low: tough, silver-grey crusts of lichen, leathery cushions of moss drinking the mist along the edges of the vibrating pools, and the occasional stunted shrub with waxy, needle-thin leaves pressed flat against the leeward side of boulders.

Animal life, by contrast, is everywhere, though adapted to a world without silence or shelter. High in the blinding air, hundreds of pale, long-winged birds hang motionless in the steady updrafts. They do not circle or flap; they simply lock their wings and ride the unvarying current like kites, their primary feathers thrumming softly in the wind, watching the empty stone below. Down in the shaded crevices and beneath the hollow limestone arches live dense colonies of stout, thick-furred rodents. Entirely deaf, with ears reduced to flat, scarred flaps, they navigate the maze of tunnels by reading the vibrations in the stone through their paws. They emerge in brief, darting bursts to clip the moss around the water pockets, carrying it back into the dark to dry, while swarms of tiny, iridescent gnats and springtails coat the wet rock rims, thriving in the fine spray kicked up by the singing pools.

Nothing here is built. There are no walls, no cairns, no paths worn into the stone. Only if you look closely near the shaded water pockets do you find the faintest trace of hands: a scatter of chipped quartzite flakes beside a dry hollow where someone once sat to knap a blade, or a simple snare of twisted sinew and bone wedged tightly into a rodent run—small, weathered remnants left behind by travelers who came for water or meat, and wisely built nothing that the wind would have to tear down.
