The plateau is old enough to have forgotten how it was lifted. At this elevation, the peaks have long since been planed away by frost and time, leaving a continental tableland of dark slate and weathered basalt that stretches until the curvature of the world takes it. Everything here has reached its final angle. The scree slopes do not slide; the boulder fields are sorted into vast, ancient polygons by ten thousand winters of freeze and thaw, their edges rounded and settled deep into the grit.

Light arrives dim and diffused, filtered through a permanent, thin ceiling of high-altitude stratus. There are no sharp shadows—only a flat, pewter-colored day that makes distances impossible to judge. The air is thin and cold, carrying the metallic stillness of high places just above or below the freezing point. Nothing here is dry, yet nothing flows. Moisture exists as a patient dampness: a dark sheen on the fractured slate, beads of condensation clinging to the underside of stones, and shallow, mineral-heavy pans of water that sit motionless in the rock basins. Because of the dissolved salts, these pans rarely freeze solid, even when rime frost feathers the windward edges of the rocks around them.

Vegetation barely holds a footing. Where dampness gathers in the seams of the stone, crustose lichens spread in slow, overlapping maps of pale ochre, verdigris, and soot-black. In the deeper hollows, tight cushions of moss and ankle-high wire-sedge cling to the grit, growing at a rate measured in millimeters per decade. Between these pockets lie miles of bare, wet stone and mist.

At first glance, the plateau appears entirely untouched—a vast, empty wilderness with nothing built upon it. There are no towers, no roads, no walls against the cold. Yet the ground hums with a faint, high-tension stillness, and the dampness smells faintly of ozone and warm copper.

Beneath the weathered crust, the plateau is an industrial engine. Across hundreds of miles, the rock has been quietly bored, wired, and turned into a solid-state refinery. Because the air is thin and the cold is deep, the machinery here runs without combustion or moving gears; it relies on superconducting conduits laid in trenches beneath the scree, massive electrostatic condenser-beds, and slow, automated electro-osmotic leaching. On the surface, the only signs of this scaled production are flush-mounted ceramic junction plates weathered to the color of the slate, hairline trenches where the lichen refuses to grow, and the occasional low, frost-caked vent-slit breathing out a silent, invisible plume of warm, dry gas. Once in a great while, across miles of empty stone, a low-slung, trackless collector-sled inches forward on wide, padded rollers—unmanned, encrusted with salt and lichen, moving at the pace of a glacier to harvest condensed minerals from the brine-pans.

The silence is absolute. The thin air carries almost nothing; the solid-state currents make no roar, and the mist settles without a patter. If you stand still, you hear only the blood in your own ears and, very rarely, the faintest, dry *tick* of a rock contracting in the cold.

And yet, the plateau is overwhelmingly alive.

Because the emptiness between things is so vast, you do not notice the fauna all at once. It reveals itself in layers, dense and silent and everywhere. Lift any flat slab of wet basalt and the underside is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with thousands of pale, blind, velvet-winged moths and flat-bodied beetles, motionless, drawing warmth from the buried induction lines below. Kneel beside one of the shallow brine-pans and the grey water is thick—almost soupy—with millions of translucent, slow-drifting isopods, grazing on the mineral-rich biofilm that forms over the electrostatic plates.

Across the open expanse, the larger creatures move in vast, loose, silent rivers. There is immense space between the herds—miles of bare stone and drifting fog—so the land still feels sparse, yet the sheer number of animals on the plateau is staggering. They are heavy-coated, broad-footed grazers and striders with thick, fibrous pads instead of hooves, built to walk without sound over wet rock. They have no vocal cords; they communicate through posture, chemical trails, and the faint static charge held in their long, oily guard-hairs.

When they migrate, they do not follow the contours of the valleys or the ridges. Instead, they walk in dead-straight, miles-long columns, cutting across the ancient stone polygons with uncanny precision—navigating by the weak, buried magnetic fields of the superconducting grids beneath their feet. Looking out from a low rise, you might see nothing but dim mist and damp slate for three miles in every direction, and then a single, silent line of ten thousand pale backs moving slowly through the fog, and three miles beyond them, another line, and another on the horizon, all heading toward the warm seepage-basins where the subterranean refineries vent their waste heat.

High above, barely visible against the pewter clouds, vast, silent gliders—birds with six-foot wingspans of mottled grey—ride the weak, slow thermals rising off the buried condenser fields. They never cry out. They simply circle in slow, patient spirals, waiting for the herds to stir something from the moss, or for a sick strider to lie down beside a warm vent-slit and freeze into the quiet, salt-crusted ground.
