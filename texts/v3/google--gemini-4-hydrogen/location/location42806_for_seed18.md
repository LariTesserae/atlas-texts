At the extreme crown of a needle-thin masonry and steel spire, far above the cloud deck, sits a single enclosed chamber no larger than a modest cathedral. From the outside, it is a weathered bronze-and-iron drum riveted to the sky; inside, it is an almost entirely empty volume of warm, roaring air.

There is no natural stone here, no soil, no bedrock. Every surface is fabricated: curved plates of oxidized copper, cast-iron ribs bolted at heavy intervals, and thick acoustic baffling made of crazed, mineral-stained ceramic. Yet the center of this space—hundreds of cubic meters of it—contains almost nothing. You can stand on the narrow, grated perimeter walkway and look straight across the hollow void to the far wall, unobstructed by pillars, scaffolding, or clutter. The air itself is the room’s primary occupant, and it is under immense, steady pressure.

The sound is deafening—not a clatter or a shriek, but a colossal, bone-deep industrial drone that saturates the space until it feels solid. Built into the thick double-hull of the walls are vast, slow-turning flywheels, steam manifolds, and heavy electromagnetic coils. They operate on a glacial rhythm. A piston the size of a tree trunk takes nearly a minute to complete a single stroke; a geared ring-shaft turns with the unhurried patience of a clock’s hour hand. Yet the torque behind these slow cycles vibrates the entire metal shell at a low, constant frequency. The air in the hollow core hums so fiercely that loose droplets of water tremble in mid-air before falling.

Despite the freezing, thin atmosphere outside the drum, the waste heat from the wall-engines keeps the interior perpetually warm and muggy. Moisture is scarce but persistent—a dampness born of condensation rather than rain. Whenever cold cloud-wisps slip through the high, slatted louvers in the ceiling, they flash into warm mist against the heated iron ribs, beading along the rivets and trickling down the curved plates in slow, mineral-heavy threads.

Along the inner walls, this warmth and dampness have nurtured a lush, dense jungle—yet it grows according to the strange physics of the room’s sound. The constant vibration of the hull creates invisible acoustic nodes and antinodes across the metal skin. Where the plates shudder most violently, the bronze is scrubbed bare, gleaming and hot to the touch. But along the still nodal lines, where the vibrations cancel out, vegetation has anchored itself in thick, geometric bands and lattices. Cushions of spongy moss, cascading curtains of broad-leaved ferns, and woody, aerial-rooted vines cling to the quiet seams. They form a vivid, dripping tapestry of green arranged in eerie, mathematical rings and chevrons around the otherwise bare metal walls.

Everything here has settled into a long, weathered equilibrium. Centuries of condensation have painted the iron with stable crusts of orange rust and the copper with chalky verdigris. The plants have wedged their roots deep into old bolt-holes, sealing ancient steam leaks with their own fibrous mass, while the ceramic baffles are glazed with a thin, permanent patina of lime and algae.

Normal daylight enters from above through a ring of thick, salt-streaked glass skylights set into the dome, slanting down through the empty center in clear, angled shafts. In that light, the room’s abundant fauna becomes visible—mostly small, strange creatures adapted to a world of empty space and ceaseless noise.

Tiny, iridescent frogs and arboreal crustaceans crowd the water-filled cups of the wall-bromeliads, insulated from the roar by the thick moss mats. Across the bare, vibrating stretches of bronze graze colonies of flat, metallic-shelled snails; their shells are ridged and pitched to the room’s exact resonant frequency, allowing them to glide over the trembling plates without shattering. Now and then, a leathery, translucent glider—something between a moth and a lizard—launches itself from the upper vines. It does not flap, but spreads wide, papery membranes to ride the slow thermal updrafts and acoustic pressure waves, drifting lazily across the vast, hollow center before catching hold of the lush lattice on the opposite wall.

Down at the bottom, the floor is a shallow, concave dish of bare cast iron, swept clean by the downward hum of the air. A thin sheen of condensation glistens at its center, where a single grated drain swallows the occasional drop of water, returning it to the slow, unseen engines humming beneath the skin of the world.
