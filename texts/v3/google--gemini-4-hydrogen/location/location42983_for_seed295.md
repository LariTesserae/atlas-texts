The first thing that meets you is not sight, but pressure: a warm, damp wall of sound that pushes against the eardrums and vibrates in the ribs. It is too dim here for details to resolve quickly—only a faint, gray-green twilight filters down through the overhead tangle, leaving the lower reaches in near-darkness. In that gloom, the noise is total. It is a layered, ceaseless roar made of millions of small things: the dry, rattling percussion of wings against hardened clay, the high, sharp clicking of echolocating bats, the resonant drumming of insects striking their heads against hollow walls, and beneath it all, a wet, rhythmic chorus of frogs rising from unseen basins.

When your eyes adjust to the thin light, you realize there is no bare rock or raw earth anywhere. Every surface—floor, pillar, arch, and terrace—has been made.

This is a town-sized expanse perched high on a mountain plateau, though the mountain itself is buried. Over centuries, generations of colonial builders—insects, birds, burrowing mammals, and mud-daubing wasps—have raised an interconnected labyrinth of hardened clay, chewed wood-pulp, saliva-cemented grit, and woven plant fiber. The structures have grown together and fused into a single, continuous architecture: vaulted galleries, buttressed ridges, tapering ventilation chimneys, and sunken courts, all terraced into the high slope. There is no natural substrate left to stand on; even the floor beneath your feet is a crusted, multi-layered shell of packed earth and old, trampled comb, worn smooth by millions of passing bodies.

The light is scarce because the city is roofed. Lush highland vegetation has rooted directly into the nutrient-rich, composted skins of the upper mounds. Thick-stemmed lianas, strangler figs, and mats of moss and fern have knitted themselves across the tops of the earthen arches, forming a dense, living ceiling that blocks almost all the high-altitude sun. What little light gets through arrives as pale, diffuse shafts through narrow gaps or down the throats of the tall mud chimneys, catching the drifting motes of dust and spore-haze before dying against the dark walls.

Outside this living roof, the mountain air is thin and cool, and heavy clouds press against the upper ridges. Inside, however, the air is thick and warm. The massive earthen walls hold heat like a kiln, fed by the metabolic warmth of countless crowded bodies and the slow, steady fermentation of damp leaf-litter and fungus gardens tucked into the deeper chambers. 

Water here is a matter of condensation and patience rather than rain or rivers. The cool mountain mist seeps through the high vents and meets the warm interior air, beading along the clay ribs and the undersides of broad fern fronds. It gathers into slow, heavy drips that fall into dark, mineral-rimmed catch-basins and shallow hollows worn into the constructed floor. Between these damp pockets, the clay is merely cool and slightly tacky to the touch, darkened by moisture but never washed away.

The architecture is ancient and settled. Nothing looks freshly broken or newly raised; the sharp edges of old mud-daubing have been rounded by centuries of condensation, gentle drafts, and the constant brushing of fur, chitin, and feathers. Where an arch has slumped, roots have braced it; where a wall has thinned, new layers of grit and pulp have been plastered over the scar. The whole valley-sized structure sits in a state of long-held equilibrium, changing only by millimeters a year.

And every millimeter of it is occupied. The density of life is overwhelming, stacked in layers from the floor to the root-bound ceiling. Blind crickets, millipedes, and beetles carpet the warm ledges and stream along worn highways in the clay. Tree frogs and geckos cling motionless to the damp undersides of arches, waiting near the catch-basins. Above them, woven nests and mud-cups hang like fruit from the aerial roots, while roosting bats pack the upper vaults shoulder-to-shoulder, their bodies shifting in dark, breathing ripples. Swiftlets thread the dimness at speed, navigating the narrow, echoing corridors between pillars without touching a wall, while snakes and large spiders wait in the dense curtains of hanging moss.

There are no tools here, no cut stone, no metal or cordage—only what jaws, claws, beaks, and secretions can shape, piled and plastered over lifetimes. Despite the deafening, frantic noise of the creatures filling every gallery, the place itself moves at a glacial pace. The drips fill the basins drop by drop; the figs thicken their grip on the clay buttresses decade by decade; the warm, heavy air rises slowly up the chimneys into the mountain mist, carrying the hum of the hidden city out into the high, quiet sky.
