The air here is thin, bright, and scalding.

At this elevation, the sky is a clear, ordinary blue, rinsed clean by altitude, yet the light drops onto a basin that radiates heat like an open kiln. There is no stone underfoot, no gravel, no soil. The entire valley-sized plateau is a taut, living floor—a continuous, engineered-looking expanse of pale, olive-ochre plant skin stretched over a submerged lattice of thick, fibrous ribs. Wherever you step, the surface gives slightly, like a wet drumhead, and warm water wells up around your boots from the shallow film sheeting across the terrace.

The scale of the place is vast and mostly empty. Looking out across the steaming flats, the horizon is miles away, rimmed by jagged, freshly grown ridges of hardened cane that haven't yet been dulled by weather. Everything in sight is built, yet nothing has been touched by a hand or a tool. The substrate itself is pure architecture: hexagonal catchment basins, long, low spillways with razor-sharp, unweathered lips, and broad, ribbed causeways that angle across the flooded flats with geometric precision.

Rising from these wide, open expanses are the towers—scattered, solitary presences separated by hundreds of yards of shimmering, ankle-deep water. They look like skeletal cathedrals or giant, half-finished cranes: tall, tapering lattices of bleached, bone-hard reed, braced by flying buttresses of braided vine and topped with broad, translucent leaf-sails. Up high, where the rising heat meets the cold mountain air, the sails constantly weep. Mist condenses along their veins in heavy, silver beads, running down the vertical flutes in steady, glittering curtains that splash onto the hot terraces below and immediately curl back into steam.

There are no birds circling the spires. There are no insects skimming the warm pools, no frogs, no scurrying things in the joints of the buttresses. Nothing breathes here except the structures themselves—and they are deafening.

The silence of an animal-free world is completely drowned out by the violence of botanical hydraulics. A deep, rhythmic *groan* rolls across the flat as a distant aqueduct shifts its weight, its freshly extruded joints swelling with hot sap. Seconds later, a sound like a rifle shot cracks through the humid air: a tensioned vine-cable, stretched too tight by a sudden growth spurt, has snapped, whipping back into the water with a heavy, boiling splash. 

Nothing stays still for long. This is a turbulent, hyperactive landscape in a state of perpetual construction and collapse. Half a mile off, a bulbous, translucent cistern-dome—swollen to the size of a house with condensed rainwater—shudders, splits along a seam, and ruptures with a low, booming *whump*. A wall of hot water sheets out across the sparse terrace, flattening nothing, because there is nothing small or delicate in its way; it simply floods the shallow basin and drains through the slatted floor into the dark, tangled under-lattice below.

Elsewhere, the architecture is furiously assembling itself. From the jagged, splintered stump of a recently collapsed tower, thick, pale tendrils are already uncoiling in real time, braiding around one another with wet, audible *clicks* as their cellulose locks into place. Within minutes, a new, sharp-edged truss angles skyward, glistening with raw sap, already reaching for the upper currents of mist.

The heat is metabolic. Put a palm against one of the main structural ribs and it feels feverish, pulsing faintly with the rapid, pressurized movement of water and sugars. The plants here do not merely grow; they build, compete, over-pressurize, and shatter. A tall lattice-sail intercepts the high-altitude moisture, channels it down to fuel a burst of frantic construction, overheats in the bright sun, and tears itself apart under its own tension—only for the wreckage to be immediately knitted back into the floor by creeping, fibrous runners.

Everywhere you look, the sharp, raw edges of new growth stand beside the wet, ragged splinters of sudden failure. The wind carries no scent of rot, only the sharp, green tang of crushed stems, boiled sap, and hot rain, blowing across miles of open, drumming skin under a bright, indifferent sky.
