The valley sits high above the cloud line, a broad, stepped tableland of dark, newly fractured stone that has not yet had time to soften. Its ridges and shelf-edges are sharp, cleaved into clean angles and flat, tilted planes of grey basalt. Despite the altitude, the air is warm and heavy with moisture. There is no wind to speak of, only a slow, steady condensation: invisible vapor rising from unseen lowlands below the rim, cooling just enough against the stone to bead on every horizontal surface and collect in the joints of the rock.

Water is everywhere here, though none of it rushes. The cliff faces weep continuously, dark with slick films that slide without a ripple into broad, shallow basins at their feet. Across the wide floor of the plateau, water stands in thin sheets over flat stone, filling natural depressions and quarried hollows alike. Because the rock is young and hard, and the gradients are gentle, the water carves almost nothing. It simply rests and overflows in silent, glassy layers, keeping the stone permanently dark and the air thick with the clean, mineral scent of wet dust and moss.

Spread thinly across this open expanse is a city of fitted stone. Its footprint is vast—stretching from the upper terraces down toward the center of the basin—yet it feels mostly empty, with wide stretches of wet rock and reed-choked flats separating its clusters. Everything is built by hand from the same sharp-edged slabs that make up the valley floor, stacked dry or bedded in pale mortar, with no sign of iron, forged fittings, or wheeled haulage. Long, straight causeways raise dry paths just inches above the standing water, linking scattered neighborhoods of low, flat-roofed dwellings, open-walled pavilions, and stepped plazas.

What binds the settlement together is its waterwork. Between the sparse clusters of houses run miles of shallow, hand-chiseled channels, level sluiceways, and square settling-tanks, all laid out with patient geometry to catch the seepage from the upper cliffs and guide it evenly across the terraces. The masonry is crisp and intact. Stone spillways lip the water from one level to the next in thin, unbroken curtains that barely disturb the surface below.

In the seams between the flagstones and along the damp margins of the canals, vegetation grows thick and undisturbed. Cushions of bright green moss swell over the mortared edges; broad-leafed ferns unfurl from shaded crevices; waxy, thick-stemmed shrubs and tall, straight reeds crowd the shallower basins. Nothing chews the leaves, tramples the borders, or nests in the stalks. There are no birds crossing the open sky, no frogs in the cisterns, no insects skimming the flat, bright surfaces of the pools.

Because nothing moves quickly here, the valley holds a deep, steady quiet. The light is clear and even, reflecting off the wet pavements and the motionless water in broad, pale sheets. Now and then, a single drop gathers on the lip of an overhang and falls into a full tank with a small, distinct *plink* that carries across an empty plaza, then fades back into stillness. The water levels remain constant, the warm air hangs unchanged, and the whole high basin rests in a slow, permanent balance—stone, water, and green growth holding their places without wear or haste.
