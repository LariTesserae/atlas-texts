The ground is not ground. Beneath the thin film of warm condensation, there is no bedrock, no loam, no ancient strata of compressed sea—only the flat, endless joinery of laid slate and hand-troweled mortar, stretching out until the dim air swallows it.

It is hot here, with the heavy, stifling heat of a kiln whose fire was raked out an hour ago, and everything is wet. The sky—if it is a sky, and not merely an upper ceiling of unlit vapor—hangs low and close, pressing a tepid, mineral-scented dampness against the skin. Water does not fall as rain so much as it *arrives* on surfaces: beading along the crisp, unweathered edges of terracotta tiles, weeping from the seams of freshly stacked mud-brick, pooling in shallow, mirror-still basins that have been pressed into the floor by something the size of a palm, or a knee, or a wooden float.

Because nothing here is worn down. Every corner is sharp enough to nick a finger. Where a stone slab meets its neighbor, the chisel-marks along the bevel are raw and powdery, untouched by wind or the passage of feet. In the damp plaster of the low causeways, you can still see the sweeping arcs of a trowel, the parallel ridges of a toothed comb, the occasional thumbprint left in wet grey slip—yet there are no hands. There are no animals, no insects skimming the warm puddles, no birds in the thick twilight, not even a thread of moss or green scum clinging to the sluice-channels. It is a world made entirely by craft, without a single living thing to claim it.

And it is vast—a low, sunken continent of terraces and empty plazas, so wide that it breeds its own sluggish weather. Great, silent fronts of steam peel off the hotter sectors of the plain and drift across the cooler slates, condensing into tepid sheets that slide across the paving without a sound. Out in the dimness, where the light is no brighter than the inside of a shuttered room at dusk, the horizon does not curve away so much as it blurs into a bruised, amber-grey haze.

Across these miles of flat, damp masonry, structures appear only rarely, separated by long, lonely stretches of bare floor. You might walk for an hour across sweating flagstones before encountering a single, waist-high wall of sun-dried clay, lashed at its corners with braided mineral twine and capped with hammered copper flashing. Beyond it, half a mile further on, stands a cluster of hollow, corbeled plinths, or a sunken cistern fed by a hand-carved runnel whose water flows steadily uphill, slipping over the lip of the basin in a silent, unbroken sheet.

Nothing stays where it is put, though the violence of its changing is strangely quiet.

There is no roar of collapse, no grinding of tectonic plates. Instead, the landscape churns with the muted, uncanny restlessness of wet clay on a wheel. A quarter-mile to your left, a flat expanse of slate begins to blister and heave, rising in a slow, soundless dome until the slabs part—not shattering, but neatly un-keying themselves—to reveal a fresh course of damp, pale brick pushing up from beneath, already mortared, already weeping water. Behind you, a low arcade of mud-brick arches that seemed solid a moment ago quietly slumps, its sharp corners softening into grey paste, its copper pins dropping with faint, distant *tinks* onto the wet floor before the paste itself flattens out, smooths itself with an invisible screed, and dries into a fresh plaza.

Sometimes the change is not physical so much as structural—a quiet breakdown in the rules of how built things occupy space. You walk beside a retaining wall that is six paces long, only to look back and find it has stretched into a causeway vanishing into the mist, while the cistern you were walking toward has folded itself inside-out, its wet, plastered interior now facing the dim sky like a blister. Distances refuse to agree with the effort of walking them. A pile of unshaped, raw-tempered clay sits beside a kiln-fired lintel, and both occupy the exact same patch of ground, flickering between states as the warm mist rolls over them, as if the world cannot decide whether it is still gathering its materials or has already finished the job.

The air carries almost no sound. The turbulence of this place is muffled by its own wetness and the softness of its unbaked surfaces. What little you hear is intimate and close: the slow *drip-drip* of condensation falling from the underside of a lintel; the faint, wet *shhh* of a freshly laid terrace settling into the void beneath it; the occasional, inexplicable *clack* of a ceramic peg dropping into place somewhere out in the dimness, miles from anything else.

If you pry up one of the loose flagstones at the edge of a shifting terrace, there is no dirt underneath. There is only another, slightly different layer of fitted stone, and beneath that, damp plaster, and beneath that, a warm, colorless void that smells of wet lime and hot iron—an emptiness waiting to be tiled over, and then torn up, and then laid down again, forever in the dim, sweating quiet.
