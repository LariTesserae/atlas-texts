The first thing that strikes anyone descending into the White Hollow is the glare. There is no sky here—somewhere miles overhead, a ceiling of ancient, permafrost-bound roots and frozen silt seals this place off from the surface world—yet the darkness you would expect underground does not exist. Instead, the air is filled with a fierce, shadowless, actinic brightness, cold as a winter noon on a glacier.

The light comes from the ground itself, and from the walls, and from the vast, arching ribs that span the emptiness. Every solid surface in this continent-sized cavern is living plant matter. There is no exposed stone, no soil, no gravel. The terrain is an unbroken, miles-thick tapestry of pale, fibrous flora—giant, hollow-stemmed bryophytes, translucent bladder-sheets, and braided root-cables as thick as ships. Beneath their waxy, frost-dusted skins, pressurized sap-veins pulse with a cold, chemical luminescence, pouring out a harsh white-silver light that bounces endlessly off the damp surfaces and hangs in the freezing air like fog-bound sunlight.

It is bitterly cold. Breath plumes instantly and settles as rime on wool and eyelashes. The air is damp—not wet enough for rain, but heavy with clinging moisture that condenses on every ridge and solidifies into thin, glassy crusts along the older stems. In the hollows of giant, cupped leaves, pockets of brackish, half-frozen water gather, ringed with needle-ice. Everything feels crisp, taut, and on the verge of snapping.

And yet, despite the total dominance of plant life, the space itself feels staggeringly empty. You can stand on a cantilevered shelf of pale, rigid pith and look out across thirty miles of open, brilliantly lit air before your eyes meet the next great curtain of hanging tendrils or the opposite wall of braided trunks. The Hollow is vast enough to brew its own quiet weather: slow, silent eddies of freezing mist that drift like ghosts across the abyss, and towering, sunlit plumes of luminescent spores rising on faint thermal currents from deep, unseen vents below.

Because of the scale, the silence is profound. Even when the landscape tears itself apart—and it does so constantly—the sound arrives only as a distant, muffled sigh or a faint, crystalline *tick* carried across miles of empty air.

For this is a violently restless place. Nothing here is settled or weathered. The forms of the terrain are sharp, jagged, and newly made: freshly split vascular ridges with raw, ragged edges, sudden spires of extruded pith, and brittle, lacy bridges that look like shattered glass. The flora grows with a manic, turbulent urgency, driven by the chemical heat of its own luminescence. Over the course of a single day, a flat mat of fibrous moss can swell with cold sap, buckle upward into a jagged ridge fifty feet high, and freeze solid. By the next week, the internal pressure may drop, or a new, faster-growing shoot may burst through from beneath, causing the entire structure to shear apart and collapse into the misty depths below. 

Walking here means crossing a world in mid-sentence. You hear the soft, rhythmic *creak* of expanding fibers beneath your boots, the hiss of pressurized moisture venting through pinholes in the bark, and the occasional sharp *crack* as a frost-rimed frond gives way. Pathways do not last. Valleys fold shut; chasms open where solid bridges stood three sleeps ago.

Animal life is rare, scattered thin across the immense, blinding void. Most creatures here are built for stillness or for the long glide between distant walls. Occasionally, a pale, leathery membrane-glider—blind, or with eyes reduced to narrow slits against the glare—will drift silently past on a three-yard wingspan, riding the cold updrafts from one island of foliage to another. Deeper in the fibrous mats, segmented, translucent borers the size of hares chew slow tunnels into the warmer, sap-rich cores, leaving behind trails of frozen, glowing mulch. 

And here and there, clinging to the rare stretches of older, thicker root-wood that resist the constant upheaval, are the faint marks of tool-makers. They are small, cautious presences in a landscape too volatile for cities or roads. You might find a cairn of hardened, amber-like resin stacked beside a fresh chasm, or a row of notches hacked into a pale trunk with a flint axe to mark a climbing route. Once in a great while, tucked under the lee of a rigid, frost-crusted leaf-arch, sits a temporary shelter: a low dome of stretched bladder-skin lashed to bone stakes with twisted plant-cordage, beside a cold ring of charred, resinous pith where someone scraped together a brief, smoky fire.

Nothing built here stays long. A hammered copper hook driven into a glowing stem is swallowed by new growth within a season, or drops into the abyss when the stem rots and snaps. Those who move through the White Hollow travel light, carrying stone knives, bone needles, and coils of fibrous rope, never trusting the ground beneath their feet to be there when they turn back—moving like tiny, dark specks across a blinding, freezing, ever-shifting cathedral of living frost.
