The first thing the basin gives you is noise, and the second is wet heat.

There is no wind to carry the sound away, and no soft surface to drink it in. Under a low, flat ceiling of pewter-gray cloud that never breaks and never shifts, the air sits heavy and warm against the skin, saturated to the point of weeping. Water beads on every sharp edge of stone, gathers in dark runnels, and drips with a steady, unhurried *tick-tick-tick* into shallow pools—a quiet baseline buried beneath a wall of reflected clamor.

You stand on a wide, low-lying plain of raw, unweathered rock. Across the open miles of the basin, the ground looks freshly broken: angular shelves of dark basalt and split shale, stepped ledges with edges still crisp enough to cut a sandal, and flat pans of pale mineral mud where water seeps up from just below the surface. There is no soil here, no grass, no moss, not even a scrubby bush to soften a horizon line. Where the stone stays wettest, slick films of rust-orange and dull olive biofilm coat the rock like grease, and pale mineral crusts rim the stagnant pools. Everything else is bare stone, water, bone, and crowded flesh.

Filling the center of the basin, sprawling outward in dense, low tiers, is the city.

Without timber, the builders have used what the raw ground gives them. Thousands of dwellings sit shoulder-to-shoulder on raised stone plinths and causeways, built from freshly quarried slabs of dark rock stacked dry or chinked with gray clay and crushed shell. The walls are thick, windowless or pierced only by narrow slits, and roofed with corbeled stone or heavy hides stretched tight over curved ribs of large bone. Alleys are narrow, slick-floored canyons just wide enough for two people to pass sideways, bridged overhead by stone lintels and sagging bone-and-sinew drying racks. Every flat roof, every stepped terrace, every retaining wall above the seepage flats is packed with baskets of woven sinew and reed-like cured gut, low-fired clay jars, and piles of split stone.

The technology is simple and loud. Everywhere, people work in the open air—squatting on flat ledges or beneath hide awnings—because the dim, steady light is the same at every hour and the air is too warm for cramped interiors. The rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of stone knapping echoes down the alleys as workers strike sharp basalt blades from cores. Elsewhere comes the dull, heavy *thud* of stone mallets crushing dried shellfish in hollowed rock mortars, the scrape of bone awls punching cured skins, and the thin, bright *ting* of native copper being hammered cold into pins and fishhooks on flat stone anvils.

All of it competes with the animals, which outnumber the people ten to one and share every surface of the city.

Because the wet mineral flats and warm, biofilm-rich shallows teem with larvae, worms, and small crustaceans, the basin supports an astonishing, tightly packed swarm of life. Millions of small, slate-backed frogs and pale-bellied toads crowd the drainage channels and the wet footings of the houses, their throats pulsing in a continuous, overlapping roar of croaks and clicks. Clouds of tiny brine-flies and biting midges hang in shimmering, stagnant columns above the warm puddles, ignored by the inhabitants. Along the upper ledges of the stone houses, dense colonies of long-legged shorebirds and squat, dark-plumed divers nest almost wing-to-wing, squawking, fighting over scraps of fish, and filling the dim air with the dry slap of feathers. Crabs scuttle freely across the wet causeways, disappearing into the chinks between foundation stones whenever a foot comes down.

The sound is physical—a constant, deafening pressure against the eardrums. With no leaves, no soil, and no wood to absorb it, every bird-screech, every frog-chorus, every shouted bargain and stone-on-stone strike bounces off the wet, angular walls of the alleys and stacks on top of the last. People speak close to one another's faces, gesturing sharply with hands stained gray from clay and fish-oil.

There is no rhythm of weather here to interrupt it. The dim, diffused light holds steady, as if the world is permanently paused twenty minutes before dusk. No breeze stirs the mist hanging over the outer flats; no storm builds on the low, jagged rim of the distant ridges. The water in the channels neither floods nor dries up, held in exact, sluggish balance by the weeping rock and the condensing sky. Smoke from small, slow-burning cooking fires—fed on rendered fish-oil and dried guano—rises straight up for a few yards before flattening out into the heavy, humid air, smelling of salt, wet mineral, and roasting shellfish.

Looking out from one of the higher stone terraces, you can see the whole valley-wide settlement at once: a dense, gray-and-black honeycomb of sharp-edged rock and stretched hide, glistening with condensation, swarming with birds and people, locked inside a warm, dim, roaring equilibrium that never shifts and never quiets.
