The basin is no wider than a small village, a shallow, saucer-shaped depression sunk into the worn-down spine of a plateau so old that every ridge has been ground to the curve of a knuckle. There are no cliffs here, no sharp edges, no loose stones—only a continuous, bone-pale floor of fused silicate, polished smooth by eons of warm rain until it holds water like a plate.

From rim to rim, the pan is flooded to the depth of an ankle. The water is bath-warm and clear as glass, resting in a flat, unbroken sheet that mirrors the sky. And the sky is blinding. Light pours down through a thin, high veil of steam—not harsh and sharp-edged, but a vast, shadowless, white-gold glare that bounces off the water and climbs back up into the mist, erasing the horizon. Everything here is bathed in brightness from above and below at once.

Across this gleaming expanse, the macro-world is sparse. Wide stretches of empty, sunlit water separate the few features that break the surface. Here and there, a low, rounded hump of bedrock rises an inch or two above the tide, crowned with a dense, isolated garden of vegetation: thickets of waxy, broad-leafed ferns, cushions of emerald moss swollen with moisture, and clusters of tall, hollow-stemmed reeds that droop under the weight of their own condensation. Between these scattered green islands, there is nothing to interrupt the flat glare except five or six small, inexplicable structures.

They are waist-high, spaced far apart, and set directly into the stone without seams or mortar. Each looks like a dark, asymmetrical cage of matte-pearlescent glass, twisted into curves that seem to fold slightly inward on themselves. If you look closely at their edges, the bright air around them appears subtly bruised—light bends a fraction too sharply, and the water lapping against their bases forms tiny, concentric ripples that never quite dissipate, even when the air is dead still. Whatever engineering shaped them belongs to an epoch that understood gravity and heat as things to be braided rather than fought. They do not hum or click, yet they are the engines of this place: they bleed a steady, gentle warmth into the pan and draw moisture down from the upper air, maintaining a perpetual, enclosed summer long after the surrounding world should have cooled.

Because of them, the basin is never still, and it is never empty.

Look down into the inch-deep water, or onto the wet backs of the stones, and the sparseness vanishes. The place is overwhelmingly, suffocatingly alive.

There is no bare mud, no unoccupied water column. Beneath the bright surface, ribbons of translucent, palm-sized flatworms undulate in layered, overlapping currents. Millions of blind, porcelain-white crabs—no bigger than thumbnails, with soft, brush-like claws—carpet the submerged rock, sifting the living biofilm in endless, shuffling tides. Between the reed-stems, schools of finger-length fish with mirror-bright scales hang motionless, then dart in synchronized bursts that never once break the surface tension.

On the smooth, weeping humps of rock and the waxy undersides of the leaves, amphibians are stacked like wet shingles: jewel-bright newts, slate-blue salamanders, and thousands of tiny, golden-skinned frogs. None of them have vocal sacs. None of them croak. They crawl slowly over one another in glistening, silent heaps, communicating through sudden flushes of skin-color and the faint, bitter scents they release into the humid air.

Even the bright air above the water is crowded. Broad-winged insects with velvet-fringed edges drift through the steam, their flight completely muffled, dodging the hair-thin filaments of fist-sized, translucent air-jellies that ride the warm updrafts rising from the dark artifacts. When a jelly snags a flyer, it simply contracts, drawing its prey up into a pale, pulsing bell, and drifts on without a sound.

Despite the frantic, layered density of life, the basin is almost entirely quiet. The heavy humidity swallows what little noise is made. There is no wind to rattle the reeds—only the slow, thermal convection of air rising off the warm water and sinking back down at the cooler, worn-down rim. If you stand in the center of the pan, the loudest thing you will hear is the soft, sporadic *plip* of condensed dew dropping from a fern-tip into the shallows, or the faintest wet *shhh* of a thousand tiny bellies sliding over polished stone.

Every few minutes, the cycle turns. One of the dark, light-bending cages pulses—not with sound or visible light, but with a sudden, brief surge of heat that makes the air above it shimmer. The warm plume rises, hits the cooler upper mist, and collapses into a silent, heavy drizzle that lasts less than a minute. As the warm droplets patter noiselessly onto the water, a fresh bloom of microscopic algae clouds the shallows around the artifact. Immediately, the porcelain crabs turn in unison and stream toward the cloud; slender, blind water-snakes uncoil from the moss-islands to intercept the crabs; and overhead, the drifting jellies sink lower, spreading their filaments wider in the sudden downdraft.

Then the drizzle stops. The glare settles back down onto the flat, ancient stone, the steam resumes its slow climb, and the basin goes on devouring and renewing itself in luminous, crowded silence.
