The land has been worn down almost to nothing. Whatever mountains once fed these basins are gone so long ago that even their gravel has dissolved into pale silt and smooth, coin-sized pebbles of quartz. What remains is a vast, low peneplain—a table of ancient clay, bleached limestone shelves, and broad, shallow pans that stretch to a flat, shimmering horizon.

There is scarcely a slope anywhere. Water does not rush here; it spreads. Fed by warm, slow-moving seepage from beneath the porous stone and by the heavy, humid air that condenses on cool nights, the water lies in thin, bright sheets across the flats. It gathers in wide, ankle-deep lagoons and creeps through braided channels so sluggish that only the drift of floating algae betrays which way the basin tilts. Between the water and the sky, the light is full and direct, bouncing off the wet, mineral-pale mud and the glass-smooth shallows until the distance blurs into a warm, silvery haze.

Because the soil is leached of almost everything except clay, salt, and ancient lime, plants keep their distance from one another. Great stretches of glistening mudflat and sun-warmed stone lie completely bare, broken only by scattered tufts of stiff, blue-green sedge and low, woody scrub that clings to the slight rises—places a mere hand’s breadth higher than the surrounding flats. Here and there, a solitary, gnarled tree with pale bark and sparse, leathery leaves stands on a mound of compacted shell and chert, its roots splayed wide across the surface like bleached ribs. Beneath the shallow water, thick, olive-colored mats of algae and bacterial felt cushion the silt, releasing tiny, occasional bubbles of gas into the warm afternoon.

Yet for all its emptiness and geological stillness, the plain is loud. With no hills or forests to catch the noise, sound carries unimpeded for miles across the wet flats.

The air vibrates with a constant, layered clamor of life. From every reed-clump and mud-rim comes the rhythmic, metallic clicking of thousands of small frogs, rising and falling in great overlapping waves that sweep across the lagoons. Clouds of midges and heavy-bodied flies hum above the warm shallows, while in the sparse, dry scrub on the rises, insects saw and rattle in the heat. Large wading birds—long-legged, heavy-billed, and pale against the dark mud—stalk the open pans in loose, scattered groups, their sharp, barking cries echoing off the water. In the deeper, tea-colored wallows where the limestone has slumped, thick-skinned, amphibious grazers lie half-submerged in the silt, occasionally shifting their bulk with a wet, sucking splash or letting out low, resonant bellows that roll across the flats like distant thunder.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The warm wind moves steadily across the open expanse, pushing shallow ripples against the worn stone shelves with a soft, continuous slap. The water rises and retreats by inches over weeks, leaving behind delicate, terraced rings of salt and dried foam on the clay. There are no paths save the wide, muddy troughs dragged by heavy bellies between one pool and the next, and no structures save the baked-earth chimneys of burrowing crustaceans and the trampled reed-platforms of nesting birds.

It is a landscape that has settled into its final posture—warm, wet, and stripped down to bone-pale stone, shallow water, and the ceaseless, noisy business of creatures thriving in the wide-open sun.
