You hear the valley long before you can make out a single edge of it.

In the dark, the sound comes at you as a thick, overlapping wall of percussion and breath: the sharp *clack* of wood striking stone, the wet *plop* of heavy drops falling into hollow clay, a slow, grinding groan like a millstone turning dry, and underneath it all, a low, booming hum that vibrates in your teeth. It is cool here—the kind of steady, cellar-like chill that settles into wool and stays there—and the air smells sharply of wet slate, green-cut timber, and raw, unfired mud.

There is almost no light. What little exists is a cold, bruised greyness that seems to seep from nowhere in particular, barely enough to catch the wet sheen on a bevelled stone edge or the pale blur of a hemp rope strung across your path. You navigate by shin and palm.

And everywhere your hand lands, it meets work.

There is no dirt beneath your boots, no bedrock, no turf. The ground is a dense, interlocking skin of hand-split shale, wedged river-cobble, and packed ash-brick, laid down in uneven terraces that step gently downward toward the low center of the basin. Every surface is raw and freshly cut. Nothing here has been softened by wind or rain; the corners of the masonry are sharp enough to nick skin, the adze-marks in the timber bracing are still pale and splintery, and the mortar between the courses is either still tacky to the touch or freshly crumbled into dry, gritty piles. You are walking across something that is still being put together, layer by layer, with no natural floor left beneath it.

The density of the construction is suffocating and intricate. Walls rise only waist- or shoulder-high before jogging sideways, bridged by rough-hewn plank causeways, stacked cairns of sorted stone, and tight, scaffolding-choked alleys where two people could barely pass. Even overhead, the space is cluttered with low lintels, criss-crossing timber struts, and hanging bundles of reeds and weighted stones. Yet through the gaps in the masonry, you can feel the true scale of the place: the echoes don't bounce back tight like a room. They travel—rolling out across a wide, low-lying expanse, town-sized or larger, terraced and crowded from rim to sump.

Water is scarce, yet everything is damp. There is no rain and no stream, only the cool air sweating endlessly against millions of square feet of cold stone and porous clay. Moisture beads on the undersides of the lintels, gathers along shallow, chisel-cut runnels, and threads downward through the tiers. In the lowest pockets—small, hand-lined sumps wedged between retaining walls—dark, mineral-heavy water sits still and shallow, reflecting nothing.

Nothing green grows here. On the dampest, lowest stones, you can sometimes feel—or faintly see in the near-dark—thin, pale smears of cold-loving slime or powdery, chalk-white mold, the only living film on the mineral skin.

It is the water, combined with gravity and raw craft, that makes the valley so loud and restless. Every few paces, the condensation runnels feed into primitive, ingenious contraptions lashed together with hide and twisted fiber: hollowed cedar tipping-troughs, counterweighted baskets of cobble, slate paddles hung from greased wooden pins. When a trough fills, it tips with a heavy *thud*, spilling its cup of water down into a lower basin and jerking a hemp cord that trips a wooden hammer against a resonant slab of shale. *Clack. Tip. Splash. Boom.* Hundreds of these mechanisms—thousands, perhaps, scattered across the dark terraces—run on their own irregular, overlapping schedules.

Meanwhile, cold drafts funnel through the narrow chinks in the dry-stone walls and blow across the mouths of hundreds of wide, hand-coiled clay pipes jutting from the embankments, turning whole sections of the valley into sluggish, moaning organs. And because the place is still settling—wet clay shrinking, unmortared stacks shifting under new weight, green wood warping—the architecture itself groans, pops, and occasionally gives way in a brief, deafening cascade of sliding slate.

Life, in the animal sense, is rare, though you are not entirely alone. Now and then, the rhythms of the valley are interrupted by something deliberate. A pale, long-limbed creature—blind, leathery-skinned, and no larger than a hound—might scuttle sideways along a vertical dry-stone face, scraping its hard, chitinous belly against the slate as it licks the chalky mold from the mortar joints. Deeper in the sumps, something slow and heavy occasionally breaks the surface of a water-pocket with a wet *slurp*, hauling itself over a cobble lip before vanishing into a drain-culvert too narrow to follow.

And somewhere out in the dark, beyond the nearest screen of scaffolding and the clatter of the tipping-troughs, you hear a sound that isn't water or wind: the steady, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of a stone chisel biting into fresh shale, adding one more piece to a valley that has not yet decided what it is going to be.
