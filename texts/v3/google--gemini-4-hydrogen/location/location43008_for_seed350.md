The water here is bath-warm, heavy with dissolved iron, and never still. It presses against everything at once—not the cold, quiet weight of an ocean abyss, but a thick, agitated broth that smells of hot grease and sulfur even before you try to breathe it.

Down at the valley floor, the rock is young and vicious. There are no rounded cobbles, no silt-softened reefs. The basin is a freshly torn scar of basalt and slate, its ledges still holding the razor-sharp edges of recent fracture. Everywhere you look, black stone steps down in jagged, unweathered shelves toward a central trough that vanishes into the murk. The elevation is low enough that the whole mile-wide valley feels like a flooded cellar, yet open enough that when a pressure-wave clears the suspended grit for a second or two, you can glimpse the far walls—raw, stepped cliffs disappearing upward into the dimness.

No sunlight reaches this depth, or if it does, it dies somewhere in the upper layers. The light is a bruised, murky gray-green, punctuated by the ugly orange bleed of submerged induction coils and the sudden, stuttering blue-white flash of an arc-weld or a shorting busbar. Those flashes don’t illuminate much; they just turn the suspended mica and torn plant-fiber into a brief, blinding snowstorm before the dimness closes back in.

And it is *loud*. Underwater, there is no distance between a sound and your ribs. The whole valley is a single, deafening engine room. A low, teeth-rattling thrum rises from the rock itself, overlaid by the rhythmic *clank-thud* of massive pistons, the shriek of cavitation around high-speed impellers, and the constant, tearing crackle of water flashing to steam against overheated housings and immediately imploding back into liquid. Every few minutes, a water-hammer shockwave rolls down the length of the basin, hitting with the force of a dropped anvil.

There are no animals here. Not a single shrimp in the cracks, no blind fish darting through the stalks, no barnacles crusting the pipes. The silence of animal life makes the vegetation feel frantic, almost violent.

Because the water is warm and choked with industrial runoff and raw volcanic minerals, the flora has exploded into a dense, suffocating tangle that fills almost every cubic meter between the rock and the dim ceiling. Thick, rubbery stalks of iron-kelp, as wide around as a thigh, anchor themselves into the sharp basalt fissures and wrap around the cooling fins of machinery. Between them, vast mats of copper-veined pondweed and fleshy, serrated reeds form layered, pulsing canopies. Nothing sways gently. The currents are too chaotic for that. The kelp whips and thrashes, snapping loose in huge, ragged curtains—only to regrow at an unnatural clip, pale new shoots unspooling from torn stumps in the wake of the very vortexes that shredded them.

The machinery isn’t laid out in neat streets or factory floors. It is scattered across the valley in isolated, heavy-gauge nodes: a submerged cracking tower bolted straight into a basalt ridge; an autonomous dredge-platform tethered by taut, humming chains; banks of oil-cooled transformers squatting in cages of welded rebar. Between these islands of steel, miles of thick, armored umbilical cables and pressurized hoses sag through the greenery like vines, vibrating so hard they blur to the eye. Nothing looks abandoned. Everything is running flat-out, overclocked and flooded, chewing through clouds of torn kelp and spitting out superheated brine and green pulp.

Around the machines, and in the wide, turbulent channels between them, the rules of the world begin to fray.

The currents don’t behave like water should. A violent gyre will spin up out of nowhere, tearing a thicket of reeds clean off a ledge, only to slam into an invisible, vertical plane where the water has suddenly thickened to the consistency of cold grease—freezing the torn leaves in mid-swirl. Pockets of liquid shear apart like panes of wet glass, leaving sharp, geometric cavities that hang open for a heartbeat before collapsing with a concussive *whump*.

Time and geometry stutter under the acoustic load. A massive hydraulic ram on a nearby dredge-tower slams shut, and you feel the shockwave in your sternum three seconds *before* the piston actually moves. Across the valley, the orange warning beacon of a pump-house flickers in two slightly offset places at once, its shadow stretching in the wrong direction, cast by a flare that hasn’t happened yet. Even the plants seem caught in the breakdown: a cluster of reeds near a humming conduit loops through a rapid, visible cycle of budding, tearing apart in the current, and snapping back to whole, repeating the same two seconds of growth and ruin while the rest of the thicket thrashes on.

It is a hyperactive, sunless, deafening place—a freshly cracked basin where raw rock, frantic greenery, and blind industry grind together in warm water that can no longer quite hold its shape.
