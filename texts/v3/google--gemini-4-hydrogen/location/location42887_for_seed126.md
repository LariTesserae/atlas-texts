The horizon is a flat, blinding seam where a pale sky meets a pale floor. Nothing rises high enough to break it. As far as the eye can reach—and the air is so dry and clear that you can see until the planet’s curvature cuts you off—there are no mountains, no hills, no cliffs of natural stone. There is only the Deck: a continent-spanning expanse of worn-down, sun-bleached architecture, ground low over ages into an endless basin of sintered ceramic, vitrified glass, and tarnished alloy.

It is warm here, a steady, dry warmth that radiates upward from the ground as much as it presses down from the overhead glare. The light is relentless. The sky is a washed-out, incandescent white-gold, and the surface below is almost as bright—high-albedo plates of chalky composite and frosted quartz that bounce the radiance back until shadows shrink to thin, razor-edged smudges beneath your boots.

Yet the ground is not smooth like a salt flat. Up close, it is exhaustingly dense. Every square meter is packed with the worn-down geometry of a machine the size of a world. Billions of knee-high cooling fins, eroded to stubby, rounded teeth, stand shoulder-to-shoulder in sweeping, continent-wide arcs. Between them lie sunken grids of optical lenses, frosted opaque by millennia of blowing silica, and overlapping hexagonal tiles that have delaminated like flaky pastry, exposing three or four tightly stacked sub-layers of hair-thin conduits, crystalline wafers, and honeycombed baffles. There is no soil anywhere. Even the dust that fills the seams is not dirt, but the fine, silvery-white powder of abraded metamaterials and pulverized glass.

The silence is almost absolute. Something in the geometry of the surface—or in the porous, micro-latticed structure of the ceramic itself—swallows sound. A dropped tool makes a dull, flat *tock* that dies instantly, without echo. There is no roar of wind, even when the air is moving; the breeze slips through the dense carpet of low fins without whistling, carrying only the faintest, dry *hiss* of electrostatic dust grains repelling one another. If you press your ear to the warm plates, you do not hear gears or rushing coolant, only a phantom pressure, a sub-audible hum that sits behind the eyes like the memory of a sound.

Despite the silence and the ancient, weathered look of everything, the place is fiercely active. It breathes in slow, planetary rhythms.

As the glare shifts across the sky, thermal tides ripple through the substrate. You can watch a vast, shallow wave of motion approach from the horizon: millions of tiny, flush-mounted louvers tilting open by a fraction of an inch in silent unison to vent trapped heat, their edges catching the light like a brief shimmer on dry water. Beneath the frosted glass panels, faint pulses of violet and amber light travel along buried waveguides—the remnants of some incomprehensible calculation, or perhaps just the autonomic reflexes of a planetary hull keeping its balance. 

The weather here is made of static and heat. Without water—not a drop of standing liquid, not a cloud, only a brief, microscopic film of hygroscopic salt-dew that forms in the deepest seams before dawn and vanishes at first light—the air organizes itself around charge. Tall, slender dust-devils spin up out of the flatlands without a whisper, composed of silver-white ceramic flour that aligns itself along invisible magnetic contours. They stand like pale, swaying pillars for hours, shedding tiny, silent sparks of blue static back into the ground before dissolving. In places where buried field-coils still leak a stray gradient, the dust refuses to lie flat; it stands on end in stiff, velvety ridges that tremble whenever a thermal gust passes over.

Life here is sparse, tucked into the margins of the machine. In the shaded crevices between delaminated plates, where a trace of morning chill lingers longest, grows a brittle, pale-olive growth that looks like lichen made of spun fiberglass. It has no roots; it anchors itself with microscopic metallic filaments to the tarnished bronze seams, drinking stray current and splitting carbon from the dry air. In wider depressions—shallow, saucer-like basins where the outer plating has completely eroded away to expose the dense, tangled latticework beneath—clumps of wire-thin, translucent reeds sprout, swaying slightly not in the wind, but in response to the shifting electromagnetic pulse of the ground.

Animal life is rarer still, and uncanny. Once or twice in a day’s walk, you might see a glider: a flat, diamond-shaped thing no wider than a dinner plate, translucent and veined with copper-colored capillaries. It has no wings that flap and makes no sound. It simply hangs three inches above the blinding floor, riding the repulsive cushion of the surface’s magnetic field, drifting from one exposed optical node to the next to siphon off a sip of focused light. When startled, it doesn’t flee into the sky; it drops flat against the tiles, shifts its refractive index to match the chalky glare, and vanishes.

Walking here feels like crossing the deck of a ship so vast it has forgotten it is a vessel. The air smells faintly of ozone, hot dust, and dry chalk. Every step crunches lightly on microscopic glass beads, and every mile looks much like the last—yet never quite the same, because the patterns of the worn-down substrate beneath your feet are endlessly intricate, shifting from dense fields of needle-fins to broad, sunken mirrors, to scarred trenches where ancient maintenance tracks have been smoothed back into the plain by a million years of dry wind. Nothing here is broken, exactly, and nothing is whole. It has simply settled into its oldest, flattest, brightest state, humming quietly to itself under an unblinking sky.
