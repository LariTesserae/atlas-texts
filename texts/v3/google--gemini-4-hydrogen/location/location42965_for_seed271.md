The place is a shallow, half-roofed hollow cut into the base of a low limestone escarpment—roughly seventy paces from one curved wall to the other, and bounded on its open side by a long, slumped ridge of pale scree. From the outside, it looks like a dark crease beneath a brow of weathered rock. Stepping over the scree and down onto the floor is like stepping into a room whose ceiling has been lowered by weight and time.

Light arrives only secondhand. The overhanging lip of the escarpment blocks the direct sky, so whatever brightness reaches the hollow has already bounced off the bleached gravel outside and the dust-colored slope of the ridge. Inside, the air sits in an even, flat dimness—not the blackness of a deep cave, just a permanent late-afternoon dusk where colors lose their sharpness and everything settles into warm greys, bone-whites, and muted umber.

The air is warm and still. The limestone above is thick enough to hold the heat of the outer world and release it slowly, so the hollow never quite cools. There is no breeze to stir it; the scree ridge acts as a dam, trapping a pocket of heavy, lukewarm air that smells faintly of wet chalk, old dust, and warm mineral grit.

Water exists here only as patience. Along the back wall, where the ceiling curves down to meet the floor in a tight, dark seam, the rock sweats. A thin film of moisture creeps down the limestone face, darkening a band of stone roughly waist-high into the color of wet slate. It does not run or trickle. It gathers in tiny, beads along the mineral ridges until gravity wins, releasing a single heavy drop every few minutes. Beneath the seam, the floor is pitted with a dozen shallow, saucer-like depressions worn into the bedrock over millennia. Each holds a skin of clear, lukewarm water no deeper than a knuckle, rimmed with a pale crust of precipitated lime. Between these saucers, the floor is dry-looking but cool to the touch, packed with fine, dampened silt that holds the faintest impression of weight before settling flat again.

The stone itself is long past breaking. Every edge in the hollow has been rounded by slow dissolution and time. The boulders that fell from the ceiling centuries ago have sunk into the silt until only their smooth, domed backs show above the floor, looking less like fallen debris and more like knuckles pushing up through skin. There is no sharp grit underfoot, no loose gravel waiting to slide; everything has found its final resting angle.

Life here is sparse and unhurried, confined to the places where the dampness reaches. Along the wet seam of the back wall, a thin pelt of pale, yellowish-green moss clings to the limestone, growing in flat, velvety mats no thicker than a fingernail. A few liverworts—dark, fleshy ribbons—spread across the dampest lips of the stone saucers, and higher up, where the rock stays dry, crustose lichens form slow, map-like blotches of rust and ash-grey. Near the outer scree ridge, where a sliver of reflected light occasionally touches the ground, a single low clump of wiry, silver-leafed scrub persists, its roots wedged deep into a crack. Between these small presences, there are wide, empty stretches of bare stone and smooth mineral floor.

There are no animals. No birds nest in the smooth ceiling; there are no spiders in the corners, no beetles crossing the silt, no lizards warming themselves on the scree. Without insects or wings, the hollow has a peculiar, mineral cleanliness. Nothing rustles in the moss. Nothing disturbs the surface of the water saucers, which hold their reflections—the dim, curved underside of the roof—with unbroken stillness.

Yet the hollow is not entirely untouched. Scattered across the floor are the faint, worn traces of old attention, made without metal or mortar and now nearly reclaimed by the stone. Near the center of the space, a ring of six flat, fist-sized stones has been set around one of the dry depressions; the stones are half-buried in silt and furred with the same grey lichen as the bedrock, leaving no hint of what they were meant to hold or mark. Near the scree ridge, where the descent into the hollow is steepest, four shallow scoops have been worn into a sloping slab of limestone—footholds pecked out so long ago that their edges are as smooth and round as natural water-worn hollows. Beside the wettest seam at the back, three small stones sit stacked one atop another, tilted slightly, balanced in a quiet equilibrium that looks almost accidental.

The sound of the place is mostly the absence of it. The scree ridge muffles whatever wind passes over the plain outside, leaving the hollow in a thick, cottony quiet. Only the slow, irregular *tock* of a water drop striking a saucer breaks the silence, followed by a tiny, expanding ring that dies against the lime crust before a second drop falls.

Nothing here hurries toward change. The seep does not dry in summer or swell in winter; the moss grows at a pace measured in decades; the warm, dim air holds the same weight from hour to hour. Left alone, the hollow simply remains—balanced between stone and moisture, empty and settled, keeping its own quiet temperature.
