The air here is thin enough to make the chest work for it, yet it is strangely warm—a dry, baking warmth that rises from the ground rather than falling from any sun. Above, the sky is a lid of near-total darkness, so thick with high-altitude soot and starless haze that it swallows the horizon whole. You can see only by the faint, sullen cherry-glow trapped in the seams of the rock, and by the brief, pale-violet sparks of static that snap in the dust when the wind shifts.

Because of the thinness of the air, the violence of the place is almost silent. 

The ground is in constant, restless motion. This is raw, unfinished geology, pushed upward so recently that no rain has ever touched it and no wind has had time to round its edges. Everything underfoot is razor-sharp: freshly fractured basalt, sheets of black volcanic glass, and brittle, blistered scoria that shatters like pottery under weight. The terrain drops away on all sides in extreme, dizzying verticals—knife-edge ridges, terraced shelves of cooling slag, and chasm walls that plunge into unseen depths where the heat thickens. Across the open miles of the basin, new fissures split open with a muted, subterranean *thump*, and tall, unstable spires of pumice and ash-crust lean, fracture, and collapse into dry avalanches that raise billowing clouds of black dust without making more than a soft, sandy hiss.

Thermal updrafts—fierce, erratic, and bone-dry—spiral up from the chasms, whipping the fine mineral dust into chaotic, silent funnels that dance across the dark ridges and vanish. Even the rocks themselves seem unsettled, ticking and pinging softly as they cool and shift, a faint, glassy percussion in the dark.

Nothing green grows here, and nothing moves on legs or wings. Yet along the lips of the warmest vents, where dry, odorless gas seeps from the crust, a sparse, unusual growth clings to the stone: mats of brittle, charcoal-colored filaments that resemble stiffened frost or spun iron wool. They are neither plant nor fungus in any familiar sense, feeding only on the mineral heat and trace vapors. When a thermal gust strikes them, they do not bend; they rattle dryly and shed fine gray spores into the dark, only to regrow along a newly opened crack a few yards away.

Scattered across these vast, empty expanses—miles apart, balanced precariously on flat shelves or tucked into the lee of jagged crags—are small, crude works of stacked stone. There is no mortar, no worked timber, no metal, and no sign of tools. They are simply slabs of raw basalt and glass-rock laid one atop another by hand: low, semicircular windbreaks, tall cairns that lean like crooked spines, and small, roofless rings barely wide enough for a single person to crouch inside. 

Some are half-buried under fresh scree; others have been split cleanly in two by a rising fault line, one half lifted three feet higher than the other. Yet a few stand intact, their dark stones still warm to the touch, waiting in the quiet, turbulent dark for whoever stacked them—or for the next tremor to pull them back down into the slag.
