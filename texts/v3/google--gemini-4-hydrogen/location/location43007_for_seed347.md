A hundred feet below the surrounding plain, the earth drops away into a wide, sunken valley—a vast karst collapse whose limestone rims are still sharp and unweathered. From above, it looks like a raw quarry cut into cold moorland, but the floor of the basin is a sprawling, saturated metropolis built without a single tool.

Because the depression is half a mile across, normal daylight fills it evenly, pale and cool, reflecting off a thousand flat surfaces of standing water. Yet no wind reaches down here. The air is cold, dense, and still, holding the scent of wet chalk, crushed sedge, and cold peat. Up on the rim, grass bends in the gale; down in the basin, the only movement of air is the slow, ghostly drift of mist rising off the springs and condensing back onto the moss.

The walls of the basin weep continuously. Groundwater forces its way out through fresh, angular fractures in the pale rock, trickling down raw mineral streaks in thin, silent curtains. There are no crashing waterfalls—only a steady, muffled *drip-drip* into thick cushions of sphagnum and liverwort that upholster every ledge and talus boulder. 

Every square foot of the valley floor is claimed. What might have been a chaotic swamp has been methodically, densely terraced into a multi-layered city of mud, wedged stone, and living timber. Across the sunken floor, generations of thick-furred, web-footed marsh-builders have engineered an unbroken grid of shallow canals, settling pools, and raised causeways. There is no bare ground between structures; lodges back directly onto retaining walls, retaining walls anchor into root-masses, and root-masses bridge the narrow waterways. 

Nothing here was cut with a blade or fastened with a peg. Every beam is a willow or alder branch gnawed down, dragged through the slick mud, and jammed into place by teeth and shoulders. The builders pack the gaps with river-clay, peat, and dead reeds, plastering the domes of their multi-chambered lodges smooth with broad snouts and flat tails. Because the valley is so wet, the fresh willow stakes rarely stay dead; they sprout in the mud walls, sending up dense, green thickets of new shoots that bind the architecture together with living roots. From a distance, the settlement looks like a tightly packed hillside town whose roofs have all burst into leaf.

The density is absolute. On the lowest tier, submerged entrances open into dark, flooded tunnels beneath the causeways, where cold-water trout and pale newts glide among the submerged willow-tangles. Above the waterline, the mud-and-wattle domes rise two and three stories high against the stepped limestone cliffs, connected by packed-earth walkways slick with algae. On the roofs of the lodges, lush gardens of sedge, bog-myrtle, and curled ferns take hold in the rich, rotting thatch. Higher still, in the crevices of the weeping cliff-face, thousands of mud-cup nests are plastered edge-to-edge, their small, feathered occupants coming and going in a quiet, constant traffic.

Despite the abundance of life, the valley is remarkably hushed. The thick, wet moss absorbs footsteps and splashes alike. A large builder slips off a mud bank into a canal with barely a soft *plop*, leaving only a widening V of ripples in the near-freezing water. Herons stand motionless on the spillway crests, watching the clear shallows; water-voles thread through the dense reed-beds, nibbling at fresh shoots; mink patrol the shadowed undersides of the root-bridges in total silence. 

The whole basin is in slow, constant motion. As meltwater and seepage swell the upper pools, small breaches open in the peat dams, and the builders immediately converge to pack them with fresh mud and chewed alder-twigs. Frost rimes the shaded northern ledges each night and melts into beads of water by midday, feeding the moss. Nothing sits idle, and nothing goes unused—a cold, thriving, water-logged city that grows, repairs, and feeds itself in the quiet dark-green light below the surface of the world.
