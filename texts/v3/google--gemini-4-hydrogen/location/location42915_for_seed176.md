High above the cloud-deck, where the air ought to be thin and biting, a narrow notch in the sheer dark rock holds a pocket of warm, suffocating wet.

It is a small place—no wider than a cathedral nave, walled on three sides by freshly fractured cliff faces and opening on the fourth to a dizzying drop into bright, sunlit cloud-tops. The stone here is young, thrust upward too recently for wind or frost to have softened its edges. Every outcrop is sharp-planed, glassy-black and slate-gray, jutting at awkward, unweathered angles. Yet none of it is dry. The rock weeps continuously. Warm, mineral-heavy water beads along the razor-sharp ridges and sheets down the vertical faces in steady, glistening curtains, fed by some deep, unseen heat below the peak and by the clouds that snag against the spire, condensing on contact.

Because of the warmth and the constant, dripping saturation, the little shelf is choked with green. Lush, broad-leafed ferns, thick pillows of emerald moss, and dense tangles of waxy vines crowd every ledge and crevice. The vegetation grows in layered, near-touching masses, draping over the sharp stone like heavy wet tapestries. Aerial roots hang in pale curtains from the overhangs, drinking straight from the steamy air. The light is the clear, unforgiving white of high noon, slanting down between the jagged walls and catching on millions of water droplets, turning the mist into a bright, humid haze.

Woven directly into this wet thicket—not replacing it, only wedged among it—are the scattered remnants and working limbs of heavy industry. There are no streets here, no sprawling yards or rows of roofs. Instead, a handful of squat, riveted iron structures sit bolted directly into the raw rock: a pressure-house with thick glass sight-gauges fogged from within, a bank of tall, finned condenser towers slick with algae, and a single massive flywheel half-buried in a bed of ferns. Heavy insulated cables, thick as a wrist, snake through the moss and climb the cliff face on porcelain insulators, disappearing into boreholes or dropping straight over the precipice into the white void below.

The place is loud, though almost nothing moves quickly. A deep, chest-rattling hum radiates from the transformer bank, constant and electrified, vibrating through the wet stone beneath one’s boots. Above it sits the steady, deafening roar of pressurized steam bleeding from a copper relief stack, and the resonant *shhh-thunk* of water-driven pistons cycling at a glacial pace. The great flywheel turns perhaps twice a minute, groaning under immense torque, shedding sheets of warm water from its oiled rim onto the broad leaves below. Every few minutes, a counterweight drops somewhere inside the pump-house with a dull, iron boom that echoes off the sharp walls and fades back into the hiss and hum.

Everything operates in a slow, patient equilibrium. The clouds drift past the open edge at a crawl. The mist rises, condenses on the iron fins and waxy leaves, drips down into the moss, and is drawn back into the clanking pipes. The plants have grown around the machinery rather than over it, adapting to the strange, metallic warmth: the ferns nearest the transformer coils have faint, copper-green veins, and thick crusts of pale mineral scale cling to the brass valves like barnacles.

Animal life is scarce. No insects buzz in the bright, steaming air—perhaps the altitude is too extreme, or the constant electrical thrum keeps them away. Only if you look closely along the warm iron conduits do you spot the rare, pale-skinned salamanders pressed flat against the rivets, unblinking, soaking in the vibration and the steady heat, watching nothing at all.
