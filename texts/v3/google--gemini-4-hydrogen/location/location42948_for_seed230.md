The settlement sits in a shallow, high-altitude saddle, cupped between two weathered ridges of pale grey limestone and bounded on its open side by a sheer drop into lower, hazy air. From ridge to ridge, you could cross it on foot in ten minutes, yet it is built with the deliberate geometry of a town: stepped terraces, retaining walls, broad paved courts, and sunken conduits all laid out on a single, long-settled plan.

Between the structures, there is mostly emptiness—wide expanses of fitted stone and swept grit that make the buildings feel like islands. A heavy, low-eaved hall of dry-laid slate stands fifty paces from a cluster of three windowless storehouses; a narrow stair climbs a retaining wall to a solitary watch-platform, leaving an entire upper terrace bare save for a row of stone-lined basins. Nothing crowds anything else. The spaces between the walls are not neglected gaps, but part of the architecture itself, worn smooth and kept clear of rubble.

Everything here is made with hand tools and patience. The limestone blocks are split with wedges and dressed with pick-marks that have been softened by centuries of wind. Where timber appears—in a heavy lintel, a barred shutter, a roof beam—it is scarce, hauled up from far below, and weathered to the colour of bone. Roofs are pitched low against the mountain wind and weighted down with flat river-stones. The edges of every stair-tread are dished in the middle from long use, and the mortar in the older walls has receded into the joints, leaving the stones settled tightly into their own weight.

There are no streams or springs at this height, only a persistent, cool dampness that gathers in the shade. In the mornings, thin cloud brushes the saddle and leaves a fine sweat of moisture on the cold faces of the stone. The town is engineered to catch it: shallow, hand-chiseled runnels cut across the empty plazas, guiding the condensation toward covered cisterns sunk deep into the bedrock. Around the mouths of these cisterns, and in the damp, sheltered seams where retaining walls meet the ground, thin strips of dark moss and tough, silver-leaved saxifrage take hold. Elsewhere, the ground is bare rock, pale dust, and the occasional low cushion of wiry grass wedged into a crack.

The light is clear, cool, and steady—the crisp, shadow-sharp daylight of high places, without glare or warmth. Even at midday, the air holds a dry chill, and the shadows cast by the walls remain cold enough to keep yesterday’s dampness from evaporating. 

It is a mostly quiet place. The wind moves across the open courts with a low, flat hiss, scraping grit over the flagstones, and now and then a drop of water falls with a hollow *tock* into one of the stone-lidded cisterns. Life is rare and unhurried: a pair of black mountain choughs walking the rim of a retaining wall, or a pale lizard flattened against a sun-warmed slate, motionless as the rock itself.

Nothing here seems to be growing, and nothing seems to be falling down. In the cool, thin air, wood does not rot quickly, iron rusts slowly, and stone barely shifts. The settlement rests in a long, quiet equilibrium—a small, orderly arrangement of rock and empty space, held steady between the ridges.
