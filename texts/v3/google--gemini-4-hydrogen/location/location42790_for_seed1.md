The basin is low and wide, flattened to the point of exhaustion. Its horizon lies far off in a bruise-colored dimness, smudged by freezing mist and the glareless pall of a sky that never quite brightens. There are no hills to break the view, only miles of wet, salt-blackened slate and iron-rich silt worn down over unimaginable ages into smooth, greasy ribs. Everything here is ancient, ground past its prime into a final, slumped equilibrium—except that the whole flat expanse is roaring like a foundry.

You hear it before you can make sense of it: a layered, deafening clamor that vibrates through the soles of your boots and rattles the fluid in your inner ear. There is the rhythmic, wet *thump-hiss* of hydraulic compression, the high-pitched whine of thousands of rotors spinning at peak load, the sharp snap of high-voltage arcs, and beneath it all, a vast, grinding undertone like tectonic plates being fed through a mill.

Yet there are no buildings. No walls, no roofs, no pipes laid by hands, no roads, no scrap-heaps of discarded tools. Nothing here was ever designed or assembled.

The ground is simply like this.

At the water’s edge—and the water’s edge is everywhere, braided into shallow, cold channels and weeping out of every pore of the ancient rock—the terrain itself has curdled into industry. Reality here has lost its grip on the boundary between geology and machinery. In the sunken flats, natural basins of brine act as vast electrolytic cells, fizzing with cold hydrogen and plating the surrounding mud with ribbons of pure, dull copper. Where freezing groundwater meets the deeper, magnetized strata, it does not bubble up as a spring; it surges through natural stone cylinders in precise, four-stroke pulses, driving columns of ice-sheathed basalt up and down like pistons. 

The air is bitter, hovering just below freezing, thick with a condensing fog that tastes of wet pennies, ozone, and old salt. By all normal laws, the sheer mechanical work happening across these miles of flatland ought to turn the basin into a steaming cauldron. Instead, the engines run *cold*. Every strike of a natural hammer, every spin of a mineral turbine, pulls heat out of the surrounding air and converts it directly into torque and voltage. Frost rimes the spinning edges of stone flywheels. Slush hisses in the channels, never quite freezing solid only because the brine is too rich and the electric current running through it keeps the water shivering.

And there is no room to step.

The basin is packed—not with vegetation, for there isn’t a single leaf, blade, or root anywhere in sight, only bare wet stone and crystalline salt-crusts—but with fauna. Every square meter of the dim flats is occupied, overlapping, jostling. It is an ecosystem built entirely out of scaled production and predatory appetite.

Across the slick slate, dense herds of *stamping-whelks*—armored, platter-sized things with shells of laminated graphite and iron—crawl shoulder-to-shoulder over the copper-plated mud. As they feed on the mineral rime, their undersides heatless-weld the loose flakes into thin, hexagonal plates, which they extrude behind them like waste. Follow a herd for ten paces and you will see a trail of gleaming, identical metal tiles laid into the freezing slush. Immediately behind them, packed just as tightly, come the *slurry-drakes*: long, blind, multi-jointed scavengers whose mandibles are twin rotary files. They shred the freshly stamped plates back into conductive dust, swallowing half and spraying the rest into the brine channels to feed the electrolytic pools.

Above and among them, the air and shallows are crowded with stranger presences. Colonial *dynamo-mounds*—hives of porous, wet magnetite and pale, shivering fat—rise a few feet out of the marsh like eroded stumps. Inside their chambers, millions of thumb-sized, turbine-winged insects beat their wings in locked, synchronized phase, generating a low, deafening hum and building up static charges until the mound’s apex discharges a jagged, blue-white arc across the mist to its nearest neighbor. The flash briefly illuminates the sheer, suffocating density of the flats: miles of glistening carapaces, spinning mineral fronds, snapping relay-crabs, and long, eel-like *busbars* that weave through the freezing puddles, bridging the currents between mounds and feeding on the stray amperage.

Predation here is loud and electrical. When a *condenser-heron*—a tall, spindly thing with glass-hollow bones and copper-filament plumes—steps delicately through the packed carpet of whelks, it isn’t looking for soft flesh. It drives its needle-beak straight into the dorsal coil of a grazing crustacean, short-circuiting the creature’s nervous system in a shower of cold sparks and drinking the sudden surge of discharged energy while the shell cracks open from the thermal shock.

The longer you stand in the dimness, the more the rules of the place slip out of alignment. 

You notice that the sound of a distant piston-strike arrives half a second *before* you see the plume of freezing spray shoot upward. Out on the open flats, a ridge of eroded slate appears larger the farther it is from you, looming like a cliff at the edge of vision while shrinking to a wet curbstone as you walk toward it. Water in the braided channels does not always flow downhill; in places where the ground’s natural current runs strong, the brine climbs gently up the rounded hummocks, pooling at the crests in trembling, humming lenses before spilling over in reverse. 

Some of the creatures seem caught in this same ontological stutter. A swarm of rotor-flies hangs motionless in the freezing fog, their wings a blur, yet their shadows on the wet stone below are three yards to the left, twitching to a different rhythm. When a larger predator—a low, wide *smelter-ray* undulating through the shallow brine—passes through a thicket of dynamo-mounds, its body briefly turns translucent, revealing not organs, but a tightly packed array of spinning, frost-covered gears that could not possibly fit inside its flat silhouette.

Then the weather shifts, and the whole packed basin answers at once.

A squall of heavy, conductive sleet sweeps in from the low horizon, driven by a wind that smells sharply of chlorine. As the wet ice coats the exposed mineral coils and the backs of a million grinding creatures, resistance across the flats drops. The ambient hum pitches upward into a shrill, unified shriek. Across miles of open, dim marsh, the dynamo-mounds begin firing in rapid, cascading chains—blue-white lightning stitching the fog together in a blinding, stuttering grid. The natural basalt pistons hammer faster, throwing sheets of freezing brine into the air, and the stamping-whelks clamp down onto the rock, their shells vibrating so violently they turn the puddles beneath them into standing waves of froth.

For a few minutes, the entire landscape operates at peak, purposeless capacity—manufacturing nothing, refining nothing, burning no fuel, only converting the bitter cold and the ancient, wet earth into pure, deafening motion and impossible light.

Then the squall passes on toward the invisible sea. The voltage bleeds back down into the mud. The roaring settles back to its steady, bone-rattling grind, and the dimness closes in again over the endless, crowded, freezing flats.
