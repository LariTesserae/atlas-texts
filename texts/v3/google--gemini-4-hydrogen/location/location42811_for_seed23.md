The first thing that hits you is the glare. At this height, the sky is a thin, hard blue with no haze to soften the sun, and the basin below acts like a concave mirror. Every flat face of wet slate, every film of runoff, every needle of rime throws the light straight back into your eyes. You have to squint through bone-slit goggles just to look down into the bowl.

It is not a large place—a weathered, steep-walled hollow carved into a high mountain saddle, no more than a hundred paces across from rim to rim. Millennia of frost and water have scalloped the pale limestone into natural amphitheater tiers, smooth-lipped and deeply grooved. Yet despite the cold—bitter enough to freeze spit before it hits the ground—the entire basin is glistening, slick, and wet.

From thousands of hairline fissures in the ancient rock, a heavy, mineral-thick brine weeps continuously. It does not freeze cleanly. Exposed to the razor-sharp air and the blinding sun, the brine flashes into frantic, unstable life: fragile lattices of needle-ice sprout from the stone in minutes, swelling into hollow crystal blisters and lacy salt-crusts, only to slump and dissolve the moment a warmer trickle of subsurface water undermines them or a passing shadow drops the local temperature by a fraction of a degree. The walls of the bowl are in constant, restless motion—growing a skin of white glass, shedding it into gray, glittering slush, and growing it again.

And yet, for all its hyperactivity, the basin is eerily quiet. 

There is no roar of wind here; the high rim deflects the mountain gales overhead. The thin air carries sound poorly, and every horizontal surface is padded with inches of wet frazil-slush and spongy salt-rime that swallows footsteps like thick felt. Even when a shelf of crystal-ice shears off a ledge, it does not crash—it gives a soft, wet *sigh* as it collapses into the slurry below. 

Into this small, blinding, shifting bowl, a dense settlement has wedged itself. Nearly every terrace, overhang, and stone pocket is occupied, layered three and four deep until the structures nearly touch. There is no mortar here, and no forged iron. Walls are built of flat, unmortared slate, shored up with ribs of horn and lashed with oiled sinew. Roofs are stretched hide and woven mats of pale, salt-cured reed, pinned down with wooden wedges and heavy stones. Narrow stairways of notched timber and bone-pegged planking crisscross the ledges, connecting cistern-troughs carved from single logs that catch the weeping brine before it can turn to slush.

Nothing in the town stays still for long. The inhabitants—bundled thick in grease-rubbed pelts and flat-soled hide boots—work in a state of ceaseless, wordless urgency. A structure left untended for a single afternoon will be pried apart by expanding needle-ice or drowned in creeping slurry. Everywhere you look, small crews are prying up flagstones with antler levers, scraping salt-bloom from doorways with flint blades, or dismantling a sagging roof-frame on one tier while another team lashes a fresh one together two yards away. They communicate mostly by hand-signs and the briefest clicks of the tongue. A sharp shout or the ring of a heavy blow can set off a vibration through the brittle crystal-crusts, bringing down an entire terrace’s worth of overhanging rime in a sudden, suffocating avalanche of slush.

The sparse vegetation—black crustose lichen clinging to the driest upper ridges, and stringy, colorless mats of brine-moss trailing in the runoff channels—cannot possibly support the sheer volume of animal life packed into the hollow. Instead, the basin’s fauna feeds on the mineral bloom and on one another, crowding the ledges alongside the people in an absurd, silent abundance.

Hundreds of plump, flightless birds with dense, velvet-soft plumage—mute as moths—pick their way along the wet slate beams, probing the slush with curved bills for pale, flat-shelled rock-crawlers that swarm wherever the brine pools. Sleek, white-furred burrowers dart in and out of the dry-stone retaining walls, stealing scraps of sinew and dried fat, while thick, soundless clouds of translucent ice-gnats hang suspended in the thin vapor rising off the sun-struck pools. Nothing barks, nothing cries out, nothing buzzes. The only sounds in the bright, freezing air are the soft *shhh* of slumping snow, the faint, dry *tick-tick* of crystal needles forming in the sun, and the rhythmic, muffled scrape of bone tools against wet stone.

When a ridge of slush finally gives way on the upper terrace, it slides down through three levels of walkways in a slow, glittering wave. Nobody yells. Three figures on the middle tier simply step aside onto a timber catwalk, wait for the wet mass to settle against a retaining wall, and immediately drop to their knees with wooden shovels to clear the path before the sun flashes the fresh slurry back into glass.
