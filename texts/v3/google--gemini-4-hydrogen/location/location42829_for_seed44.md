The whole place sits inside a low, sunken basin no more than a few hundred paces across, ringed by a weathered limestone-and-brick dike that has long since vanished beneath a curtain of creeping fig and fern. From the outside, it looks like a dense, steaming grove of old-growth swamp trees. Only when you step down through the gap in the dike—onto a spongy mat of braided roots and bolted iron grating—do you realize the grove is a town, and the town is a factory.

There is no bare earth here. The ground is a springy, waterlogged mattress of living roots, peat, and decades of compressed bark-mulch, bridged by layered catwalks of tarred timber and perforated steel. Where the water table rises above the root-mat, it forms dark, tea-colored channels and rectangular settling tanks lined with moss-furred concrete. Everything is wet. Moisture beads on the undersides of leaves, clings to the galvanized housings of electric motors, and runs in thin, steady trickles down the brick chimneys. The air is thick and warm—roughly the temperature of a greenhouse at midday—heavy with the smell of wet hemp, hot machine oil, rotting peat, and sharp, green sap.

The architecture and the forest have grown into a single, load-bearing structure. Massive, buttressed trunks of swamp-cypress and strangler-fig serve as the primary columns for two- and three-story machine sheds, drying lofts, and pump-houses. Cast-iron brackets, porcelain insulators, and steel I-beams are bolted straight into the living wood, and in many places the bark has swelled around the hardware, swallowing the bolt heads and holding the iron tighter than mortar ever could. Corrugated tin roofs are patched with sheets of copper and weighted down by thick mats of epiphytic moss and sprawling philodendrons. Overhead, a dense canopy of broad leaves filters the daylight into an even, greenish-gold glare, broken here and there by open skylights and iron-framed clerestories where plumes of white steam vent upward.

Despite the sluggish, patient pace of the place, it is loud. A constant, layered roar fills the basin, bouncing off the wet leaves and brick walls until it feels less like noise and more like air pressure. Deepest in the mix is the steady, ground-vibrating hum of the central steam-dynamo, housed in a half-submerged brick vault near the dike. Above that sits the rhythmic *chug-hiss* of reciprocating water pumps, the slap and whine of wide canvas drive-belts running along overhead line-shafts, and the endless, irregular drumming of condensation dripping from high branches onto tin roofs.

The industry itself moves at the speed of rot and evaporation. This is a works built for slow, wet processing—steeping, retting, pressing, and curing. Along the central channels, massive wooden paddle-wheels and Archimedes screws turn with agonizing slowness, lifting dark, nutrient-thick water from the lower sumps into elevated wooden flumes. Long rows of cast-iron vats, heated by steam-jackets wrapped in mildewed burlap, steep bundles of tough marsh-fiber for weeks at a time. Heavy hydraulic presses, driven by electric pumps, squeeze resin and oil out of pulped bark at a rate of an inch an hour. Everything is mechanized, wired, and piped, yet nothing hurries. Gauges needle slowly; conveyor chains crawl; thick brown slurry creeps along open troughs.

Because the works are old and well-settled, the materials have reached a kind of truce with the damp. The cast iron is covered in a dark, stable patina of oil-soaked rust and grey-green lichen. Copper steam-pipes have turned a deep verdigris. Brick retaining walls bulge slightly where giant roots have shouldered them aside decades ago, then stopped moving, leaving the masonry wedged in a permanent, crooked balance.

Very few animals live inside the basin. The constant mechanical din, the copper-tainted runoff in the settling ponds, and the sheer density of pipes, walkways, and human traffic keep birds and larger creatures outside the dike. Now and then a small frog turns up in the cooler intake ditches, or a cloud of midges hangs over a warm fermentation tank, but the only real life here is the plant mass and the workers who tend the valves. The vegetation fills every cubic foot left unclaimed by a moving belt or a scalding pipe: duckweed skins the still tanks, maidenhair ferns sprout from the mortar joints of boiler-houses, and thick, ropey vines climb the guy-wires of the electrical poles, wrapping the entire compact city in a wet, humming, green coat.
