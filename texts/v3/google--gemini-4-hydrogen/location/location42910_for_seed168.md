The air is thin, warm, and bone-dry, the kind of high-altitude clarity where distant ridges thirty miles away look close enough to cut your fingers on. Under a bright, cloudless sky, a vast plateau steps downward in broad, angular terraces. Nothing here has been rounded by rain or wind. The basalt and pale quartzite are freshly fractured, their edges razor-sharp, their faces still showing the raw, glassy striations of how they first cooled and heaved upward. Between the terraces lie wide, hollow basins of compacted mineral dust—alkali, crushed feldspar, and fine graphite—stretching out in flat, uninterrupted expanses.

Across these empty flats, separated by miles of bare stone, sit the groves.

They are startlingly lush—dense, layered thickets of vegetation that rise out of the dry rock without a drop of water to sustain them. Instead of sap, their stems and broad, paddle-shaped leaves are swollen with heavy, non-conductive oils and silvery, liquid-metal emulsions. The canopies are thick and heavy, colored in deep matte blacks, oiled-bronze, and oxidized verdigris, overlapping like the scales of a dense forest. Beneath the broad-leafed crowns, thick cushions of fibrous, copper-tinted moss pack tightly into the sharp crevices of the raw stone, holding the dry dust in place. There is no rot here, no dampness, no fallen leaves turning to mulch; when a frond dries out, it simply stiffens into a brittle, papery wafer of pure carbon and sits unchanged on the ground.

There are no buildings anywhere—no walls, no roofs, no roads, no fences. Yet the plateau is an industrial landscape, operating at a vast, quiet scale.

Where the raw rock splits along the terrace edges, thick veins of native copper, quartz, and magnetite have extruded directly from the crust in clean, geometric ribbons. In the bright, warm sunlight, the whole plateau acts as a giant, solid-state electrical engine. The piezoelectric quartz ridges flex microscopically under the thermal weight of the sun, feeding steady, high-voltage direct current into the copper seams. The lush groves are wired directly into this grid. Their root systems—pale, metallic taproots that bore straight into the unweathered stone—draw the current up into their canopies, using the electricity to crack dry carbon dioxide out of the thin air and bind it into waxy, oil-rich tissue.

Out on the open, empty flats between the groves, the ground itself performs a slow, mechanized sorting. Invisible electrostatic fields, generated by the frictionless drift of dry thermal currents over the graphite pans, lift and separate the mineral dust into neat, parallel windrows—pure iron filings in one long, shimmering line, pale silica in another, fine carbon powder in a third. Scattered across the basins are natural extrusion vents: low, un-housed mounds of fused slag and magnetite where deep subterranean pressure pushes up continuous, cooling rods of refined alloy. They emerge silently, inch by hour, gleaming in the sun like extruded wire before snapping cleanly under their own weight and lying in neat, metallic stacks along the rock.

Despite all this production, the plateau is almost completely silent.

There are no combustion engines, no steam valves, no clanking gears. Everything runs on direct current, static charge, and slow, frictionless mineral creep. If you stand in the middle of a vast, sun-drenched basin, the only sound is the faintest, dry *tick* of a cooling alloy rod, or a whisper-quiet static hiss from the tips of the metallic-leafed canopies when the afternoon heat peaks. No birds cross the bright sky. No insects hum in the oiled-bronze thickets. There are no burrows in the dust, no webs between the sharp rocks, no tracks anywhere.

And nothing changes. Without water, nothing rusts or erodes; without animals, nothing is grazed or scattered. The alloy rods are slowly pulled back down into the crust at the far edges of the basins through hairline tectonic faults, remelted, and extruded again. The groves grow to an exact, balanced density dictated by the voltage of the rock beneath them, shedding carbon wafers at the same rate the mineral flats absorb them. Under the steady, normal light of the high sky, the entire valley exists in a warm, hum-quiet equilibrium—an unbuilt, waterless factory running forever in the open air.
