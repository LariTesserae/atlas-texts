High in the thin air of a young mountain range, a basin no wider than a cathedral nave holds a pool of meltwater so clear it barely seems to have a surface. From above, the sky is a bright, high-altitude blue, and the sun pours straight down into the water without diffusing, throwing sharp-edged shafts of light across the submerged walls. There is no murk here, no drifting green haze. The water is cold—the kind of cold that stiffens joints and numbs skin within seconds—and it sits almost still, stirred only by the faintest, patient convection.

The rock that holds it has not had time to soften. Recently fractured granite, dark slate, and raw veins of pyrite and native copper jut inward in angular shelves and jagged teeth. Nothing has rounded these edges. There is no moss, no kelp, no swaying film of algae to drape over the sharp corners. Aside from thin, rust-colored mineral crusts in the deepest crevices, the stone is bare and nakedly geological.

Yet the basin is crowded. It is packed nearly wall to wall, floor to mid-water, with things that clank, hum, and spark.

Nothing here was built. There are no rivets, no poured concrete, no smelted pipes. Still, the basin sounds—and behaves—like an inundated factory floor. Because water carries vibration without mercy, the small, stone-walled space is deafening. A low, continuous, tooth-rattling *thrumm* vibrates through the water column, layered with the rhythmic *clack-grind* of heavy valves and the sharp, percussive *crack* of electrical discharge echoing off the unweathered granite.

Every inch of the sharp rock is claimed by a dense, layered fauna that has evolved to mine, press, and electrify the mineral-rich cold.

Anchored into the jagged ledges are the **capacitor-barrelers**—thick, ribbed, cylindrical organisms the size of oil drums, their shells grown from alternating layers of magnetite and chalky aragonite. They sit in tight clusters, near-touching, drawing the faintly acidic water through their upper vents at an almost imperceptible trickle. Internally, they separate ions across stacks of biological membranes, hoarding voltage in the cold. They do not hurry. A single barrel takes four or five minutes to reach its threshold. When it does, a ragged arc of blue-white light snaps from its copper-tipped crown to a neighbor’s, accompanied by a sharp,concussive *pop* that sends a visible shiver through the surrounding water.

Draped between these living batteries like thick, sulfur-yellow cables are **conduit-worms**. They are sluggish, eyeless, and as thick as a forearm, their hides dull and rubbery to insulate their insides. They scarcely move from day to day, instead acting as living wiring—channeling slow, steady pulses of direct current from the high, sunlit ledges down to the shadowed floor, where the voltage drives chemical reactions in the mineral silt and warms the immediate millimeter of water around their bellies just enough to keep ice from forming.

Wedged into the talus at the bottom are the **pressers**—massive, geometric bivalves and armored, multi-legged crawlers whose carapaces are plated with naturally precipitated iron sulfide. They operate on hydraulic patience. A presser will spend a full minute slowly, imperceptibly drawing its heavy, toothed valves apart, taking in a mouthful of sharp pyrite scree. Then, with a sudden, hydraulic *boom* that shakes loose grains of sand from the ledges above, the valves slam shut, pulverizing the stone into a fine metallic slurry. The sound bounces endlessly between the hard walls of the small basin.

Suspended in the open, brightly lit mid-water—tethered to the rock by tough, translucent filaments of spun silica—hangs a dense forest of **rotors**. These are colonial organisms shaped like spoked wheels and stacked turbines, their paddles stiff and calcified. They do not spin rapidly; the currents here are too weak, and the water is too cold for frantic motion. Instead, they turn with a heavy, agonizingly steady cadence, one full revolution every half-minute. As they turn, internal plates of quartz and tourmaline rub together under tension, generating a continuous, grinding *whir-groan* and faint, rhythmic sparks of piezoelectric light that flicker inside their translucent hubs.

Everything in this flooded room is pressed close together, layered in tiers of function: the rotors catching the faint drift above, the barrels charging on the sunlit shelves, the cables drooping through the clear light, the pressers booming in the cold rubble below. The air-breathing world above is silent and empty, just sharp stone and thin wind. Down here, beneath the bright surface, the water is thick with presence—a slow, loud, unbuilt engine of flesh and mineral, grinding through its endless, patient shifts in the freezing light.
