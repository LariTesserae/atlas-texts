The first thing that hits you is not the sight of it, but the pressure against your eardrums—a solid, vibrating wall of sound so thick it feels like stepping into warm surf.

The place is small, no wider than a modest hamlet or the nave of a great cathedral, sunk into a low, bowl-shaped depression in the earth. A steep, unbroken rim of ancient, tangled root-cliffs seals it off from whatever lies beyond. From the lip of the basin, you can take in the whole settlement in a single glance, bathed in clear, ordinary daylight.

Yet there is no stone here, no brick, no iron, no trace of a chisel or a saw. Every street, arch, gallery, and tower is grown from living plant matter.

It is an unmistakable city—layered, organized, and infrastructural—built entirely by biology. Towering, hollow-stemmed reeds thick as oaks rise from a floor of compacted, springy root-mat, braiding themselves into slender buttresses and vaulted arcades. Broad, leathery leaves stretch taut between the trunks like awnings and suspended plazas. Hollowed gourd-chambers hang in tiers from fibrous cables, pierced by oval apertures that serve as windows and doorways. Narrow viaducts of grooved bark carry thin trickles of water from one terrace to the next, feeding shallow, cupped leaf-cisterns at the intersections of the lanes.

Despite the density of its design, the basin feels shockingly empty. The architecture is all lattice, span, and hollow shell. Vast, dizzying voids of open air separate the slender plant-towers; the plazas are suspended over nothing, and the chambers themselves are mostly cavernous, echoing interiors. You could stand on a high walkway and look straight down through three tiers of braided bridges to the damp root-floor far below, with nothing in between but warm, sunlit air.

Warmth clings to the basin, steady and heavy, carrying the sharp, green scent of crushed stems and wet cellulose. There are no rivers here, no deep standing lakes—only a persistent, intermittent dampness. Condensation beads along the waxy undersides of the roof-leaves and gathers in the grooved bark, dripping in steady, rhythmic bursts into the cisterns below. Every few hours, a brief, warm mist drifts down over the rim, slicking the walkways before evaporating back into the hollow air.

This is an old place, long settled into its own balance. The bark of the lower arcades is scarred and polished smooth by centuries of passing claws and feet. Dead fronds rot cleanly into mulch at the base of the pillars, feeding the new pale-green shoots that constantly replace them. Nothing here is hurried or newly scarred; the city has been in this exact equilibrium for ages.

And it is deafening.

The hollowness of the plant-architecture turns the entire basin into a single, resonant instrument. Warm air rising from the low floor rushes through millions of porous capillaries, split reeds, and drum-tight leaf membranes, producing a continuous, booming organ-drone that rattles your ribs. Layered over this is the clamor of the fauna, which swarms across every surface in numbers that defy the empty space.

Translucent, multi-legged climbers pack the undersides of the bridges, scraping their chitinous plates in rhythmic, ear-splitting chirrs. Clouds of iridescent, needle-winged insects boil through the open voids, their collective hum pitched high enough to make your teeth ache. Long-limbed, membranous gliders—half-lizard, half-bat—launch themselves from the upper gourd-towers, shrieking as they stoop to snatch climbers off the vertical trunks, while pale, throat-sacced amphibians cling to the damp cisterns and bark in booming, synchronized choruses.

There is no quiet corner. The sound does not fade with distance; instead, the hollow walls catch every drip, every wingbeat, every snap of jaws, and throw it back amplified, layered into a continuous roar that presses against your chest.

If you stay long enough for your eyes to adjust past the normal daylight and the familiar, worn shapes of the streets, you begin to notice that the place does not quite hold together.

The echoes are wrong. A drop of condensation falls from a high leaf, strikes a cistern, and the splash rings out—except you hear the splash two seconds *before* the drop detaches. You watch a glider swoop through an archway on the far side of the basin, and its shriek arrives directly behind your left ear, dry and close, as if it had screamed inside your collar.

The geometry of the empty spaces slips when you aren't looking directly at it. A suspended bridge that spans twenty paces from left to right takes forty paces to cross from right to left, though the braided vines beneath your boots never stretch or shift. Shadows cast by the noon sun fall at contradictory angles on adjacent towers, one leaning east, the next leaning straight down into the void. Occasionally, a breeze ripples across the taut leaf-walls and makes them briefly translucent, revealing extra corridors, stacked galleries, and deeper streets extending outward—spaces that cannot possibly fit inside the small, visible perimeter of the root-cliffs.

The animals seem native to this fraying. A swarm of insects will bank sharply around a pillar, pass through a patch of empty air, and emerge a moment later slightly out of phase with themselves—trailing faint, shimmering afterimages that keep buzzing for several seconds after the bodies have moved on. Predators hunt across the seams: a glider dives straight into the solid bark of a hollow tower, vanishes without a sound, and bursts out of a cistern three tiers lower, jaws clamped around a thrashing amphibian.

You stand on a damp, braided balcony in the middle of the warm daylight, surrounded by vast, empty air, with the roar of a living city pressing against your skull—knowing that the floor beneath you is solid, living root, and that the space across the street might not be the same size twice.
