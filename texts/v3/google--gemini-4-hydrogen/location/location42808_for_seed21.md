High up, where the air ought to be thin and biting, there is instead a small, cupped shelf of stone that holds the heat like an open palm.

It is no larger than a cathedral nave—perhaps two hundred paces from the sheer, jagged wall at its back to the ragged lip where it drops away into a bright, haze-softened void. Everything about the rock is young and unfinished. There are no rounded boulders here, no soft gravel or wind-smoothed ledges. The terrace is freshly fractured black basalt and pale, glassy silicate, thrust upward recently enough that every ridge is still razor-edged, every crack still sharp enough to split a boot-sole.

The sun strikes it with an ordinary, steady, high-altitude clarity, yet the heat is relentless—a thick, radiating fever that rises from the stone itself and hangs trapped within the amphitheater of cliffs. And yet, the place is wet.

Water does not arrive here as rain. It weeps directly from the seams of the jagged rock and condenses out of the shimmering, overheated air. Along the back wall, dark mineral veins sweat continuously, sending thin, steaming sheets of water gliding down the sheer faces. The water collects in shallow, irregular basins chipped into the floor, never quite deep enough to swim in, always warm to the touch—some near simmering. From these pools, it rises again in faint, ghostly ribbons of vapor that vanish a few feet above the surface, only to re-condense on the cooler, shaded undersides of the overhanging crags. It is a closed, restless loop, active and silent.

Most of the terrace is bare stone, giving the space a stark, open stillness—much empty, glistening black rock between one thing and the next. Scattered across this expanse, however, are four or five artifacts that do not belong to any recognizable age of building.

Near the center, half-sunken into a basin of steaming water, lies a disc of matte-gray material about the width of a cartwheel. It has no seams, no bolts, and no apparent thickness at its edge; it simply interrupts the rock. Where water washes over it, the droplets do not flatten or spread—they bead into perfect, trembling spheres that roll across the surface as if repelled, occasionally leaping a fraction of an inch into the air before evaporating with a soft *tink*. Further toward the cliff-lip, three slender, bone-white struts rise from the basalt in an asymmetrical tripod, leaning inward toward a point in empty space. There is nothing suspended between them, yet the air in the center of their triangle bends the sunlight slightly, like heat-shimmer over tarmac, and the rock directly beneath them is dry as ash, even while everything around it drips.

In the margins—where the weeping rock meets the hot pools, and around the base of the scattered, humming artifacts—life takes hold in sudden, lush bursts. Because there is no soil, the plants anchor themselves directly into the wet crevices, forming dense, isolated islands of green against the stark stone. Thick, waxy-leafed ferns the color of bruised plums uncurl in the steam; clusters of translucent, bladder-bodied mosses swell with warm condensation until they glow like wet glass in the sun. Along the edges of the deepest pools grow tall, whip-thin reeds with silica-heavy stalks, spaced wide apart like lonely sentinels.

Nothing here moves fast, and almost nothing makes a sound. The high, jagged rim keeps the mountain winds at bay, leaving the terrace wrapped in a heavy, damp quiet. The only noises are small and intimate: the steady, arrhythmic *tock* of heavy drops falling from an overhang into a hot basin, the faint *hiss* where a stray splash hits a sun-baked ridge, the dry, barely audible *tick* of the silica reeds flexing in a rising thermal.

If you watch the wet pockets long enough, you begin to notice the fauna—rare, patient things built for the scalding damp. A pale, flat-bodied amphibian, no bigger than a thumb, glides along the submerged edge of a basalt shelf, its skin nearly transparent, revealing a slow, rhythmic pulse inside. On one of the bone-white struts, a single crustacean with a shell grown from precipitated white minerals clings motionless, scraping an invisible film from the artifact’s surface.

As the afternoon sun shifts, the shadows of the jagged cliffs lengthen across the shelf, and the cycle subtly alters. The rock cools by a fraction of a degree; the condensation thickens; the weeping walls run a little faster, feeding the warm pools before the evening chill can claim them. The matte-gray disc hums at a pitch just below hearing—a faint vibration felt in the soles of the feet—and the small, steaming sanctuary settles into its quiet, alien rhythm, suspended high above the world.
