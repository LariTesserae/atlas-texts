At this altitude, the air is thin enough to taste like cold metal, and the sky is a bleached, overhead glare that never softens. The valley—if it can be called a valley, stretching wide across several miles between distant, jagged rims—is not hollow. It is packed.

From ridge to ridge, there is scarcely a gap wide enough to turn a shoulder without brushing stone. Millions of pale, fluted mineral fins, travertine blades, and eroded calcite towers rise side by side in a dense, interlocking thicket. They stand ten to thirty feet high, worn into smooth, concave scoops and razor-thin ribs, all aligned along the same prevailing axis like the gills of a vast, petrified fish. There is no soil between them, no gravel floor—only narrow, slick chutes where one formation merges into the base of the next.

Everything is wet, and everything is blinding.

A cool, steady mist condenses directly out of the thin air wherever it strikes the chilled stone. Water does not gather in quiet pools here; it sheets. Every bone-white and pale-gray surface is glazed in a continuous, trembling film of runoff that catches the high-altitude sun and throws it back in a thousand fractured mirrors. Even in the narrowest alleys between the stone fins, there is no true shade—only a fierce, bouncing whiteness reflected off wet calcite walls. The temperature stays locked in a damp chill, just above freezing, cold enough to numb bare fingers on the rock, never cold enough to crust the runoff into ice.

And the noise is absolute.

A relentless, unvarying wind pours down over the high rim and drives straight through the packed labyrinth of stone flutes. Because the formations are spaced so tightly and carved to nearly uniform proportions, the entire valley acts as a single, miles-wide acoustic chamber. The wind does not gust or sigh; it locks into a deafening, brassy roar, a physical pressure against the eardrums that vibrates in the teeth and sternum. Beneath the wind-howl lies the secondary roar of water: millions of steady, parallel curtains of runoff slapping onto stone ledges and echoing endlessly between the hard, wet walls. Neither sound rises nor falls. There are no lulls to measure the volume against. After a few minutes, the roar stops feeling like something happening *in* the air and begins to feel like a solid property of the space itself, as fixed as the rock.

Nothing grows here. Not a thread of moss, not a crust of lichen, not a stray seed caught in a seam. The mineral surfaces are scrubbed clean by the constant slide of water and the scouring wind, leaving only bare, pale silica and gypsum, faintly striated where ancient mineral layers were laid down and then worn back into balance. Where the stone is darkest, it is only a slick, wet slate-gray, stained by thin, invisible films of mineral-eating bacteria that give the rock a faint, greasy sheen underfoot.

The strangest thing about the place is its stillness inside the uproar. Despite the deafening sound and the constant sheen of moving water, nothing changes. The wind holds the same speed hour after hour. The sky remains the same hard, cloudless white-blue. The water weeps from the exact same seams at the exact same rate, neither flooding the narrow chutes nor drying to a trickle. The stone fins have already been carved to the shape the wind and water want them in; they stand in a finished, balanced equilibrium, neither visibly crumbling nor growing.

Life is rare, and what little exists here keeps to the margins of the noise. In the tightest, leeward crevices where the stone fins fuse together and the wind-roar drops to a dull, bone-rattling hum, small, pale, flat-bodied creatures—something like blind, thick-shelled limpets or waxy, segmented arthropods—cling tightly to the weeping rock, grazing on the invisible mineral slime. They do not move quickly, and they do not react to sound; in a world where the air never stops screaming, vibration is just the texture of the floor. Far overhead, well above the blinding glare of the wet stone teeth, an occasional pale glider rides the steady updrafts without beating a wing, never descending into the bright, deafening maze below.
