The heat arrives before the sight of the place: a dry-edged, oven-thick pressure rising through the access shaft, smelling of baked dust, ozone, and sweet, stewed sap. When the shaft opens into the chamber, the space is small enough to feel the walls pressing back—roughly the size of a narrow cathedral, carved out of dark, stratified basalt—yet so densely packed with overlapping shapes that you cannot see from one side to the other.

There is no sun here, and no sky. The ceiling is a low, soot-blackened vault of fractured stone, maybe eighty feet overhead, hung with curtains of braided copper cable and thick, calcified roots. Light is scarce and bruised-looking: a dim, murky twilight cobbled together from a few surviving indicator-filaments high on the walls, the dull cherry glow of overheated resistor-coils, and the pale, feverish luminescence of the plants themselves.

Because the chamber is only damp, not wet, there are no pools or streams. The floor is a crust of rusted iron-flour, fallen mica flakes, and baked clay, interrupted only by occasional dark, glistening patches where condensation has gathered on a cooler seam of rock and dripped down. Yet the air feels thick in the lungs, heavy with mineral vapor that never quite condenses until it touches something receptive.

Out of that meager moisture and oppressive heat, the vegetation has grown lush and suffocatingly dense. It is nothing like surface greenery. Here, the flora is thermophilic and strange, thriving on the chamber’s radiant warmth and the stray electrical currents leaking from its old machinery. Broad, leathery fronds the color of tarnished pewter and bruised plum crowd every horizontal surface, their undersides ribbed with waxy channels designed to trap rising vapor and hoard it in swollen, translucent bladders. Hanging from the overhead conduits are dense, tangled beards of fibrous, pale-orange tendrils that sway slightly in the rising thermals. Where these tendrils brush against a live busbar or a cracked ceramic insulator, they do not char; instead, they fuse to the metal, swelling into knotty, bark-like galls that emit a dim, steady amber glow—tiny biological filaments lighting the gloom around them.

Scattered across the chamber floor, half-swallowed by this crowding growth, are the remains of an old industrial installation. They are not neat rows of machines, just three or four monumental, cast-iron heat-exchangers and a pair of sunken dynamo housings, connected by sagging catwalks and corroded manifolds. Everything here has existed together for so long that the boundary between geology, industry, and botany has eroded into a single, settled equilibrium. Stalactites of green verdigris and rust-colored mineral sinter hang from the pipe joints, anchoring them to the floor. Thick, woody vines have grown straight through the louvered vents of the transformer casings, using the copper windings inside as trellises, cementing the machinery in place.

Despite the lack of movement, the chamber is loud—a thick, oppressive wall of sound that vibrates in the teeth. It is not the frantic clatter of an active factory, but the resonant, amplified roar of a massive, slow-cycling system. Deep beneath the floor, a surviving geothermal turbine or induction core turns at an agonizingly sluggish pace, sending a continuous, sub-bass thrum up through the basalt. Layered over it is the high, steady singing of superheated steam forcing its way through pinhole leaks in the iron manifolds, and the wet, rhythmic *thump* of a single enormous pressure-bladder somewhere in the gloom, expanding and venting once every minute, shaking loose a fine, dry rain of rust-dust from the ceiling.

Nothing skitters in the dust. There are no insects drawn to the glowing plant-nodes, no bats clinging to the basalt ribs, no blind cave-things waiting in the damp patches beneath the pipes. The only things that stir are the rising curtains of heat-haze, the imperceptible creep of mineral crust along a dripping flange, and the patient, silent unfurling of another pewter-colored frond reaching toward the hum of a live wire. The chamber has found its balance—hot, dim, deafening, and entirely closed off from the world above—running on a clock measured in decades of rust and condensation.
