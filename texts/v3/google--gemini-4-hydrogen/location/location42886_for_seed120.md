It is barely larger than a potter’s drying-shed, yet your ears pop the moment you are inside it, and the water in the shallow terracotta pans simmers at a touch.

The air outside—beyond the stretched, oiled-hide membranes that bulge and shudder along the ribs of the ceiling—is a thin, screaming blue miles above any sea or mountain. You can hear it. Even muffled by three layers of mud-daubed reed and taut rawhide, the upper-atmosphere wind hits the exterior like a fist on a drumhead, filling the tiny room with a low, relentless *thrum* that vibrates in your molars. Against it, bone-pipe vents wedged into the corners whistle and spit needles of scalding steam, and fat drops of condensation slap rhythmically onto broad, waxy leaves. It is loud in here, a wet, percussive clamor with nowhere to dissipate.

Everything is close enough to touch without taking a step. There is no bare earth, no bedrock, no sky—only a packed, interlocking warren of hand-built terraces, hanging woven-willow cradles, and stacked, unglazed clay baffles that leave barely an elbow’s width of crooked passage down the center. The light is dim and bruised, filtering through small panes of scraped horn and oiled gut set high in the rafters, tinted a murky olive by the sheer density of foliage pressed flat against them. Underneath, a single sunken brazier of packed ash and dung-charcoal glows a dull, sullen cherry, driving the heat up until the air feels like wet wool in the lungs.

Nothing in the room has ever been worn down. There is no dust, no rot, no softened edge. The grey clay plastered over the wattle walls is still slick and dark with slip, bearing the fresh, sharp ridges of thumbprints and bone scrapers. The sinew and hemp lashings holding the hanging shelves are pale, rigid, and unfrayed; the hammered copper pins wedged into the joint-work gleam like new teeth. It is a place that has not finished arriving. A half-coiled jar resting on a wooden ledge does not sit idle in the heat—its wet rim trembles almost imperceptibly, slowly extruding another millimeter of raw, glistening mud on its own, as if the potter’s hands had left an invisible momentum spinning in the clay.

Because it is so packed, and because the rules that hold things apart have thinned out at this altitude, the architecture and the vegetation have begun to mistake each other for themselves.

Broad, heat-bloated leaves—deep green and slick with condensed steam—uncurl directly from the mortar joints between fired mud bricks. Pale gourd-vines braid themselves seamlessly into the knotted hemp rigging, pulling the knots tighter as they swell, until you cannot tell where the cordage ends and the stem begins. Roots do not search for soil; there is no soil. Instead, white, tuberous fingers wrap around the bellies of freshly thrown pots, sinking into the wet terracotta and turning the clay soft and fibrous where they touch. Along the veins of the largest fern-fronds, the surface is embossed with the unmistakable cross-hatching of a woven reed mat.

Water obeys no single direction here. Drawn out of the boiling pans and the sweating leaves, it condenses in sheets against the frigid outer membranes—chilled by the stratospheric void just a finger’s width beyond the hide—and rolls inward. Drops bead along the underside of a flat clay lintel, hesitate, and then slide sideways along a taut bowstring of gut, climbing the stem of a drooping moss-bundle to pool in a pinched-mud cup that hangs upside down from the rafters. Sometimes a droplet falls straight down, strikes a hot stone baffle, and vanishes into a hiss; sometimes it hangs mid-air in the dimness for three heartbeats, trembling to the bass note of the wind, before darting laterally into the throat of a pitcher-plant.

In the tight, dark crevices behind the lowest shelves, space itself seems poorly mortared. If you peer between two freshly lashed reed baskets where the wall ought to be, you do not see the back of the mud-daub. Instead, the gap opens onto a dizzying, silent sliver of the same dim, steaming room repeated downward—and below that, another, smaller and brighter with cold blue sky—stacked like nested bowls over an impossible, vertiginous drop. A stray tendril of vine reaches out into that gap and simply stops, its tip wavering in a draft that smells of ozone and ice.

Animal life is scarce in the scalding gloom, limited to what can endure the pressure and the noise. Clinging to the weeping belly of a water-jar is a single palm-sized, translucent salamander, its skin so thin you can see the slow, dark pulse of its throat as it laps at the wet clay slip. Every few minutes, from somewhere deep inside the packed tiers of leaf and pottery, a solitary, heavy-shelled snail rasps its radula against fresh terracotta—a dry, rhythmic *scritch-scritch* that cuts briefly through the hiss of the steam vents.

The room breathes in jagged, active cycles. The brazier mutters; the hide walls flex inward with a sharp *crack* as a gust tears past outside; a shelf of wet pots settles a fraction of an inch tighter into its vine-wrapped cradle; a new leaf splits its sheath in the dim amber light, already slick with water that is already rising toward the ceiling. Everything is pressed shoulder to shoulder, wet, hot, and freshly made, suspended in the screaming dark at the top of the world.
