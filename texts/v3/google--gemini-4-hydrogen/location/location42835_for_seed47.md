The light here is ordinary—a clear, steady, pale daylight that sharpens every edge—and the air is cool enough to raise gooseflesh, dry as old paper. What takes a moment to register is the silence.

You stand on a shelf of pale, vitrified stone, looking out over a drop so steep and deep that the bottom is lost in haze. Across the gulf, miles away, a wall of ochre dust three kilometers high is tearing itself apart. It boils upward in tight, violent spirals, shears sideways in a sudden cross-current, and collapses into a churning veil of silt. You brace for the roar, the thunder of displaced air, the hiss of grit. Nothing arrives. Not even a tremor through the soles of your boots. The air is too thin, too dry, or too strangely emptied of medium to carry a storm’s voice; the world thrashes in near-total quiet, broken only by the faint, dry tick of a cooling pebble or the rasp of your own breathing.

This is a place of vast, hollowed-out distances. Most of what you see is open space. The terrain does not form crowded mountain ranges so much as widely separated skeletons: solitary spires, razor-thin fins of basalt, and colossal, wind-planed arches standing kilometers apart like the ribs of a sunken continent. Everything has been worn down to its final, most stubborn core. There are no jagged, youthful peaks here, no fresh talus slopes. Eons of dry abrasion have polished the cliffs until they look like weathered bone or melted wax, pitted with smooth, hollow alcoves and long horizontal flutes where ancient winds—or things older than wind—have eaten away every softer seam.

Water is almost a rumor. In the deepest, permanently shadowed notches of the cliffs, a thin rime of mineral frost clings to the stone, sublimating directly into vapor the moment a stray reflection warms it. No streams have cut these chasms in living memory; the dry salt-pans far below, glimpsed briefly when the dust-curtains part, are crusted white and cracked into plates that haven't seen rain in geological ages.

Yet nothing is still. The emptiness between the monoliths is in constant, chaotic motion.

Great, soundless gales sweep through the canyons, driven by sharp thermal drops and something less predictable—erratic, invisible shears in the air that seem to rise from the abyssal floor itself. Loose mineral flour and microscopic metallic chaff, the ground-down residue of an ancient crust, are whipped into towering, hyperactive vortices that twist across the void, collide, and dissolve in seconds. Now and then, a fist-sized stone on a nearby ledge simply lifts an inch, hovers trembling in a silent eddy of force, and shoots sideways into the gulf, vanishing without a sound.

Scattered across these immense distances are the remnants of whatever once built here—or whatever this world was engineered to be. They are rare enough that you can walk for hours along a ridge without seeing one, and then come upon a single, incomprehensible object: a slender, matte-grey needle rising half a kilometer from a wind-scoured saddle, its surface entirely unpitted despite the ages; or a broken ring of dark, pearlescent alloy spanning a chasm, its fractured ends smooth as cut glass, humming with no sound you can hear, only a faint prickle along the hairs of your forearm.

Some of these scattered artifacts are still active, in a broken, turbulent way. Down in the haze, a sunken cylinder the size of a fortress periodically vents a silent, shimmering plume of ionized dust that climbs straight into the sky like an inverted waterfall, scattering the surrounding storm-fronts before winking out. Elsewhere, a cluster of small, windowless polyhedra drifts slowly along a cliff face, tethered to nothing, dragging thin ribbons of lifted grit in their wake like iron filings behind a magnet. The physics that holds them up—and that occasionally twists the air into sudden, noiseless squalls—feels worn thin, like an old machine running without a governor, its energies leaking out into the dust and stone.

Life here is sparse, tucked into the margins of the violence. In the leeward crevices of the ancient arches, away from the worst of the scouring grit, dry mats of pale, silicate-shelled lichen cling to the rock. They drink the fleeting vapor of sublimating frost and seem to feed, in part, on the static charge built up by the passing storms. Here and there, a few brittle, wire-thin stalks—more like spun fiber than wood—grow out of hairline cracks, bending flat against the stone whenever a silent gust sweeps over them, their tiny, hook-covered fronds combing trace moisture and organic dust from the thin air.

And if you watch the open gulfs long enough, you will see the gliders. They are rare—maybe one or two in a day—vast, paper-thin creatures with translucent wingspans the width of a small boat, almost weightless. They never flap. They ride the chaotic, silent updrafts between the distant spires, banking sharply through the boiling dust-plumes to snatch airborne spores or tiny, armored mites blown off the cliffs. They pass across the clear daylight without a whisper, just a brief, drifting shadow over the ancient stone, before the empty miles swallow them again.
