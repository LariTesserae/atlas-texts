At nearly five thousand meters, the air is thin enough to make the chest work for every breath, and wet enough to coat the lungs in cold mist.

This is a broad, high-altitude basin—a valley several miles across, cradled between jagged, unnamed ridges that vanish into a low ceiling of pewter cloud. Nothing here has been softened by time. The rock is newly thrust up, raw and angular: dark basalt and fractured slate, cleaved into sharp planes and knife-edged talus that has not yet been rounded by millennia of ice or wind. Every stone looks as though it were broken yesterday. In the dim, flat light, the wet surfaces shine like oiled iron.

Water is everywhere, though there are no deep lakes or rushing rivers. Instead, the cold, saturated air condenses directly onto the stone. Every cliff face and fractured outcrop weeps a slow, steady film of moisture that gathers in the crevices, darkens the scree, and collects in shallow, black-bottomed pools along the basin floor. Where the ground flattens into wide, boggy shelves, the meltwater turns sluggish and cold, rimmed with thin, brittle fringes of ice in the deeper shadows.

Across the vast, open floor of the valley, almost nothing stands upright. There are no trees, no shrubs, no human marks—not a cairn, not a footpath, not a scrap of wire or rusted tin. The ground is completely untouched. Between the wet rock aprons and the mist-hung ridges, the space feels hollow and immense, with long, empty stretches of slick stone and fog separating one feature from the next.

Yet the valley is not isolated from the world below it. Even without a single built structure in sight, the weight of an industrial civilization presses up against the rim of the basin. The mist carries a faint, sharp tang of coal smoke, sulfur, and ozone, drifted upward from unseen lowlands miles away. In the quietest hollows, the rainwater pooling on the slate wears a delicate, iridescent sheen of hydrocarbons, and the pale mineral crusts along the weeping seams are faintly stained with soot. Far off to the south, where the cloud deck thins toward the horizon, a bruised, sodium-amber glow bleeds upward from beneath the mist—the only sign of the factories and smelters churning in the valleys far below.

Mostly, the basin is quiet. You hear the thin, persistent *drip-drip* of water falling from sharp rock edges into shallow pools, the low hiss of wet wind threading through the scree, and the occasional dry *clack* of a loose stone shifting in the frost. But if you stand still on the bare granite, you can feel something else through the soles of your boots: a faint, steady, sub-audible thrumming traveling up through the dense, newly fractured bedrock, like the distant beat of massive turbines or deep-earth pumps miles away. Now and then, muffled by the heavy clouds overhead, the low drone of a cargo plane passes invisible above the ceiling, vibrating briefly in the wet air before fading back into the drip of the stone.

Because the valley looks so empty and cold, the sheer abundance of life comes as a surprise. Vegetation is sparse—only tough, low-lying tufts of wiry sedge, dark-green cushion plants wedged into the lee of boulders, and slick mats of liverwort and lichen feeding on the constant seepage. Still, wherever water pools or rock retains a trace of warmth, the basin teems.

The shallow, soot-rimmed bogs are dense with cold-tolerant frogs and dark, slow-moving salamanders, their eggs clustered in the mineral-tinted water. Under every flat slab of slate, tiny pale crustaceans and stonefly larvae crowd the wet films. In the brief lulls when the wind drops, clouds of midges and alpine flies erupt from the sedge-tufts, hanging in thick, vibrating ribbons over the black pools.

Those sudden hatches set the whole valley in motion. Flocks of high-altitude pipits, finches, and dark-winged swifts burst out of the fog in tight, wheeling formations, skimming inches above the wet scree to gorge on the swarming insects, then vanishing back into the grey mist as abruptly as they arrived. Along the edges of the marshy shelves, lean, thick-furred alpine foxes and pale-coated stoats patrol the vast empty distances between the water pockets, trotting silently over the sharp, wet stone, noses low to the ground. High above them, pale-eyed harriers quarter through the dim air just beneath the cloud base, riding the cold updrafts, watching for anything that moves against the dark, weeping rock.

A sudden gust sweeps across the basin, driving the mist sideways and rattling the ice-fringed sedges. The birds scatter; the insects drop back into the moss; the foxes slip behind a ridge of raw slate. For a long moment, the valley returns to its vast, cold stillness—nothing visible for miles but wet stone, thin mist, and the faint, distant hum of an unseen world trembling beneath the rock.
