Up here, the horizon is a pale, uninterrupted line so distant it seems to curve, and the sky sits close enough to touch the crowns of the highest ridges. It is a vast, high-altitude tableland—a continent of stepped, weathered stone worn down over eons into broad terraces, shallow basins, and rounded whalebacks of dark basalt and granite. Nothing is jagged anymore; the rock has settled into its final, patient geometry. Yet you almost never see the stone itself, because every available millimeter of the plateau is occupied.

It is a cool, crisp world, bathed in clear, normal daylight that turns bright and silvery wherever it catches a wet surface—and every surface is wet. Clouds do not merely pass over this altitude; they form directly against the land, condensing out of the thin air onto millions of tiny leaves, hairs, and moss-tips. Water does not rush or roar here. Instead, it weeps from the terraced ledges, beads on the tips of sedges, and slides silently down into deep, saturated cushions of sphagnum and liverwort. There is no bare dirt, no gravel, no open sand. The vegetation is packed so tightly that it forms a continuous, springy, multi-layered pelt several feet thick—dwarf willows knotted into the peat, dense mats of waxy-leafed shrubs, hanging curtains of lichen, and beneath them, an endless, waterlogged labyrinth of roots and decaying fiber.

Because the turf is so thick and the air so thin, the entire plateau is wrapped in an extraordinary quiet. Footsteps vanish into the sponge of the moss. Even a steady wind makes barely a hiss through the low, stiff grasses. If you stand still, the only sounds are the faint, irregular *plip* of condensation dropping into a hidden pool and the soft, wet rustle of something shifting unseen in the undergrowth.

Yet the ground beneath your boots is not entirely still, nor entirely wild.

If you kneel and part the thick, cold moss, or peer down into one of the countless dark sinkholes where the peat gives way to flooded stone, you begin to notice the other architecture of the place—an architecture with no walls, no roofs, no towers, and no roads. There are no buildings on the plateau, not a single constructed shelter or standing frame, yet the entire highland operates like a vast, submerged engine.

Deep within the saturated peat and the fractured bedrock below, an industrial network runs at continental scale. Thick, braided cables of tarnished copper and polymer—indistinguishable at a glance from blackened taproots—snake for hundreds of miles beneath the moss, pulsing with steady, low-voltage direct current. In the flooded basalt shafts, silent hydraulic turbines, grown over with pale freshwater sponges and encrusted in mineral scale, turn endlessly under the sheer, patient weight of percolating meltwater. There is no clanking of iron, no belching of smoke; the machinery is wholly liquid-cooled, pressurized, and muffled by meters of living turf. Only a faint, sub-audible thrum—felt more in the soles of the feet and the back of the jaw than heard—betrays the scaled, continuous work happening underfoot.

The landscape and the industry have long since grown into a single, seamless metabolism. The water in the shallow, terraced pools tastes faintly metallic, sharp with dissolved salts and a trace of ozone. In certain basins, the moss grows unnaturally warm and bright emerald, nourished by the waste heat of buried transformers and electrolytic cells that crack the acidic bog-water into gas, quietly venting thin, odorless wisps of vapor into the cool air. Where mineral-rich brine is pumped up through natural siphons to evaporate in broad, shallow pans of weathered rock, dense crusts of pale sulfur and copper sulfate rim the edges like frost, immediately colonized by specialized, metal-tolerant lichens.

Life here is abundant, dense, and adapted to both the chill and the current. Without room to spare, the fauna lives layered into the vegetation and the water. Heavy-coated, broad-hoofed grazers move in slow, silent herds across the misted ridges, their wide feet spreading their weight so they neither sink into the mire nor disturb the buried conduits. They rarely vocalize; in the muffled air, they communicate through posture and the low-frequency thump of a hoof against the peat. Beneath them, the waterlogged root-mats teem with soft-skinned amphibians, blind burrowing newts, and vast colonies of beetles whose carapaces have taken on a dull, metallic sheen from the heavy-mineral seepage—insects that navigate the foggy plateau by sensing the magnetic fields of the underground power lines.

Predators, too, have learned the rules of this quiet, electrified marsh. Low-slung, thick-furred carnivores with broad, sensitive snouts prowl the edges of the terraced pools, hunting not by ear or eye in the thick mist, but by reading the faint electrical disturbances made by a moving muscle in the wet turf. When they strike, it is sudden and almost noiseless—a brief splash, a ripple across a copper-rimmed pool, and then the moss closes back over the stillness.

High overhead, the vast weather of the plateau turns slowly on its own axis: cold air rising, condensing into bright, damp cloud-sheets, feeding the weeping rocks, turning the silent turbines in the dark, and charging the wet, packed earth with a quiet, ceaseless purpose.
