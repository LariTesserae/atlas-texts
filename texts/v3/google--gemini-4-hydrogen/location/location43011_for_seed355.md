The air hits first—not as a breeze, for there is no wind here, but as a thick, feverish weight that settles onto the skin and refuses to evaporate. It is hot, hovering near the temperature of a living body, and so saturated with moisture that breathing feels like drawing warm mist through damp cloth. Above, there is no sky, no stars, no horizon where earth meets air; there is only an unbroken, humid darkness that presses down close enough to touch.

You are standing in a low, sunken basin—a drowned valley or a wide, flat delta—where the ground barely rises above the water table. Everywhere, the surface is wet. Shallow, lukewarm water covers most of the expanse, ankle-deep in some stretches, knee-deep in others, broken by broad, flat banks of dark, slick silt. The water is clear enough to stain your skin with a faint, oily slick of dissolved minerals, though in the near-total dark you can only tell its depth by how high the warmth climbs up your legs.

Out of this steaming flatland rises a city.

It is unmistakably urban in its logic—laid out in long, ruler-straight causeways, sunken plazas, terraced ziggurats, colonnades, and hollow, windowless towers—yet it is spread thin across the basin. Great, empty expanses of shallow lagoon and dark mud separate one district from the next. A solitary, multi-tiered amphitheater might sit half a mile out in the dark water, linked to a cluster of honeycomb-walled galleries only by a narrow, elevated walkway that vanishes into the mist.

What makes the city impossible is its making. There is not a single cut stone, not a trace of mortar, not a hinge, beam, or tool-mark anywhere. The entire settlement is *accreted*—grown out of the mineral-heavy water like colossal, geometric coral. The walls are made of a pale, porous travertine, pitted like old bone and rounded by centuries of condensing steam. Arches rise in clean, deliberate curves, only to melt seamlessly into the flat terraces beneath them, as if the earth itself had dreamed of civic planning and slowly calcified the thought. Everything is ancient and deeply weathered, stained with dark tide-lines near the water and softened at every edge.

Despite the scale of the place, it is almost entirely silent. The heat and humidity seem to swallow sound before it can travel. Stepping through the water produces no bright splash, only a dull, heavy *thud*, as if the liquid is slightly thicker than water ought to be. There are no birds, no insects, no fish stirring the shallows—nothing animal moves here at all. The only life is sparse and blind: scattered rings of pale, leafless stalks that cling to the damp crevices of the travertine with suction-like pads, swelling silently with warm gas over the course of hours until their translucent skins split without a sound. Elsewhere, thin patches of black, velvet-textured crust stain the bases of the colonnades, drinking the endless condensation.

What light exists comes from no sky. Now and then, when a mineral crust fractures from the heat, a dull, greyish phosphorescence leaks from the crack for a few seconds before fading. Otherwise, you navigate by touch, by the radiant heat holding in the stone walls, and by the subtle pull of the water.

And yet the basin is restless, caught in cycles that quietly ignore the rules of the physical world.

The water does not obey gravity cleanly. In one wide lagoon, the surface tilts at a slight, steady angle, held there by nothing, never spilling over the mudbank at its lower edge. In another, concentric ripples travel *inward* from the darkness toward a still center where nothing has fallen, vanishing into a pinhole of flat water.

Every few hours, the city undergoes a silent tide. The water does not rush in from anywhere; it simply *rises* from everywhere at once—oozing up through the porous floors of the plazas, weeping out of the travertine columns, condensing out of the hot air in heavy, noiseless sheets. As the lower terraces submerge, the pale stone softens slightly, like wet chalk. When the water eventually recedes—sinking straight down into the mud without a gurgle—it leaves behind fresh, paper-thin layers of mineral crust. Over time, doorways seal themselves shut, new steps precipitate out of nowhere, and old causeways thicken and widen.

Even the space between the buildings refuses to stay fixed. You can walk a straight, colonnaded avenue, counting the arches by hand in the dark, and find that the distant tower you were approaching has not grown any closer, while the open lagoon behind you has folded into a sunken courtyard you never passed. Two parallel walls will subtly draw together—not by angling inward, but by the distance between them thinning out, compressing the hot, wet air until it feels heavier against your chest.

In the distance, a mineral shelf gives a single, muted *tock* as it settles under the heat. A saturated mudbank slumps softly into the shallows. Then the thick, steaming quiet closes back in, and the dark city goes on slowly, silently rewriting itself in the warm water.
