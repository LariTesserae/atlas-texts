The whole place is wedged into a fresh rift in the basalt, no wider than a city square and sunk deep enough that the surface is only a pale, ragged seam of gray light far overhead. Because the rock is young—fractured recently by heat or blasting—the walls have not been rounded by time. They drop in sharp, glassy steps and angular facets, raw black stone still showing the conchoidal scars of its breaking.

Into this narrow, cathedral-sized shaft, an entire town’s worth of brick, cast iron, and riveted plate has been packed. Every ledge is claimed. Boiler-houses of soot-darkened red brick are mortared directly onto jagged stone shelves, their slate roofs pitched so steeply they nearly brush the undersides of the iron catwalks above them. Tier upon tier of narrow galleries, switchback stairs, and suspended pipe-bridges cross the void, near-touching, leaving only a thin, crooked well of air down the center. Where two structures crowd too closely, a corner of brickwork has been notched away to let a copper flue pass, or a steel stanchion is bolted straight through a masonry wall.

The heat down here is immense and dry, radiating from the deep rock itself and doubled by the banked furnaces and insulated tanks. It sits in the shaft like a heavy fluid. Only in narrow, unexpected pockets does the air turn damp: along the shaded undersides of the cold-return mains, where moisture beads on sweating iron and falls in slow, irregular drops. Some drops land in stone troughs carved to catch them; others strike the scorching jackets of the lower manifolds and vanish instantly with a sharp, brief *tst*.

Light is dim and amber, coming mostly from caged filament bulbs wired along the trusses and the dull cherry glow seeping through the draft-slits of the kiln doors. In the haze of heat-shimmer and fine mineral dust, the upper tiers vanish into soot-colored shadow, while the lowest floor—a paved sump of wet basalt flags and grated drains—catches only faint reflections of the lamps above.

Despite the density of engines, pulleys, and electrified conduits, the pit is mostly quiet. This is a slow, heavily muffled industry. Thick wrappings of tarred felt and plaster bind the steam pipes, swallowing their roar into a low, visceral hum you feel in your teeth before you hear it. Overhead, massive brass flywheels turn at the pace of a clock’s minute hand. Greased counterweights descend along vertical guide-rails without a rattle. Every few minutes, a hydraulic gate somewhere in the brick tiers shifts with a soft sigh of displaced air, or a row of louvers clicks open a fraction of an inch to vent a pocket of superheated gas. The whole settlement is in constant, deliberate motion—pumps stroking, condensers weeping, brine-tanks circulating—yet it moves with the hushed, patient cadence of a sleeping lung.

Nothing scuttles in the corners. There are no rats in the drains, no moths circling the caged bulbs, not even mites in the dust; the heat and the mineral fumes have left the shaft entirely empty of animal life. 

Instead, the only living things are sparse, stubborn plants that have claimed the seams where water and warmth meet. Along the dripping joints of the copper condensers, pale, waxy ferns with translucent ribs unfurl in tight clusters, drinking the distilled sweat of the machines. On the warm, damp mortar near the floor sumps, thin crusts of rust-colored moss grow in straight lines, following the mortar joints as if they were veins of ore. A few brittle, hollow-stemmed reeds have even rooted in the sulfur-silt of the overflow channels—tall, colorless stalks that rattle softly whenever a draft rises from the grates, though no wind ever blows down here from the world above.

A strange, slow geometry governs the place. The mineral-rich condensation leaves pale, coral-like crusts of salt and lime on the moving parts, building up along the spokes of the slow wheels and hanging in delicate, glassy stalactites from the undersides of the catwalks. In the dim light, it is hard to tell where the ironwork ends and the rock-crust begins, or whether the machines are refining the heat of the earth or simply keeping themselves alive in the dark—ticking, sweating, and turning in the quiet, airless oven.
