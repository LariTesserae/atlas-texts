You step down into a sunken, walled-in hollow no larger than a cathedral nave, and immediately the earth disappears beneath your feet. There is no soil here, no exposed stone—only a springy, waterlogged mattress of living and semi-petrified plant fiber. Layers of pale, braided rhizomes, bloated water-tubers, and dense felts of liverwort form a crust several feet thick over a hidden sump. Press your boot into it and warm, tea-colored water wells up around the sole, smelling of tannin, wet iron, and crushed celery.

Above and around you, the space is completely claimed. Thick, cartilaginous stems—ribbed and gray-green like heavy industrial hosing—rise from the root-mat in crowded ranks, leaving barely enough room to turn your shoulders. Higher up, they fan out into overlapping parasols of waxy, gutter-shaped leaves. Normal daylight pours down onto the top of this canopy and filters through in a warm, bright, greenish-gold haze, illuminating curtains of beaded air-roots and clusters of translucent bladder-pods that hang in every gap. The air is warm and saturated, heavy with a perpetual, slow-motion drizzle: moisture condenses on the broad leaves, gathers in their central cups, and spills over in thick, lazy drops.

Yet the hollow is not quiet, and it is not purely wild. Scattered through the packed greenery, half-swallowed by the vegetation, are the heavy bones of an old, electrified pumping station. 

Near the center of the basin sits a monumental cast-iron reciprocating pump bolted to a submerged concrete plinth, its flywheel taller than a man. Nearby, three cylindrical copper pressure vessels lean slightly into a cradle of strangler-figs, and a rusted steel gantry runs overhead, carrying thick, tar-sheathed power cables that vanish into the weeping embankment walls. The structures are few and spaced apart, relics of some long-forgotten drainage or extraction effort, but they are still running.

The sound is immense, though it moves at a glacial pace. A deep, sixty-cycle electrical hum vibrates through the waterlogged root-mat, so constant and heavy you feel it in your teeth. Overlaid on that drone is the slow, patient labor of the machines. The great flywheel turns once every forty seconds. As the piston draws back, the iron groans under immense hydraulic tension, the water in the root-mat shudders, and the hollow stems of the surrounding reeds begin to resonate. Then comes a deafening, wet *clank* as a brass relief valve trips, blasting a plume of warm, mineral-scented vapor into the canopy, followed by a long, cavernous hiss of draining condensers. 

Everything here has settled into a strange, long-established balance. The iron is pitted with a stable crust of rust and verdigris that the moss grips like bark; in places, rubber gaskets have rotted away entirely, and thick, woody vines have grown *through* the bolt-holes, holding the pressurized flanges together by sheer root-tension. The plants have adapted to the machinery in ways that feel almost deliberate. Pale, leafless creepers wrap tightly around the live high-voltage conduits, their tissues swollen and faintly warm, thriving on the electromagnetic hum. Along the steam pipes, hollow cane-stalks grow in rigid, parallel rows of varying lengths, naturally tuned to the bass frequency of the pump so that the entire grove drones like a pipe organ on every stroke.

Almost nothing with eyes or legs lives in this warm, loud sanctuary. There are no birds in the upper leaves, no insects skimming the puddles that gather in the rusted drip-trays. Only once in a long while, if you watch the slick, wet casing of a junction box, you might see a single, pale, blind slug inching across a warm brass plate, completely unbothered by the thunderous, patient sigh of the valves above it.
