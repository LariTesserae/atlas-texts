At the roof of the world, the stone has not yet learned to be round.

Continent-wide massifs of black basalt and fractured, pale-veined slate jut into the thin, biting air with edges still sharp enough to split a boot-sole. There are no rolling foothills here, no soft scree slopes; the uplift is young, and every ridge is a snapped bone, every valley a raw cleft dropping thousands of meters into mist-choked abysses. Above it all hangs a clear, unblinking daylight—neither harsh nor dim, just the steady, crisp illumination of high altitude, throwing razor-edged shadows across the jagged rock.

Despite the cold—a persistent, marrow-deep chill that hovers just above freezing—nothing is locked in ice. Instead, the world weeps. The high-altitude air is saturated, and wherever it meets the cold stone, it condenses into a perpetual, glistening film. Cliff faces sweat. Overhangs drip in steady, metronomic curtains. Water pools in every angular fracture and spills over the sharp lips of the plateaus in sheets that never freeze, kept liquid by a faint, continuous tremor in the bedrock and a slight, metallic salinity that makes the runoff shimmer like oil in the clear light.

Out of this cold, wet stone grows a lushness that seems almost defiant. There is no soil to speak of, yet the ledges, crevices, and sheltered shelves of the mountains are choked with thick, fleshy flora. Cushion-mosses the size of boulders, swollen like dark-emerald sponges, cling to near-vertical faces. Dense thickets of hollow-stemmed, waxy reeds and bruised-violet fronds drink the condensing mist directly through their skins, sweating the excess back out in heavy, clear beads. They do not sway in gusts—there are no sudden storms here, no shifting seasons—they simply stand, slick and turgid, locked in a slow, cold-blooded saturation.

And nothing moves among them. No beetle crawls along the wet stems; no bird cuts the thin air above the chasms; no fish turns in the pooling runoff. The silence of animal absence is total.

Yet the place is deafening.

Spanning the unimaginable distances between the jagged peaks—strung across chasms a mile wide and bolted into the unweathered cliff faces—are the cities. They are not crowded warrens of brick and timber, but vast, skeletal, gridded settlements of poured concrete, riveted iron, and high-tension steel. From a distance, the scale is staggering: miles of empty, terraced plazas, wide avenues paved in wet flagstones, and tiered viaducts that march straight across the continent. Up close, they are hollow. Great windowless factory-blocks rise forty stories into the cold air, their interiors mostly empty volume housing single, colossal turbines or banks of humming transformers. Streetlamps burn with a steady, pale-amber glow in the middle of the day along avenues where no foot has ever fallen.

Between one industrial node and the next lie kilometers of bare, weeping rock and silent moss. The density of the world is that of a plucked harp: immense spans of empty air and raw stone vibrating between widely spaced, rigidly organized presences.

The sound comes from everywhere at once, a physical weight pressing against the ribs. Because the world is caught in a frozen equilibrium, nothing ever winds down or spools up. Thousands of tons of condensed water roar down sheer, concrete-lined chutes, driving subterranean dynamos, only to be pumped straight back up to the high reservoirs through pressurized iron arteries that sweat frost and condensation. High-voltage lines strung on towering pylons snap and hiss continuously in the wet air, bleeding pale blue corona discharge into the mist. Beneath it all sits a bone-deep, unvarying bass thrum—the combined resonance of planetary-scale engines running at a locked, unchanging RPM.

The sound never swells, never fades, never changes pitch. It is an acoustic architecture as permanent as the basalt.

In the spaces where the urban grid meets the wild rock, the strangeness of the equilibrium shows itself most clearly. The lush, cold-adapted plants have grown right up to the edges of the electrified conduits and the warm, vibrating casings of the step-down transformers, and there they have stopped. They do not choke the vents or pry apart the concrete; the steady-state chemistry of the mineral runoff and the constant, low-level electromagnetic hum keep them in a rigid, geometric truce. Dark-teal ferns form neat, glistening borders along the drainage grates. Thick mats of moss insulate the base of every iron pylon, drinking the slightly warmed condensation that drips from the cooling fins, never growing an inch taller than the cold air allows.

If you stand on one of the vast, empty sky-bridges—concrete arches spanning a void so deep the bottom is lost in white mist—you can see the whole system at once. Jagged, un-eroded peaks marching to a distant, curved horizon. Lush, wet greenery clinging to impossible cliffs. Miles of empty, orderly streets and towering, isolated factories, all bathed in steady daylight and drowning in a continuous, mechanical roar. Nothing ages, nothing migrates, nothing weathers. The water falls, the turbines turn, the water is lifted again, and the cold mist settles onto the leaves, forever.
