The chamber is roughly the size of a small cathedral nave, carved or split out of raw basalt deep beneath the surface, though no ceiling is visible in the dark. The rock here is young—recently fractured by whatever pressure opened the pocket—with edges sharp enough to catch cloth or skin. Nothing has been smoothed by wind or rushing water. Every plane of stone is flat, angular, and hot to the touch, radiating a dry, steady fever from the earth below.

It is almost entirely dark. What light exists comes from the scattered machinery: a few dull amber indicator bulbs set into cast-iron junction boxes, and, behind thick, soot-rimmed sight glasses on two heavy transformer housings, the faint cherry-red glow of heating elements idling at low current. The light does not travel far. It catches the wet sheen on a nearby brass valve or the sharp ridge of a basalt shelf, leaving the wide spaces between them in near-total blackness.

The room is mostly empty floor—vast, uneven slabs of hot, bare stone crossed occasionally by thick, braided copper cables and insulated conduit bolted directly into the rock. At wide intervals stand the installations themselves: tall, riveted iron condenser towers, squat pressure vessels, and banks of porcelain-insulated coils. None of them are running at full capacity. They operate on a slow, patient, almost imperceptible cycle. Every twenty minutes or so, a solenoid clicks softly inside a sealed casing. A faint, low-frequency hum vibrates through the soles of one’s boots for a minute, barely audible, and then subsides back into silence.

In this heat, the air feels parched, heavy with the smell of baking stone, warm grease, and ozone—until you approach the condensers. The machinery is built to draw heat and moisture out of the surrounding rock, and its cooled copper manifolds sweat continuously. Water exists only here, in these small, localized pockets: a thin film of condensation beading along metal flanks, gathering at the lowest bends of pipes, and dripping slowly onto the stone below.

Wherever the water falls, the chamber erupts into dense, dark life. 

There is no sunlight here, so the plants have no use for green. They grow in thick, overlapping mounds of bruised violet, soot-black, and waxy, translucent bone-white. Broad, velvety leaves as heavy as leather fan out from tangled stems, crowding each other for space around the base of a sweating iron tower or spilling out of a shallow basalt basin beneath a dripping valve. They are fiercely lush—fat with stored water, slick with condensation—yet strictly confined. A meter away from the drip-line, the rock is bone-dry and barren again, leaving each clump of vegetation isolated like a dark island in a sea of hot, sharp stone.

The plants do not grow upward. They are thermotropic, angling their broad, dark surfaces toward the warm sides of the transformer housings or flattening themselves along the heated floor to drink in the ambient radiation. Their roots, pale and fibrous, wrap tightly around the copper conduits, prying into hairline fractures in the fresh basalt and staining the metal with pale verdigris. In the faint amber glow of a pressure gauge, you can see that the veins of the older leaves have taken on a dull, metallic sheen from the mineral runoff they absorb.

Nothing moves here except the plants and the slow drip of water. There are no insects, no bats, no scuttling things in the crevices, no webs strung between the porcelain insulators. When a thick leaf grows too old and detaches, it does not get broken down by beetles or worms; it simply lies on the hot stone beneath the canopy, slowly stewing in the damp heat until it collapses into a dark, mineral-rich paste that the roots below quietly reclaim.

The silence is nearly absolute. If you stand still in the dark, the only sounds are the soft, irregular *tock* of a heavy drop of water hitting a waxy leaf, the faintest metallic tick as an iron pipe expands a fraction of a millimeter, and the slow, heavy pulse of your own blood in the stifling air.
