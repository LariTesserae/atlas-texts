The whole town sits inside a single, sharp-rimmed cleft in the lowlands, no more than two hundred paces from lip to lip. From the marsh-edge above, where pale sedge gives way to raw, newly fractured slate, you can take in the entire settlement at a glance. It looks less like a village built on rock than a quarry that has been meticulously refilled—every cubic yard of the sunken basin packed tight with stacked, flat-roofed dwellings, retaining walls, and stepped, shoulder-wide alleys.

The rock itself is young and unforgiving. Whatever uplift or collapse opened this hollow happened recently enough in the earth’s memory that nothing has been worn round. The perimeter cliffs are sheer, jagged steps of dark gray slate and pale chert, their faces still bright along the cleavage planes. There are no gentle slopes. Where the stone breaks, it breaks into blades and wedges, and the builders have used those blades just as they fell, stacking them dry without mortar. Corners are pinned with split-slate pegs or bound at the lintels with rawhide strips and scarce, salt-bleached driftwood. Copper chisels and flint-tipped mauls have left crisp, pale-gray tool marks along the thresholds, but nothing here is smoothed by time.

The air in the basin is cool and heavy with moisture, held under an even, milky daylight. Because the hollow sits only a little above the surrounding marsh, groundwater presses constantly against the fractured walls. Cold, clear springs weep from the horizontal seams of the slate, not in roaring cascades, but in a thousand quiet, persistent trickles. Every wall is damp to the touch; every shaded corner beads with condensation.

With so little room inside the boundary, the town has grown inward and upward until no open ground remains. There are no plazas, no yards, no wasted margins between houses. One household’s slate-shingled roof is the next household’s walkway, bordered by low stone curbs to keep feet from slipping into the drainage channels. Doorways open directly onto narrow staircases cut into the rock or wedged between the flanks of neighboring buildings. Three and four stories deep, the structures lean against one another for mutual bracing, their gaps stuffed with wet peat and woven marsh-reed to keep out the draft.

What makes the town’s density even stranger is that half of its built volume belongs not to living quarters, but to water. The entire settlement is an interlocking hydraulic engine, constructed with hand-tools and patience. Along the upper tiers, shallow stone gutters catch the weeping springwater and lead it into stepped rows of slate settling-pans. From there, it passes through wicker baskets packed with crushed charcoal and coarse sand, drips down into covered, subterranean cisterns, and finally escapes through narrow, stone-lined runnels beneath the lowest floorboards toward the marsh.

An unusual stillness governs this active machinery. For a place so crowded, it is remarkably quiet. The wet stone and moss-chinked walls drink up sound, and there are no wide spaces for voices to echo. Water does not crash here; it sheets thinly over flat slate, producing only a soft, continuous hiss. Now and then, a wooden tipping-trough—counterweighted with a river-cobble lashed in rawhide—fills to capacity, nods forward with a dull *clack*, empties its measure into a lower filter-bed, and swings back into place. Between those slow, irregular beats, you hear only the scrape of a bone scraper against a pan or the muted patter of water dripping from an eave.

The water carries something strange up from the deep fractures—a cold-precipitating silica or pale mineral salt that skins everything it touches. Over seasons, the roofs, sluice-gates, and lower walls acquire a thin, translucent glaze, like frozen tallow or frosted glass. Where the flow is slowest, in the corners of the settling-pans, the mineral grows in delicate, brittle needles that crunch underfoot like thin ice, even though the air is well above freezing. The townspeople harvest this crust with copper-edged paddles, scraping it into reed hampers before it chokes their channels.

Life beyond the human presence is sparse. Vegetation finds almost no purchase on the sharp, slick stone; only tufts of dark wire-grass cling to the upper rim, while liverworts and pale, crustose lichens map the dampest mortarless joints. Animals are rarer still. Occasionally a slate-colored wader drops from the marsh to pick through the lowest runnels, and if you lift the wooden lid of a dark cistern, you might catch the flicker of tiny, blind, translucent shrimp turning in the cold water.

Even the inhabitants seem shaped by the tight, wet geometry of the basin. They move along the stepped roofs in soft-soled rawhide, keeping their voices low, stepping aside at the narrow landings with practiced economy. Around them, the rock sweats, the troughs fill and tip, and the pale mineral skin thickens by a fraction of a millimeter each day—a small, crowded world held in cool, damp motion, entirely contained within its jagged ring of stone.
