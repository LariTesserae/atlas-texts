The place is a narrow, high-altitude notch—no wider than a church nave, though its walls drop and climb into blackness—carved out of a mountain ridge by a recent fault-slip. It sits high enough that the air is thin and bitingly cool, just above freezing in the gusts, yet the surrounding slopes are steep and wet enough to hold a choking, hyper-dense cloud forest. At night, under a thick lid of storm cloud, there is almost no light at all. You know the shape of the cleft not by seeing it, but by the way the wind compresses as it forces its way through, and by the dense, vibrating obstacle course of rock, wet wood, and running iron packed into every cubic meter.

The stone itself is young and unforgiving. A landslide or quake sheared the face not long ago, leaving raw, unweathered basalt and fractured slate. Nothing has had time to round off. The ledges are razor-edged, the talus slopes made of sharp, interlocking shards that shift and clatter underfoot. There are no streams here, no standing pools—the pitch is too steep and the rock too freshly cracked—yet everything is damp. Cold mist drives horizontally through the gap, condensing on the undersides of leaves, beading along the sharp ridges of stone, and trickling down in thin, intermittent films that never quite become rain.

Out of that dampness and sharp rubble, the vegetation grows in a frantic, layered tangle. It is too high for tall timber, so the forest here is stunted, gnarled, and impossibly thick: dwarf trees with twisted, mossy trunks no thicker than a thigh, wedged into every crack; dense stands of mountain bamboo; coarse, leathery ferns and bromeliads clinging to vertical stone; and deep, sodden cushions of moss and lichen that bridge the gaps between boulders. Because the cleft is small and the walls are steep, the plants grow almost on top of one another, crowns interlocking, roots wrapping around bare rock like cables. In the dark, they form a thrashing, claustrophobic wall of wet leaves and springy stems.

Nothing has been built here—there are no walls, no roofs, no gravel pads, no roads leading in or out—yet the cleft is full of heavy industry. Bare, unhoused machinery has been winched or slung in and bolted straight onto the fresh rock with steel anchors and turnbuckles. Skid-mounted diesel-electric generators sit tilted on steel frames wedged between boulders, their exhaust stacks venting hot, oily fumes straight into the canopy. Thick, armored power cables and hydraulic hoses snake through the moss like black vines, strapped to tree trunks and rock ribs with nylon ties and rusted U-bolts. High-pressure manifold skids, cooling fans, automated valves, and transformer boxes crouch in the undergrowth, completely exposed to the mist, with only their heavy-gauge steel casings to keep the water out.

In the darkness, the only light comes from the machines themselves: a scattering of tiny, waterproof amber and red status LEDs blinking on control panels, the faint orange glow of a hot exhaust manifold glimpsed through thrashing fern fronds, and, now and then, a sharp blue-white crack of an electrical arc jumping a damp insulator. The rest is black-on-black silhouette—jagged rock against storm sky, whipping branches against rock.

The whole notch is in violent, continuous motion. A nocturnal gale funnels through the gap, hitting the sharp rock ribs and breaking into chaotic eddies. The bamboo clacks together like dry bones; the gnarled trees groan and whip their wet crowns; loose slate skitters down the chutes in sudden, rattling avalanches. The machines fight the weather and the load—governors surging as pumps strain against resistance, cooling fans whining at high RPM, steel cables humming taut in the wind, sheet-metal housings vibrating with a low, relentless rattle. The combined noise is a constant, physical pressure: the roar of wind, the hiss of millions of wet leaves, the clatter of falling stone, and the flat, mechanical drone of engines running blind in the dark.

Nothing lives here that can walk or fly. There are no birds roosting in the thrashing branches, no insects drawn to the tiny red indicator lights, no rodents threading the talus. The air is too cold, the wind too violent, and the ground vibrates too hard with sixty-hertz hum and diesel shudder. It is strictly a contest between three things: the raw, newly broken mountain trying to slide, the dense, wet forest trying to swallow everything in its path, and the bolted-down iron hammering away through the night, slowly shaking itself loose against the stone.
