It is a sunken, enclosed place, no wider than a large cathedral and roofed over by a low, ribbed vault of fused roots—yet the first thing you feel is how much empty air it holds. Cool, damp air, smelling of wet cellulose and cold iron, hanging still in a near-total darkness.

If you stand on one of the lower walkways, there is almost nothing within arm’s reach, and nothing for dozens of paces in any direction except the black, chill volume of the space itself. A sparse skeleton of a town hangs suspended in this hollow, its tiers separated by wide, vertiginous drops. There is no bedrock beneath it, no soil anywhere. The floor of the basin, the walls that curve up to the ceiling, the streets, and the buildings themselves are all a single, continuous, lightless plant.

It is pale, waxy vegetation, the color of peeled chestnuts and bruised tallow, entirely devoid of chlorophyll. Where it forms the ground, it is dense and fibrous, ridged like the underside of a bracket fungus. Where it rises into architecture, it narrows into slender, widely spaced pillars, catwalks of braided cambium, and hollow, gall-like tenements clustered along the perimeter walls. Almost nothing fills the center of the void; a solitary boiler-house hangs there on three taut, glistening tendons, a hundred feet of empty dark below it and fifty above.

Everything here is unfinished, with the raw, unweathered sheen of something still being extruded. Nothing has been worn down by wind or footstep. Along the base of the walls, sharp, translucent mineral concretions are only just beginning to crystallize out of the plant’s sweat, forming jagged, embryonic ledges of salt and silicates. The streets—narrow, elevated strips of bark-plate—end abruptly in soft, wet meristematic tips that inch outward by millimeters an hour, knitting themselves toward opposite ledges they have not yet reached.

And out of the plant, an industrial town is growing.

It is not overgrown with vines; rather, the machinery is *budding* from the woody nodes. Riveted copper flanges emerge seamlessly from pale bark, still soft at the seams. Overhead gantry cranes of bone-white timber swing slowly on greased wooden bearings, lowering nothing into empty air. Along the catwalks, cast-iron lampposts stand at regular intervals, though their glass housings are unformed, replaced by wet, translucent membranes that faintly glow a cold, bruised violet when a surge of current passes through the vascular cables beneath the floorboards. You can hear the electricity more as a pressure in your teeth than a sound—a sub-audible thrumming, punctuated now and then by the sharp, tiny *click* of a relay switching somewhere across the dark.

Otherwise, the place is hushed. There is no wind. The only regular sound is the *plink* of condensation falling from the upper trusses into shallow, still cisterns below—though not all of it falls straight. In certain pockets of the air, droplets of water detach from a pipe, drift sideways at a gentle angle toward a spinning brass flywheel, and vanish into its rim without wetting it. Elsewhere, a vertical string of beads hangs motionless in mid-air, trembling slightly as if threaded on an invisible wire.

In the dim, intermittent flicker of those violet arcs and the occasional cold spark from an overloaded root-transformer, you begin to see how crowded the sparse surfaces actually are.

The void is empty, but every solid inch of the suspended town is teeming. Blind, translucent amphibians with suction-cupped toes cling to the underside of the iron-bark girders, completely silent. Long, segmented creatures the color of wet chalk crawl through the exposed gear-trains of the gantry cranes, secreting a thin, clear mucus that keeps the wooden teeth turning without a squeak. Velvet-winged moths—thousands of them, pale as ash—lift off the tenement walls in noiseless clouds whenever a dynamo cycles up, drifting across the dark gaps between buildings like flakes of soot. And behind them come the predators: long-limbed, eyeless things with joints that bend the wrong way, stalking along the catwalks at a brisk, clicking trot that barely registers over the drip of water.

The longer you watch, the less the boundary between the town, the plant, and the animals holds.

A moth lands on a brass pressure-valve; its wings stiffen, flatten, and darken into sheet-metal, becoming a lever that trips a piston. At the top of its stroke, the iron piston-rod sweats a bead of warm sap and curls into a tight, pale fern-frond before retracting back into its cylinder as steel. Down one of the narrow alleys between two gall-tenements, a staircase climbs three flights of wet timber, turns a corner, and deposits you on the underside of the exact same landing you just left—except here, the water in the gutter runs upward, and the tenements are no longer hollow wood, but flickering, half-rendered brick, translucent enough to see the dark, empty air behind them.

Nothing here seems to notice the contradictions. The town keeps assembling itself in the cool, damp dark. Pistons pump condensation into pipes that end in nothingness. Tendrils of pale root reach out across the gulf, crystallizing into raw stone at their tips, while the silent swarms feed and hunt along the railings, waiting for the next street to finish growing.
