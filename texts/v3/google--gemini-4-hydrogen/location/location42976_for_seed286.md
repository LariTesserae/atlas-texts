Miles above the lower continents, held within a rimless, plateau-scale depression of the planet’s crust, lies an ocean that does not know it is a mountain. The water is everywhere—deep, cool, and astonishingly clear—stretching from horizon to horizon beneath a steady, unclouded daylight that penetrates effortlessly into the submerged vastness. There is no surface chop to break the light into dancing ribbons; the water column hangs in a state of crisp, uniform clarity, the exact same cool temperature at ten meters as at five hundred. No thermoclines ripple the view. No seasons turn the light amber or dim. It is a permanent, bright, underwater noon.

Down here, there is no stone. Even the ancient basin itself has long since been swallowed and replaced by a single, continuous continent of living plant tissue. The floor of this high sea is a dense, fused mat of pale slate-green and iron-stained rhizome, meters thick, weathered over eons by steady, unvarying currents into broad, smooth terraces and gentle, sweeping valleys. Nothing jagged remains. Every contour is settled, rounded, and ancient—a landscape eroded into final balance, where the substrate itself is alive, breathing, and slowly, imperceptibly mineralizing from beneath.

Yet for all this total, unbroken carpet of vegetation, the open water above it is strikingly empty. You can drift for miles through the cool, bright blue-green without encountering a single vertical obstacle, only the vast, smooth floor rolling away below and the empty, sunlit water column stretching above.

Then, far ahead, a solitary column rises—and then another, three miles to the east, and another barely visible in the distance to the north.

They look, at first glance, like submerged industrial refineries. Vast, fluted towers of dark, fibrous bark and calcified silica rise hundreds of feet toward the bright surface, trailing thick, pitch-black cables that sag in long catenaries across the empty miles of water to link tower to tower. Huge, rhythmic plumes of shimmering gas vent continuously from crown-like apertures at their peaks.

But there is not a single weld, rivet, or laid brick anywhere in this world. No hand ever shaped these towers; no mind ever drew their blueprints. They are entirely, seamlessly grown.

Up close, the scale and the sound hit at once. Underwater, sound travels fast and heavy, and this place is loud—a relentless, chest-vibrating industrial roar that fills every cubic meter of the clear water. Each solitary plant-tower is a living hydraulic engine. Its thick-walled trunk is hollow and lined with immense, muscular bladders of vascular tissue that contract in an unvarying, piston-like rhythm: *thump-hiss, thump-hiss, thump-hiss*. With every stroke, thousands of tons of cool water are drawn in through vast, pleated root-intakes along the floor and forced upward through internal chambers, spinning massive, seamless impellers of dense heartwood and biomineralized chitin. 

You can hear the impellers through the trunk walls—a deep, steady, mechanical whirr, lubricated by pale mucilage extruded from glands along the wooden bearings. Outside, along the lower buttresses, vast banks of translucent, leaf-like membranes flutter in tight formation, acting as natural electrolytic cells. They strip dissolved minerals and split water at an industrial scale, feeding a constant, high-voltage direct current into the thick, rubbery latex-sheathed kelp cables that run along the floor. The cables hum—a heavy, unbroken sixty-cycle thrum that vibrates in your teeth and sends faint, shimmering halos of micro-bubbles rising from their insulated hides.

Where the cables meet the living floor, swollen, tuberous nodules glow with a dull, internal heatless chemistry—biological smelters that refine dissolved iron, copper, and silica from the water, weaving the metals directly into the fibrous lattice of the ground to reinforce the substrate against the constant vibration of the engines.

This strange, mechanized ecology teems with life, though it is a life entirely adapted to the hum and the current. Great, ribbon-like schools of silver-plated, articulated eels—abundant and fearless—weave through the wide, empty corridors of water between the towers, using organs of biological magnetite to navigate along the steady electromagnetic fields radiating from the power-vines. Along the spinning silica-and-wood joints of the turbine trunks, thousands of pale, armored crustaceans swarm like living ball-bearings, clicking and scraping as they graze on the extruded lubricating sap, keeping the natural machinery from ever fouling.

Out in the sparse mid-water, bulbous, translucent grazers drift like slow dirigibles. They feed on the fine, metallic moss that crusts the floor nodules, accumulating static charge in their gelatinous hides until they brush against a grounded root-tendril, discharging a brief, silent corona of blue light into the cool water before drifting on.

What is most uncanny about this place is not the fusion of forest and factory, nor the deafening underwater roar, nor the immense, high-altitude sea itself—it is the absolute stillness of its time. Nothing here is growing larger, and nothing is decaying. The oxygen vented by the roaring hydraulic crowns is consumed at the exact rate it is produced by the abundant fauna and the floor-oxidizers. The minerals shed as fine, gleaming silt by the spinning wooden turbines settle onto the weathered terraces and are reabsorbed into the rhizome mat in an exact, closed loop. 

There are no storms, no droughts, no power surges, no breakdowns. The temperature never shifts by a tenth of a degree; the light never dims; the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of the plant-engines never misses a beat or changes its tempo. This vast, loud, electrified garden has reached its final operating speed, locked in a permanent, humming equilibrium that will run, unchanged and untended, forever.
