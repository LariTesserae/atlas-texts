The plateau sits so high in the sky that the air feels thin enough to scrape the lungs, yet the heat rising off the ground is relentless, dry as an open kiln. Under a sharp, unblinking white-gold sun, the landscape stretches out in a wide, unbroken basin ringed by distant, saw-toothed ridges—an open expanse that looks empty only from afar.

Up close, there is almost no flat ground to spare. Ages of wind, thermal expansion, and slow mineral weeping have carved the plateau into a dense, settled labyrinth of weathered basalt columns, blistered rhyolite mounds, and knife-edged travertine fins. The rock is packed tight—ridges shoulder against shattered scree-slopes, honeycombed boulders choke the shallow draws, and deep, narrow fissures split the stone every few paces. Every surface has the worn, rounded corners of something that has baked and cooled in the same place for ten thousand years.

Despite the searing temperature, the plateau is not wholly dry. The heat is driven both by the fierce, high-altitude sun and the warm crust beneath, while moisture arrives in brief, localized ways. Before dawn, cold air spills over the outer ridges and meets the radiating stone, leaving a thin, clinging dampness in the leeward cracks. Subterranean steam-seams exhale into shaded pockets, feeding tepid, mineral-heavy puddles tucked beneath overhanging rock shelves. These wet seams are stained dark against the pale, sun-bleached stone, rimmed with crusts of white salt, ochre iron, and slick, olive-green mats of heat-loving algae.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Only a few things take root here: leathery, slate-colored lichens hugging the damp undersides of overhangs; low, woody scrub with leaves like waxed needles; and pale, swollen succulents wedged so tightly into the rock-cracks they look like blisters on the stone itself.

What fills the gaps between the rocks is animal life—crowded, quiet, and ceaselessly active. Because the air is thin and the sun is punishing, nothing wastes breath on noise. The plateau is nearly silent, save for the dry rustle of scales, the soft *tick* of claws on basalt, and the faint, papery *shhh* of wings catching a thermal. Every shaded fissure is packed with flat-bodied, slate-hued lizards stacked three deep to escape the noon glare, alongside clusters of armored, copper-shelled beetles that gather along the damp mineral seams to drink condensation. Overhead, broad-winged gliders with translucent, heat-venting membranes ride the invisible columns of rising air, banking without a single wingbeat as they scan the crevices below. Along the warm, steaming puddles, long-legged, pale-feathered stalkers step with agonizing slowness, spearing blind, pallid worms and fissure-crabs from the mud without making a ripple. Even the rock blisters aren't always stone—some are the hardened, porous hives of colonial burrowing insects, faintly warm to the touch and breathing slowly through pinhole vents.

Scattered across this crowded, baking terrain are the sparse, weathered works of an automated industry. They sit far apart—a lone structure here, a pair of pylons on a distant ridge—yet they are unmistakably modern and mechanized. Squat, riveted cylinders of blackened iron and white ceramic are bolted directly over the strongest steam-fissures, silently converting ground-heat into current. Tall, skeletal condenser-towers, strung with fine copper mesh, stand on the high points of the basalt fins to comb moisture from the brief morning mists, funneling the trickle down insulated pipes into buried cisterns. 

Thin, taut power lines and narrow metal conduits link these scattered stations, disappearing over the horizon toward some unseen, distant grid. There are no roads, no towns, and no people in sight—only the occasional small, windowless relay shed with a dusty solar-vent spinning lazily on its roof. The machines run with an eerie, near-silent efficiency: a faint, sub-audible hum from a transformer box, the rare *clack* of a pressure valve releasing a puff of dry steam, the slow, rhythmic click of a metering gauge.

By mid-afternoon, the heat-haze shimmers along the tops of the rock fins, blurring the distant condenser-towers against the sky. The lizards press deeper into the wet cracks, the gliders spiral higher into the thin, bright air, and the scattered engines keep pulling power and water from the hot, packed stone in the quiet.
