The sky is a pale, blindingly bright blue, thin and cold, stretched taut over a plateau so high and vast that the horizon curves away without a single mountain to interrupt it. There is no soil here, no exposed bedrock, no mud, and no water. The ground beneath—stretching for thousands of miles in every direction—is entirely plant.

It is an ancient, desiccated continent of compressed lignin, sun-bleached root-plates, and petrified-yet-living vascular mats. Eons of relentless, dry wind have planed the surface into wide, smooth steppes and carved deep, fluted canyons into the strata of packed bark and hollow, silicified reeds. Everything is bone-dry and pale: ivory, ash-gray, and faded straw, reflecting the high-altitude glare until the landscape seems to hum with white light. 

For miles at a time, the surface is strikingly empty. Vast, open expanses of grooved, woody pavement lie bare between solitary vertical presences. Yet the emptiness is neither quiet nor still.

The sound is immediate and deafening. Because the substrate itself is a miles-deep lattice of dry, hollow tubes and taut, papery membranes, the high-altitude gales do not merely blow across the plateau—they *play* it. The wind howls through submerged fissures in a continuous, chest-vibrating drone, overlaid by the dry, deafening clatter of loose husks and the high, whistling shriek of air forced through thousands of natural flutes. 

And beneath the wind is the rhythm of the engines.

Scattered miles apart across the sparse steppe, rising out of the pale floor like solitary, leafless towers, are the great dynamo-spires and pressure-trees. Nothing here was built; there are no walls, no roads, no made artifacts. Instead, the flora itself has evolved into an exposed, continent-scale industrial system. Deprived of water, the deep root-networks draw up mineral salts, heavy metals, and volatile hydrocarbons from the far-below crust, refining them through dry, anaerobic fermentation within pressurized subterranean chambers.

Every few minutes, a spire a mile away shudders. A massive, thick-walled valve of dense ironwood slams open with a concussive *crack* that echoes across the flatlands, venting a roaring column of superheated turpentine-vapor and methane. Above it, a crown of dry, copper-veined fronds—arranged in rigid, overlapping turbines—catches the violent gale, spinning with a grinding, wooden roar. The friction of dry fiber against dry, metal-dusted air builds immense static charges along the spire’s bristling, needle-like bark. Blue-white arcs of electricity snap continuously between the high branches, igniting the vented gas in rhythmic, thunderous detonations that shake the hollow ground underfoot.

The air is never clear, even without clouds. It is turbulent with dry, glittering storms: swirling fronts of fine silica dust, metallic-coated spores, and golden, highly flammable resin-powder churned up by the spinning crowns and blown into towering, fast-moving eddies. 

Despite the scarcity of vertical cover and the complete absence of liquid water, animal life is abundant—mostly hidden from the blinding glare until the pressure-cycles shift. The hollow, vibrating interior of the plant-continent teems with dry-jointed, chitinous creatures the color of tarnished brass and bleached bone. They do not drink; they derive their moisture from the breakdown of dense, oily resin-cakes extruded by the roots, and they cluster in the warm, rhythmic chambers beneath the venting spires. When a turbine-tree finishes a combustion cycle, swarms of winged, hard-shelled scavengers erupt from the surface fissures, riding the violent thermal updrafts to snatch stunned insects out of the static-charged air before diving back into the shaded canyons below.

Larger, multi-legged grazers—flat-bodied and plated in thick, insulating keratin—patrol the wide, empty miles between spires. They move in fast, erratic bursts across the pale bark-steppe, using long, chisel-like mandibles to bore into the pressurized vascular ridges, drinking the thick, cold, non-aqueous sap before the wound seals itself with a hiss of drying resin.

At this scale, the plateau generates its own dry, violent weather. Across the distant, blinding horizon, dark curtains of electrically charged pollen-dust sweep across the flatlands, lit from within by webs of dry lightning. The wind never drops, the ground never stops thrumming, and the scattered, untouched spires grind and spark without pause—an ancient, cool, deafening factory made entirely of dry wood, wind, and fire.
