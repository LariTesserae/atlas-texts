The whole place fits inside a shallow, oval depression no more than three hundred paces across—a sunken basin whose rim is formed by an ancient, tightly braided wall of petrified root-stock, pale grey lichen, and weathered mortared stone. Beyond that boundary, the world drops away or disappears into mist; inside it, the air is cool, still, and lightly damp, holding the even, unremarkable daylight of an overcast late morning.

There is no bare soil here. The ground is entirely a living, multi-layered mat of vegetation that has settled into its own permanent contours over centuries. Thick, springy cushions of blue-green sphagnum and tightly woven sedge-roots form rolling terraces, interrupted here and there by shallow, dark pockets of standing water where condensation has pooled in the hollows. Beneath the moss, the substrate is dense and woody—a gnarled, subterranean lattice of creeping ash-roots and compressed peat that gives slightly underfoot, absorbing every footstep into a dull, wet silence.

Across this wide, quiet expanse of living turf, an unmistakable urban geometry has been laid down—not crowded, but meticulously organized and widely spaced. Elevated walkways of oiled cedar and riveted iron trestles run in straight, intersecting avenues above the damp moss, linking four or five widely separated industrial complexes. Between the buildings lie broad, empty stretches of undisturbed moss-terrace and sunken drainage channels, giving the settlement the air of a city whose blocks were pulled apart to let the ground breathe.

Yet the infrastructure is dense and layered. Beneath the planked avenues, clamped to the iron pilings, run neat, bundled arteries of insulated copper cable, tarnished brass condensation-pipes, and narrow pneumatic tubes. Numbered enamel plates mark the junctions. Streetlamps on cast-iron stanchions stand at regular intervals along the empty boardwalks, their glass globes beaded with moisture. Along the basin’s western rim, multi-story dwellings and bunkhouses are built directly into the living root-wall—tiered structures of brick, slate, and pleached willow-trunks whose living branches have been trained over decades to form load-bearing arches and window frames.

Everything here is mechanized, electrified, and almost entirely silent.

At the center of the basin stands the main press-house, a tall, narrow building of peat-brick and corrugated iron wrapped in climbing ivy. Inside and out, slow-moving machinery is in constant, unhurried motion. Low-voltage electric motors, housed in thick peat-lined boxes that muffle their hum to a barely perceptible vibration, drive wide leather belts and heavy wooden gear-trains. Overhead, a single-track cable-tram glides silently along a greased steel wire, carrying slatted crates of pale, fibrous plant-matter toward the drying-kilns on the northern terrace.

The industry of the basin is bound up with the peculiar biology of its flora. Growing along the flanks of the workshops and across the open terraces are broad, waxy-leafed *bladder-sedges*—unusual, slow-pulsing plants that draw moisture from the cool morning air. By dawn, their translucent, melon-sized sacs are swollen tight with clear condensation; as the daylight warms the basin, automated brass needles pierce the membranes or mechanical rollers gently compress them, draining the water into slate-lined cisterns and electrolytic vats. Elsewhere, thick, fibrous *nerve-vines* are strung between iron pylons alongside the copper lines; when a low electric current is passed through them, the vines stiffen rigid as steel cables, serving as living, self-tensioning guy-wires for the elevated walkways and crane-arms.

Despite the constant activity—sap rising in the pleached walls, broad leaves pivoting slowly to follow the diffuse light, pistons gliding in oil-baths, water dripping rhythmically from copper gutters into collection troughs—the basin is profoundly quiet. Animal life is rare. No birds nest in the root-cliffs, and no insects buzz over the dark water-pockets; at most, a pale, thumb-sized slug can be seen grazing the damp mortar of a retaining wall, or a solitary grey moth resting on the warm housing of a transformer.

The only sounds are small and localized: the soft *tink* of a drop of condensation hitting a metal pan, the faint, muffled *shhh* of a slow-turning belt, the occasional dry creak of a wooden truss adjusting to the humidity. Everything—the weathered, settled forms of the root-walls, the spaced-out brick factories, the silent electric looms and dripping vats—operates at the same cool, deliberate pace, locked in a long-established balance between machine and leaf.
