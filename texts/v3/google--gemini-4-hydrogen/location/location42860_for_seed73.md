At this altitude, the sky should be thin and bitter. Instead, the air presses against the skin like a damp wool blanket fresh from a boiler.

You are miles above the cloud-deck, standing on the broad, stepped shelf of a mountain basin so high that the world beyond its rim simply drops away—a sheer plunge of weathered cliff-faces vanishing into a flat, blindingly white sea of cloud-tops far below. The daylight here is crisp and direct, unfiltered by thick lower air, casting sharp, dark-edged shadows across the stone. Yet the basin is stifling. A relentless, wet heat rises from the bedrock itself, baking the air trapped within the amphitheater of peaks until it shimmers.

The rock is old, dark basalt and pale, iron-stained sinter, carved over millennia into smooth, rounded flutes and terraced ledges. Nothing here is jagged anymore; the stone has settled into a patient, heavy equilibrium with the water that never stops moving through it. And water is everywhere—not in violent, crashing torrents, for the slope is too stepped and the air too still for that, but in a million slow, weeping seams. The cliff-walls sweat continuously. Warm, mineral-rich water seeps from hairline fractures, sheets down over mossy overhangs, and collects in wide, shallow pans of travertine that steam gently in the bright sun. Along the flatter shelves, the ground gives way to a spongy, ankle-deep marsh of warm mud and tangled roots, where the air hangs thick with the smell of wet mineral salt, crushed fern, and overripe sap.

Scattered across this high valley—spaced so far apart you might walk for an hour between them—are the quiet, half-swallowed remains of whatever built the heat. 

They do not look like machines. Near the center of a steaming marsh-shelf, a cylinder of matte-gray material no taller than a person juts from the mud at a slight tilt, its surface pitted with ancient lichen and crusted with white mineral scale. Further up the ridge, a seamless, dark-glass ring lies half-buried in a bank of ferns, looking like a fossilized rib. There are no humming turbines, no vents belching smoke, no blinking lights. Whatever physics these artifacts operate by is beyond anything modern engineering could map; they seem less like engines and more like *anchors* or *exhaust-tips* for something unimaginably vast happening deep beneath the mountain—a process so slow it might take a thousand years to complete a single cycle.

Their only obvious effect is the fever they give the stone, and the way the local rules bend softly around them. Within a dozen paces of the gray cylinder, the falling condensation loses its urgency. Fat, heavy droplets of water roll off the waxy leaves and drift sideways, or hang trembling in the hot air like glass beads before slowly drifting toward the artifact's surface, where they flatten and vanish without a hiss. Even the light seems slightly thicker near the buried ring, refracting the bright noon sun into faint, oily halos along the edges of wet leaves.

Life has packed itself into every available inch of this high-altitude hothouse. Because the UV light is fierce at this elevation and the heat is constant, the vegetation is thick-skinned, waxy, and aggressively layered. Towering tree-ferns with dark, almost black-green fronds form a dense, interlocking canopy over the terraced shelves, shading a chaotic understory of broad-leafed succulents, rubbery vines, and hanging curtains of coppery moss. The plants drink directly from the condensing air; their leaves are cupped and channeled to catch the constant drip, funneling warm water down their trunks into swollen, above-ground root-mats. Near the scattered artifacts, the flora grows in subtle, unsettling patterns—vines spiraling upward in tight, mathematically precise helices around invisible field-lines, or patches of moss arranging themselves in concentric, pulsing rings of pale green and rust.

For a place whose geology and machinery move at a glacial pace, the valley is remarkably loud. 

There is no wind up here to speak of, yet the air is filled with a dense, layered roar of sound that bounces cleanly off the sheer stone walls of the basin. Every broad leaf acts like a drumhead for the heavy, continuous *pat-pat-tock* of condensation falling from the upper canopy, punctuated by the sharp *hiss* of droplets hitting sun-baked stone ledges. Beneath that percussion lies the chorus of the fauna—and the fauna is everywhere, thriving in the warm, wet thickness. Thousands of armored, iridescent beetles click and rasp in the bark of the tree-ferns, creating a metallic, rhythmic static that rises and falls in slow waves. From the warm pools and marsh-edges, fat, translucent-skinned amphibians inflate pale throat-sacs and let out deep, resonant *booms* that vibrate in your chest, each call echoing across the stone amphitheater until it overlaps with the next. High in the waxy canopy, flocks of long-legged, featherless gliders shriek and chatter as they hop between the fronds, shaking loose sudden, warm showers of trapped water onto the steaming moss below.

Nothing here hurries. The heat is constant, the water cycles patiently from rock to air to leaf and back to rock, and the mineral crusts thicken by a fraction of a millimeter each decade. You can stand on a warm, weeping ledge, listening to the deafening, steady hum of the insects and the slow drip of the cliffs, looking out past the lush, impossible jungle to the edge of the world—where the mountain drops away into empty sky, and the pale cloud-sea stretches unbroken to the horizon.
