The place is a sunken hollow—a wide, low-ceilinged pocket tucked beneath an overhanging shelf of dark, iron-rich sandstone, no larger than a small nave. To reach it is to descend a short, settled slope of angular scree that has long since stopped shifting; the stones lock together underfoot like an old, dry pavement, worn smooth on their upper faces by centuries of dust.

Overhead, the rock arches low and heavy, blocking out almost the entire sky. Only a narrow, ragged seam of darkness remains visible along the far rim, letting in the barest suggestion of slate-grey light—just enough to separate the silhouettes of boulders from the deeper black of the recessed walls. It is a permanent, heavy dusk inside, thick and motionless.

Yet the air is strikingly warm. The surrounding stone holds heat with the stubbornness of an unlit kiln, radiating a steady, dry warmth into the hollow. There is no draft to stir it, no cool current rising from below. The air tastes faintly of baked clay, powdered silica, and old resin, so dry it pulls moisture straight from the skin.

Everywhere, the ground shows the long, patient work of time that has finally come to rest. The floor of the bowl is a flat pan of pale, flour-fine silt, broken into wide, curled polygons that have baked as hard as unglazed earthenware. Near the walls, where the softer mineral veins weathered out millennia ago, the sandstone is pitted with shallow, dusty alcoves and rounded ribs that feel like dry bone. Nothing here is sharp except the scattered cobbles of dark chert and flinty stone that lie half-buried in the silt—raw, untouched nodules resting exactly where they fell from the overhang ages ago.

Life exists here, but it is sparse, patient, and widely spaced, leaving broad expanses of bare mineral floor between one presence and the next. Rooted deep in the hairline fissures of the rock, a few gnarled, low shrubs lean outward—woody, leafless, with bark frayed into grey fibers. They survive on whatever faintest trace of moisture lies deep beneath the stone, growing so slowly that their forms seem fixed in place. Between them stand isolated tufts of papery, silver-grey bunchgrass, stiff as dry bristles, and along the darkest, warmest ledges, thin crusts of black and rust-colored lichen cling to the rock face like mineral stains.

There are no animals. No tracks cross the fine silt; no beetles burrow beneath the cobbles; no spiders span the pitted alcoves, and no birds roost under the lip of the overhang. Without wind or movement, the hollow is held in a deep, velvety silence. Now and then, a single grain of sand detaches from the ceiling and lands with a tiny, dry *tick* on the cracked clay below, or a brittle stalk of grass gives a faint, papery whisper if brushed against.

Nothing decays here, and nothing hurries. A fallen twig lies beside its parent shrub, bleached bone-white, intact for years because there is not enough dampness to rot it. The dust lies thick and unbroken on the flat stones. Held in its warm, dark equilibrium, the hollow remains just as it has been—an untouched, quiet chamber of baked earth, ancient rock, and sparse, enduring scrub.
