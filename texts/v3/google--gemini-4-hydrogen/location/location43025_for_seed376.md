The valley sits high enough that the air feels thin in the lungs, cold and sharp under a clear, ordinary daylight. Looking across its breadth—miles of shallow basins, terraced ridges, and sunken flats—there is no bare stone to be seen, no gravel, no exposed soil. Everything is plant. The valley’s ancient, weathered contours are formed entirely from compacted, lignified peat and overlapping strata of petrified bark, settled over millennia into the smooth, heavy curves of an old landscape.

Yet nothing on the surface is still.

Across the entire expanse, the vegetation behaves like a vast, unbuilt factory running at full capacity. There are no walls, no metal, no made things here—only dense, layered thickets of cold-hardy flora that have evolved into engines, conduits, and refineries. Thick, muscular vines the width of tree trunks lie braided across the spongy ground, pulsing with slow, peristaltic waves as they pump dark, mineral-rich slurry up from deep, unseen root-furnaces. Between them rise crowded stands of hollow, silver-barked reeds, their joints swollen into translucent bladders of acidic sap. Faint blue-white arcs of static electricity flicker silently from one frost-rimmed tip to the next, discharging excess charge into the damp air.

Despite the frantic, chaotic energy of the place, it is profoundly quiet. Every woody piston, every coiled spring-tendril, every ridge of hardened cellulose is wrapped in inches of sodden, felt-like micro-moss. The cold mist that drifts through the valley condenses on this dense fleece, weighing it down with icy moisture and turning the entire landscape into a sound-deadening blanket. When a thirty-foot vascular tower—grown to full height in a matter of hours—finally buckles under the weight of its own crystalline resin deposits, it collapses into the deep turf with nothing more than a dull, muffled thud felt in the soles of the feet.

The air is damp with pockets of freezing vapor. Scattered across the valley floor are dark, still pools of condensation and extruded sap-water, tucked into basins of woven root-mat. Around their edges, low, broad-leaved plants act like flat-bed presses, slowly folding inward to crush fallen seed-pods, squeezing out beads of refined sulfur and pale, metallic dust that drift away on the cold breeze like dry snow.

Everything here is in a constant, turbulent cycle of rapid assembly and sudden breakdown. Tendrils wind themselves tight with osmotic pressure, hold for an hour, and then snap-release in a blur of motion, flinging iron-hard seeds across the canopy. Pressurized spore-boilers swell until their waxy seams split, venting silent plumes of warm, damp gas that instantly freeze into delicate rime on the surrounding needles. As soon as a grove exhausts its local pocket of nutrients or short-circuits from a sudden frost, it withers and slumps, and the aggressive, acid-secreting mats beneath immediately dissolve it, routing the raw mass straight back into the nearest surging conduit.

Animal life is almost absent. Occasionally, a heavy-shelled, pale beetle can be seen inching along the insulated bark of a power-reed, scraping pure mineral crust from a joint, or a solitary, thick-furred glider will cross high overhead in the clear light, riding the thin air well above the snapping, electrified, silently churning floor of the valley.
