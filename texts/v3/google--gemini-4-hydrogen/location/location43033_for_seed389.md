The air up here is so thin that the heat feels dry and weightless, like standing in front of an open kiln with nothing between you and the sky.

The city—if it is a city, and not a single sprawling refinery that grew rooms for its tenders—sits atop a newly risen shelf of black and ochre rock, miles above the lower world. From the edge of the terrace, there is no horizon so much as a pale, washed-out drop into haze, where the rest of the planet lies buried beneath a flat lid of cloud. Up here, the light is steady and plain, a clear, unblinking midday brightness that casts sharp, short shadows and bleaches the tops of the roofs to the color of bone.

Nothing has worn this stone down yet. The ridges and outcrops around the settlement are raw, glassy, and knife-edged—freshly extruded basalt and blistered pumice that look as though they cooled only a season ago, before wind or rain could round a single corner. In places, the ground still sits in jagged, stepped plates, tilted at awkward angles where the crust pushed upward and stopped. There is no soil, no grit of sand, no moss or lichen to soften the seams. Even the dust is scarce; what falls from the sky or flakes off the rock is sharp, crystalline ash that crunches like salt underfoot.

Into this unweathered shelf, the built world is packed tight—tiered, bolted, and cantilevered across a wide, shallow basin that slopes toward a central vent-field. The structures are dense and layered, stacked four and five stories deep along narrow, stepped alleys so close together that a person walking through them can brush both walls with their elbows. Everything is made of sintered slag, cast iron, pale ceramic, and thick plates of tarnished copper, joined by heavy rivets and ceramic insulators. There are no windows to speak of, only slotted louvers and thick, amber-tinted sight-glasses set into boiler housings and junction boxes.

Despite the crowding and the machinery, the place is startlingly quiet. At this altitude, the air is too thin to carry sound far; a hammer struck two streets away arrives as a dull, dry *tick*, stripped of its ring. Instead, you hear the city through the soles of your boots: a faint, continuous vibration, like a distant train that never quite arrives.

The industry here is patient. There are no roaring furnaces or clanking stamping-mills. The work is mostly electrical and thermal, driven by the heat rising from the young rock beneath. Tall, slatted cooling towers and vast arrays of copper-finned heat exchangers crowd the upper terraces, their parts turning at speeds so slow you have to watch a spoke for several seconds to be sure it is moving. Thick, braided busbars run overhead on ceramic standoffs, humming so softly you only catch it when you pass directly beneath a transformer. In the lower yards, rows of squat, cylindrical vats sit in silence, growing mineral crusts and plating out heavy metals by trickle-current over months. Even the cranes along the quarry-cuts move in long, measured arcs, paused for minutes at a time between lifts.

What makes the place strange is how closely the machines mimic the rock they are bolted to. Where steam escapes from the deep boreholes, it doesn't billow away in white plumes; it is trapped under broad, low hoods of corrugated slate and bled off through miles of sweating, uninsulated pipe. Along the shaded undersides of these pipes, and in the deep, narrow chasms between the factory blocks, the air turns unexpectedly damp. Warm condensation beads on the riveted iron, gathers along the seams, and drips steadily into narrow channels chiseled straight into the raw basalt.

These damp corridors are the only places where the heat feels heavy. A faint, metallic mist hangs waist-high above the drainage gutters, smelling of wet rust, sulfur, and hot ceramic. The water never pools for long—it is too hot for that, and too precious—but runs in thin, oily threads toward sunken cisterns and dark, mineral-encrusted sumps where it is pumped back down into the heat-exchangers.

There are no plants anywhere—not a blade, not a spore-mat, not even a stain of green in the wettest corners. The only things that move of their own accord are rare, and easy to miss if you aren't looking for them. Occasionally, clinging to the sweating underside of a condenser-manifold, you will spot a pale, leathery filter-feeder the size of a palm, its segmented body pressed flat against the warm copper to drink the runoff. Out on the open, sun-baked ledges where the thermal updrafts rise off the black stone, thin, long-winged gliders—more cartilage and membrane than bone—sometimes hang motionless in the thin air for hours, drifting from one heat-stack to the next without a beat, before vanishing back over the rim of the shelf.

Down in the alleys, the city continues its slow, quiet work. A valve bleeds a thin sigh of steam into a catch-pan. A copper vane shifts a fraction of an inch against the hot, thin wind. Across the whole wide basin, thousands of tons of metal and raw, unweathered stone sit baking in the plain daylight, edging forward by millimeters and milliamps, patient as geology.
