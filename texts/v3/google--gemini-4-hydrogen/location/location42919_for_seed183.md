The air here is thin enough to make the lungs work for it, cool and sharp with mist, yet the light is blinding. At this altitude—far above any treeline, perched on the ribs of a range that scrapes the upper sky—there is nothing to soften the glare. The sun, or whatever pale, overexposed furnace hangs behind the high haze, pours white-gold light onto wet stone until the entire valley seems to shine from the inside out.

It is a hanging basin, miles wide, hemmed in by jagged walls of dark slate, pale quartz, and fractured obsidian. Though the vista is open—you can see clear across the terraced expanse to the distant, saw-toothed rim—the valley floor itself is crowded. It is packed tight with weathered stone needles, overlapping shelves of shale, slumped boulders, and narrow, steep-sided gorges. Every surface is slick. Water is everywhere, not as a quiet lake, but as a restless, weeping presence. The rocks sweat. Thin sheets of snowmelt and condensed cloud-water sheet down the black basalt, pool in shallow stone pans, spill over lips of quartz, and mist back into the cool air, only to condense again on the next overhang.

There are no trees, no grass, no moss—not even a stray lichen softens the rock. Nor is there any movement of beast, bird, or insect. The closest thing to life is the mineral crusting along the wettest seams: brittle, coral-like blooms of white salt and ochre iron-oxide, deposited slowly over centuries by the dripping water. Everything else is bare stone, glass, and water, worn smooth or carved sharp by ages of weather.

And yet, the erosion here does not make sense.

Along the western wall, a wide cataract drops from a high ledge, roaring loudly enough to vibrate in the teeth. Halfway down, the falling water strikes an invisible seam in the air—a fault line in the world’s weight—and shears sideways. Half the torrent continues to the basin floor; the other half bends at a right angle, racing horizontally across a gap of empty air before slamming into a sheer pillar of slate. The pillar itself is deeply notched where the sideways river has chewed into it for ten thousand years. Its stone is hollowed into smooth, horizontal flutes, proving that whatever broke the rules of this place broke them long ago, and the rock has had time to settle into the wound.

Everywhere you look, the geometry of the valley stutters. Shadows do not always match the stones that cast them; in the fiercest glare, a spire of obsidian will throw a shadow that detaches slightly at the tip, trembling like a reflection on water. Near the center of the basin, where three wet gorges meet, sound behaves strangely too. The valley is loud—a constant, layered roar of rushing water, clattering shale, and wind screaming through narrow mineral flutes—but the echoes arrive out of order. You hear the sharp *crack* of a frost-split rock peeling off a high cliff a full second before you see the slab fall and shatter on the scree below.

Despite the hostility of the place, someone—or many someones, over long stretches of time—has been here. Their marks are scattered thinly across the terraces, small and stubborn. Low, dry-stone windbreaks are wedged into the lee of larger boulders, built from flat slate slabs stacked without mortar. Every few hundred paces along the main ridges, a cairn rises, its stones bound together with twisted strips of tarnished copper or pinned with rough, hand-hammered iron spikes driven into cracks.

These are not homes, and there are no roads—only faint, chipped footholds cut into the slickest quartz ramps with stone chisels, and small, square basins hollowed out by hand to catch the sideways-flowing water. Near the central gorge, a series of crude bronze rings are hammered into a vertical cliff face. From each ring hangs a length of braided sinew or knotted cord weighted with a smooth river stone—plumb lines, meant to measure true down. None of them hang straight. One tilts twenty degrees to the north, trembling in the wet draft; another floats nearly parallel to the cliff, pulled toward a dense, humming seam of dark basalt where the air tastes faintly of ozone and wet copper.

The valley is never still. Even in the absence of beasts or plants, the place churns with its own cold metabolism. Midday brings a sudden, heavy condensation: the mist thickens until the distant rims vanish in white glare, and water begins to bead on every flat surface, running upward along certain quartz veins in thin, glittering threads before snapping free and falling as rain. Wind funnels down from the extreme peaks, slamming through the crowded stone needles with a resonant, organ-like howl, driving sheets of spray across the obsidian ledges.

When the wind drops for a breath, the only sound left is the steady, rhythmic *drip-drip-hiss* of a million wet stones, and a low, sub-audible thrumming that rises from the bedrock itself—as if the mountain were a vast, cracked bell, still ringing from whatever struck it before the world’s edges were worn smooth.
