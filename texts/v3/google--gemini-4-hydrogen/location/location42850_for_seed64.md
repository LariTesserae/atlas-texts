Four miles above the salt-flats, tucked into the leeward flank of a sheer gneiss spire, there is a cleft no wider than a hallway and no deeper than a small nave. From the outside, it is invisible—just another shadowed seam in a wall of vertical stone that rises into the thin, colorless sky. Inside, the air is cool, bone-dry, and loud enough to rattle the teeth in your jaw.

There is no room to stand upright without brushing against something. Every square inch of the pocket is occupied, wedged tight in a three-way entanglement of weathered stone, heavy iron machinery, and a dense, waterless jungle that thrives in the dark.

The sound hits first: a massive, unvarying wall of noise. Three cast-iron dynamo housings, bolted straight into the bedrock with thick, rusted tie-rods, run at a permanent, unwavering cadence. They are driven by something deeper in the mountain—perhaps a dry-gas thermal differential or the constant, razor-thin shriek of high-altitude wind channeled through a hidden intake—and they never speed up, never slow down, never sputter. The air vibrates with a deep, sixty-cycle iron hum, overlaid by the crisp, deafening *clack-hiss-clack* of dry pneumatic valves cycling in unison. Around them, ten thousand stiff, papery fronds vibrate in sympathetic resonance, adding a dry, rustling roar like a waterfall made of parchment.

Despite the noise, nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing is wet. There is no condensation on the cold iron, no dampness in the crevices, no mud on the floor. The air tastes of ozone, crushed graphite, and old, heated mineral oil. When you touch the stone, it is chalky and static-charged; fine, pale dust clings to the rock in motionless, furry ridges along lines of magnetic force.

The vegetation is shockingly lush, though it shares nothing with the green, watery growth of lower worlds. It is a dry, dense tapestry of silver-black wire-moss, stacked shelves of brittle, wax-coated fungus-plates, and thick, choking curtains of jointed silica-reeds that crowd every ledge and spill over the catwalk grates. There are no leaves in the usual sense, only broad, leathery fans the color of tarnished pewter and tight coils of copper-colored tendrils that wrap around the ceramic insulators of the dynamos.

These plants do not drink. Their roots are dry, fibrous filaments that wedge into the microscopic pores of the gneiss and into the greased seams of the machinery. They feed on the stray electromagnetic fields leaking from the busbars, on the faint hydrocarbon mist of the bearing-lubricants, and on the static-heavy mineral dust drifting in from the cliff-face. Where a copper coil hums warmest, the wire-moss grows thickest, forming dense, velvet-textured cushions that muffle the sharper edges of the ironwork. It is a symbiosis of sheer persistence: the plants insulate the aging wires with their dry, carbon-rich mats, and the machines, in turn, shed the precise trickle of heat and charge the plants require to hold their form.

Light is scarce and flat. A single, jagged vertical slit high in the outer wall admits a thin, cold blade of high-altitude daylight—pale, dusty, and motionless. Through that narrow gap, there is nothing to see but an empty expanse of slate-gray sky and the distant, knife-edged shoulders of neighboring peaks, far below. The rest of the illumination comes from the machines themselves: a handful of thick, caged filament bulbs glowing a dull, steady amber, and the faint, hair-thin needles of violet corona-discharge that hiss silently at the tips of the exposed brass terminals.

There are no animals here. No mites in the moss, no flies drawn to the amber bulbs, no birds nesting in the upper ledges. Nothing scuttles, nothing breathes, nothing dies and rots. A fallen frond does not decay; it simply dries to pure silica and gray ash, eventually ground into powder by the constant, tiny vibration of the floor plates and swept by static into the corners.

Scattered among the foliage are the sparse artifacts of whoever bolted this place into the mountain—a cracked porcelain switchboard with frozen, oxidized knife-switches; a single, riveted steel ladder whose rungs are half-swallowed by shelf-fronds; a row of brass pressure-gauges whose glass faces are clouded with dry salt-bloom, their needles resting dead-steady on the same painted hashmarks they have pointed to for decades. There are no workbenches, no tools left behind, no sleeping quarters, no names stamped into the iron. Just the engines, humming in their cramped stone socket, and the dry garden packed tight around them.

The whole pocket sits in a state of frozen equilibrium. The rock has been scoured by dry wind into smooth, established flutes; the iron has settled into a thick, unchanging skin of dry black oxide; the plants have filled every millimeter of available space and stopped, growing so imperceptibly slowly that their dense tangles seem carved from horn and wax. The temperature never shifts from its crisp, cool baseline. The roar of the dynamos never wavers by a single beat. Sealed high above the world, tucked inside its narrow seam of stone, the place simply continues—loud, crowded, dim, and utterly still.
