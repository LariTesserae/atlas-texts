At first, there is only the sense of height—not a cliff-edge drop, because there is no cliff, and no ground to fall toward—just a warm, damp, bruised-green twilight stretching out in every direction until the mist swallows it.

You are standing on a rib of braided wood as wide as a river. Beneath your bare feet, the bark is dark, slick, and slightly yielding, worn silky-smooth by centuries of water and passage. There is no stone anywhere in this world, no soil, no bedrock. Everything solid is plant, and every plant has been coaxed, bent, notched, and lashed into place by hand. The rib curves away from you in a gentle, monumental arc, disappearing into a ceiling of haze half a mile overhead, while beside you, the edge drops off into a void so vast that its far side is only a faint, vertical smear of charcoal against the dim amber air.

This is a world of hollowness. For every cubic pace of living, woven timber, there are ten thousand paces of open, humid sky.

The light never brightens beyond a deep, indoor dusk. Far above—miles above, perhaps, though distance here is unreliable—an unbroken skin of overlapping, broad-leafed canopy and coarse thatch filters the sky into a flat, warm glow. The air is thick with the heat of a feverish greenhouse, even at this altitude. Moisture is constant, not falling as rain so much as *accumulating*: condensing out of the slow-turning clouds that drift through the empty chasms, beading along the waxy undersides of leaves, gathering in the deep grooves of the twisted bark until it spills over in thin, silent threads. Along the sides of the great arches, hollowed gourds and stitched leather bladders hang from bone pegs, catching the endless drip.

Look closely at the rib beneath you, and you see the obsessive, patient labor that built a continent out of air and vine. There are no saw marks, no iron nails, no mortar. Instead, there are notches chipped with flint adzes where living trunks were pressed together until their cambium fused. There are colossal lashings of stripped bast-fiber—hundreds of cords twisted into cables thick as a thigh—bound around the junctions in swollen, ancient knots that the growing wood has half-swallowed. Where a bridge spans the abyss to a distant pillar, it is nothing more than three braided ropes of sinewy vine, pegged with sharpened bone and stabilized with cross-sticks lashed in place by hand. The craft is humble, slow, and almost unimaginably old. The wood around the oldest knots has turned the color of wet slate, scarred by rot and healed over with smooth, pale callus-tissue, settled into an ancient balance between growth and decay.

Yet the silence is what undoes you.

A world this wet and this crowded with life should roar. Instead, it is muffled, as if wrapped in wet wool. Water drips onto broad leaves without a *plip*; your own footsteps are swallowed by the spongy moss-crust. Even if you shout across a gap toward a neighboring arch, the sound seems to die twenty paces from your mouth, eaten by the warm, heavy air and the porous walls of living phloem.

And the place is teeming.

Out in the dim, empty volume between the arches, shapes drift. Vast, pale gliders—nothing more than living kites of veined, damp membrane stretched over hollow cartilage—ride the warm thermal updrafts rising from the unseen depths. They never flap. They bank slowly around the braided pillars in total silence, their trailing tendrils brushing the mist. Along the undersides of the ribs, where the bark weeps nutrient-rich sap, clusters of long-limbed, soft-skinned climbers move with deliberate, unhurried grace. Pale amphibians with frilled, translucent throats cling to the wet cordage, pulsing faint, rhythmic flashes of blue-green light at one another across the void—a whole conversation carried out in absolute hush.

Predation happens at the same quiet pace. Beneath a sagging rope-bridge, a blind, multi-jointed stalker hangs motionless, disguised as a bundle of dead moss, waiting for a grazing climber to step onto the wrong knot. When it strikes, there is only a sudden, boneless reach, a brief thrashing of limbs in the mist, and then stillness again. Below, in the lower, damper reaches where dead branches slump into soft, rotting hammocks of peat and lichen, swarms of tiny, pale scavengers strip fallen gliders to cartilage in minutes, working without a whisper.

It is only after you have walked for a few hours—or what feels like a few hours—that you begin to notice the geometry is wrong.

Gravity here is loyal to the wood, not to any center of the earth. As you follow the great rib upward into its arc, the tilt never feels steep; "down" quietly turns with the curve of the braided timber. When you look back, the section you walked across an hour ago hangs vertically behind you like a wall, and a climber on the far side of a chasm is strolling casually upside down along the belly of an arch, her hair hanging *upward* toward the bark. Out in the open voids between the structures, weight thins out entirely. A dropped gourd does not plummet; it drifts sideways in a lazy spiral, caught in a warm eddy of mist, hovering before it settles gently against the nearest massive junction.

And the distances do not add up. You cross three narrow, swaying bridges in a triangle—from Pillar A to Pillar B, from B to C, and from C back to A—only to arrive at A and find it is twice as thick as when you left it, its ancient knots unfamiliar, and the bridge you just crossed trailing off into an empty fog that wasn't there before. Sometimes, peering through a gap in the latticework across three miles of dim, wet air, you can make out the distinct, scarred curve of the very rib you are standing on, hanging upside down in the distance.

Even the boundary between what was grown and what was made has frayed. In the oldest, most weathered junctions, you find vines tied in tight, complex knots that have no ends—seamless, closed loops of living wood that could never have been tied without cutting, yet show no scar of a blade. Stone scrapers and bone needles left wedged in the damp bark do not rust or rot; instead, after seasons of mist, they soften at the edges and sprout pale, calcified tendrils, rooting themselves into the timber as if tools here are merely dormant seeds waiting for enough water to wake.

Every few days, the weather shifts. Not a storm—there is no wind strong enough to rattle a leaf—but a slow, continent-wide *exhalation*. A warm, dense fog wells up from the bottomless hollow below, thick enough to blind you at arm's length. As it rises, you feel it through the soles of your feet: a faint, sub-audible tremor, a deep, rhythmic flexing of the entire woven world. Somewhere in the dark distance, a lashing older than memory shifts by the width of a finger. A knot tightens. A chasm narrows by a mile, or opens by two. Then the mist condenses on the waxy leaves, begins its silent, steady weeping once more, and the gliders drift back out into the dim, empty air.
