At this altitude, the world has been worn down to a single, immense table. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground flat epochs ago, leaving a high, near-level plateau that stretches for leagues under a low ceiling of freezing mist. There is no bare stone left, and no true soil. The entire expanse is buried beneath a continuous, fathoms-deep mattress of living vegetation—a cold, saturated skin of black peat-moss, pale crustose lichen, and braided, fibrous root-nerve that squelches under every step and weeps dark water whenever the wind presses down on it.

The light is a permanent, bruised pewter, filtered through bruised cloud-bellies that drag right across the moss. It is bitterly cold, hovering just above the point where the air would lock into ice. Instead, everything stays slick and dripping. Sleet falls in wet, heavy sheets, and the atmosphere itself condenses onto every surface, beading on the fur of animals and the hairy leaves of the sedge until it runs off in steady, freezing rivulets.

Despite the total dominance of the plant-mat, the landscape feels starkly empty. Across the miles of flat, open bog, the vertical presences are sparse and widely scattered—lonely silhouettes spaced far apart in the dimness. There are no walls here, no roads, no rivets or poured concrete, nothing built by hand. Yet the plateau is loud with the unmistakable, relentless clamor of heavy industry.

Out of the unbroken carpet of moss, the vegetation has organized itself into vast, feral engines. Miles apart, towering botanical-mechanical formations rise from the bog like solitary refineries, grown entirely from hardened bark, pressurized sap-vessels, and conductive fiber. Giant hydraulic reeds, thick as smokestacks, slam freezing groundwater upward in rhythmic, ear-splitting strokes, venting plumes of supercooled steam and sour, metallic mist from split crowns. Around their bases, sprawling mats of voltaic lichen store and discharge raw electrical current, snapping with blinding blue-white arcs that magnetize the falling sleet, pulling it sideways into trembling, hovering curtains before it drops.

Nothing about these formations is stable. The entire plateau is locked in a state of frantic, turbulent churn—a slow-motion storm of creation and self-destruction. A kilometer-wide stretch of the flat peat-crust will suddenly begin to torque, buckling upward into immense, interlocking gears of turf and root that grind against one another with a deafening, wet roar, extruding long, steaming ribbons of warm, greasy resin. Twenty minutes later, the tension snaps; the gears collapse back into flat, weeping sphagnum, and the extruded resin shatters in the freezing wind, sinking back into the mat to be digested.

This is where the rules of the place fray. At this height, reality does not hold its shape cleanly. Water routinely forgets which way is down, beading along the undersides of giant fern-fronds and racing *upward* against the sleet to feed churning, high-pressure vapor-heads. In the spaces between the steam-plumes, the flat horizon sometimes folds over itself like wet paper, showing the same distant electrical arc twice, a half-second out of sync. Shadows detach from the mist-shrouded stalks and continue swaying in a wind that stopped blowing minutes ago.

Yet the plateau teems with life. Fauna thrives in the chaotic margins between the freezing emptiness and the scalding, electrified botanical-engines. Across the wide, empty flats, large herds of pale, heavy-pelted grazers move at a frantic, loping trot—six-legged beasts with broad, copper-dense hooves that ground the static charge of the moss with every stride, leaving trails of faint, sizzling sparks in the wet peat. They are noisy, calling to one another in deep, brassy honks that carry for miles over the flat terrain.

Closer to the churning root-pistons and slurry-vents, life becomes dense and hyperactive. Clouds of blind, chitinous flyers—pale as lard and loud as hail—swarm in the warm, greasy exhaust, breeding in the pressurized valves of the bark-boilers and scattering in shrill, clicking tornadoes whenever a section of the living machinery tears itself apart. Long, segmented scavengers with translucent hides slither through the saturated moss-channels, drinking the acidic dew that condenses around the voltaic arcs and stripping the brittle, iron-rich bark from collapsed stalks before the bog can swallow them whole.

The sound never drops below a dull, vibrating roar. Even standing in the middle of an empty league of moss, with nothing visible for miles in any direction but flat, weeping lichen and grey mist, the air shakes. The bass thud of hydraulic root-hammers carries through the wet peat beneath your boots; the hiss of condensing steam, the wet tearing of shifting turf, the sharp crack of static, and the distant, echoing clamor of the herds all blend into a single, cold, tireless hum—the sound of an ancient, untouched world grinding itself endlessly into something else.
