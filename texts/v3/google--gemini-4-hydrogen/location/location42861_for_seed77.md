The ground is not rock, nor soil, nor sand, though it is made from all three—pressed, fired, and laid flat to the horizon.

Everywhere, as far as the eye can follow the curve of the heat-haze, the surface is a continuous, low-lying pavement of pale ochre mudbrick and unglazed terracotta, set in broad, shallow beds of lime mortar. There is no natural substrate visible beneath it; if you dig at a seam, you find only another course of brick, and beneath that, tamped clay and crushed shell. Yet nothing here feels ancient. The corners of the tiles are crisp enough to catch a fingernail. The trowel-marks and thumb-grooves in the mortar are still sharp, unblunted by wind or footfall, as if the whole continental basin had been laid down only a season ago and left to cure under an open, bright sky.

Because the plain sits so low—a vast, sunken pan with no hills to break the air—the heat gathers and stays. It is a dry, baking, kiln-like heat, rising off the pale clay in slow, trembling sheets. Nothing mechanical hums or turns here; there are no pipes, no pumps, no metal fittings. Everything is earth-material, arranged in immense, quiet geometry.

Across the emptiness, the built surface is mostly flat, broken only at wide, lonely intervals by low, knee-high retaining walls, square sunken catch-basins, or long, straight channels no deeper than a forearm. These structures are scattered sparingly, separated by miles of bare, unbroken brickwork. They have no roofs and no doors; they are simply slight alterations in the level of the floor, meant to catch, hold, or guide whatever water finds its way down.

And water does find its way, though never enough to flood. The freshly laid sub-layers are still sweating out their last dampness in the heat, and the air itself is heavy enough to carry moisture from the distant, unseen edges of the basin. In the hours before dawn, when the bricks finally cool, dew beads along the shaded northern lips of the low walls and trickles into the mortar joints. By mid-morning, the sun has burned most of it away, leaving dark, damp stains in the recessed basins and shallow, tepid puddles that mirror the white glare of the sky.

The sheer scale of the hot pavement drives its own weather. By afternoon, the rising thermals over the baking clay pull moist air inward, building towering, flat-bottomed clouds miles above the plain. Now and then, a warm squall drops out of them—a brief, localized curtain of rain that sweeps across a few square miles of brickwork. Where it hits, the dry clay drinks it greedily, darkening to a deep rust-red and sending up a sudden, sharp scent of wet dust and hot lime. The rain hisses softly against the warm tiles, pools briefly in the low channels, and within an hour is mostly steam again, leaving only a thin crust of salt along the edges of the mortar.

In those damp seams—and only there—life has begun to take hold. Where windblown dust has settled into the crisp cracks between bricks, sparse tufts of grey-green saltbush, low purslane, and wiry, jointed reeds have sprouted. They grow in thin, straight lines that trace the grid of the mortar, spaced far apart, never thick enough to form a meadow or a thicket. Each plant sits in its own small halo of shade, its roots wedged tight into the lime, drawing up the condensation that lingers beneath the tiles long after the surface has dried.

There are no animals to disturb them. No beetles click in the scrub, no birds cross the high, bright sky, no lizards dart along the retaining walls. The silence is vast and granular. If you stand still, the only sounds are the faint, dry *tick* of a brick expanding in the noon sun, the soft, papery rasp of wind moving through a solitary clump of reed, or the distant, muffled sigh of a warm gust dragging fine silt across acres of unweathered clay.

Everything here is in motion, yet nothing hurries. The mortar is still hardening; the bricks are still settling into their beds, hairline cracks opening here and there as the basin dries unevenly. Salt blooms in white, feathery rings around the drying puddles, and the sparse plants drop their seeds into the next seam down, slowly claiming a world that has barely finished being made.
