The valley does not have a floor so much as a bottom-most tier of drainage. From ridge to ridge, the entire mountain cleft has been terraced, shored up, and roofed over in concrete, dressed stone, and riveted iron, until the original geology is only a rumor behind weeping retaining walls.

It sits high enough that the clouds do not pass over it; they pass *through* it. At midday the light is a flat, dim slate-gray, diffused by low mist that never fully burns off. In the deeper tiers—under the cantilevered loading bays and the underside of the viaducts—it is perpetual dusk, lit by small, caged work-lamps of amber and pale sodium that throw wet halos onto the fog. The air is cool, around ten degrees Celsius, and carries the sharp, clean smell of wet iron, crushed fern, ozone from the overhead traction lines, and damp mortar.

Every surface is occupied. There is no bare ground and no wasted gap. The valley is packed from its lowest sluice-channels up to the cloud-scraped ridgelines with a dense, interlocking strata of brick boiler-houses, slate-roofed sorting sheds, corrugated steel walkways, glass-paned cultivation halls, and concrete cisterns. Where one structure ends, another is bolted directly onto its flank or bridged across to it with a steel truss. Between and over and around the masonry, the vegetation has filled every remaining millimeter. Thick, sodden cushions of moss pad the tops of every brick parapet; giant ferns erupt from drainage seams; ivy and creeping brambles drape the underside of iron catwalks like wet curtains; and every flat roof not used for rail-switching is crowded with terraced beds of shade-tolerant shrubs, willow coppice, and broad-leaved greens.

Despite the density of the machinery, the valley is remarkably quiet. The thick mats of wet foliage and the heavy, saturated air swallow sound before it can travel twenty yards. There are no combustion engines roaring here, no hammering foundries. The industry runs on water-head, gravity counterweights, and quiet electric traction. What you hear is a low, continuous texture: the soft hum of step-down transformers bolted to brick walls, the wet *shhh* of water sheeting down mossy spillways, the occasional muffled clunk of a hydraulic valve seating itself, and the faint, high singing of steel cables under tension.

Yet nothing is still. The valley moves with a restless, silent, anthill-like urgency. 

Overhead, a crisscrossing web of aerial cableways and monorail tracks cuts through the mist at different angles. Every few seconds, a suspended steel skip or a covered freight car glides out of the fog, its electric trolley-wheel whispering along the copper wire, and vanishes into a receiving bay on the opposite slope. Below, on the narrow stepped terraces, automated conveyor belts—shielded under rusted tin hoods—carry crushed slate, damp timber, and crates of harvested greens down toward the central sorting hubs. 

The weather itself adds to the turbulence. Wind funnels down the high cleft in erratic gusts, tearing the fog into fast-moving ribbons that reveal and conceal whole districts in the space of a breath. One moment a tier of glasshouses glows dimly across the gorge, its panes streaked with condensation; the next, a wall of gray vapor slams across the gap, leaving only the dripping iron railing in front of you and the pale, disembodied glow of a distant switch-lamp.

Water is never deep here, but it is never absent. It beads on the galvanized handrails, drips steadily from the underside of every conveyor bridge, and darkens the cobbles of the steep service stairs. Every retaining wall has its weep-holes—short lengths of iron pipe jutting from the stone—and each one trickles a thin, constant thread of mountain runoff into stone-lined gutters that race along the edges of the walkways. The concrete is stained with decades of mineral streaks, rust-bleed from bolt heads, and dark green algae. Nothing looks new; the whole immense apparatus has settled into the mountain over generations, its edges rounded by damp, its ironwork patched and re-patched with newer plates over older, flaking paint.

Life, in the animal sense, is scarce. You might walk a mile of dripping catwalks and see nothing but a few pale moths clinging to a lamp-cage, or a solitary blackbird slipping silently into a thicket of roof-ferns. The work of the valley—the shifting of gates, the routing of cars, the constant, frantic-looking ballet of cranes and winches—goes on largely by relay, timer, and distant control, with only the occasional figure in an oiled canvas coat appearing on a gantry to grease a bearing or clear a jammed sluice before stepping back into a misty doorway.

Looking down from the upper tiers, the valley looks less like a town and more like a vast, vertical switchboard overgrown with cloud-forest: a dim, cool, endlessly shifting tangle of wet brick, green leaf, and gliding steel, humming softly to itself inside the clouds.
