Up here, the mountain range has forgotten it was ever sharp. Eons of dry, abrasive wind have planed the peaks down into a single, immense tableland—a high, flat shelf of ancient gneiss and iron-stained stone suspended miles above whatever lies below. The air is thin, breathless, and unexpectedly warm, holding a steady, dry heat like the interior of a room whose radiator was left on a century ago and never turned off. 

There is no sky to speak of, only a ceiling of absolute, lightless black. In the absence of sun, moon, or stars, distance is measured by the faintest pinpricks of industrial phosphorescence: a dull, bruised-amber filament glowing inside a distant relay tower; a hairline crack of cold blue static along an insulator; the occasional, motionless red eye of a pressure gauge that has read the same figure for ten thousand years. You do not see the horizon; you simply notice where the sparse, widely spaced lights stop.

The plateau is a city, though not one built for crowds or haste. Its scale is vast and deliberately emptied out. Broad, ruler-straight avenues of vitrified slag and packed mineral dust stretch for kilometers between structures, wide enough to swallow entire neighborhoods of emptiness. Along these dry boulevards stand the buildings: windowless, monolithic factories, squat transformer halls, and towering, ribbed condensers spaced hundreds of meters apart, as if they were kept at a cautious distance from one another to prevent arcing. 

Everything here is layered and ancient. The oldest foundations—thick, beveled plinths of poured basalt—have been scoured smooth by time until they resemble natural outcrops, their corners rounded like river stones despite the fact that no river has touched this altitude in geological ages. Built atop and into them are the sharper, heavier geometries of the industrial era: riveted iron housings, ceramic bushings the size of silos, and miles of thick, braided copper busbars running along elevated stone viaducts. Yet even the metal has reached a kind of truce with time. Whatever could rust has already turned to a dense, velvety patina of black oxide that sheds no dust and asks for no repair.

It is a place of almost total stillness. There is no weather here—no rain, no mist, no shifting of seasons. Water exists only as a memory or a technicality: sealed inside heavy, thick-walled sight-glasses as a clear, viscous dielectric fluid that never evaporates, or trapped in microscopic, salty pockets deep within the stone. Nothing grows. Not a lichen, not a thread of moss, not even a dry husk of dead root. The ground is pure mineral—crushed quartz, graphite grit, and the fine, metallic ash of long-cooled furnaces.

And yet the city is not dead. If you press your palm against the casing of a substation, or rest your ear against one of the broad, warm concrete pylons, you feel it: a low, steady, unwavering hum. It is not the roar of active production, with pistons slamming and raw ore tumbling down chutes. Nothing is being made here anymore, and nothing is being consumed. Instead, the entire plateau operates in a state of closed, thermodynamic equilibrium. Vast, buried flywheels spin in vacuum chambers without friction; dry-type transformers step voltage up and down in an endless, pointless loop; heat rises gently from miles of subterranean conduits, keeping the thin air at its constant, lukewarm temperature, and dissipating into the dark at the exact rate it is generated.

Sound barely travels in the thin, dry air, and there is almost nothing to make it. You hear your own boots crunching on the mineral grit, and beneath that, the faint, sub-audible thrum of the grid—a vibration felt more in the teeth than in the ears. Every few hours, somewhere across the dark expanse of an empty plaza, a single relay might click shut, or a massive porcelain insulator might let out a dry, sharp *ping* as it adjusts to a microscopic shift in load. Then the quiet settles back in, heavy and unchanged.

Life is rare enough to feel like a rumor, or a glitch in the architecture. Every few kilometers, clinging to the warm, ridged fins of a silent heat-exchanger, you might find one of the plateau’s few native inhabitants: flat, segmented creatures with shells like unglazed porcelain and no eyes at all. They do not forage or hunt. They press their soft, conductive undersides against the warm metal, drinking in stray static charge and shedding flakes of dry, grey mineral dust. Occasionally, something larger moves in the dark between the factories—a slow, multi-legged silhouette dragging a thin, metallic filament behind it across the slag, pausing at the base of a pylon for days at a time before moving on.

Walking the plateau feels like walking inside the mind of an immense, sleeping machine that has already solved every problem it was given and now simply maintains its own temperature in the dark. Nothing erodes further. Nothing grows. The lights do not flicker, the air does not cool, and the sparse, towering geometry of the city waits in its warm, dry silence, utterly indifferent to the passage of time.
