There is no dry land left here, only the memory of it ground down to a continent-wide shelf of slick, iron-dark shale and rolling stone. Whatever mountains once stood on this world dissolved eons ago; the horizon is a flat, unbroken smudge in every direction, lost in a low, bruised twilight that never brightens past the color of tarnished pewter. 

The air is warm—thick as wet wool and heavy with the smell of sulfur, boiled silt, and old brine. It is so saturated with vapor that breathing feels like drinking. There is no real separation between the sky and the ground; the cloud-ceiling hangs so low it drags across the flats in tattered, lukewarm curtains, and warm rain falls not in drops, but in shifting, vibrating sheets that sweep sideways, upward, or hang suspended in the turbulence until a gust shears them apart.

Across this endless, low-lying plain, the water does not sit in basins. It moves in vast, shallow sheet-tides—walls of coffee-colored brine, miles wide and waist-deep, driven by no single moon but by the chaotic, sloshing momentum of a world-sized puddle. They race across the flats from opposite horizons at once. When two sheet-tides meet, they do not simply splash; they rear up into towering, frothy ridges that slam together with the weight of collapsing buildings, hanging in the dim air as churning curtains of spray before dropping back down to scour the shale.

Beneath the water, the floor is packed tight. Every square inch is occupied by smooth, featureless stone nodules—billions of them, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a millstone, worn into perfect, slick eggs by eons of tumbling. There is no room between them, and they never stop moving. Driven by the racing currents, the entire floor of the world shifts and rolls like a continent-sized ball-mill.

The noise is a physical weight. You do not hear it so much as you are trapped inside it: the deep, tectonic *chatter* of a billion stones grinding against one another beneath the silt, the endless freight-train roar of colliding tides, and the shriek of warm wind tearing through dense curtains of mist. The atmosphere itself seems to hum at a frequency that vibrates in your teeth and sternum. Shout at arm’s length, and the sound is swallowed whole before it leaves your lips.

And in the midst of this deafening, crowded churn, the rules of the world have begun to fray. 

Perhaps it is the sheer age of the place, or the relentless violence of the currents, but matter and time no longer settle cleanly into one shape at a time. A breaking wave will sometimes stutter—repeating the same three-second crest over and over in the exact same spot, overlapping with itself until it forms a rigid, roaring knot of water that stands like a glass wall while the rest of the tide slams around it. Elsewhere, the silt and brine will briefly remember a shape they held a moment ago, freezing into sudden, fleeting architectures—hollow domes, spiraling chimneys, or flat, sheer ledges of mud-water that hum like struck bronze for ten heartbeats before dissolving back into boiling foam.

Reflections cannot be trusted. In the rare, fleeting hollows where the water stills for a breath between bores, the surface does not show the low, gray clouds overhead. It might show the underside of the rolling stone-bed, or a sky tilted ninety degrees to the horizon, or the faint, ghostly double of a wave that will not arrive for another minute. Sound, too, comes unstuck from its causes: a dull, violet flicker of lightning inside the cloud-deck is preceded, ten seconds early, by the piled-up, overlapping crack of its own thunder arriving from three directions at once.

Life here is sparse, stubborn, and built to endure the grinding. Miles apart, clinging to the few exposed ribs of ancient bedrock that rise a few inches above the rolling stones, are isolated mats of black, rubbery kelp-grass and pale, leathery bladder-reeds. They have no brittle stems; they lie flat against the rock, braided into the cracks, vibrating like taut strings under the rushing brine. 

Fauna is rarer still. Occasionally, something massive and flat-bodied—a creature like a leathery, blind ray the width of a house—glides *through* the shifting stone-bed, its cartilage body flexing around the rolling boulders as it filters the warm soup. On the higher bedrock seams, pale, thick-shelled things the size of boulders clamp themselves down so tightly they seem part of the stone, sealing their rims against the chaos for weeks at a time. High above, barely visible against the dragging belly of the clouds, silent, kite-shaped shadows sometimes skim the turbulent ceiling, never landing.

There are no roads, no walls, no metal, no trace of tools—only the faintest, scattered marks that something with hands or bodies once paused here. On the slightly higher humps of shale, where the sheet-tides thin to ankle-depth, a few of the smooth stone-eggs have been stacked into tight, squat cairns, wedged together with dried bladder-weed and packed silt so densely they survive the bores. Beside them, shallow rings have been pressed into the softest mud, or braided strands of kelp-grass knotted around bedrock spurs—small, wordless anchors left behind in a world that is too old to remember its own shape, and too violent to ever sit still.
