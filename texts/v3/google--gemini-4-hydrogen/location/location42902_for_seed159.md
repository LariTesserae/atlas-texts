Far below the surrounding plateau, the earth has pulled apart into a mile-wide basin whose walls rise in sheer, jagged steps toward a bright, open sky. Sunlight pours straight down into the depression, flat and unforgiving, catching on surfaces that have not yet learned how to be smooth. Nothing here has been rounded by wind or patient rain. The floor of the rift is raw, emerging geology: dark basalt blisters still cracked from cooling, razor-edged talus slopes, and broad, brittle terraces of pale travertine that precipitate out of the ground almost as fast as they fracture.

It is warm down here—not the dry bake of a desert, but a feverish, close warmth that rises from the stone itself. The air smells of wet chalk, iron, and crushed shell. Moisture arrives in fitful, localized bursts: thin plumes of steam sighing out of hairline fissures, beading on the undersides of basalt overhangs until it drips in slow, heavy drops. Shallow, braided trickles of mineral-heavy water wind through the lowest depressions, pooling in raw, saucer-shaped pans before vanishing into porous salt-crusts. Away from the seeps, the flats are dry and flaky, underfoot like dried plaster, yet a few paces away the stone sweats.

There is no green anywhere. Not a blade, not a frond, not even a tuft of moss softens the mineral glare. In the warmest, wettest channels, a slick, rust-colored microbial film coats the gravel—the closest thing to stillness the basin allows—but everything else that lives here moves, crawls, swims, or flies.

The fauna is overwhelming, layered so densely into the open space that the ground itself seems to be in constant, shimmering motion. Because there are no plants to anchor the soil or filter the light, the entire food web runs on mineral blooms, microbial slime, and relentless predation. The shallow pools are thick with translucent, darting swimmers and armored grazers whose ridged shells look indistinguishable from the fresh-quarried stone around them. Across the dry travertine flats, vast, colliding carpets of pale, many-legged arthropods surge and scatter, scraping at sulfurous rinds, clambering over one another in frantic, shifting drifts. 

Above them, the air is crowded. Clouds of papery, mica-winged flyers wheel in intersecting spirals, their bodies catching the midday light as they dive to pluck smaller things from the rock. In the shadowed crevices of the talus, leathery, splayed-legged scavengers wait out the heat, only to heave themselves up through the brittle crusts when something larger dies or collapses, sending fresh cascades of sharp stone clattering down the slopes. Even the geology is half-animal: along the steaming fissures, colonial tube-dwellers build porous, calcified chimneys out of their own secretions and the blowing mineral dust, raising fragile towers that swell over days and shatter in an instant.

Nothing here stays intact for long. The basin operates in a state of constant, turbulent turnover—an anthill-energy where creation and destruction happen in the same breath. A thin crust of gypsum forms over a warm seep, swells with trapped vapor, and caves in beneath the weight of a passing swarm. A burrowing thing undermines a shelf of raw talus, triggering a localized landslide that immediately draws hundreds of click-jawed scavengers to whatever was crushed beneath. The sound of the place is loud and unceasing: a dry, papery roar of millions of wings, the granular hiss of chitinous legs on salt-crust, the sharp *clack* of tumbling stones, and the wet, rhythmic sigh of the steam vents underneath it all.

Tucked into the margins of this riot—along the slightly firmer ledges and under the lee of overhanging basalt—are the scattered, temporary marks of tool-users. There are no settlements here, only the faint, precarious geometry of brief visits: a low windbreak stacked from unmortared, razor-edged slabs; a row of bone pegs driven into a crack, hung with bundles of dried, hollow carapaces lashed together with sinew. On a flat shelf above a steaming pool, someone has left a knapping floor—a crescent of gleaming black volcanic-glass flakes scattered around a hammerstone, already half-dusted with salt and patrolled by scouting beetles. Across a narrow, hissing rift, two long rib-bones have been bound with twisted cordage to form a crude, swaying bridge. 

None of it looks built to last. Anything left untouched for a week is either encrusted with mineral rinds, undermined by burrowers, or picked clean by the swarming floor. The makers come down from the rim, gather what they can carry—salt, glass, dried shell, fat—and climb back out before the ground shifts beneath them again.
