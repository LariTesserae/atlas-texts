The air hits you first—warm, close, and thick with the smell of wet bark, ozone, and crushed chitin. You are well below the surface, at the bottom of a sunken, cathedral-sized hollow where the concept of stone has been entirely forgotten. There is no bedrock visible anywhere. The floor, the curving walls, and the vaulted ceiling eighty feet overhead are woven from a single, continuous mass of living plant-matter: colossal, braided taproots as thick as houses, matted pale-gray moss, and swollen, fibrous tubers that have grown together over centuries into a seamless, breathing shell.

Everything here is damp, though there is no standing river or open lake. Moisture arrives as a slow, constant sweat. The living walls glisten with condensation, and every few seconds a heavy, lukewarm drop detaches from the high root-canopy and plummets into the gloom, striking a rusted iron grate or a broad, leathery leaf with a sharp *plink*. Shallow puddles gather in the hollows of the floor-roots, their surfaces filmed with pale pollen and oil.

Despite the richness of the living walls, the cavern itself feels strangely sparse and open. The central void is largely empty air, dim and cavernous, broken only by widely spaced pillars of fused wood and the scattered, layered geometry of an old industrial settlement. Suspended between the root-columns on tensioned steel cables and riveted iron trusses are the platforms of a vertical factory-hamlet: corrugated tin sheds, walkways of perforated grating, cylindrical brass pressure-tanks, and heavy, cast-iron dynamo housings. They are arranged with a clear, pragmatic logic—catwalks linking pumping stations to sorting hoppers, overhead rails carrying suspended iron buckets from one side of the hollow to the other—yet there is generous space between each structure, leaving wide pockets of shadowed emptiness where the humid air hangs undisturbed.

The light is dim and fiercely localized. No sunlight reaches this depth. Instead, the gloom is punctuated by caged amber incandescent bulbs and sputtering blue-white arc-lamps bolted to the ironwork. Their glow barely penetrates ten feet into the damp air, leaving the upper vaults and the deeper root-crevices in near-total darkness, illuminated only by the occasional, sickly-green pulse of bioluminescent fungus clinging to the undersides of the platforms.

And the place is deafening.

The sound is a constant, layered wall of noise that bounces off the curved, fibrous walls of the hollow. Beneath everything is the industrial pulse: the heavy, rhythmic *clank-hiss-thud* of steam-driven piston-pumps, the high-pitched whine of electric turbines, and the rattle of conveyor chains. Yet the machinery is almost drowned out by the sheer, overwhelming volume of the fauna.

Life does not merely inhabit this place; it saturates every square inch of solid surface. The living plant-walls are in perpetual, creeping motion. Millions of blind, pale-shelled beetles and segmented, finger-long millipedes stream in dense, glistening ribbons along the root-ridges, their legs producing a dry, incessant *shhhk-shhhk* like rain on tin. Colonial silk-weavers drape the undersides of the iron catwalks in thick, damp curtains that vibrate whenever a piston fires. Larger, stranger things cling to the damp bark like heavy, plated barnacles—lungless, siphon-mouthed grazers the size of dogs that slowly rasp at the moss, letting out wet, resonant clicks that echo across the void.

In the sparse open air between the platforms, the traffic is just as fierce. Swarms of translucent, fluttering midges orbit the caged arc-lamps in dense halos, while swift, leathery predators—wing-spanned and eyeless, navigating by rapid, metallic-sounding sonar-clicks—bank and dive through the girders, snatching prey mid-air before vanishing back into the dark upper canopy.

What makes the hollow truly strange is how thoroughly the industry and the ecosystem have grown into one another. This is no fresh intrusion; the ironwork is pitted with rust, the brass is green with verdigris, and the living wood has slowly, patiently wrapped itself around the anchor-bolts and girders like bone healing around shrapnel. The machines are not fighting the cavern; they are part of its metabolism.

Heavy brass taps are driven deep into the largest taproots, drawing off a thick, amber sap that is piped to humming centrifugal separators. Elsewhere, wide iron funnels equipped with rotating wire brushes scrape the swarming chitinous life off the walls, feeding it into grinding hoppers that smell sharply of ammonia and roasted meal. In return, the fauna has colonized the technology. Thick, gelatinous slugs—translucent enough to show the faint, coiled organs inside—cluster in dense, pulsing mats around the electrified copper conduits, seemingly feeding on the electromagnetic hum and the ionized air around the transformers. Their secreted slime coats the junction boxes in a slick, insulating glaze that the maintenance crews—whoever and wherever they are—have long since stopped scraping away.

The whole ecosystem runs on a restless, interlocking cycle. Every twenty minutes or so, the central pressure-tanks release a long, shrieking burst of waste steam to clear their valves. The sudden heat and vibration ripple through the living substrate; the root-walls subtly flex, sweating out a fresh wave of sweet condensation. Instantly, the cavern erupts: the grazers unseal their siphons to drink, the beetle-ribbons surge toward the dampened bark, the winged predators drop from the ceiling in a whistling cloud, and the automated intake fans spin up with a roar to clear the sudden fog of spores and mist.

Then the steam dissipates, the fans settle back into their low, grinding drone, and the dim, warm, deafening hollow returns to its established, swarming balance—an ancient engine of iron and living wood, humming away in the dark beneath the world.
