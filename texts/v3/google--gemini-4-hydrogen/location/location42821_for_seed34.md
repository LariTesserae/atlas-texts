The plateau does not end at the horizon; it only greys into mist. From any high roof, the land is a single, gently tilted table of dark slate and pale limestone, stretching far enough to breed its own weather. Over the expanse, the sky is a bright, cool vault, and the sun—clear and thin at this altitude—strikes the wet stone with a flat, silver glare.

Everywhere, the rock has been lifted, split, and stacked into a city without mortar or timber. There are no wide spans here, no arches keyed by iron, no broad plazas. Because the stone was laid dry by hand—wedged with chinking shards and locked by its own weight—the buildings press close together, sharing thick, battered walls for strength. They rise in three and four uneven stories, stepping down the natural contours of the highland in a dense, unbroken honeycomb. Lanes between them are narrow, often no wider than a pair of shoulders, and they run deep, shaded by overhanging corbeled eaves and bridged by flat slate walkways. Over centuries, the masonry and the bedrock beneath it have settled into a single, weathered crust. Frost and rain have rounded the sharp edges of the lintels; stairs carved directly into the stone are worn into shallow, glistening troughs.

Despite its scale, the city is almost entirely silent. The tight, layered geometry of the walls breaks the high-altitude wind into slow, soundless eddies before it can ever howl. Even the water—and the place is soaked with it—moves without noise.

The plateau sits at the lower edge of the cloud belt, and the air is in constant, quiet motion. By mid-morning, the sun warms the dark slate just enough to draw thin veils of vapor from the deeper alleys. By afternoon, vast banks of cloud drift in across the highland, heavy and cool, brushing directly against the upper terraces. Wherever the mist touches stone, it condenses. Walls weep in slow, darkening streaks. Flat roofs, pitched at a careful, almost imperceptible angle, catch the moisture and sheet it toward carved stone runnels.

There are no roaring gutters or splashing spouts. The entire settlement is graded to slow water down, to spread it thin so it will not undermine the dry-laid foundations. It slides in laminar, glassy films over moss-cushioned stone, slips into square settling basins, and disappears into subterranean cisterns that honey-comb the lower levels. The only sound for miles might be the single, muffled *tock* of a drop falling into a dark well, or the faintest hiss of mist brushing wet lichen.

Vegetation is sparse and clings low to the damp joints. Pale, crustose lichens—grey, sulfur, and rust—map the windward faces of the stones. Thick, dark-green velvet moss packs the chinks between slabs, swelling with water until it feels like cold sponge. In the shallow silt-traps at the bottoms of the alleys, where dust and fine grit wash down from the upper tiers, small tufts of wiry sedge and waxy-leaved rock-plants take hold. There are no trees, no shrubs, nothing tall enough to catch the wind or cast a shifting shadow.

Life here is rare and unhurried. A long-legged, slate-colored bird may stand motionless in the ankle-deep mirror of a roof-basin, waiting for the tiny, pale amphibians that breed in the cisterns. Occasionally, a small, thick-furred mammal slips through a drainage gap in a retaining wall, leaving damp prints that evaporate in minutes when the clouds part. Whether the city is abandoned or merely tended by a handful of unseen, soft-footed keepers is hard to tell; the stone channels are clear of heavy rubble, as if someone still walks the terraces once a season to lift a fallen slab, yet no smoke rises from any chimney, and no voice carries down the narrow lanes.

When the cloud bank finally tears open toward evening, the light pours across the wet, terraced expanse at a low angle. Thousands of roofs and stepped walls flash at once, turning the grey city into a vast, intricate mirror—still, cool, and quietly breathing water back into the sky.
