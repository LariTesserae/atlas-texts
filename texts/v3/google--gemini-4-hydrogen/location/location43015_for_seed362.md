There is no ground here, only the weave.

Beneath a sky the color of bleached linen—so bright it washes the edges out of things without ever warming them—stretches a low, continental basin of dry, living machinery. You stand on a springy, interlocking floor of pale, lignified cabling and flattened, fibrous bark, bolted together with tarnished brass rivets and grown into seamless hexagonal trusses. If you pry up a slat, there is no soil beneath it, no bedrock, no dampness. Only deeper layers of the same dry, papery, iron-grained rootwork, descending into a cool, dustless dark where distant flywheels turn in silence.

The air is crisp, chill, and bone-dry. There is no water anywhere in this world—no puddles, no sap that wets the skin, no mist on the horizon. The plants that form every wall, gantry, and smokestack here are xerophytic hybrids of cellulose, silica, and copper-bearing tissue. Their trunks are fluted like cast-iron columns, their bark the texture of dry parchment stretched over tight wire. Above, the canopy is a dense, layered ceiling of translucent, wax-skinned leaves and slatted photovoltaic fronds, angled with mechanical precision to drink the blinding, heatless white light.

Everything is built, and everything is grown. There is no distinction between the trellis and the vine, the factory and the grove. A row of towering cooling-ferns rises from a floor of woven bamboo-plate, their stems hollow and ribbed like accordion hoses. As the cold wind moves across the basin, the ferns flex in unison, pumping dry, pressurized air down into the root-grid below. Beside them, vast, flat-topped banyan-dynamos spread their buttress roots across acres of floor; inside their split, woody husks, copper-veined heartwood coils around cores of lodestone, spinning on bearings lubricated with fine, dry graphite dust.

Despite the ceaseless motion—pistons of petrified reed sliding in and out of resinous sleeves, conveyor-tendrils ferrying pale, papery seed-pods across high catwalks, louvers opening and closing in the canopy to track the glare—the world is almost completely silent. 

There is no hiss of steam, no roar of combustion, no splash or drip. The machinery runs on static charge, magnetic induction, and dry pneumatic pressure. When a hundred-foot flywheel turns overhead, its wooden teeth meshing with a gear of living hornbeam, the only sound is a faint, papery *shhh*, like a dry leaf dragged across slate. Now and then, a distant relay clicks—a sharp, solitary *tock* that carries for miles across the flat expanse—or a static arc snaps silently between two silver-tipped thorns, leaving only the smell of ozone and toasted chaff.

The scale is immense and oddly flattened. From a high gantry, the industrial forest extends to a horizon that seems too far away, curved upward slightly like the inside of a shallow dish. Weather moves across it in vast, slow patterns: fronts of dry, electrified dust that turn the white light momentarily amber; cold downdrafts that set millions of louvered leaves rattling in a single, continent-wide sigh; and tall, slow-moving whirlwinds of pale pollen that spiral upward until they vanish into the glare.

Because the place is very old—weathered into a state of settled, dusty permanence—the rules that hold it together have worn thin in places, and the flora has grown around the gaps.

Walk down a straight colonnade of riveted cypress-pillars, and you will find that the shadows cast by the blinding overhead light do not quite match the shapes above you; a stationary gantry casts a shadow whose gears are still turning, or a row of fronds casts no shadow at all until you step past them, at which point the darkness snaps onto the floor a second too late. Along the older aqueducts—dry troughs of hollowed-out cedar that carry streams of fine, white quartz sand—perspective refuses to behave. A pipe will pass behind a thick trunk and emerge from the front of it, seamlessly spliced, without ever having curved. 

In the denser thickets, where catwalks and ironwood branches braid together six or seven layers deep, space itself feels pleated. You can look through a gap in a wickerwork bulkhead and see a sunlit turbine-grove three miles away, yet if you reach your hand through the gap, your fingers brush against the cool, dusty leaves of that distant grove as if they were inches from your face. The workers—or whatever ancient intelligence first grafted these engines—built for this. Conveyors plunge straight into solid, opaque knots of burl-wood and emerge, undisturbed, from different trees half a mile to the left.

Animal life is scarce, an afterthought in a world that feeds on light and static. Occasionally, a pale, stick-legged creature the size of a heron picks its way across the woven floor—half chitin, half dry pith, with compound eyes like faceted quartz. It pauses by a junction-box of grown cork, probes a seam with a needle-like proboscis to sip a bead of pure, non-aqueous dielectric oil, and then freezes, indistinguishable from the struts around it. Once in a great while, a moth with wings of beaten copper foil drifts through the white glare, drawn to the silent hum of an induction-trunk, before vanishing into a fold in the air that was not there a moment ago.

What the whole vast apparatus is manufacturing is impossible to tell. The conveyor-vines carry blank, hexagonal wafers of compressed plant-fiber into the towering, windowless boles of the central refineries; from the other side, pneumatic tubes shoot identical wafers back out into the canopy, where they are slotted into the leaves to replace those that have grown brittle and turned to dust. It is an endless, dry, coolly illuminated equilibrium—a continent-sized clockwork garden, quietly maintaining itself in a reality that has long since stopped insisting on straight lines.
