The sky here has no mercy in it. At this altitude, the air is too thin to scatter much of the light, so the sun hangs like an exposed filament in a pale, metallic blue, pouring down an unfiltered glare that turns every sharp edge of the terrain into a blade of white fire. It is freezing—deeply, cleanly cold—yet the light is so fierce it hurts to keep your eyes open without squinting through tears.

Beneath that blinding sky stretches a continental uplift so young the rock hasn’t learned how to crumble. There are no rounded hills, no soft river valleys, no soil-draped slopes. The ground is freshly fractured: vast, tilted shelves of pale quartzite, knife-edged ridges of raw basalt, and deep, narrow tectonic clefts where veins of native copper, pyrite, and magnetite lie exposed to the air like freshly cut bone. Even the shadows are razor-sharp, pools of ink-black cold tucked behind jagged fins of stone.

And yet, almost none of the rock is bare.

Every horizontal shelf, every cleft, every leeward face is packed—wedged tight, layered, saturated—with a living, grinding, electrified industry that has no makers. Nothing here was built. There are no foundations, no bolts, no ruins of vanished engineers. Everything that hums, smelts, vents, and sparks across these thousands of miles grew out of the crust itself, evolved to turn thin sunlight, raw ore, and bitter cold into a single, planet-scale metabolism.

To stand on a ridge is to be deafened. The sound is not the chaotic clamor of a jungle, nor the shriek of wind, though the air is loud with both. It is a continuous, wall-to-wall industrial roar: a low, chest-rattling thrum of millions of pressurized chambers, overlaid by the rhythmic *hiss-clack* of steam valves, the dry, agonizing grind of heavy plates sliding past one another, and a sharp, relentless crackle of high-voltage static. The air smells fiercely of ozone, hot oil, and wet rust.

Look down into any basin and you see no ground, only the backs of the **smelter-herds**—vast, locomotive-sized organisms packed carapace to carapace like cobblestones in a street. Their shells are not mere horn or bone, but thick, layered composites of chitin and smelted iron-sulfide, polished to a dull, blinding sheen by the wind and the friction of their neighbors. They move with the patience of glaciers. A single creature may take an hour to shift its weight a few inches, grinding its armored flank against the next with a shriek of metal on stone. 

Inside them, slow biological furnaces burn. They feed on the crushed, mineral-heavy rock beneath them and the thickets of dark vegetation growing between their legs, breaking both down in pressurized, acid-lined guts. From rows of calcified dorsal stacks, they vent long, steady plumes of white steam and sulfurous vapor. In the sub-zero air, that exhaust does not billow away; it freezes almost instantly into a shimmering fog of ice needles and mineral dust, drifting slowly down to coat the world in a damp, metallic rime.

Water exists here only in that hesitant, clinging way—never as rivers or lakes, just a perpetual, freezing dampness. Where the warm flanks of the giants press against the stone, the rime melts into slick, oily films of condensation that seep into the crevices, feeding the flora.

The vegetation is lush in a dense, armored, light-hungry way. Packed into every gap between the smelter-herds are thickets of soot-black and deep-indigo stalks—pigmented as dark as possible to drink in the fierce sun and hoard heat. Their leaves are thick, rubbery, and glistening with a waxy, hydrocarbon-rich sap that resists freezing. Some species grow like translucent silica-glass bulbs, trapping pockets of solar warmth inside themselves; others form towering, fibrous cooling-spines that wick the condensed moisture from the air and drip it down onto the grinding mats below.

Clinging to these dark thickets, and swarming over every exposed seam of the giant carapaces, are the smaller fauna—and there are billions of them, an overwhelming, interlocking carpet of living machinery. 

Long, segmented **dredge-chains** wind through the damp undergrowth, passing crushed stone, shed metal scales, and dead plant matter from mouth to mouth in a slow, continuous peristaltic conveyor that turns raw scree into rich, oil-soaked slag. Above them, colonies of **arc-weavers**—fist-sized, copper-bristled things—link legs across the dark stalks. They flex in vast, synchronized waves that take minutes to ripple across a hillside. Each slow contraction builds a piezo-electric charge; when the wave crests, thousands of tiny blue-white sparks snap across the gaps between them, filling the thin air with that sharp, ceaseless crackle and driving nitrogen out of the cold atmosphere to feed the roots below.

Even the air above the basins is occupied. Vast, slow-drifting **condenser-kites**—thin, membrane-winged creatures ribbed with hollow bone—hang motionless on the rising columns of warm exhaust, their undersides furred with frost-catching filaments. They drift at walking pace, heavy with harvested water, waiting for their internal bladders to fill before they descend, once every few weeks, to empty themselves over the root-mats.

Nothing here hurries. The dynamic of the whole continent is set to a deep, unyielding tempo. A wave of steam-venting rolls across a valley over the course of an afternoon; a smelter-herd migrates fifty yards in a season, chewing its way along a copper vein; a spark-colony takes an entire morning to charge and discharge. Because everything is packed so tightly—no room between presences, no empty niche, no wasted surface—every motion is transmitted through the mass like pressure through water. 

And because the scale is so vast, the plateau makes its own weather. From a high peak, you can see the curvature of the haze: a low, gleaming ceiling of frozen steam and ozone-blue mist hanging above the basins, lit from above by the merciless sun and from below by the faint, scattered flicker of millions of biological arcs. The light bounces between the ice-fog, the quartz ridges, and the polished metal backs of the herds until the whole world seems to glow from within—a freezing, deafening, brilliantly lit factory that nobody built, running on a clock that measures its shifts in years.
