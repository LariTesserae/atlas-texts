Below the flat, wind-scoured rim of the outer plain, the earth drops away along a clean, jagged fault—a wide, sunken basin thirty or forty meters deep, open to the sky. From above, it looks like a wound in the bedrock that has not yet had time to heal or soften. There is no soil horizon along the lip, no rounded gravel, no weathered talus slopes. Every cliff face, ledge, and fallen block is razor-edged and freshly cleaved, showing raw mineral grain—pale limestone striated with dark chert, and angular slabs of unpolished basalt whose fracture lines still look sharp enough to cut skin.

Yet the basin is not empty. It is packed, wall to wall and floor to terrace, with a dense, interlocking town.

Because the rift is wide and open above, normal daylight pours straight down into the depression, bright and unfiltered, casting hard, geometric shadows across the raw stone. The air down here is noticeably cooler than the plain above, holding a steady, cellar-like chill. Where the sun strikes the upper tiers of the east wall, the rock warms slightly; in the deep, narrow slots between buildings at the bottom, the cold lingers all day, drawing fine beads of condensation from the damp air onto the unweathered stone faces.

For a place so thoroughly occupied—every ledge, alcove, and patch of floor wedged tight with dwellings, workshops, and storage cubbies—it is remarkably quiet. There is no roar of industry, no clatter of carts. The streets are too narrow for wheels, often no wider than a pair of shoulders, and the town's builders work with a practiced, hushed rhythm. Tools are simple: wooden mauls wrapped in cured hide to dull the strike, copper-tipped wedges, bone chisels, and braided hemp slings. The loudest sounds are the soft, muffled *thud* of wood on wedge, the dry scrape of wicker baskets being hauled up timber scaffolding, and the steady, resonant *plip* of seep-water dropping from cold rock seams into slate catch-basins. Voices stay low, partly out of habit and partly because the packed mud-brick walls, hung reed mats, and tight alleys swallow echoes before they can travel.

Water here is precious and intermittent, gathered rather than flowing. There is no river, only the slow, persistent weeping of groundwater along the fresh bedding planes of the sunken walls, augmented by morning condensation and the occasional brief rain that turns the basin floor slick. Every damp seam has been claimed. Shallow channels are chipped directly into the raw rock to guide the trickle into communal stone troughs and plastered cisterns. Where water pools in the mortar joints or along the shaded base of retaining walls, the basin's sparse vegetation takes hold: thin, dark-green mats of liverwort, crustose lichens just beginning to speckle the fresh basalt, and small tufts of pale sedge. Here and there, on sunlit flat roofs, residents tend shallow wooden boxes of hardy, low-growing herbs, watered by hand from clay jars.

The architecture itself is layered and opportunistic, growing directly out of the emerging geology. Dwellings are built from the very stone pried out of the basin's expanding walls—angular, dry-stacked blocks chinked with lime-and-ash mortar, topped with flat roofs of packed earth and timber poles. Because space is saturated, roofs double as courtyards, drying racks, and thoroughfares, connected by steep stone stairs and lashed wooden ladders. There is almost no open ground left; even the central floor of the basin is a tight lattice of low workshops, mortar-mixing pits, and cistern covers, leaving only winding, shoulder-width lanes between them.

Everything is in quiet, continuous motion. Crews of stone-pryers slowly widen the lower terraces, driving wooden wedges into natural cracks and wetting them so the swelling timber splits the raw rock cleanly. Others haul baskets of damp rubble up to the rim or mix fresh lime in stone troughs. Above the packed roofs, a few rare swifts dart through the shaft of daylight, nesting in high, unreachable fissures in the cliff face, while an occasional pale rock-lizard suns itself on a freshly split slab before slipping into a crack.

The basin feels suspended in a state of active becoming—neither ancient nor finished. The rock has not yet learned to crumble into dirt, the mosses have barely begun to map the wettest stones, and the town itself is still wedging itself deeper into every newly opened seam, quiet and crowded under the open square of sky.
