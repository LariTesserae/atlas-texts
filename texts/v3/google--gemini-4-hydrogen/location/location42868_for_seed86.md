Far below the surface, the ground opens into a valley that never sees the sky. It is a vast, sunken rift-cavern, wide enough that the far walls dissolve into a flat, pewter haze. High overhead—hundreds of feet up, where the ceiling has split along old fault lines—narrow, crooked fissures admit a thin, diffused light. Whatever weather exists on the surface never reaches down here; the light is always dim, always the same flat grey, casting no sharp shadows and keeping no track of the hours.

The stone is young and unforgiving. The collapse that hollowed this place is recent enough in geological time that nothing has been rounded off. The floor is a wide, echoing expanse of bare bedrock, broken only at long intervals by jagged talus piles, fallen slabs the size of houses, and solitary, sharp-edged pillars. Between these features lie broad stretches of empty, angular stone. You can walk a long way across flat, fractured limestone without passing anything taller than your ankle, the air cool and completely still against your skin.

There are no rivers here, no wind, no seasons. The temperature holds in a steady, stone-chilled equilibrium. Moisture arrives only as a slow, patient condensation that beads along the coldest rock faces and gathers in shallow, mirror-flat pools inside natural basins. Away from these damp seeps, the rock is dry and clean. Plant life is sparse, clinging only to the places where a seep meets a faint shaft of grey light from the roof-slits: thin, pale ferns with translucent fronds, slick mats of dark moss, and low, dusty crusts of lichen.

Yet for all the emptiness of the stone, the valley is overwhelmingly alive—and deafeningly loud.

Every upper ledge, crevice, and ceiling pocket is packed with roosting bats and cave swiftlets, hanging in dense, shifting carpets that never quite settle. Beneath them, the sharp-edged talus is alive with pale cave crickets, harvestmen, and millipedes, crowding the dry cracks in bristling, twitching masses. Around the shallow mineral pools, slick-skinned salamanders and tiny, colorless crustaceans throng the water’s edge, while beetles and pale spiders work the damp guano mounds that accumulate below the roosts. The sheer volume of life presses in from every surface, filling the margins of the empty stone.

Because the valley is a vast, hard-walled chamber of sharp, unweathered rock, every sound is caught and multiplied. There is no soft earth or foliage to absorb it. The air vibrates with a constant, echoing roar: the high, metallic chittering of tens of thousands of birds and bats, the dry, percussive hiss of wingbeats, the endless *tick-tick-tick* of insects scraping against limestone, and the hollow, amplified *plink* of condensation dripping from high ledges into the pools below. The noise rises and folds back on itself, a continuous, inescapable wash of sound that never drops to a quiet hum.

Nowhere in the valley is there a wall, a hearth, a cairn, or any kind of shelter. Nothing has been built here. Yet scattered along the flat ledges beside the pools and beneath the swiftlet roosts are the unmistakable traces of simple, deliberate craft. Heavy cobbles, battered at the ends from use as hammerstones, rest beside piles of cracked snail shells. Freshly knapped flint flakes—still sharp-edged, unweathered as the rock around them—lie tucked into natural crevices alongside bone awls and small, hand-hammered copper hooks. 

They belong to foragers who move through this unchanging twilight without ever trying to alter it. In a place where the roof is already stone, the air is already cool, and nothing ever shifts, there is no reason to stack one rock upon another. They come only to harvest—knapping a blade, prying a nest from a ledge, cracking shells against the flat bedrock—and when they leave, they tuck their simple tools back into the sharp, damp dark, where the steady roar of the cavern closes over their tracks.
