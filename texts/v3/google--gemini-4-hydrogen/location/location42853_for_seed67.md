The horizon is a ruler-straight line of pale, warm haze, broken only by the low, repetitive silhouettes of cooling baffles and transformer housings stretching out for hundreds of miles. There is no bedrock here, no soil, no natural contour—nothing that was not cast, stamped, extruded, or poured. Even the elevation feels suppressed, a continent-spanning floor stepped down by mere inches across vast distances, holding the sky low and flat over a bright, diffused glare.

The light is even and white, like an overcast noon that never shifts toward evening. Above the endless plain, the atmosphere is thick with its own sluggish weather: a warm, breathless layer of condensing vapor that hangs just above the structures, neither clearing into blue nor darkening into storm. Everything is warm—not the scorching heat of a furnace, but the heavy, enveloping warmth of a running machine room, hovering steadily in the mid-thirties Celsius, saturated with moisture.

Everywhere, the world is sharp-edged and unweathered. The terrain consists of interlocking plates of dark cast iron, pale refractory ceramic, and poured polymer-concrete. The seams between slabs are crisp; the bevels on the drainage channels and the teeth of the expansion joints are as clean as the day they left their molds. There is no rust, no crumbling aggregate, no rounded corners. Yet nothing is dry. Because the air sits perpetually at the dew point, every surface slightly cooler than the mist weeps continuously. Water beads along the undersides of overhead busbars, gathers in fat, trembling rows along the ribs of heat exchangers, and falls in slow, steady curtains into the shallow basins below.

These basins cover most of the low plain—vast, flat settling pans holding two or three inches of warm, tea-colored water. The water does not ripple with wind; it moves only in a faint, glassy creep toward flush-mounted overflow grates, replenished at the exact same rate by condensation and the silent discharge of weeping manifolds. Nothing floods, and nothing dries out.

The industry here is total, yet strangely reticent. There are no smokestacks, no roaring turbines, no clanking conveyor lines. Instead, the plain is packed with low-profile, solid-state, and oil-cooled machinery: endless rows of squat, finned radiator banks, submerged copper-sheathed conduits, humming rectifier stations, and miles of perforated steel catwalks hovering a foot above the water. The power grid is live and dense—you can feel the sixty-cycle thrumming in your teeth and the soles of your boots, a pervasive, sub-audible vibration that replaces normal sound. Aside from the rare, soft *tink* of metal settling into thermal equilibrium and the steady, hush-like pattering of condensate dripping into the pans, the world is remarkably quiet.

There is no plant life. Not a blade of grass, not a cushion of moss, not even a mat of green algae softens the angles of the iron and concrete. The water holds only dissolved minerals and a faint, slick film of non-living chemical scale.

Yet the place is overwhelmingly, claustrophobically alive.

Every square inch of the built world is crowded with animals. They are packed so densely into the volume of the air, the water, and the structural crevices that the machinery often looks furry or swollen until you step closer and see the individual bodies. Because there are no plants, the fauna has grown directly onto the industrial substrate, feeding on mineral-rich condensate, stray electrical gradients, chemical slimes, and one another.

Along the warm, ribbed sides of the transformer housings, vast, flat molluscan sheets—pale grey and translucent amber, like slabs of wet felt—drape themselves over the metal fins, absorbing heat and rasping mineral crust with unseen radulae. Beneath the catwalks, dense, silent swarms of papery, winged things cling in thick, shivering blankets to the insulated power cables, their pale bodies vibrating faintly in time with the electromagnetic fields, never buzzing.

In the shallow basins, herds of spindly, six-limbed grazers—the size of herons, with matte-black, ceramic-hard carapaces that mimic the sharp geometry of the architecture—wade shoulder-to-shoulder through the warm water. They move in slow, deliberate processions, dipping needle-like proboscises into the submerged grates to siphon up soft, eyeless filter-feeders that choke every drainage slot in pulsing, slate-blue clusters. Even the grazers make no vocalizations; the only sound of a thousand-strong herd passing is the faint, dry *tick* of chitinous feet tapping against submerged iron plates.

There is a strange, ceremonial stillness to how all this life interacts. Predation happens without panic or chase. A tall grazer calmly spears a drifting mantle-slug and draws it up out of the water; a knot of pale sump-polyps slowly folds over a fallen flyer. Nothing flees, because there is nowhere different to go—no higher ground, no darker burrow, no season to wait out.

Time seems to have reached a flat, warm equilibrium here. The water stays at the exact same depth; the mist neither thickens nor burns away; the machines hum at a constant, unchanging load; and the immense, silent crowds of creatures shift over the sharp-edged metal like a living circuit, endlessly maintaining a balance that was finished long ago.
