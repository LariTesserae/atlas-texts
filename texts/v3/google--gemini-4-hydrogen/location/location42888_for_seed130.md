There is no dirt here, and no stone that was not poured, cast, or bolted into place. For miles in every direction, the ground is a single, continuous apron of ancient aggregate and pitted iron plating, laid down so long ago that the edges of the slabs have been rounded into smooth, shallow troughs. Rain and condensation do not soak in; they sheet across the worn depressions in a cool, ankle-deep film, migrating slowly toward the rusted grates of sunken culverts.

The sky—if there is one above the low, motionless ceiling of wet cloud—gives almost no light. Everything exists in a deep, greasy twilight, the color of wet slate and old oil. Distance is measured not by what you can see, but by how the darkness thickens. Far out across the flat, empty expanses, massive silhouettes interrupt the gloom: scattered, solitary structures spaced miles apart, like the pilings of a dismantled pier. Between them lies nothing but slick, empty pavement, shallow standing water, and the occasional sunken rail-line whose steel has been worn down flush with the concrete.

Before you ever reach one of these structures, you feel it in your teeth and your sternum. The air is deafening. It is not the sharp clamor of a busy yard, but a colossal, sustained, low-frequency roar—a physical pressure that flattens thought. Deep beneath the floor, and rising through the scattered housings, enormous turbines and hydraulic rams are still turning. They run in slow, heavy, overlapping cycles: a subterranean thrum that shakes the standing water into tight, geometric ripples, punctuated every forty seconds by the pneumatic shriek of a pressure-valve venting cold mist into the dark. 

Up close, the machines look half-melted into the ground. Centuries of damp and vibration have eroded their sharp angles; cast-iron casings are blistered with thick, black scale, and brass fittings have wept green stains down the concrete plinths. Yet inside the grated pits, behind cages of corroded wire, flywheels the size of houses continue to spin, lubricated by black, water-thinned grease that slicks the surrounding floor. A few dim, cold filaments—pale amber or sickly green—still burn behind thick, salt-crusted glass, casting small, wet halos onto the puddles below and briefly illuminating the drifting curtains of spray.

Nothing walks, crawls, or flies here. There are no birds in the rafters of the condenser towers, no insects skimming the black water, no rats in the dry conduits. The noise alone would shatter anything with delicate ears, and the cold is too steady, too damp. 

Yet the place is not entirely sterile. In the lee of the warm exhaust vents, and along the mineral-rich seams where the slabs have parted, sparse, strange vegetation has taken hold. These are not green plants; they are pale, leathery, almost translucent things—hollow-stemmed reeds and mats of rubbery, soot-colored lichen that feed on the sulfur in the runoff and the heat leaking from the buried pipes. They do not sway in the wind, for the air is largely still; instead, they shiver continuously in time with the bass vibration of the engines, their tips blurring in the dim light. 

The whole valley operates like a vast, forgotten organ that no one remembers how to turn off. Water condenses out of the cool air, sheets across the anciently flattened slabs, drains into the roaring subterranean intakes, is heated, pressurized, churned through the great iron housings, and vented back out as cold mist to repeat the cycle. The machines are slowly grinding themselves and their foundations into fine, metallic silt, settling deeper into their own vibrations, yet the rhythm holds—ancient, deafening, and entirely indifferent to the dark.
