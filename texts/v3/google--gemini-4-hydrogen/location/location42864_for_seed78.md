Above the cloud-floor, there is almost nothing to stand on except what grows, and what grows is stretched thin across miles of empty, freezing air.

The foundation of the world is young rock—black basalt and raw, unweathered slate thrust violently upward into razor-edged spires and jagged, fresh-fractured ridges. No rain has softened these peaks; no glaciers have rounded them. They rise out of abyssal drops like broken teeth, miles apart, their flanks sheer and sharp enough to slice leather. Yet almost nothing walks on the stone. The stone is only the anchor.

Between the distant peaks, a single, continent-spanning flora has woven itself into the sky. It is not a forest—there is no soil, no canopy, no crowded undergrowth—but an immense, sparse, hollow architecture of living tissue suspended over the void. Cables of dark, leathery wood, some as thick as a river and others no wider than a wrist, span the chasms in taut, catenary arcs. They braid together at the rock-spires, anchor themselves with pale, gripping tendrils into fresh cracks, and throw out secondary webs of fibrous, papery struts across the emptiness. If you look down between the strands, there is only black air dropping away for thousands of feet.

It is a cold, dark place. Light barely reaches here—only a dim, bruised indigo at the highest rim of the sky, and the occasional cold needle of starlight when the high mist tears open. The cold is sharp and persistent, the kind that numbs fingers within minutes, yet the landscape is not locked in dry ice. The giant spans are faintly warm from within, driven by slow, fermenting metabolic heat deep inside their hollow cores. Where that slight warmth meets the freezing altitude, the world turns perpetually damp. Condensation beads on every waxy, charcoal-colored surface. Windward edges grow delicate, brittle crusts of rime ice, while the sheltered undersides of the cables weep a slow, steady trickle of cold, tannin-dark water into sagging pockets of matted fiber.

Because the space is so vast and the vegetation so taut and hollow, the place is extraordinarily loud.

High-altitude weather builds across the emptiness—sudden, sweeping gales, freezing sleet-squalls, and dense banks of mist that roll between the spires like slow avalanches. As the wind drives through the lattice, the world becomes an instrument. Air screams over the unweathered knife-edges of the basalt. Miles-long root-cables thrum in deep, chest-rattling bass notes that travel straight up through the soles of your boots. Hanging beneath the main spans are colossal, deflated-looking bladders—thin-walled, papery organs that inflate and collapse with barometric shifts, booming like distant drums when gusts strike them. Even in a lull, the plants themselves are never still: they groan under shifting ice-loads, snap their frost-crusts, and hiss as valve-like pores open along the stems to drink the passing mist.

Life here clings to the strands and rides the updrafts between them. In the near-total darkness, fauna is abundant but clustered, moving along the living bridges like fleas on a wire. Heavy, long-limbed browsers with hooked, calloused pads creep silently along the undersides of the cables, scraping off the pale, faintly luminescent fungi that thrive in the damp joints. Gliders with wide, leathery membranes launch themselves from the high spires into the black void, riding the roaring thermals and navigating entirely by sound—emitting sharp, percussive tongue-clicks that bounce off the sharp rock faces and the taut cables. Swarms of damp-winged, chittering insects bore into the softer pith-nodes to lay their eggs in the warm, fermenting dark, drawing sleek, blind predators that hunt by pressing their jaws to the wood to feel the faintest vibration of footsteps a quarter-mile away.

Human presence—or whatever builds here—is small, scattered, and precarious. Every few miles, tucked into the lee of a major cable-junction or wedged into the dry cleft of a basalt spire, sit tiny, lashed structures. They are built from what the place offers: frames of stripped frond-ribs bound with braided bast-cord, roofs of resin-stiffened bark, and windbreaks of slate shards chipped directly from the young peaks. There is no metal to speak of—only knapped stone blades, bone needles, wooden toggles, and simple clay or hollowed-stone braziers. Inside those shelters, tiny, carefully tended embers of dried, oily pith-cakes burn with a low, smoky orange glow—fragile sparks separated by miles of howling, damp, living emptiness.

To travel here is to move by touch and ear along narrow, vibrating bridges in the dark, listening to the pitch of the wind in the cables to judge the coming storm, and feeling the vast, hollow world breathe and creak beneath your feet.
