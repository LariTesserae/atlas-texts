The first thing you notice is the clarity, and the second is the noise.

You are submerged in a cool, pale-blue expanse so pure it feels less like swimming in an ocean and more like hanging suspended in a vast block of optical glass. Light pours down from above—clean, even, unmuddied daylight, as if the sky just beyond the surface is a bright, high-altitude overcast. There is no silt, no suspended grit, no drifting cloud of plankton to scatter the beams. You can see for kilometers in every direction, and for kilometers there is nothing to see: just empty, illuminated water, cool against the skin, tasting faintly of ozone and cold iron.

Yet the emptiness is deafening. 

Sound travels effortlessly here, and the world is in violent motion. A low, continuous roar fills the water—the sound of colossal, invisible rivers slamming into one another in the open void. Every few minutes, a deep, concussive *boom* rolls through your chest, sharp as a thunderclap, where a shearing current tears itself apart and a pocket of cavitation collapses miles away. Beneath the roar of the water, there is a slower, metallic groaning that vibrates through the soles of your feet even when you aren't touching the bottom: the sound of stone being pushed upward from below.

Despite being underwater, you do not feel the crushing weight of an abyss. The pressure is oddly light, thin and brisk, like standing on a high mountain pass. You are, in fact, high up—miles above the planet's true crust, suspended in a continental sea that rests atop an elevated, freshly extruded plateau. If you look up, the surface is a restless, silvered ceiling churning with whitecaps and violent troughs, yet it feels strangely close, and the light breaking through it is sharp and cool.

Far below, emerging from the floor of this empty sea, the geology is raw and unfinished. There are no sandy plains, no rounded pebbles, no soft silt beds. The bottom is a stark, jagged expanse of black basalt and pale, glassy silica, fractured into razor-edged ridges and solitary needle-spires that rise like broken teeth into the bright water. Because the world here is still coming into being, the stone has not yet learned to wear away. Edges that should have been smoothed by a century of surf remain surgically sharp. In places, pale mineral plumes seep from cracks in the dark rock, solidifying instantly into brittle, crystalline fins that catch the daylight.

There is something quietly wrong with how the water treats the stone. Currents strong enough to snap a ship's mast tear past these fragile spires at sixty knots, yet the rock does not crumble. At the very boundary where water meets mineral, the turbulence seems to lose its teeth—slipping past the raw edges without grinding them down, as though an invisible, frictionless skin separates the storm from the emerging earth. 

There are no machines here. No pipes, no pylons, no sunken ruins, no hulls. Nothing has been built. Yet the physics of the place feels unmistakably *held*. Tiny bubbles knocked loose from the churning surface do not always rise straight up; some catch on unseen lines of force, drifting sideways in crisp, parallel ranks before snapping out of existence with a faint *ping*. The water itself seems subtly organized even in its chaos—vast, continent-spanning currents wheeling around one another in turbulent, self-correcting gyres, held aloft on this high plateau by a planetary-scale field that no visible engine generates. It is an ocean under construction, or an ocean being kept in a state of suspended birth.

Nothing swims here. There are no fish to dart among the silica needles, no crabs scuttling in the crevices, no whales groaning in the distance, no gulls wheeling above the silvered surface. The silence of animal life makes the mechanical roar of the water feel even larger.

The only living things are sparse, stubborn pioneers. Clinging to the leeward flanks of the tallest basalt spires—and occasionally torn loose to drift for miles through the empty blue—are long, pale-green and translucent ribbons of hardy kelp-like weed. They have no roots to speak of, only glassy holdfasts fused directly to the raw stone. In the violent currents, they stream out horizontally like tattered white flags, whipping and snapping in the bright, cold flood.

Out in the open water, miles from any spire, the scale of the place becomes dizzying. The turbulence here is its own weather: towering, slow-motion maelstroms that span leagues, twisting the daylight into vast, geometric caustics that sweep across the empty void like searchlights. Cold fronts of denser, even chillier water collide with warmer upwellings, creating shimmering, refractive walls that distort the horizon before collapsing into roaring, chaotic eddies. You hang weightless in the middle of it—chilled, deafened, surrounded by miles of blindingly clear nothing, watching a brand-new world churn itself into shape.
