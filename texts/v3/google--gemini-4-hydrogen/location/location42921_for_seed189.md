There is no soil here, and no stone. You are deep beneath the surface, sealed inside a pocket carved not out of earth, but out of living wood and pith—a single, newly ruptured blister tucked somewhere inside a root-system so vast it has replaced the geology around it.

The chamber is small enough to feel intimate, an irregular oval perhaps a few strides across, yet the air inside it is startlingly empty. Nothing hangs from the ceiling. No tangled vines choke the middle. The interior volume is a clean, hollow void, stripped of clutter, so that the few things present—a stray drifting spore, a single taut thread of spider-silk or phloem—stand out across what feels like an immense, aching distance. When you reach toward the far wall, the space between your hand and the bark seems to stretch and thin, as if the pocket contains more emptiness than its measurements allow.

Everything that bounds this emptiness is plant. The floor is a springy, pale mat of compressed cambium, slightly giving underfoot like a drumhead. The walls curve inward in sharp, freshly torn ridges—raw, unweathered splinters of translucent heartwood and wet, fibrous bast. It is a young wound. Sticky beads of clear resin still well along the jagged seams where the cavity recently split open under its own internal pressure.

Despite being buried lightless fathoms down, the room is filled with ordinary, bright afternoon light. It does not glow from the walls like bioluminescence, nor does it beam from a crack above. It simply *is* there—warm, crisp, shadow-sharp daylight suspended in the hollow air, illuminating every microscopic root-hair and glistening film of dampness. Only the shadows give it away: they fall slightly upward, or gather in tight, dark pools beneath things that aren’t blocking any visible ray, as if the light is leaking out of the plant’s memory of a sky it touched decades ago.

The air is warm and close, carrying the sharp, green tang of crushed stems and wet cedar. Moisture arrives in fits and starts. There is no rain, no stream, only a slow, cycling sweat that beads along the upper ridges of the pith. Every few minutes, the walls visibly swell as a surge of sap passes through the outer bark, squeezing the condensation loose. A single heavy drop detaches and falls through the bright void.

When it strikes the taut membrane of the floor, the sound is deafening.

The entire pocket acts as a resonant chamber, a hollow botanical instrument tuned to an uncomfortable volume. The drip lands like a mallet on wet hide. Beneath it, the room is never quiet: there is the constant, hydraulic groan of water being hauled upward through unseen xylem tubes, the sudden, pistol-sharp *crack* of fresh wood fibers parting as the chamber slowly expands, and a low, vibrating hum that you feel in your teeth—the sound of a massive biological engine running at full speed just behind a thin wall of bark.

In the damp, bright quiet between cracks, you can see the faintest evidence that something with hands has been here, or lives here still. There is no shelter, no built thing, no stacked wood or woven roof—the plant already *is* the enclosure. Instead, there is only raw, manual intervention: a chipped flake of pale stone wedged tightly into a weeping seam to pry it open; three lengths of stripped, knotted bast-fiber tied around a bulging root-node, funneling the intermittent condensation down into a shallow basin scraped directly into the living floor. The basin holds a finger’s depth of lukewarm, slightly sweet water.

If you watch the water long enough, you notice the reality of the place slipping its gears. Ripples move across the basin *before* a drop falls from the ceiling. The torn edge of a splintered root slowly heals backward, un-splitting itself for a few seconds before snapping open again with a sharp bang, caught in a stutter of growth. Near the rim of the scraped basin, a single creature rests—pale, blind, segmented like a woodlouse the size of a thumb, its carapace thin enough to show the bright daylight passing straight through its gut. It does not flee; it simply waits for the next sap-tide, motionless in the roar of the growing walls.

This is a place that shouldn't have room to exist, and knows it. It is a temporary lung, freshly torn open in the dark, flooded with stolen noon and loud with the frantic, unspooling pulse of a plant that has forgotten the difference between root and sky.
