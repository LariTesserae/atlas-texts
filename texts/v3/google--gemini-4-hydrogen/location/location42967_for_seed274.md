The air hits you first: warm, thick, and completely still, smelling of wet limestone, iron, and the faint, clean staleness of water that has been sitting in the dark for a long time. 

It is not a large place. From one side to the other, it is no wider than a small courtyard or the nave of a modest chapel, and the ceiling hangs low enough that in places you have to duck. Yet there is nowhere to stand back and take it all in at once. The entire hollow is choked with stone. Thick, fluted columns of pale-brown and cream travertine rise from the floor to fuse with the roof; curtains of rippled flowstone hang down like heavy, wet hides; and between them, bulbous stalagmites crowd so close together that a person has to turn sideways to slip between their slick, rounded flanks. There is no empty ground. Where the formations don't touch, the space between them is filled by stepped, crescent-shaped basins of mineral-rimmed water, nested one against another like scales.

Light comes from a single, narrow, crooked cleft high up where the hillside above has slumped and wedged itself shut with boulders. Only a dim, grey-green twilight filters down through the chink—just enough to catch the wet sheen on the nearest pillars and turn the surfaces of the pools into dull pewter. Deeper under the overhangs, the light dies out into soft, warm shadow. Because the cleft is so choked with wedged rock and tree-roots from the unseen surface far above, the sun never reaches in directly; the brightness barely shifts from noon to dusk, holding the hollow in a perpetual, muted late-afternoon gloom.

Everything is wet. Not rained-on, and not flooded by any rushing current, but saturated from within. Warm water—near body temperature, fed from deep fractures in the low-lying rock—seeps continuously through the porous limestone walls and drips from the tips of a thousand mineral icicles. Every surface glistens with a thin, unbroken film of condensation. The rimstone pools are brim-full, their surfaces taut and glassy, overflowing so slowly over their scalloped lips that the movement is only visible as a faint, trembling distortion in the reflection of the ceiling.

In this warmth and wet, no true plants grow. There are no ferns, no mosses, no roots dangling from the cleft—only the pale, velvety crusts of mineral-tolerant molds and thin, slick biofilms of sulfur-yellow and rust-orange that coat the submerged ledges like paint. The rock itself is old and settled into its shapes; the sharp edges of ancient fractures have been rounded off by millennia of lime-heavy water, leaving everything smooth, bulbous, and heavy-looking, as if the stone were made of cooled wax.

The silence is close and muffled. Because the chamber is small and packed with stone baffles, sounds do not echo; they simply drop dead into the humid air. There is no wind, no rustle of leaves, no distant roar of a stream. Only the slow, irregular *plip* of heavy drops falling from the ceiling into the full basins, and the nearly inaudible, glassy whisper of water creeping over a stone lip. 

Life here is scarce and unhurried. If you hold still by the edge of a warm pool for long enough, you might spot a single, pale amphipod—no bigger than a grain of rice—sculling lazily near the bottom, or a tiny, translucent snail glued to the underside of a submerged rim. Once in a great while, a blind, slate-colored salamander rests half-submerged on a slick shelf, its throat pulsing so slowly you might mistake it for another lump of wet calcite.

People have been here, though not often, and not recently. Their marks are few and small, wedged into the tight geography without trying to change it. Along the narrowest passage between two fused columns, someone long ago pecked shallow footholds into the slick travertine with a stone chisel or a bronze point; the edges of the notches are now glazed smooth under a fresh millimeter of pale mineral crust. On a dryish ledge tucked high under an overhang, three small, thick-walled bowls of unglazed red clay sit in a row, placed there to catch a slow, steady drip from a stalactite above. One has tipped and cracked; the other two are brim-full and rimmed with white salt. Nearby, the ceiling above a flat stone shelf is faintly smudged with old soot—the ghost of tallow lamps held too close—and a notched rib-bone, darkened and heavy with absorbed lime, lies half-embedded in the flowstone floor.

Nothing here hurries, and nothing seems to wait. The warmth of the rock balances the cool seeping down from the choked cleft above; the water enters at the exact pace it drains away through invisible cracks in the basin floors. Tomorrow, and a year from now, the same drop will gather at the tip of the same stalactite, hang heavy in the dim, warm air, and fall into the same still pool.
