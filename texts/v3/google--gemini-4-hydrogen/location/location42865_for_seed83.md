There is no sky here, and almost no light. To know the vault, you have to read it with your skin.

You are deep beneath the surface, inside a bounded, cathedral-sized chamber built entirely of vitrified brick, glazed ceramic tile, and riveted iron. No raw stone shows anywhere; if there was ever a cavern wall, it was sealed away generations ago behind three-foot-thick masonry arches and cast-iron lagging. What remains is a dense, suffocatingly packed volume—a three-dimensional labyrinth of infrastructure jammed so tightly together that you can rarely stand upright or walk three paces in a straight line. 

The air is fiercely hot, hovering just below the threshold where breathing hurts. It doesn’t blow; it simply sits, heavy and dry against your face, radiating off banks of bundled copper pipes, massive cylindrical pressure-vessels, and tiered iron catwalks that brush your shoulders as you squeeze past. Without light—save for the occasional, almost imperceptible cherry-red smudge behind a thick, soot-caked mica sight-glass—you navigate by thermal shadow. A scorching manifold warns you back an inch before your knuckles graze it; a slightly cooler brick pier offers a brief, rough-textured anchor in the dark.

Despite the heat, the vault is not entirely dry. Along the upper tiers, a few uninsulated return-lines carry something cooler—or at least less fiercely hot—and the temperature differential sweats moisture out of the stagnant air. It gathers in beads along verdigris-crusted brass elbows and drips, with a patient, echoing *tink*, into shallow ceramic gutters and mineral-rimmed catch-basins below. Where these drips hit, the air turns muggy and smells of hot iron, damp clay, and a faint, sweetish tang like scorched almonds.

Time here has settled into a long, balanced weariness. Nothing is new, and nothing is actively crumbling. The ironwork has passed beyond flaking rust into a smooth, velvety skin of dark magnetite. Along the brick seams, centuries of slow seepage have dissolved and re-deposited the mortar into pale, waxy curtains of travertine that hang like frozen tallow between the pipes. Even the catwalk gratings have been worn smooth in narrow tracks, though no one has walked them in a long while.

The place is mostly quiet, wrapped in a thick, muffled hush. There is no roar of engines, only a deep, sub-audible thrumming you feel in your teeth—the pulse of something massive and distant moving through the floor. Every few minutes, a pipe *pings* as it shifts a fraction of a millimeter in its brackets. Somewhere in the center of the clutter, a single vertical piston-rod—thick as a tree trunk and slick with dark oil—rises at a glacial pace, perhaps an inch every quarter-hour. When it reaches the top of its stroke, a weighted brass lever-arm high in the dark tilts, a hidden valve sighs a brief, soft plume of vapor, and the rod begins its slow descent. What it is equalizing, or what it powers, is impossible to tell; its linkages disappear into the bricked-up floor and ceiling, locked in an endless, self-contained equilibrium.

Life here is sparse, clinging strictly to the damp, slightly cooler pockets around the condensation troughs. Pale, threadlike roots and mats of velvety black moss wedge themselves into the salt-crusted mortar where the drips fall. In the dark, if you hold your hand still near a wet flange, you might feel the slow scrape of a blind, porcelain-shelled snail grazing the mineral rime, or hear the single, dry click of a pale, heat-tolerant cricket that chirps once every few minutes, as if keeping time with the piston. 

You cannot see the ceiling, only feel the weight of the heat pressing down from it. Every gap is filled with another layer of valves, counterweights, and ceramic insulators, all resting in the dark, ticking softly, waiting out a cycle that no longer seems to need anyone to watch it.
