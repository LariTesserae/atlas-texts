The basin sits high enough that the world beyond its rim is mostly thin air and cold blue distance, yet inside its walls the air is thick, warm, and heavy as a wet wool coat. You can cross the whole place in four minutes of careful walking. It is no larger than a village square or the nave of a great church, cupped inside a ring of sheer, black-glass cliffs that rise like snapped fingernails.

Nothing here has been worn down. The rock is brand new—extruded, not weathered. The ledges and stepped terraces of the floor meet at razor-sharp ninety-degree angles, their surfaces still bearing the fine, concentric ripples of cooling mineral melt. No frost has cracked them; no stream has rounded a single edge. When water sheets down the cliff faces, it does not follow gullies, because there are none. It simply slides in wide, unbroken curtains over the glassy obsidian-dark stone, gathering in flat, shallow pans that reflect the dim, bruised-gray sky like oiled mirrors.

The light is always low, filtered through a permanent, low-hanging lid of warm vapor that clings to the rim of the basin. Looking up, you do not see clouds moving; you see a ceiling of mist that slowly condenses, thickens until it sags like wet canvas, and then lets go in sudden, warm downpours that last ten minutes and stop as abruptly as a shut tap. Just beyond the cliff-rim, the mountain wind howls past in freezing gusts, but it cannot get in. An invisible meniscus—the edge of whatever keeps this pocket warm and wet—seals the top of the basin. When rain hits that upper boundary from the outside, it freezes into sleet and slides off the dome; inside, the air stays breathless and muggy, smelling of hot slate, ozone, and crushed fern.

Scattered across the raw, empty expanses of the basin floor are seven or eight objects that clearly do not belong to geology, though they seem to be what the geology is growing around. They are not ruins—nothing here is old enough to be a ruin—nor are they buildings you could walk inside. One is a pale, matte-white pillar, thick as a tree trunk, tilted at an impossible forty-degree angle without any visible brace, its base seamlessly fused into the black rock as if the stone were poured around it while still liquid. Another is a low, skeletal lattice of dull bronze rods hovering three inches above a steaming pool, rotating so slowly you only notice it has moved when you look away and look back. 

There is no hum of engines, no blinking lights, yet the air around these scattered artifacts feels dense and slightly greasy, like the space between two strong magnets. Near the tilted white pillar, the sheeting rainwater refuses to puddle; it curves gently *upward* along the stone, creeping in thin, silver ribbons toward a fist-sized seam in the pillar’s side, where it vanishes with a faint, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* like a slow heartbeat. Elsewhere, a flat disk of dark, grainless ceramic sits flush with the terrace floor, and every few hours it shudders, extruding another millimeter of fresh, hot, iridescent slag that creeps outward and cools into a new step of stone. The basin is still being built, slowly, from the center out.

Because the rock is so young and bare, almost nothing green grows here. Vegetation is a scarce, hesitant thing—mostly pale, rubbery lichens that cling to the vertical seams where mineral dust has caught, and a few sparse clusters of wire-thin, leafless stalks that sprout from the cracks around the bronze lattice, swaying slightly even when the air is dead still. They look less like plants than like copper filaments, and they are slick to the touch, coated in a clear, sugary mucus.

What *does* thrive here—loudly, violently, and in startling numbers—is the animal life. 

The empty spaces between the sparse plants and the scattered machines are packed with motion and noise. Because the unweathered glass-rock walls are hard and smooth, every sound bounces and multiplies until the small basin rings like the inside of a struck bell. The chorus is deafening: a constant, overlapping racket of wet clicks, low booming croaks, and high, metallic rasps.

Swarms of palm-sized, translucent things—half-way between tree frogs and armored beetles, with long, suction-tipped toes—cover the weeping cliff faces. They move in sudden, synchronized bursts, racing sideways across the vertical glass to snatch the tiny, pale midges that breed in the warm mist. Their throat-sacs inflate like oiled bladders, producing a resonant, percussive *clack* that echoes off the opposite wall a fraction of a second later. Down in the shallow, warm pans of water, sleek, eyeless predators the length of your forearm—segmented, pale gray, and propelled by undulating ribbon-fins—hunt by listening to the ripples. When a frog-beetle slips on the slick stone and splashes, three or four of the pale swimmers converge at once, thrashing the mirror-flat water into foam before dragging the prey under.

Above them all, gliding through the dim, misty air between the cliffs, are the canopy-drifters: wide, flat, papery creatures shaped like torn kites, with no visible wings—only fringed, muscular edges that ripple continuously to keep them aloft in the heavy air. They never land on the sharp, raw rock. Instead, they hang suspended near the warm vapor ceiling, trailing long, sticky threads down into the mist to snag jumping insects, or occasionally swooping low to skim the surface of the pools, letting out a long, mournful, foghorn-like groan that vibrates in your teeth.

The whole basin runs on a tight, frantic loop. A machine-seam draws in mist and sweats warm water; the water feeds the mineral-slime and the sparse, wire-like stalks; the midges cloud up from the slime; the frog-beetles clatter down the cliffs to eat the midges; the blind swimmers churn the pools for the frog-beetles; and the kite-things groan overhead, waiting for anything that rises too high. Every twenty minutes, the ceiling of vapor grows too heavy, drops a warm, roaring curtain of rain that drowns out even the animals for a brief spell, and then clears back to the same dim, steaming hush—until the first sharp *clack* echoes off the fresh black glass, and the noise starts all over again.
