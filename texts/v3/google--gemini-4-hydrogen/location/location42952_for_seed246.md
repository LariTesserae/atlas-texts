The first thing that hits you is the noise—a massive, hollow, organ-pipe roar that seems to come from beneath your boots as much as from the sky.

You are standing at the rim of a shallow, sun-bright basin, no wider than a small village, sunk into the flattened, slumped crown of a mountain range so old its peaks have lost all memory of jaggedness. Around the horizon, the world is nothing but smooth, grey-white knuckles of bedrock, planed down by eons of ice and wind until the ridges look like the worn-down molars of a buried giant. The air is thin, clear, and bitterly cold; your breath turns to white smoke and is instantly torn away.

Inside this bounded hollow sits a town—or the wind-polished skeleton of one.

It is an unmistakably urban place, layered and deliberate: stepped terraces, narrow, sunken alleys, bridged walkways, and stacked, multi-story chambers rising in clustered tiers from the basin floor. Yet there is not a single cut edge, not a drop of mortar, not a scrap of metal or timber anywhere in it. Every wall is built of dry-stacked slate plates and water-rounded cobbles, wedged together by sheer, patient geometry and bound in place by thick, woody mats of ancient root-fiber. The stairs have been dished into smooth, undulating ramps by time and frost; the archways are worn thin as rib-bones; the flagstones of the central plaza are furrowed into glassy, pale channels.

For all its layered construction, the place feels startlingly empty. Wide expanses of bare, swept stone separate the clusters of towers. Most of the stacked chambers are hollow shells, open to the sky or perforated by smooth, wind-bored apertures, so that you can look straight through three walls at once and see only the bright, empty daylight on the other side.

That hollowness is the engine of the roar. The high-altitude wind sweeps over the worn mountain rim, drops into the bowl, and drives straight into the town’s baffled alleys and chimney-like towers. The entire settlement acts as a vast, un-tuned acoustic instrument. A constant, deafening low-pitched thrumming vibrates through the stone, rising into a brassy, multi-toned howl whenever a gust shifts across the upper terraces.

Despite the biting cold and the lack of rivers or rain, the town is not dry, and it is not dead. A thin, bright sun glares off the pale stone, warming the south-facing walls just enough to wake the moisture trapped in the rock. From the shadowed eaves and the deep seams between the cobbles, condensation beads, gathers, and drips—a restless, intermittent dampness that keeps the lower tiers slick and dark.

Along these wet seams, the vegetation is shockingly lush. Thick, heavy pelts of emerald and bruised-violet cushion-moss upholster the sunken streets, swollen with trapped meltwater. Curtains of pale-green, frost-skinned beard-lichen—some as thick as a forearm and ten feet long—hang from the bridged walkways, clattering like dry bone when the wind whips them. Fleshy, translucent rosettes of alpine bladder-wort crowd the drainage channels. They are strange, hydraulic things: as the sun warms the damp stone, the plants drink the condensation, swelling visibly until their turgid, ice-sheathed bladders hiss and pop, venting tiny plumes of cold vapor into the roaring air.

Everything here is in rapid, noisy motion. A cloud shadow slides across the basin, the temperature drops three degrees in as many seconds, and the trickling meltwater flash-freezes inside the porous stone. Sharp, rifle-crack fractures echo off the empty plazas. Sheets of rime-ice delaminate from the moss-curtains and shatter on the flagstones below. A moment later, the sun breaks back through, and the dripping begins again.

In the wide, swept spaces between the buildings, almost nothing moves. Animals are rare enough that you could watch the basin for hours and see nothing larger than a frost-numbed midge clinging to the underside of a lichen-frond. Only occasionally, during a lull between the loudest gusts, might you catch sight of a pale, thick-fleeced creature—heavy-shouldered and silent, with broad, split hooves suited to the glassy stone—stepping delicately out of a hollow archway to crop the swollen bladder-wort, before vanishing back into the deafening, sun-lit maze of the empty town.
