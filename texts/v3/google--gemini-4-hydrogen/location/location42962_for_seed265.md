There is no light here. Not dimness, not the gray smudge of a clouded moon, not even the brief orange speck of a spark. Light simply does not propagate in this basin. If you strike flint against steel, your knuckles feel the sharp jar and a sudden pinprick of heat, and the air smells briefly of scorched iron, but nothing shines. If an electrical arc jumps a gap between two copper busbars, it arrives only as a dry *snap*, a gust of ozone, and a sudden bloom of warmth against your cheek.

You stand in a vast, low-lying valley, open for miles in every direction, though distance is measured only by how long it takes a sound to die or how long you must wade before the terrain changes. Everything is warm—not baking, just a steady, enveloping, body-temperature dampness that clings to the skin. The air is saturated, right on the edge of raining without ever quite committing to a downpour. Moisture condenses directly onto every exposed surface: your eyelids, the hairs on your arms, the cold-drawn copper of cables, the sharp edges of the stone.

The ground beneath the water is young and cruel. It is a recently fractured basin of black flint, vitreous slate, and raw, unweathered basalt. Nothing has had time to round off. Beneath the ankle-deep, tepid water, the rock juts up in razor-edged shelves and flat cleavage planes, slick with a thin film of mineral oil and bacterial slime. Walk carelessly and the stone slices straight through boot-leather. The water itself is slow, shallow, and faintly viscous, pooled across miles of flat marsh-floor, weeping continuously from horizontal cracks in the low ridges and dripping back down from the heavy air.

Despite the openness of the valley, the space around you is crowded. It is a dense, layered world where too many things occupy the same cubic meters at once. Reach out a hand in the dark and your fingers brush the wet, knife-sharp edge of a basalt fin—and, nested in the exact same volume of air, slightly out of phase, you feel the warm, ribbed ceramic of an insulator, and the dry, papery rustle of a reed that isn't growing out of the rock so much as *through* it. Reality here has a sluggish, overlapping grain. Objects share space without quite colliding, like pages of wet vellum pressed together. You can walk ten paces across flat water and pass through three different shorelines that haven't settled on which one is currently there.

Scattered sparsely across this dark, flooded plain are the machines. They are industrial, heavy, and built to a massive scale, yet they sit miles apart in the dark, connected only by sagging catenaries of thick, rubber-wrapped cable strung from crooked iron stanchions. You find them first by their heat and their smell—hot grease, wet rust, ozone, and boiled mineral salts—and by a low, sub-audible thrumming that you feel in your molars long before your ears register it.

These are patient engines. A cast-iron flywheel the size of a barn turns in a sunken concrete pit, completing a single revolution every twenty minutes. Its gear teeth are coated in pale tallow; as it turns, it makes only a soft, wet *shhhk* against the condensing air. Beside it, banks of tallow-filled transformers hum at a frequency so low it feels like a slow tide pulling at your ribs. Long-stroke beam pumps dip into black, rectangular vats of brine, lifting and lowering with geological indifference. Somewhere out in the dark, a mile away across the flat water, a heavy relay clicks shut—*tock*—and silence settles back over the marsh for another quarter of an hour.

It is mostly quiet. There is no roar of furnaces, no clatter of assembly lines. The soundscape is an endless, hushed tapestry of water dripping from steel onto water, the soft *sluck* of mud settling, and the faint, continuous hiss of high-voltage current bleeding silently into the humid air.

Life here has abandoned the eye and packed itself tightly into the dark. Vegetation is sparse—only what can survive without sun on sharp, young rock and warm, brackish seep. Along the warm flanks of the pump-houses and clustered around the base of energized stanchions, pale, rootless mats of fungal felt soak up the stray heat. Here and there, brittle, hollow stalks of translucent silica-weed grow out of the shallow water, clicking faintly like glass wind-chimes when something brushes past them.

The fauna, however, is abundant—almost suffocatingly so. The warm, wet dark teems with blind, tactile, electro-receptive things that crowd every layer of the valley.

In the water around your shins, long, segmented filter-feeders cling to the razor-sharp basalt edges, their soft, leathery gills fanning the oily current. They brush against your ankles—dozens of them per square meter, cool and slick and entirely unafraid. Above the surface, the air is thick with silent, velvet-winged flyers the size of your palm. They navigate by the warmth of skin and the static charge of the power lines, streaming past your face in dense, near-touching ribbons without ever making a sound, leaving only a faint, musky scent of damp fur and a tickle of wingtips against your forehead.

Larger things wade through the basin on tall, stilt-like legs tipped with sensitive, whisker-fringed pads. You hear them before you feel them: a slow, deliberate *plip... plip...* as they step carefully between the submerged flint blades and the humming submerged cables. They seem to perceive the stacked layers of the valley better than you do; sometimes you feel the warm displacement of air as one passes within inches of your chest, even though your outstretched hand meets only the flat, weeping face of a slate ridge.

The rules of cause and effect are as slow and frayed as the terrain. A stone dropped into a brine vat splashes three seconds before your hand lets go of it. A sudden gust of warm wind smells of scorched copper twenty minutes before a distant breaker trips. If you stand still long enough in the dark, listening to the patient *drip-drip* of the air and the glacial turning of an unseen flywheel, you begin to lose track of which layer of the valley your feet are standing in—whether the warmth against your calves is the shallow marsh-water, the exhaust of a buried condenser, or the flank of some vast, blind wader resting motionless in the same space as you, waiting for the next slow cycle to turn.
