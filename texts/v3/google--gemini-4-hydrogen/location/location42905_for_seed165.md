The place sits so high that the sky has lost its color. Overhead, it is a bruised, slate-grey smear—neither night nor day, just a permanent, dim twilight that casts no sharp shadows. At this altitude, the air should be too thin to carry much of anything, yet the valley is deafening.

It is not the sound of wind. Nothing here moves enough to make wind. The air is dead still, cold enough to numb exposed skin in minutes, and locked in a flat, unwavering equilibrium. Yet the entire basin rings with a continuous, teeth-rattling roar, like the sustained chord of a struck iron plate the size of a mountain range, layered under a dry, static hiss. You cannot tell where it comes from. If you press your palms over your ears, the hiss fades, and the low chord only grows louder, vibrating up through the soles of your feet and the cartilage of your ribs.

Below the dim sky, the valley opens out—a few miles across from rim to rim, though the distances feel unreliable. The terrain is mostly *empty*. Rather than a solid floor, the basin is a hollowed-out bowl spanned by a few immense, weathered ribs of pale grey stone. They arch across a drop that disappears into a cold, featureless mist below, leaving vast volumes of empty air between one ridge and the next. Everything here is eroded to a settled, ancient smoothness: no jagged scree, no fresh fractures, no crumbling ledges. The stone looks as though it finished wearing down ten thousand years ago and simply stopped.

Yet the rock is damp. A thin, slick film of supercooled moisture coats the stone surfaces, never freezing into proper ice and never evaporating. In the shallow depressions along the stone ribs, dark pockets of water gather. They do not sit level. A pool in a flat basin might tilt at twenty degrees, its surface held taut and glassy against an invisible incline, reflecting a sky that does not match the one above—a sky with too many stars, or the faint, bleached smear of a sun that isn't there. When mist gathers in the hollows between the stone bridges, it hangs in motionless, wet sheets, heavy enough to soak wool on contact, never drifting with the hours.

Because nothing changes, the sparse life here has adapted to an eternal present. Clinging to the undersides of the stone arches—where the dampness beads thickest—grow patches of pale, rubbery lichen and clusters of translucent, hollow stalks no thicker than quills. They do not sway. They do not seem to grow, at least not on any scale a visitor could witness. They simply occupy their corners of the wet stone, drinking the cold condensation.

Fauna is rare, and easy to miss against the vastness of the hollow air. Now and then, a creature the size of a hound—flat-bodied, covered in overlapping plates of dull, slate-colored chitin—can be found pressed motionless into a damp crevice. They have no eyes and no ears; instead, long, fleshy filaments sprout from their flanks, trembling slightly in response to the constant roar. When they move, it is with agonizing, deliberate slowness, feeling their way along the ribs of stone by reading the standing waves of the sound. Above the chasms, translucent, four-winged things like pale moths sometimes hang suspended in mid-air, held aloft not by flapping, but by the sheer acoustic pressure of the valley, vibrating in place like dust motes caught in a beam of light.

Here and there, along the broader stone bridges, are the only signs that anything ever passed through with intent: small, scattered cairns of river-smooth stones, balanced three or four high without mortar. No tools were used to shape them; no metal, wood, or worked bone lies anywhere nearby. In a few sheltered alcoves carved by ancient erosion, shallow depressions have been worn into the rock—places where something once sat, or knelt, long enough to polish the stone.

The strangest thing about the valley is not the tilted water or the deafening stillness, but the way space refuses to quite agree with itself. Walk along one of the great stone ribs toward the opposite rim, and the distance seems to stretch or fold without warning; a cairn you passed twenty paces back may sit ahead of you, undisturbed, still glistening with the exact same beads of cold moisture. Two stone arches in the distance appear to pass cleanly through one another at their apex, neither broken nor joined, occupying the same point in the dim air without friction.

Nothing falls here, because nothing loosens. Nothing rots, because it is too cold and too static. The valley simply holds its breath—loud, wet, empty, and suspended at the top of the world, waiting for nothing at all.
