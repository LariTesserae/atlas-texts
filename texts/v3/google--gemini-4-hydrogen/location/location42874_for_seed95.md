The plateau lies so high and has been worn down for so long that the horizon is almost level, broken only by the gentle, slumped swells of bedrock that were once mountain ranges. Time has taken every sharp edge. The stone—a pale, porous limestone streaked with ochre and salt-grey—has settled into wide, dish-like pans, low terraces, and broad whalebacks smoothed by eons of wind.

At midday, the light is clear and ordinary, neither harsh nor dim, falling evenly across the miles of open ground. The air is warm, carrying the dry, mineral scent of sun-heated chalk, yet the landscape is not entirely arid. In the shallowest depressions, where the rock dips a few feet below the surrounding flats, the stone stays dark and cool. Every night, the high-altitude air drops just enough to wring a thin, warm mist from the sky; it pools in the pans and beads along the leeward curves of the boulders. By mid-morning the mist burns away, leaving only a lingering dampness in the crevices, a sheen of moisture on the shaded undersides of things, and shallow, brackish film-pools no deeper than a palm.

Vegetation here is scarce and stubborn. Here and there, wedged into fissures too narrow for anything else, grow sparse, silver-green tufts of wiry scrub and flat, leathery rosettes that hug the rock. They look less like plants than like stains of verdigris on old metal.

What covers the plateau instead—what makes up the actual surface you walk on—is alive, and it is everywhere.

At first glance, the terraces seem paved with cracked, knobby flagstones. Look longer, and you see the flagstones are breathing, very slowly. The entire highland is carpeted in an overwhelming, layered density of animal life, packed so tightly that bare bedrock is almost a rarity. Broad, flat-backed creatures with pitted, stone-colored carapaces lie wedged edge-to-edge across the flats, fitting together like an irregular mosaic. In the damp hollows they stack two or three deep, their shells crusted with dust and salt. When the morning mist settles, they shift a fraction of an inch—a slow, collective grinding—to lift their edges and expose pale, pleated gill-folds to the condensation.

Rising from the seams between these living pavements are thousands of taller, stranger forms. Clusters of narrow, stalked grazers stand motionless on calcified legs, crowded together in dense thickets along the rims of the basins. Their bodies are dry, segmented, and mottled grey, angled toward the sun like deadwood. Draped across the leeward faces of the low ridges hang overlapping curtains of papery, translucent-winged things, hooked to the stone by tiny claws, their membranes rustling faintly only when a stray thermal lifts from the warm rock. Beneath them, in the dampness of every overhang and crevice, smaller, blind, many-legged things are packed in damp, glistening knots, waiting out the daylight.

Despite the staggering number of bodies—millions upon millions of them, touching, overlapping, competing for every square inch of surface—the plateau is profoundly quiet. There is no birdsong, no chattering, no roar of wind, for the flattened terrain offers little for the air to catch on. If you stand still and listen, the silence breaks only into tiny, dry textures: the faint *tick* of a carapace expanding in the noon warmth, the soft, wet sigh of a spiracle closing as the mist evaporates, the slow, gritty rasp of thousands of tiny mouths scraping microscopic film from the backs of their neighbors.

Movement here happens on a patient clock. Nothing darts or flees. A stalked grazer might take an entire afternoon to bend its neck toward a damp crevice; a column of pale, shell-less things creeping across a stone pan leaves a glistening trail that dries behind them before they have traveled a body’s length.

Scattered across the open miles are the only signs that anything here alters the stone itself. Low, unmortared rings of weathered cobbles sit half-buried in the living crust, alongside rough, chest-high cairns and shallow, domed windbreaks. There are no tool marks on any of the rocks—no chipped edges, no split faces. Every stone has simply been lifted, carried, and wedged into place by clumsy, heavy limbs or gripping mandibles. Most of these structures are partial, slumped, or abandoned, their stones long since colonized by the flat-backed pavements and draped with sleeping curtains of wings.

Yet occasionally, out on the pale flats, you can spot one of the builders: a broad, heavy-shouldered creature, its hide as cracked and ancient-looking as the plateau itself, slowly nudging a loose cobble across the ground with its forehead. It will take months to add a single course to its wall, building a hollow ring to trap a little extra night-mist in a world that has nowhere left to erode, and no reason to hurry.
