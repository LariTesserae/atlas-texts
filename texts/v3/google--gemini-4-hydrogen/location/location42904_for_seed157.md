High in the stone teeth of a mountain range, tucked beneath an overhanging brow of ancient, dark limestone, lies a hollow no wider than a small nave and perhaps twice as tall. From the outside it is nothing—a shadowed crease in a ridge permanently wrapped in cloud. Inside, the world closes in to a single, self-contained throat of rock, sealed from wind and weather, held in an unchanging, bruised-indigo gloom.

Far overhead, a jagged slot in the roof lets in the barest smear of light—not sunlight, only the thin, pewter-grey dilution of the mist-bank pressed against the mountain’s flank. It never brightens to day and never quite drops to pitch black. Down on the floor of the hollow, shapes are only wet sheen and pale silhouette. There are no shadows cast, because there is no direction to the light; everything sits in the same even, cool dimness, at a temperature that never rises above a damp, marrow-deep chill.

The rock here has long since finished breaking. Millennia of slow seepage have melted every sharp edge into bulbous shelves, smooth vertical flutes, and rounded ledges of dark stone. Water does not run in streams or crash in falls; it simply *is*. The air is saturated, heavy with condensing mist that beads on every surface. The walls sweat. Fat drops gather along the undersides of stone lips and let go in a slow, unhurried rhythm—*plip, plip*—into shallow, mirror-still pools at the bottom of the basin. The pools never rise and never shrink; what drips from the ceiling seeps away at the exact same rate through the mossy gravel beneath.

Nothing bare remains on the stone. Every ledge, every crevice, every half-submerged boulder is smothered under a lush, saturated coat of pale-jade and charcoal-green moss, thick as folded wool and heavy with water. Curtains of translucent, silvery ferns hang from the upper flutes, thriving on the faintest ghost of twilight and the mineral-rich drip. Gnarled, slow-growing shrubs—pale-barked and stunted—braid their roots directly through the wet bryophytes, leaving no gap between plant and stone. To take a step in this hollow would be to sink ankle-deep into living, dripping sponge.

And every inch of that sponge is occupied.

The place is packed to its seams with life, layered so tightly that there is scarcely a palm’s breadth of unoccupied space from the gravel floor to the upper ledges. Pale, lungless salamanders wedge themselves by the dozens into the wettest cracks, their skins slick and nearly transparent. Thumb-sized snails with dark, ribbed shells plaster the undersides of the fern fronds in motionless clusters. In the shallow pools, soft-bodied, feathery-gilled amphibians hang suspended just above the silt, barely stirring for hours at a time. Fat, velvety beetles graze the moss tips without a sound.

Dominating the ledges and the braided roots are the dwellers—hundreds upon hundreds of small, thick-furred, long-fingered creatures, something like a cross between a slow loris and a mountain marmot, with huge, dark, light-starved eyes and damp, silvery coats. They sit shoulder-to-shoulder along the rounded stone shelves, hunched in dense, breathing rows, or cling to the hanging roots in the dark. 

They build nothing. There are no stacked stones, no woven roofs, no cleared paths, no hearths. The rock and moss already hold them, and they have never altered a single contour of the hollow. Yet in their long, damp hands they hold simple tools, worn smooth by lifetimes of use: palm-sized river pebbles for cracking the calcium shells of the snails; slender splinters of pale bone, lashed with single strands of tough fern-fiber, used to probe the deep moss-cracks for grubs or to part each other’s wet fur; small, unhafted flakes of dark chert for scraping fat-rich lichen crusts from the weeping walls. When a bone pick is dropped into the moss, it sinks slowly into the wet carpet, coated in lime, until another hand finds it years later.

Despite the staggering crowd of bodies, the hollow is almost entirely quiet. There are no calls, no chirps, no territorial shrieks. In a space so small and so tightly packed, noise would be an intolerable disturbance. The dwellers communicate through slow touches, the brushing of whiskers, and the shifting of weight. The only sounds are the spaced-out drip of condensing water, the faint, wet rustle of fur against moss, and now and then the soft, precise *tap-tap* of a stone against a shell.

Nothing changes here. There are no seasons beneath the overhang, no thermal currents, no wind to stir the ferns. The moss grows at the exact, glacial pace that the beetles and snails graze it; the snails breed at the exact pace the dwellers harvest them. Yesterday, today, and a century from now are the same damp, cool, crowded moment—an equilibrium so complete that time itself seems to have pooled at the bottom of the stone well, still as the water, dark as the rock, and full to the brim.
