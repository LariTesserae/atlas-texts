High on the back of an ancient, wind-planed plateau, where the bedrock has been ground down over epochs into something as smooth and featureless as a river stone, the world seems entirely dead. There is no surface water anywhere—no streams, no dampness under turned pebbles, not even mud at the bottom of cracks. The air up here is thin, warm, and bone-dry.

Yet if you walk far enough across the pale, grit-scoured stone, you come upon a slight depression choked with a mat of twisted, iron-hard roots and swollen, waxy-leaved scrub. Beneath that tangled ceiling, hidden from the plateau’s open sky, lies a pocket no wider than a small courtyard and perhaps four stories deep: a single, ancient sinkhole whose walls have been worn by wind and time into smooth, concave bowls.

From the rim, it looks like a clogged well. Inside, it is a city, a thicket, and a swarming hive all at once.

The light down here is perpetually dim, stained amber and olive by the dense canopy of dry-adapted vegetation that roofs the opening. Because there is no rain to speak of, the plants have grown thick-skinned, fibrous, and greedy, sending taproots hundreds of feet down through hairline fissures in the ancient stone to sip from deep mineral aquifers far below. Their branches interlock above the hollow like a basket weave, trailing curtains of dry-moss, papery seed-pods, and succulent vines down into the gloom. Every inch of the smooth stone walls that isn’t occupied by a house is gripped by rosette-shaped agaves, thorny euphorbias, and leathery, dust-coated figs. The air smells sharply of crushed resin, warm dust, and sweet, fermenting sap.

Because all the moisture for miles around is locked inside these plants—and inside the bodies of the creatures that eat them—the hollow holds an overwhelming crush of animal life. You cannot put your hand on a ledge without startling a cluster of flat, pale geckos or a line of armor-plated beetles. Weaver-birds have stitched thousands of bottle-shaped nests into the hanging roots, filling the upper gloom with their weight. Below them, among the ledges and rafters, rock-hyraxes, dry-land crabs, and fierce, slender-bodied weasels dart through the shadows. At night, fruit bats drop from the canopy to gorge on the waxy blossoms, while centipedes the length of a forearm patrol the dust.

And woven straight through this biological riot is a dense, layered human settlement.

The people here build with what the hollow gives them: fine mineral dust mixed with plant-mucilage to make a hard, pale plaster; bundles of stripped succulent-fiber lashed with sinew; and smooth, wind-rounded stones wedged into the bowl’s natural alcoves. There is no room to spread out, so the town grows inward and upward, clinging to the concave walls in five or six precarious tiers. Balconies of woven cane overhang one another so closely that neighbors across the gulf can pass baskets on hooked poles. Ladders of knotted wood and bone-runged rope hang everywhere, swaying gently in the warm updrafts.

Their tools are simple and worn smooth by hand—flaked chert knives for scoring the bark of sap-trees, heavy stone querns for grinding dried seeds and roasted locusts into meal, bone needles, and fired clay jars sealed with beeswax and tree-gum. If there is metal at all, it is an heirloom: a hammered copper awl or a dull bronze blade traded up from the lowlands generations ago, kept wrapped in oiled leather.

The sound inside the pocket is constant and loud, amplified by the smooth, curved stone walls. There is the dry, percussive rattle of millions of insects in the canopy, the sharp chirring of lizards, the scolding of birds, and the steady, rhythmic *thock-thock* of wooden mallets pounding plant fibers on flat stones. Voices rarely rise to a shout—there is no need in a space this tight—but the murmur of hundreds of people cooking, arguing, mending nets, and tending clay ovens blends with the animal din into a thick, unbroken hum.

Nothing here is still for long. A child climbs a vine-choked support beam to rob a weaver-bird’s nest, sending a shower of dry chaff down onto a woman splitting reed-stalks below; a snake drops from the ceiling-roots after a rat, sparking a sudden shriek of alarm and a flurry of sticks from the nearest balcony; a dust devil spins lazily across the talcum-fine floor of the pit, lifting the smell of woodsmoke, guano, and roasting chitin up toward the tangled roof. 

The plateau above remains empty, silent, and bleached by the sun. Down in the dim, warm belly of the stone, life presses shoulder to shoulder against itself, drinking every drop of greenness the ancient rock will yield.
