The sky here is the deep, bruised cobalt of very high places, almost violet at the zenith, and the sun sits in it like a naked arc-lamp. Everything below that sky is made. As far as the eye can reach—across a broad, stepped basin miles wide and plunging down through tier after tier of terraces, viaducts, and retaining walls—there is no raw stone, no soil that was not hauled or compounded, no cliff that was not laid course by course in brick, poured slag-concrete, and riveted iron.

It is an ancient, high-altitude superstructure, weathered until its geometry has softened into the settled, rounded contours of a mountain range. Corners of lime-mortared masonry have worn smooth under centuries of wind and grit; cast-iron stanchions have oxidized to a thick, plum-black patina; broad concrete aprons are crazed with hairline fissures and stained in long vertical streaks of rust and mineral salt. Yet for all its altitude, the basin is warm. The sheer density of the built mass—hundreds of vertical feet of sun-absorbing brick, dark slate, and heat-retaining metal—traps the fierce solar glare like a kiln, and from deep within the lower tiers rises the steady, dry-sweet exhaust of boilers and friction.

Water is scarce enough to be precious, yet the air is never wholly dry. Every afternoon, ragged, sunlit cloud-banks drift straight through the upper and middle terraces, snagging on the architecture. Stretching above the ridges and between the chimney-stacks are miles of tall condenser-frames—vast, tilted sails of galvanized wire mesh, slatted zinc, and oxidized copper fins. As the mist passes through them, they weep. A slow, patient pattering falls from thousands of metal edges into zinc gutters, down terracotta downspouts, and along open stone runnels that thread through the walkways. Where a pipe-joint leaks or a cistern overflows, dampness pools in the shaded masonry joints, dark and cool against the sun-baked brick.

Out of those damp seams and irrigated troughs, the place erupts into green. Vegetation packs the terraces in dense, overlapping layers, softening the hard industrial lines until the whole valley looks half-swallowed by a vertical orchard. Tough, thick-leaved figs and olive-trees burst from masonry planters wedged between transformer housings; sprawling gourds and broad-leafed squashes spill over parapet walls, their tendrils wrapping around porcelain insulators and steam-pipes. In the cooler, dripping alleys beneath the great aqueducts, cane-brakes and ferns crowd the stone channels, while the shaded undersides of iron catwalks are bearded with damp moss. Above, in the blinding glare, drought-hardy succulents and thorny creepers cling to the mortar lines of the highest smokestacks, drinking only the afternoon cloud-drip.

The sound of the basin is continuous and loud, though nothing inside it hurries. It is the roar of vast, patient work. Across the valley, immense beam-engines and spoked cast-iron flywheels turn at the speed of a slow breath, driving air-compressors, water-lifts, and miles of overhead cableways whose iron buckets creep along greased wire ropes like beads on an abacus. Every few minutes, a pressure-relief stack somewhere in the middle tiers vents a long, whistling sigh of white steam that echoes off the terraced walls. High-voltage lines hum with a thick, metallic thrum between porcelain-strung pylons. Beneath the mechanical din runs the dry, insistent rattle of cicadas in the fig trees, the clatter of metal shutters shifting slightly as thermal expansion tightens the bolts, and the constant, percussive *plink-plink-shhh* of condensation dripping into metal troughs.

Through this bright, noisy labyrinth, animals move in numbers that match the crowding of the plants. Swifts and swallows by the tens of thousands stitch the air between the condenser-sails, nesting in the hollow undersides of concrete arches. Feral rock-doves and fat, iridescent starlings crowd the warm tops of boiler-houses, while high-altitude kestrels and pale-winged buzzards ride the rising thermals off the sun-warmed masonry. On the iron walkways and along the brick cornices, tribes of long-tailed, thick-furred monkeys—pale-faced and grease-smudged—travel the pipe-racks with casual precision, warming their bellies against insulated steam-lines or raiding the gourd-vines. Lizards flatten themselves onto every sun-struck copper flashing, and bees work the blossoms of the terraced gardens in a low, golden haze, their hives tucked into disused junction boxes and the hollow cores of masonry piers.

There is a quiet strangeness to the way the place behaves, a sense that its cycles belong to a longer clock than a human day. Great louvered sun-shields of timber and sheet-tin, mounted on worm-gears along the upper ridges, groan and pivot a few degrees each hour to follow the sun, casting shifting bands of cool shadow across the gardens below. Heavy counterweights made of cast-slag blocks rise and fall through open shafts at the pace of an hour hand, regulating water pressure across districts. Looking down through the grated floors of the catwalks, there is no earth to be seen—only deeper, dimmer tiers of brick vaults, dripping pipes, and humming dynamos descending into blue shadow, as if the mountain itself were nothing more than an old machine that has grown a skin of leaves, feathers, and rust.
