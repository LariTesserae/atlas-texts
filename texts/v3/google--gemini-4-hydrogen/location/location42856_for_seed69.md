There is nothing here to see, and nothing here has eyes to try. 

The darkness is not a shadow cast by something else; it is the baseline condition of the continent. Miles above an unseen ocean, a vast, ancient shelf of weathered basalt and pitted limestone stretches out beneath a permanent, lightless ceiling of warm cloud. The air is thick, blood-warm, and saturated to the point of drowning. You do not walk through it so much as part it—a heavy soup of condensing vapor, mineral steam, and drifting, oily spores that coat every surface in a slick, lukewarm film.

Because the plateau sits so high, it catches the planet’s great atmospheric currents head-on. Vast weather systems—slow, continent-wide spirals of low pressure—roll across the tableland in multi-day cycles. They bring no lightning, only shifts in barometric weight and endless, warm deluges that drum against the stone and turn the terraced valleys into roaring, braided mazes of black water. The rock has been enduring this for geological ages. Every edge has been rounded off. The cliff-faces weep constantly from horizontal seams, their surfaces smoothed into undulating, slick curtains of travertine and moss, carved into deep, echoing sinkholes and broad, settled basins where water pools before spilling over into the next tier down.

Out of this wet, warm stone grows a dense, suffocating jungle that has never known a sun.

The flora feeds on what the planet and the air actually offer: geothermal heat seeping up through fractures in the highland crust, dissolved sulfur and iron in the runoff, and the stray electrical currents bleeding into the soil. Thick, leathery fronds—pale, waxy, and heavy with condensation—crowd together in overlapping tiers, their edges serrated to catch the constant updrafts rising from the abyssal drop-offs at the shelf’s edge. From the upper boughs of massive, bulbous canopy-trees, curtains of fibrous aerial roots hang down like wet rigging, brushing against the ground and drinking directly from the mist. Nothing grows sparse here; every cubic yard of the lower air is claimed by tangled vines, spongy mats of copper-veined moss, and clusters of pneumatic spore-bladders that swell silently with swamp gas until the pressure grows too great and they rupture with flat, percussive cracks.

It is a loud world, because sound is the only way anything knows where anything else is.

The baseline is water: the hiss of warm rain on broad, rubbery leaves, the subterranean glug of flooded karst-tubes, the steady, chest-rattling thunder of cataracts plunging into unseen gorges. Layered over that is the machinery.

The plateau is sparsely built, yet heavily industrialized. Scattered miles apart across the vast, dripping expanse stand solitary, windowless installations—massive atmospheric condensers, geothermal turbine-towers, automated ore-crushing mills, and high-voltage substations anchored deep into the bedrock with flared concrete buttresses. There are no roads connecting them, no towns huddled around their bases, and no lights anywhere on their iron hides. Why put a lamp on a boiler in a blind world? Instead, they announce themselves through vibration and roar.

A mile before you reach a pumping station, you feel its rhythm in the soles of your feet: the slow, heavy *thrum-clack* of subterranean pistons driving mineral slurry through buried pipelines. As you draw closer, the air warms further and smells sharply of hot grease, wet rust, and ozone. The machines run continuously, automated and unstaffed, built to outlast the weather. Steam-valves vent on strict, pressure-governed schedules, shrieking into the mist and Scalding the surrounding foliage back into a neat, cleared ring of bare, steaming stone. Heavy-gauge power cables, thick as a man’s thigh and slick with condensation, sag between solitary iron pylons that march straight across the chasms, humming with a deep, sixty-cycle buzz that makes the teeth ache.

Life has folded itself around these scattered monuments of iron and current. 

The copper-veined mosses grow thickest along the buried conduits, vibrating faintly as they siphon off stray electromagnetic fields. In the warm, dry pockets beneath the turbine housings, colonies of blind, chitinous grazers cluster by the thousands, scraping mineral scale from the cooling pipes and clicking in rapid, overlapping chorus to map the shifting vibrations of the floor. 

In the wet dark beyond the clearings, the fauna is abundant, dense, and fiercely territorial. Movement is constant. Heavy, soft-padded browsers push through the hanging root-curtains, tearing off mouthfuls of sulfur-rich fern while emitting low, foghorn bellows that bounce off the rounded canyon walls, returning to them as a tactile map of the terrain. Smaller, segmented things skitter through the saturated leaf-litter, navigating by the electrical pulses of each other’s nervous systems.

Predators here hunt by acoustic deception and seismic ambush. Some—long, low-slung, and armored in slick, mud-colored plates—crawl along the edges of the industrial clearings and wait for the steam-valves to blow. Under the deafening cover of the venting steam, they rush whatever has strayed too close to the warm pipes. Others, hanging motionless in the upper root-tangles, have evolved hollow, resonant throat-sacs capable of mimicking the exact rhythmic *clank-hiss* of a distant pump-head or the low hum of a sagging cable. They draw curious scavengers and juvenile grazers up into the canopy, guiding them closer with the comforting, familiar pulse of warm machinery, until the mist parts and jaws snap shut.

High above it all, unseen and unfelt except as a sudden drop in pressure, a new weather front begins to turn across the plateau. The wind shifts, carrying the smell of crushed spore-pods and hot machine oil from twenty miles away. The hollow reeds along the gorge-rims begin to whistle in a rising, discordant chord. Down in the wet dark, the turbines adjust their pitch to match the load, the moss shudders, and the whole vast, blind machine of the highland keeps turning.
