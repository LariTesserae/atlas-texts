Cold comes first, a numbing, near-freezing weight that presses in from every direction at once. Light barely reaches down here—only a bruised, slate-grey dimness that filters through hundreds of feet of unseen water above and dies within a few arm’s lengths, swallowed by the murk.

Nothing about this submerged valley is empty. Though it is an open, sprawling trough—wide as a mountain basin, stretching out beyond the reach of sight—the space itself feels packed, almost claustrophobic. The water is thick with suspended grit: ancient glacial flour, pulverized shell-dust, and dark mineral silt, all churned into a dense, swirling broth. You cannot look through it; you only look into it, watching pale flecks and dark flakes of detritus crowd past one another in tight, erratic spirals.

Beneath that heavy soup lies the floor, and it is just as crowded. Eons of water-work have stripped this basin of anything sharp or dramatic. There are no jagged cliffs, no spires, no open mudflats. The bedrock has been ground down into broad, shallow dishes, and every square inch of those dishes is jammed tight with cobbles, boulders, and heavy, iron-dark nodules. Every stone has been tumbled until it is egg-smooth, worn by millennia of friction into dull greys, blacks, and rusted browns. They lie shoulder to shoulder, wedged into one another, leaving no room between presences—only narrow, silt-choked crevices where grit settles for a heartbeat before being ripped away again.

Because the basin never rests. It is a place of constant, chaotic, benthic weather. Cold, hyper-dense currents pour down from the unseen shelves at the valley’s edges, colliding with deep, swirling eddies that twist across the floor without warning. Whole walls of suspended silt billow up like dark smoke, blind the water for minutes at a time, and collapse elsewhere. Under the shove of these underwater squalls, the packed cobble-fields themselves shudder and creep; heavy, rounded stones roll an inch, clack against their neighbors, and settle into a new lock. 

Yet for all this violence, the valley is eerily quiet. There is no surface here to break into waves, no air to trap bubbles or carry a roar. The turbulence moves with a heavy, muffled insistence. All you hear is the faintest, dry-sounding *shhh* of sliding silt, the low, barely audible thrum of water pressing against stone, and now and then a dull, muted *tock* as two boulders knock together in the gloom.

Life here is sparse, solitary, and built to endure the grind. Vegetation barely gets a foothold; only in the tightest lee of the largest boulders do stunted, leathery fronds of dark-red seaweed or pale, crusty patches of coralline algae cling to the rock, shredded at the tips by the constant sandblasting of the currents. 

Fauna is just as rare. Now and then, a pale, flat-bodied skate lifts from the cobbles like a peeled slab of grey skin, rides a shifting eddy for a few yards, and vanishes back into the silt-cloud. A slow, heavy-shouldered bottom-fish with milky eyes drifts past, hugging the troughs. In a tight gap between three jammed boulders, a pale octopus hunkers down, its skin scarred from the moving grit. It is one of the few things here that uses what it finds: with two muscular arms, it drags a flat, water-worn flake of flint across the mouth of its crevice, wedging it against the current like a shield.

There are no walls here, no sunken buildings, no roads or monuments—nothing built to last. Yet scattered among the natural cobbles are the small, patient traces of simple hands. Half-buried in the shifting gravel lies a river-stone notched around its middle, still trailing a frayed stub of sinew—a net-weight lost from some unseen boat or ice-hole far above. Nearby, a bone hook, snapped at the barb and polished silky-smooth by decades of tumbling silt, rolls gently in a shallow eddy. Once in a great while, a weighted wicker basket or a stone-anchored snare drifts down through the murk, tumbles across the packed stones until its lashings rot or snap, and is slowly broken apart. 

Even these small tools do not stay distinct for long. The valley treats chipped flint, carved bone, and ancient bedrock the same way: it packs them together, rolls them in the cold dark, and grinds them quietly back toward dust.
