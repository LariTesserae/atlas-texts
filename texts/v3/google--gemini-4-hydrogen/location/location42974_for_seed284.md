From the outside, the spire looks uninhabitable—a newly thrust-up needle of fractured black slate and raw granite, its ridges still sharp enough to slice boot leather. At this altitude, the air is too thin and bone-dry for snow to gather. There are no glaciers, no meltwater trickles, not even frost; only a cool, sun-bleached desert of jagged mineral slabs standing at the top of the world.

Halfway up the sheerest face, hidden behind a knife-edged buttress, a vertical cleft splits the rock. Slipping through the narrow gap is like stepping into the casing of a vast, wool-muffled clock.

The outer wind vanishes. Inside, the fissure widens just enough to hold a town—barely thirty paces from wall to wall, yet plunging straight down into dimness for hundreds of meters. Every inch of that vertical slot is claimed. It is a pocket-city wedged tight between two raw faces of stone, built out of riveted iron plates, corrugated tin, and dry timber struts jammed directly from rock to rock with iron turnbuckles.

Because the mountain is young and still settling, nothing here is permitted to be permanent. The entire settlement is in a state of quiet, frantic flux. Overhead and underfoot, a dense, tangled cat’s-cradle of steel cables, leather drive-belts, and copper-wire conduits hums with constant motion. Iron ore-skips and mesh-sided workshop cages rise and plummet past one another, inches apart, on counterweighted pulleys. Crews in felt-soled boots crawl across the swaying gantries, endlessly unbolting platforms, winching them ten feet higher or lower, and re-anchoring them to chase a shifting seam of rock or to relieve the strain of a fresh hairline fracture in the slate. It has the restless, swarming energy of an anthill or a crowded bazaar, except that every movement is vertical and tethered.

And yet, for an industrial warren packed with electric motors, crushers, and sorting mills, the cleft is eerily quiet.

The thin high-altitude air carries sound poorly, and the builders have wrapped every bearing in felt, lined every chute with oiled hide, and mounted every motor on thick pads of compressed mineral wool. You do not hear the roar of a factory; you hear a dry, hushed *shhh* of powdered rock sliding down gravity-chutes, the soft *whir-slap* of hundreds of stitched belts turning overhead, and the faint, steady *thrum* of low-voltage copper coils. Now and then, a counterweight settles with a dull, muffled *tock*, or a spark snaps softly off a carbon brush.

Light reaches this place sparingly. Far above, a crooked, razor-thin ribbon of pale sky admits a cold blade of daylight that catches the suspended mica dust, turning the upper shaft into a dim, glittering haze. Lower down, where the walls pinch closer together, the town relies on strings of caged amber-filament bulbs and the occasional brief blue-white wink of an arc-welder. The air smells of ozone, dry slate dust, and warm machine grease. There is no dampness anywhere; even the static electricity builds unchecked on the moving belts, making loose threads and hair stand softly on end.

Nothing green grows in the cleft. The closest thing to plant life are dry, chalky crusts of grey lichen clinging to the undersides of old timber shoring, and brittle, paper-thin shelf fungi that feed on the rare spots where grease or human breath has left a memory of moisture.

Instead, the dimness is thick with animal life, all of it adapted to the cool, dry machinery. Flocks of pale, moth-winged cliff-swifts dart silently between the swaying cables, nesting in the warm hollows beneath the electric hoppers. Wherever a motor housing radiates a pocket of dry heat, flat, copper-shelled beetles cluster by the thousands, their tiny legs making a sound like dry sand shifting as they lick the film of oil from the casings. Long-tailed, velvety rock-voles scurry along the insulated power conduits like tightrope walkers, raiding the hanging lunch-tins of the riggers. Beneath almost every catwalk, translucent spiders have spun dense, grey blankets of silk that trap the floating mineral dust, turning the undersides of the ironwork into soft, drooping curtains of felt.

Everything here is layered, near-touching, and perpetually in motion—tiny machine shops bolted directly above sleeping-lockers, electrostatic dust-separators humming beside narrow cook-stalls that brew dry, bitter leaf-infusions in sealed kettles. In the dim, cool hush of the split rock, the whole town ticks, shifts, and rebuilds itself around the restless stone, suspended in the dry dark.
