The first thing that hits you is not the sight of it, because there is barely enough light to see more than fifty yards ahead, and what light exists is the flat, bruised gray of a sky that sits directly on the ground. It is the noise.

A continuous, physical wall of sound—so loud it vibrates in your sinuses and behind your teeth. There are no birds in it, no insects, no footsteps, no calls. Nothing with lungs or wings lives here. The roar comes entirely from the wind hitting the stalks, from the water moving through them, and from the ground itself.

You are standing—or rather, wedged—high on a continental plateau miles above any sea. The air is cool, hovering just above freezing, heavy enough to taste like wet slate and ozone. At this elevation, the cloud deck does not hang overhead; it *is* the atmosphere, a slow, churning fog that condenses on every surface without ever quite deciding to rain. Water sheets off your sleeves within seconds. It beads along the dark, razor-edged ridges of the flora, gathers into cold globules, and drops in an endless, percussive patter into the tangle below.

If you force your hands down through the vegetation—and it takes force, because there is no open space here, not a hand’s breadth of bare air between one stem and the next—you will never touch soil. There is no dirt, no loam, no rotting leaf-litter. Beneath the packed, interlocking mat of roots lies raw, unweathered stone. Freshly extruded basalt and dark, glassy mineral shelves, fractured into sharp, clean planes that have never been rounded by a river or softened by a single century of frost. The continent is geologically embryonic, still settling into its own weight. Every few minutes, a deep, unmuffled *crack* travels up through the soles of your boots—a tectonic slab shifting half a millimeter miles below, its vibration carried cleanly through the rigid, crystalline rock and amplified by the taut root-network gripping it.

That is the first impossible thing: a landscape this raw and un-eroded should be barren. Instead, it is suffocatingly full.

The plants do not grow *on* the rock so much as they interpenetrate it. No soil is needed because the flora operates on rules that ordinary botany cannot account for. Pull a single blade from a thicket—if you can snap its fibrous, metallic-sheen stem—and the cut edge does not bleed green sap. It weeps a clear, cold, faintly viscous fluid that smells of petrichor and hot copper, and the cross-section glints with microscopic, iridescent striations. At the molecular level, these tissues are lattices of engineered carbon and superconducting membranes, grown wild and self-replicating across the entire landmass without a single seam, weld, or monument to mark whoever seeded them. There are no towers here, no conduits, no ruins, no roads. Nothing was *built*. The landscape simply grows, and in growing, it performs work.

In the dimness, because there is so little sunlight to feed on, the vegetation harvests the continent itself.

Thousands of square miles of tightly packed, hollow-stemmed reeds, waxy indigo-black fronds, and dense, springy moss-mats act as a single planetary transducer. The high-altitude wind sweeps unimpeded across the vast plateau, bending the canopy in massive, rolling waves that generate their own downdrafts and micro-squalls. As the hollow stalks flex and collide, they whistle and groan like an organ the size of a hemisphere. Inside them, millions of microscopic osmotic valves snap open and shut in rapid, rhythmic cascades, pumping cold condensation up from the weeping rock fractures against the low air pressure. You can hear the cavitation—a dry, static-like *crackle* running constantly beneath the wind’s roar, the sound of water boiling cold inside capillary tubes thinner than a hair.

Look closely at a frond in the gloom, and you can see the faint, wrong physics of it. The condensed water droplets do not always obey gravity; along the central ribs, where a weak electrostatic charge builds from the wind-friction, beads of water creep *upward* toward the tips before flashing into mist or dropping away. The surfaces of the leaves have an oily, diffraction-grating sheen that bends the dim gray light around their edges, making the thickets look slightly out of focus, as if the air between the stems were thicker than the air above them. Occasionally, deep within the packed undergrowth where two different species grind against each other in a gust, a brief, cold spark of blue-violet luminescence flares and dies—not heat, not fire, just excess potential bleeding off into the wet air.

Everything is in motion, yet nothing goes anywhere. The fog rolls across the endless, undulating carpet of dark green and slate-black, feeding the leaves; the leaves pump the water down into the raw fractures of the rising stone; the stone groans and shifts, opening new fissures for the roots to thread into, locking the unformed crust in place. 

You can walk for days—if you can call pushing through waist-deep, spring-loaded, razor-edged density walking—and the horizon never changes. There are no clearings. There are no peaks rising bare above the growth, because the growth climbs every sharp, fresh escarpment the moment it breaks the surface. There is only the vast, cool, dim ceiling of cloud, the deafening roar of a continent-wide machine disguised as a marshy highland thicket, and the relentless, indifferent hum of a world still under construction, already completely occupied by itself.
