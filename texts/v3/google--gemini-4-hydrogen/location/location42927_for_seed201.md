The bowl sits high enough that the sky above it is not blue, but a thin, bruised indigo—the color of cold iron—and the sun that clears its jagged eastern lip strikes with the flat, unbuffered weight of a hammer.

It is not a large place. From the fractured eastern sill to the sheer western tooth, a stone thrown hard by a strong arm might cross a third of it. It is a single, high-altitude amphitheater, carved out of raw, pale granite and thick seams of milky quartz that have not yet learned the softening touch of time. Every edge here is an accusation. The ridges are knife-thin, the talus slopes at the base of the walls are made of shards that still ring like porcelain when shifted, and the rock faces show the pale, dusty scars of recent frost-shattering.

Because the bowl is shaped like a tilted saucer, and because the stone is so pale, the light does not merely fall into it; it collects. By mid-morning, the air is blinding. The glare bounces off the quartz veins and the thin skins of rime-ice, filling the hollow with a fierce, shadowless brilliance that makes the eyes water. Yet the air itself stays bitingly cold. You can stand in the center of the glare and see your own breath hang in a sharp, crystalline plume, even while the back of your hand tingles from the sun’s radiation.

At first glance, the basin looks empty—a hollow cup of white stone and cold sky, with vast, clean distances between one boulder and the next. The thinness of the air swallows distance and sound alike. If a pebble slips down the scree, the clatter dies before it reaches the opposite wall.

Look down into the seams, however, and the emptiness reveals its trick.

Wherever the pale rock cracks—along the sheltered lees of boulders, in the narrow ledges where snowmelt pools before it can evaporate, in the deep vertical fissures that hold the noon heat—the stone is packed with a dense, dark lushness. It is a low, hugging vegetation, engineered for survival in a solar oven set inside a freezer. Thick cushions of black-green moss swell like sponges along the damp seams. Waxy, rosette-shaped succulents with leaves the color of dried blood wedge themselves into cracks no wider than a finger, their centers beaded with condensation. Tough, woolly-stemmed ferns uncurl from beneath overhanging slabs, drinking the brief, daily trickle of thaw-water before the afternoon freeze locks it away again.

The water here is never a stream, only a dampness that comes and goes on a strict clock. At dawn, everything is brittle and dry, locked in white needles of hoarfrost. By noon, the reflected heat turns the frost directly into vapor and slicks the dark mosses with moisture. Shallow, natural depressions in the granite hold thin, sky-bright mirrors of water, barely an inch deep, ringed by a dark halo of wet stone.

And with the midday dampness, the bowl wakes up—quietly, furiously.

The place is crowded with life, though it is a life that occupies the margins and the air rather than the open floor. The moment the rock warms, thousands of glossy, pitch-black beetles—no larger than lentils, built to drink the sun—pour out of the moss cushions to graze on the film of algae in the melt-pans. They move in silent, shimmering rivers across the white stone. Above them, the hollow air fills with motion: dozens of small, long-winged white birds, nearly invisible against the quartz glare until their sharp black wingtips flash, wheeling and diving without a single cry. They do not sing; at this height, breath is too precious to waste on noise. Only the faint, dry *whuff* of air through primary feathers marks their passes as they snatch the beetles off the stone.

In the deeper crevices of the scree, small, thick-furred rodents with oversized, translucent ears slip from shadow to shadow, harvesting the dark succulent leaves and stuffing their cheeks in frantic silence. Watch long enough, and you see the thin, pale-coated stoats that hunt them—long as a forearm, almost entirely white save for their black noses, flowing over the razor-sharp talus like spilled milk. Nothing here stalks slowly. The warm window is too short, perhaps three hours between the morning thaw and the afternoon chill, and the entire food chain runs through its paces at a sprint, completely mute.

Scattered through this high, bright silence are the faint marks of hands.

They are small, patient interventions, built from what the bowl already offers. Along the eastern rim, someone has stacked flat shards of granite into three low, crescent-shaped windbreaks, no higher than a crouching shoulder, their stones wedged tightly without mortar. In the center of the basin, beside the largest of the natural melt-pans, a series of shallow, rectangular troughs have been pecked into the raw quartz with heavy stone hammers—crude, hand-chipped catch-basins meant to hold the noon condensation a little longer before it sinks into the cracks.

Near the base of the western wall, tucked under a deep overhang that stays out of the wind, sits a single abandoned blind. Its low walls are chinked with dried black moss. Inside, resting on a flat slab of rock, are two notched bone needles, a small bundle of sinew gone stiff with cold, and a flake of dark flint knocked from a river cobble carried up from far below. Whoever comes here does not live here; the air is too thin, the night too lethal. They climb up only for the noon rush, to set hair-snares along the rodent runs or gather the dark, oily mosses before the sun drops.

By mid-afternoon, the light begins to slant, losing its angle against the pale walls. The temperature drops like a stone. A thin, dry wind spills over the jagged lip, carrying no smell except the sharp, metallic tang of cold ozone. The damp halos around the water-pans freeze instantly into matte gray glass. The beetles vanish back into the black cushions; the rodents seal themselves into the scree; the white birds drop into the deep fissures of the cliff face.

In less than an hour, the frantic engine of the bowl locks solid, leaving only the blinding white stone, the crude stone troughs, and the immense, breathless quiet of the high peaks.
