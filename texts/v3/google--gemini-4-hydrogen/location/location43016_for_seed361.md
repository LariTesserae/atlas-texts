High on the crown of a primordial mountain range—so old its peaks have long since been planed down into a rolling, sun-bleached tableland of iron-stained gneiss—the world seems empty. The air up here is thin and warm, smelling of dry dust and hot quartz. For miles, the bedrock is smooth as a river-stone, worn to its final, settled geometry by wind and time.

You do not see the hollow until your boot is nearly over its lip.

It is a single, sunken pocket in the ancient pavement, barely forty paces across and perhaps twenty feet deep, carved out eons ago where a softer mineral vein dissolved beneath the stone. Stepping down into it is like stepping into a warm, damp room left open to the sky. The bright, ordinary daylight pours straight in, sharp and high-altitude, throwing crisp shadows across the bowl’s curved walls.

The first thing that strikes you is the noise.

Because the hollow is shaped like a shallow parabolic dish of smooth, dense rock, every sound inside it is trapped, bounced, and magnified into a physical weight. The air vibrates with the relentless, saw-toothed roar of thousands of thumb-sized, copper-winged cicadas clinging to the warm stone. Beneath their shrill wall of static comes a wet, percussive *tock-tock-tock*—hundreds of small, jewel-skinned frogs packed into the damp crevices, their throat-sacs pulsing in overlapping rhythms. Overhead, swifts and cliff-martins dive straight down from the thin sky into the bowl, their wings ripping the air as they snatch insects mid-flight with audible snaps of their beaks. It is deafening, a tight, furious concert confined to a single room of stone.

The fauna here is overwhelming precisely because there is nowhere else for it to be. Across the barren, flattened miles of the plateau, this pocket is the only refuge. Every crack, overhang, and sunlit ledge is contested. Long-legged, stick-thin mantids stalk the bare rock margins; iridescent beetles swarm over damp fissures; and when a high cloud briefly dims the sun, translucent, segmented grazers emerge in synchronized waves from beneath the moss to rasp at the mineral films on the stone.

Yet for all the biological frenzy, the space itself feels surprisingly sparse. The vegetation is lush—thick, velvety mats of copper-colored sphagnum, hanging curtains of maidenhair ferns, and clusters of fleshy, translucent-leaved succulents that glow like green glass in the sun—but it grows in distinct, separated islands. Between these green cushions lie broad, bare expanses of smooth, ancient rock, warm to the touch and swept clean by the movement of creatures. You can walk easily between the clumps of life, with plenty of open stone and air around you.

There is no stream here, no pond, not even a proper puddle. The hollow is merely damp. A persistent, bath-warm humidity clings to the lower walls, darkening the gneiss in irregular streaks. Fat droplets gather slowly at the tips of fern fronds and fall with heavy, solitary *plips* into palm-sized cups worn into the floor.

The moisture does not come from the sky—rain up here is rare—but from the scattered, half-buried artifacts embedded in the bowl’s floor.

There are perhaps seven or eight of them, spaced widely apart in the bare stone, weathered down until they are almost indistinguishable from natural concretions. One looks like a flattened, matte-black river pebble the size of a dinner plate, fused seamlessly into the bedrock; another is a pale, slotted ring of ceramic-like material jutting at a slight angle from a wall, its edges rounded by millennia of wind-borne grit. They have no seams, no vents, no visible power sources. Yet the air directly above each one shimmers with a faint, cold-free condensation, pulling water straight out of the dry highland atmosphere through some quiet manipulation of local phase states that current physics has no clean name for.

Around these nodes, things behave with a subtle, persistent strangeness. Water beads on the nearby rock in precise, hexagonal lattices before breaking loose to trickle downward. Dust motes caught in the sunlight drift into slow, lazy orbits around the matte-black disk before dropping suddenly to the stone. The shadows cast by the slotted ring do not quite agree with the angle of the afternoon sun, leaning a few degrees too far toward the center of the hollow.

Even the animals treat the artifacts differently. The cicadas never land on them, leaving a neat, bare halo of stone around each piece, while tiny, silver-backed mites congregate at the edges of those halos, drinking the strangely structured dew that sweats from the buried metal. Occasionally, a deep, sub-audible *thrum* ripples out from the stone—not loud enough to hear over the frogs and insects, but strong enough to make the water droplets hanging from the ferns shiver and momentarily hop upward before gravity reclaims them.

Above the rim, the wind skims across the vast, flattened wasteland of ancient rock, carrying nothing. Down inside the pocket, the sun warms the damp stone, the birds dive, the beetles rattle, and the old machines quietly sweat water into the thin air, keeping a single, roaring teaspoon of jungle alive at the top of the world.
