The whole place sits inside a sharp-edged scoop in the low country, scarcely half a mile from rim to rim. It looks like the earth split open recently—within the last few centuries, not long enough for wind or frost to round the lips of the dark basalt cliffs that wall it in. Every ledge and fracture is crisp, angular, and black-gray, catching the clear daylight on flat, unweathered facets.

Down in the hollow, the air is cool and thin, carrying the clean, metallic chill of damp stone. There is no river here, no pond, not even a proper creek. Yet the ground is never quite dry. Moisture gathers out of the cool air itself, beading in patient films along the undersides of rock overhangs and sweating down the flanks of cold iron pipes. Where those drips fall, they collect in shallow, concrete-lined catch-basins and narrow rock fissures, leaving the rest of the basin’s gravel-and-flagstone floor dry enough to kick up dust if you drag a boot.

Across this wide, sparse floor, an industrial settlement has been laid out with plenty of room to breathe. It is built in the manner of a town—paved avenues with raised curbs, numbered brick substations, elevated catwalks, and two-story control houses with narrow, barred windows—yet the buildings stand far apart, separated by broad, empty aprons of crushed stone and vacant terraces. A tall, riveted water-less condenser tower occupies the center, its iron ribs painted a faded oxide red. Three hundred paces away, a cluster of low, flat-roofed workshops hugs the western cliff; to the east, a line of steel gantries marches up the stepped rock toward a row of silent tenement-style quarters. Everything is wired together by overhead power lines and thick, insulated conduits that run along the ground like black roots.

And the whole place is deafening.

The noise doesn't come from hurry; nothing here moves fast. The heavy machinery inside the brick pump-houses and open-air transformer yards runs on long, patient cycles. Enormous cast-iron flywheels turn so slowly you can count the spokes as they pass, yet their bearings groan with a bass resonance that vibrates in your teeth. High-voltage coils behind chain-link fences emit a relentless, sixty-cycle roar, amplified by the sharp stone amphitheater of the cliffs. Every twelve minutes, a pressure relief valve atop the central condenser lets go with a prolonged, ear-splitting *hiss-clank* that echoes off the basalt walls for twenty seconds before settling back into the deep mechanical thrum.

Because the rock is young and full of unweathered mineral seams, it rings. If you press your palm against the cliffside, you can feel the slow, heavy pulse of the air-compressors traveling straight through the bedrock.

In the wide spaces between the humming machines, life has taken hold with startling density. Wherever a pipe sweats or a concrete basin holds an inch of condensation, vegetation explodes in lush, cool-climate tangles. Waist-high bracken and dark, glossy hart’s-tongue ferns crowd the bases of the electrical pylons. Thick cushions of emerald moss upholster the north-facing brick walls and the cold iron flanges of the valves, drinking from the mist that drifts off the cooling vents. Broad-leaved dock, wild rhubarb, and tough, woody vines spill out of the rock crevices, creeping along the edges of the empty avenues and wrapping around unused streetlamp posts.

The fauna is just as thick, and oddly tuned to the machinery. Swifts and glossy, green-backed starlings nest by the hundreds inside the hollow steel trusses of the gantries, completely unbothered by the roar. They have learned the twelve-minute rhythm of the central condenser; in the final seconds before the pressure valve blows, the entire flock lifts off the ironwork in a single, silent cloud, wheeling in the bright daylight above the noise until the hiss subsides, then dropping back to their perches in unison.

Down in the damp, fern-choked ditches beside the conduits, pale, fist-sized tree frogs and long-legged beetles thrive in the cool shade. The frogs don't croak in the usual way; their throats swell and pulse in long, low vibrations that lock into the hum of the transformers, making it impossible to tell where the electrical drone ends and the chorus begins. Small, thick-furred marmots—their coats dusted gray with basalt grit—live in burrows under the paved aprons, popping up between the empty concrete plinths to watch the slow-turning gears, freezing whenever a relay clicks shut.

There is no rush to the place. The iron rusts slowly in the intermittent damp; the moss inches across the flagstones at a millimeter a year; the great compressors heave in and out like sleeping lungs. Overhead, the sky is wide, bright, and ordinary, pouring clear light down onto the sharp black rock, the lush green crevices, and the sparse, thundering town of machines.
