At this altitude, the darkness has weight. You do not see the valley so much as you hear its dimensions—a vast, freezing basin miles across, bounded by high mountain ridges that catch the low clouds and wring them dry. There is no sun here, no moon, no artificial streetlamps. The only light is an occasional, accidental spark: a pale blue piezoelectric flash deep inside a shifting wall, or the faintest ghostly sheen where wet stone catches starlight filtered through three layers of cloud.

Yet the place is packed. Every cubic yard of the dark is claimed.

The valley is filled with a city that is still coming out of the ground. It has no builders in sight, no scaffolding, no rubble of demolition—only raw, unweathered geometry extruding upward from the mountain’s basalt bones at the pace of a growing fingernail. Sharp-edged towers, cantilevered bridges, and hexagonal conduits push out of the bedrock with edges so fresh they can slice leather. Nothing here has been rounded by wind or softened by age. The architecture is dense and multi-tiered, a vertical labyrinth of narrow alleys, stacked galleries, and sunken plazas that span the whole high-altitude bowl.

The engineering belongs to a physics that treats gravity and heat as optional. Massive slabs of dark, matte-ceramic alloy hang over the abyss without visible struts, held in place by silent, invisible tensions. Stranger still are the thermal sinks—slender, needle-like spires and recessed grates woven into every block—that continuously pull heat *out* of the surrounding air and dump it somewhere deeper than the crust. Because of them, the entire city sits just a breath above freezing. The thin mountain air cannot hold its moisture against the cold; it condenses constantly on every sharp edge, turning the whole metropolis into a weeping apparatus. Water sheets down the sheer, unpolished faces of the towers in thick, sluggish curtains, pools in the sunken tiers, and drips from every overhang in a relentless, freezing rain that never fell from the sky.

There is not a blade of grass, not a patch of moss, not a single root anywhere in the valley. The cold and the dark leave no room for leaves. Instead, the wet, mineral-rich surfaces support only a thin, slick film of black chemosynthetic slime—and upon that, an overwhelming, suffocating abundance of animal life.

The city is *loud*. Even before your eyes adjust to the nothingness, the sound hits you like a physical wall: a deafening, multi-layered roar that bounces off the unweathered stone until direction loses meaning. It is the sound of millions of bodies packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the freezing dark.

Because nothing can see, everything announces itself. The air vibrates with a constant, overlapping storm of clicks, rasps, and sub-bass bellows. Blind, heavy-shelled grazers the size of calves wedge themselves into the narrow, wet streets in vast, sluggish herds, scraping the black mineral film from the walls with serrated tongues. Their breaths come in synchronized, wet snorts that fog the frigid air. Above them, clinging to the undersides of the impossible cantilevers and packed three-deep inside the drainage conduits, are countless smaller things—pale, segmented climbers with rubbery, cold-proof hides and dozens of hooked limbs, chittering in rapid-fire echolocation bursts that merge into a sound like gravel pouring onto tin.

In the deeper, flooded tiers, larger shapes move through the briny, ice-cold runoff. You hear them before you feel their wake: long, slow exhalations through blowholes, followed by the heavy, booming *thrum* of territorial calls that rattle your teeth and set the sheets of falling water trembling.

Despite the noise and the crowding, nothing in the valley hurries. The cold dictates a terrible, patient economy of motion. The grazers take minutes to shift a single hoof along the slick stone. The climbers hang motionless for hours, letting the condensing water wash over their carapaces, conserving every calorie of chemical warmth. Even the city’s own processes—the slow, silent extrusion of a new spire from the bedrock, the gradual thickening of a mineral crust—unfold across decades.

You stand on a high, wet parapet, the freezing mist soaking through your clothes in seconds. The wind at this elevation is heavy and sluggish, carrying the sharp, metallic tang of raw stone, ozone from the thermal sinks, and the musk of a million damp, unseen hides. In the pitch black, a yard away, something large shifts its weight on the slick ledge, its carapace scraping softly against the razor-sharp wall, and lets out a long, clicking sigh into the roar of the valley.
