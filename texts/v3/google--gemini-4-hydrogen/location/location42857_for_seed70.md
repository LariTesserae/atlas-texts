High above the surrounding lowlands, cradled inside a jagged crown of unweathered basalt peaks, lies a drowned valley. The basin sits at an elevation where the air outside would be thin and biting, yet everything inside the rim is submerged beneath a vast, warm, freshwater sea.

Because the mountain bowl is geologically young, the submerged terrain has none of the soft, slumped curves of an old seabed. Looking out across the valley, the water is so clear—scrubbed free of silt by the sheer hardness of the stone and the absence of strong currents—that you can see for hundreds of meters through the bright, sunlit depths. The floor drops away in sharp, stepped terraces and knife-edged ridges of dark, obsidian-flecked rock. Steep slopes of freshly fractured talus rest at precarious angles, their corners still crisp and angular, as if the mountain shattered yesterday and the water rose immediately to lock the rubble in place.

Light pours down through the surface with the fierce, unfiltered clarity of high altitude. It illuminates the entire water column in a steady, warm, golden-green daylight, casting crisp, wavering shadows from the upper ridges onto the valley floor below. There are no crashing waves on the distant surface, no tides to churn the depths; the surrounding peaks block the wind, leaving the water in a state of near-total, bath-warm stillness. Deep beneath the fractured limestone and basalt, broad, lazy geothermal seepages keep the entire basin at an even, enveloping warmth—never hot enough to boil or vent violently, just enough to hold off the alpine chill.

Into this bright, motionless warmth, plant life has packed itself into every available cubic meter. The valley is a dense, layered labyrinth of strange, slow-growing flora. Rising from the sharp-edged terraces are vast stands of *bladder-stems*—tall, rigid, hollow-cored stalks the thickness of a human wrist, crowded so closely together that their translucent, amber-and-emerald fronds nearly touch. Each frond is studded with swollen, glassy bladders filled with trapped oxygen, holding the plants upright like a flooded forest of cypresses. 

Between the stalks, the water is hung with thick, overlapping curtains of silver-green filaments. They drape from stem to stem like wet silk, catching thousands of tiny, pearlescent oxygen bubbles that cling to the threads rather than rising, suspended in the sluggish, mineral-rich water. Below, the razor-sharp rocks are wrapped in a tight, velvety skin of deep crimson and chartreuse moss-like mats. The growth is thick and cushiony, yet it follows the geometry of the stone so faithfully that every jagged edge and fracture remains visible beneath the bright felt.

Almost nothing moves fast here. Currents are barely perceptible—a slow, patient thermal drift that shifts only as the highland sun warms the upper layers by day and lets them cool slightly at night. A detached leaf or a loosened pearl of gas takes minutes to drift the length of an arm. 

The place is nearly empty of animals. There are no darting schools of fish, no busy scavengers churning the floor. If you wait long enough, you might notice a solitary, pale-shelled grazer—segmented and translucent, the size of a loaf of bread—inching along a crimson rock-edge with glacial patience, or a single ribbon-like filter-feeder hanging motionless between two amber stalks, indistinguishable from the curtains of algae until it slowly coils a fraction tighter.

Because there is no surf, no rushing current, and almost no fauna, the valley is profoundly quiet. The water muffles everything into a heavy, velvet hush. Only occasionally does a single, tiny, glassy *plink* carry through the stillness, when an oxygen bladder finally tears free from a frond and begins its long, unhurried rise toward the bright sky far above.
