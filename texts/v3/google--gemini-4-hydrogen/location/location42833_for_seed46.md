High up on a nameless ridge, tucked into a crease of stone no wider than a courtyard, the mountain has forgotten how to be only rock.

From the outside, there is nothing to mark it—just a sharp, unweathered fold in the high-altitude crust, open to a bright, ordinary sky. Sunlight pours straight down into the hollow, clear and thin at this elevation, throwing razor-edged shadows across a floor that has never known rain. There is no moisture here at all. The air is scorching, bone-dry, and still, vibrating with a baked, radiative heat that rises off the stone like the breath of an open kiln.

The geology itself feels unfinished, as though it were extruded from the earth's mantle only yesterday and stopped mid-thought. Nothing has been rounded by wind or grit. The walls of the pocket are stepped in raw, glassy terraces of black basalt and pale quartz, their edges sharp enough to slice leather. In places, the stone still holds the frozen ripples of its cooling—bubbles of trapped gas, jagged mineral seams, and clean, geometric fracture lines that look less like broken rock and more like an unbuilt floor plan.

No one has ever built anything here. There are no walls, no rivets, no mortar, no tools, no ruins. Yet the pocket is unmistakably industrial.

It takes a moment for the eye to accept what the heat and the faint, dry hum in the air are already telling you. Along the center of the hollow, a thick shelf of raw magnetite rests inside a natural groove of mica. Moving at a pace too slow to catch in a single glance—perhaps a finger's width an hour—the magnetite slab is rotating. No axle holds it; no hand pushes it. It simply turns against the mica with an agonizing, patient torque, driven by the deep thermal expansion of the mountain below. As the two raw minerals grind together without lubricant, they generate a steady, dry electrical current that arcs invisibly into the surrounding stone.

Everywhere in the pocket, matter is performing scaled production without a factory. In shallow, sun-baked depressions, dry heat and stray voltage coax beads of pure bismuth and tin out of the raw ore; the molten droplets crawl toward the cooler edges of the rock and solidify into neat, identical, hexagonal wafers, stacking themselves in tidy, unguided rows along magnetic lines. There is no purpose to it, no consumer, no assembly—just a local breakdown in the rules of what rock is supposed to do.

Between these wide stretches of bare, humming mineral, the vegetation grows in sparse, extravagantly lush clusters.

There is no water for roots to drink, so the plants do not use water. They anchor directly into the hot cracks where the electrical charge is thickest, thriving on heat, dry static, and vaporized sulfur. Each cluster is a dense, intricate tangle of dark, metallic-green fronds and thick, waxy stems—lush in its own crowded architecture, though separated from the next clump by yards of empty, scorching stone. Their leaves are broad and pleated like heat-sinks, veined with threads of raw copper that drink the ambient current. When you brush past one—if you can stand the blister-hot surface of it—it does not feel wet or fibrous, but heavy and taut, filled with a thick, non-aqueous dielectric oil that smells faintly of hot resin and ozone.

At the centers of these botanical thickets, the plants send up tall, sparse stalks bearing clusters of translucent, ceramic-hard pods. Inside each pod, a tiny filament glows dull orange, slowly baking dry spores into uniform, carbonized pellets. Over the course of weeks, the pods split open with a quiet, dry *tick*, spilling their harvest onto the bare stone below, where the slow magnetic drift of the floor gathers them into neat, dark drifts.

There are no birds here, no beetles, no lizards darting between the copper-veined leaves. Nothing breathes, nothing hunts, nothing scurries. The only movement is the glacial turning of the stone rotors, the imperceptible unfurling of a heat-sink frond toward the midday sun, and the slow, steady drift of mineral dust settling into geometric lines.

It is almost entirely quiet. If you stand still in the bright, thin light, the heat pressing against your skin, you hear only the faintest sixty-cycle hum rising through the soles of your boots—the sound of an untouched, unbuilt world quietly running its own engines in the dark of a sunlit crack, making things no one will ever use.
