At the crest of a needle-sharp peak, well above the tree line and the lower ridges, the mountain has been hollowed out into a single, cathedral-sized stone eye.

It is not a cave so much as a natural lantern of dark, wind-polished gneiss. The roof has long since collapsed or been scoured away, leaving a jagged oculus open to the sky, while two tall, uneven arches pierce the eastern and western flanks. Between those walls, the place is almost entirely empty air. Standing on the narrow, curved sill of the western lip, there is nothing in front of you but a forty-foot drop to a sloping stone floor, and beyond that, sheer void—an unbroken plunge through drifting cloud-rags to the valleys miles below.

The air here is thin, cool, and relentlessly loud. High-altitude gales funnel through the western arch and roar upward through the roofless crown, turning the whole chamber into a vast, damp organ pipe. The wind does not merely blow; it thrums against the ribs of the stone in a deep, shifting bass note, punctuated by sharp, whistling gusts that tear through the upper crevices. Even when the main gale drops for a few heartbeats, the silence never settles. Water is always slapping onto stone, birds are always screaming, and the rock itself seems to hold a residual hum.

Everything solid is slick with moisture. Clouds roll straight through the western portal like smoke, brushing against the chilled gneiss and condensing into a steady, weeping film. Thin sheets of water slide down the curved interior walls, gathering in ancient, saucer-shaped basins worn into the stone. The rock is old and settled—its edges rounded by millennia of freeze, thaw, and grit-laden wind—so the water follows well-established channels, spilling from lip to lip in bright, erratic curtains.

In the center of the chamber, there is nothing at all—only the rushing air and the occasional diagonal shaft of crisp, high-altitude sunlight breaking through the cloud deck. Yet along the boundaries, wherever the wet stone catches the light and escapes the worst of the blast, life clings in thick, lush profusion.

The ledges and arch-lips are upholstered in dense, springy mats of deep-green and rust-colored moss, swollen with cloud-water like soaked sponges. Trailing curtains of silver-green beard lichen hang from the undersides of the stone ribs, streaming horizontally in the updraft. Wedged into every crack are tough, fleshy rosette plants with translucent, bladder-like leaves that trap mist; when the sun hits them directly, the taut skins warm and occasionally pop with a soft, wet snap, releasing a tiny bead of water onto the moss below.

Despite the emptiness of the central void, the place teems with frantic, cyclical motion. Hundreds of dark, sickle-winged swifts nest in the pitted pockets of the upper walls, erupting from the stone in chattering clouds to snatch the pale cloud-flies that breed in the soggy moss. On the broader ledges, stout, thick-furred rock-marmots bark sharp warnings at each other while cropping the fleshy rosettes, darting back into stone crevices the moment a shadow crosses the oculus. A pair of pale-bellied falcons rides the vertical thermal just outside the eastern arch, hovering nearly motionless against the gale before folding their wings to stoop straight down through the empty center of the chamber, scattering the swifts in a burst of shrill, echoing cries.

Weather passes through in minutes. A bright, dazzling squall of sun turns the weeping walls to silver and sets the mist steaming off the moss; then another dense cloud-bank slams into the mountain, plunging the chamber into cool, swirling grey, drenching the lichens anew, and driving the insects back into the turf.

Human presence here is faint, old, and sparse—little more than a few marks left by people who knew how to work with stone, wood, and bone, and who did not stay long. Tucked into the leeward curve of the southern wall sits a low, dry-stone windbreak, its flat rocks wedged tight with dried moss. A single weathered ladder of mountain-ash poles, lashed with blackened sinew and anchored into the gneiss with hammered copper pins, climbs twenty feet up to a sheltered niche. Inside the niche lies a small, cold hearth-ring of charred juniper twigs, long since washed pale by the damp.

High up in the eastern arch, someone has strung a line of hollowed horn and bird-bone tubes across a narrow gap in the stone. They are green with lichen and frayed at the bindings, but they still work: whenever the wind shifts to the northeast, the bones clatter against the rock and emit a thin, reedy chorus that cuts right through the low roar of the stone, echoing out into the empty sky.
