Miles above the lower world, there is a plateau so vast it bends with the curve of the planet, and so old that its mountains have long since forgotten they were ever sharp. Eons of patient rain and wind have ground the bedrock down into a gently rolling, continental shelf—a tableland of near-flatness suspended in the warm upper air. You cannot see the edges of it from anywhere within; the horizon is always just a hundred paces away, swallowed by a thick, motionless haze.

Yet you never touch the stone. The rock is buried—thirty meters down, or three hundred—beneath an unbroken, living mattress of vegetation that has been growing over itself for millennia. There is no soil here, no sand, no bare mineral earth. The ground is entirely plant: a dense, waterlogged architecture of interlocking roots, compressed peat, and ancient, rubbery stems that have half-turned to amber-colored lignite while still veined with pale, living fungal threads. When you step, the surface gives way like a soaked sponge. Clear, warm water wells up around your ankles, lingers, and is quietly drunk back down by the silvery bryophytes when you lift your foot.

Above this endless mat, the world is dim and permanently warm, held in a muggy, greenhouse stillness. A double ceiling hangs overhead. First, the plateau’s own high-altitude cloud deck, which never quite clears; and just beneath it, the Overweave—a continental canopy of broad, leathery, overlapping fronds that interlock like the scales of a sleeping beast. No direct sunlight ever reaches the floor. Instead, the light filters down in a bruised, olive-and-tea twilight, shadowless and diffuse, the color of deep pondwater. 

Because the plateau is so high, the warm vapor rising from the world below condenses directly onto the billions of waxy leaves and hair-fringed vines of the canopy. It does not storm here. There is no wind to speak of—only a slow, humid drift that takes days to cross a shallow basin. Instead, the air itself weeps. A constant, unhurried rain of heavy droplets falls from the Overweave, *plinking* into dark, mirror-still pools that gather in the gentle dips of the bog, or *patting* softly onto the broad leaves of the mid-story.

The plants here do not grow like trees; they are bundled, braided columns of hollow, cartilage-soft tubes—siphon-reeds that drink from the mist above rather than the ground below, pumping water downward in slow, rhythmic tides. Between them hang curtains of pale aerial roots and strange, translucent epiphytes: clusters of water-filled bladders, swollen like cloudy grapes, that act as lenses to focus the dim light onto tiny, glowing green cores within. The air is thick with them, layered and near-touching, so that moving through the plateau feels less like walking through a forest and more like wading through the submerged, fibrous interior of a single, planet-sized organism.

Despite the density of life, it is a profoundly quiet place. The wet moss and soft, porous bark swallow sound before it can travel. There are no snapping twigs—nothing here is dry enough to snap—and no rustling leaves. The loudest noise is the occasional, deep *groan* of a massive, water-swollen reed-column shifting its weight over the course of an hour, or the soft, wet *suck* of something lifting its foot from the peat.

And yet the fauna is everywhere, abundant and unhurried, adapted to a world without haste or sharp edges. Because it is too dim for sight-hunting and too tangled for speed, the creatures here are soft-bodied, silent, and slow. Along the undulating cushions of the floor crawl herds of wide, low-slung grazers, their backs so thickly overgrown with the same silvery moss and tiny ferns as the ground that they look like shifting hillocks. They move at a crawl, scraping the soft outer skin from the siphon-reeds with rasp-like mouths, communicating only through faint, musky scents that hang in the damp air. Above them, in the dim space between the columns, broad-winged drifters glide on paper-thin, veined membranes—not flapping, just riding the faint rising warmth of the composting floor below, trailing thread-thin feelers to catch floating spores. Beneath the surface, in the warm, dark water-channels of the peat, blind, pale segmented things tunnel endlessly, keeping the ancient mat alive.

Change here is measured in decades. A fallen vine does not rot away in a season; it lies where it drops, slowly wrapped in pale root-threads over thirty years until it becomes just another rib in the floor.

Scattered through this endless, biological stillness are the faintest marks of something built—though nothing here knows the use of a tool. Now and then, braided into the fork of two siphon-reeds, you find a hollow, woven chamber: living shoots bent and knotted together with teeth or nimble claws over many years, fused into a dry-ish, teardrop-shaped alcove just big enough for a crouching body. They are always empty when you look inside, smelling faintly of wet bark and old musk. And once in a great while, where a shallow pool has worn thin, a smooth, soap-soft nub of ancient granite pokes an inch above the moss—a rounded, unrecognizable fragment of something stacked or carved before the plants swallowed the world, now just another slick, green stone for the tree-snails to cross.
