High above the world—so high that the distant plains below are lost in a flat, featureless smear of heat-haze—the mountain no longer has a peak. Whatever jagged violence first thrust this granite massif into the thin upper air ended ages ago. Eons of wind, grit, and the slow expansion and contraction of warm stone have ground the summit into a cluster of smooth, elephantine domes, rounded as river cobbles and pitted with ancient, honeycomb hollows. 

Tucked into the saddle between three of these worn domes lies a single sheltered bowl, no wider than a cathedral’s nave. On two sides, the granite swells upward into smooth, overhanging walls; on the other two, the world simply ends in sheer, dizzying drops. 

Inside this tight stone hollow, there is no water—no spring, no trickle, no dampness in the cracks. The granite is bone-dry, radiating a steady, baking warmth absorbed from the upper sky and fed by slow thermal updrafts rising from the valleys miles below. Yet the bowl is lush, choked with a dense, dusty-green abundance that clings to every ledge and crevice. Thick, fleshy rosettes of silver-jade succulents wedge themselves into the bedrock’s natural pockets. Curtains of gray-green air-plants, fibrous and dry as horsehair, hang in dense beards from the overhangs, drinking only the invisible ghost of humidity that rises on the evening thermal. Gnarled, slow-growing cliff-figs and thorny caper-bushes twist out of hairline fractures, their roots thicker than their trunks, their leaves waxy and tough. Every flat surface of the ancient stone is crusted with velvet-thick mats of ochre and slate-colored lichen.

Because the surrounding domes rise high and close, and because a permanent veil of high-altitude dust dims the sky, the hollow sits in a perpetual, warm twilight. Direct sunlight never reaches the floor. The light is the color of smoked amber and old pewter, broken only by the small, steady flames of tallow-wick lamps set into recessed stone niches.

Into this dim, bounded pocket, an entire town has been packed—layered, stacked, and wedged into the rock until the built and the natural are nearly indistinguishable. Multi-story dwellings of meticulously fitted, mortarless dry-stone rise four and five tiers up the curving walls, their backs formed by the living granite itself. Narrow stairways, worn smooth as soapstone by generations of bare feet and sandals, switchback between the roofs. Woven-hemp bridges and lashed-timber catwalks span the gaps between overhangs. There is no iron here, only stone, sun-baked clay plaster, bone needles, hammered copper rivets, and gnarled wood shaped by hand-adzes.

It is a loud, crowded place. The stone bowl acts as an amphitheater, catching every sound and bouncing it off the rounded walls until the air hums. Thousands of cliff-swallows nest in the clay-plastered eaves, their wings whirring and their high, sharp chatter never fully stopping. Fat, blunt-nosed rock-hyraxes bark from the succulent-choked ledges, competing with the dry, metallic rasping of cicadas hidden in the thorny scrub. Small, nimble goats with bells of hammered copper tied to their necks clatter across the stone roofs, and lizards dart along the warm lintels. 

Beneath the animal din runs the steady, rhythmic noise of human density: the *thock-thock* of wooden looms in the dim doorways, the heavy *grind-grind* of stone pestles crushing roasted roots and dry seeds in bedrock mortars, the low murmur of voices calling across alleys barely wide enough for two people to pass shoulder to shoulder. 

Nothing here moves fast. In the thin, warm air, breath is conserved and gestures are economical. Moisture is gathered with infinite patience: fine nets of woven goat-hair are stretched across the lip of the chasm to comb the faint, pre-dawn mist from the rising updrafts, coaxing a few precious drops into clay amphorae before the dry heat returns. The plants grow millimeters in a decade; the stone settles at the pace of continents; the town itself feels less like something built upon the rock and more like a slow, dense crust of life that has taken centuries to thicken inside the mountain's oldest, quietest scar.
