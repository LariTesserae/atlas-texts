Up here, the sky has the pale, rinsed clarity of high altitude, and the light is ordinary—bright, cool, and uncolored, the kind of daylight that leaves no excuse for what you are seeing.

The plateau stretches until the curvature of the world gives up. It is a continental shelf of primordial stone, lifted kilometers into the thin air and then left there for so long that every mountain has been ground down to its final, stubborn ribs. There are no jagged peaks, no dramatic cliffs—only an endless, gently undulating expanse of frost-shattered slate and dark basalt, smoothed by eons of wind into broad, shallow bowls and long, low terraces. Across this vastness, the weather makes itself. Great, flat-bottomed banks of cold fog drift slowly from one horizon to the other, trailing skirts of mist that catch on the stone ridges and leave everything damp to the touch.

Most of it is empty. You can walk for days across the bare, ancient rock—pale gray with lichen, dusted with dry rime in the mornings—without seeing anything taller than your ankle. The air is cold enough to stiffen your fingers, thin enough to make every slight rise feel like a climb, and so quiet that the crunch of your own boots on gravel sounds like an intrusion.

Then, without warning, the stone dips into a wide basin, and the world thickens.

The basins are where the moisture settles, and they are startlingly lush. Because there is no soil to speak of—only crushed slate and centuries of compacted peat—the plants have learned to anchor themselves directly into the rock and drink from the air. Giant cushion-mosses, dark as wet iron and dense enough to bear weight, swell over the boulders like overturned boats. Above them rise thickets of tall, hollow-stemmed reeds and broad-leafed, waxy ferns whose edges are fringed with fine, glassy hairs. When a fog bank rolls through the basin, these hairs comb the mist out of the cold air. Within minutes, the entire thicket begins to weep. Clear, icy water beads along the fronds and patters down onto the slate, gathering into black, slow-moving channels that thread through the greenery.

There are no animals here. No birds cross the huge, pale sky; no insects hum in the damp moss; nothing rustles in the fern-brakes or darts through the water channels. The silence has a peculiar, hollow weight to it, as if the world were a room whose furniture had been removed. Yet the basins are not still. In the absence of beasts, the plants keep their own brisk, silent pace. As the midday sun warms the dark leaves by a few degrees, thick-walled seed pods twist open with tiny, dry *snaps*. Pale, rubbery fungi swell along the damp edges of the peat, drink the morning condensation, and collapse back into clear water by afternoon. The mosses creep visibly over the course of a season, their advancing edges lifting flat stones like slow, green levers.

And built directly into these lush, wet hollows are the cities.

They are sprawling, tightly organized settlements of dry-stacked slate, adze-hewn timber, and hammered copper—cities built with hand-tools and patience, layered over centuries. Narrow, flagstone streets step down the contours of the basins, crossed by stone-lined aqueducts and wooden sluice-gates. Multi-story houses with steep, woven-reed roofs lean over the canals, their timber frames pegged together with oak pins and bound at the joints with green-patinated copper straps. There are market plazas paved in herringbone basalt, terraced gardens retained by mortarless walls so tightly fitted a knife blade cannot slip between the stones, and squat, clay-tiled kilns and workshops lining the outer edges.

Everything is in order. Everything is active. And no one is there.

At first, the absence feels like a recent evacuation—as if the inhabitants had just stepped indoors. A bronze chisel rests beside a half-carved door lintel, the stone dust still dry and unblown on the workbench. On a wooden pier above a canal, a hemp rope tied to a copper-bound bucket dips slowly into the black water, rises on a creaking wooden windlass, and tips its load into a stone trough. No hand turns the crank. The windlass simply moves, pauses, and resets, its wet hemp cordage glistening in the normal, cold daylight.

If you stay in one of the cities for more than an hour, you begin to notice that the rules holding the world together have worn thin—as eroded as the ancient stone beneath your feet.

The water in the stone aqueducts does not always obey the slope. In the lower quarters, a channel will carry condensation-water briskly *uphill* for two hundred paces, silent and unhurried, before spilling over a weir into a basin of iron-dark ferns. Shadows lag behind the drifting fog banks; you can stand in a square of bright, cold sunlight while the cloud that cast a shadow over it has already drifted half a mile away, leaving the darkness behind like a forgotten coat.

In the older districts, the boundary between what was built and what grew has frayed entirely. A dry-stone retaining wall runs straight and true for thirty yards, then its courses of slate begin to soften, branching seamlessly into the fibrous, living root-mat of a giant cushion-moss without a seam where mineral ends and vegetable begins. Elsewhere, the ancient bedrock beneath a plaza has begun to peel upward in thin, translucent sheets, like the pages of a wet book, revealing not deeper rock underneath, but the underside of the same plaza’s cobblestones, upside down.

Distance itself is unreliable across the vast, flat expanse. From the rim of one basin, you can see the slate roofs and reed-thatch of another city five miles away across the barren rock, clear as glass in the thin air. Yet if you walk toward it, you may pass through its outer kilns twice before reaching its central square—or find that the street you entered by is three hundred years older on its left side than on its right, both sides freshly swept, both sides damp with the same cold mist.

Every few hours, a new front of fog rolls in from the vast emptiness beyond the basin. The air drops another degree. The glassy hairs on the ferns catch the mist and begin to drip. Inside the empty workshops, wooden counterweights shift with a muffled *thud*. Somewhere down a terraced alley, a sluice-gate drops into place with a wet *clack*, redirecting the uphill water toward a freshly sprouted bed of reeds. And then the quiet settles back in—vast, cold, brilliantly lit, and quietly coming apart at the seams.
