It is a place that feels like the roof of the world, if the roof had been planed flat by wind and time until nothing sharp remained, and then carpeted, edge to edge, with a single, living skin.

Miles above the unseen lowlands, the plateau stretches out in a gentle, uninterrupted swell. The underlying stone—ancient granite and pale, pitted limestone—has been worn down over eons into a vast, near-level tableland. You rarely see the rock itself. Only here and there does a smooth, glass-polished dome of it break the surface, like the back of a whale breaching a green sea. Everywhere else, the ground is not earth at all, but vegetation: a thick, springy, millennia-old weave of fibrous roots, pale-green cushion-moss, and tough, silica-ribbed sedge. It is a continuous botanical mat, yards deep in places, stretched taut over the worn-down ribs of the mountain. 

Because the mat bridges the ancient, eroded hollows of the stone beneath, the landscape is strangely hollow underfoot. Each step sends a faint, muffled vibration through the turf, as if you were walking across the skin of an immense, loosely strung drum. Beneath the living surface lie dark, dry, wind-scoured voids where nothing grows—empty chambers between the root-pillars and the polished bedrock. Above, the world is breathtakingly open. There are no forests to block the eye, no cliffs rising overhead; the horizon is a crisp, distant line twenty miles away, where the plateau simply drops off into bright air.

Despite the extreme altitude, the air is surprisingly warm. A steady, gentle heat radiates upward from the slow, ongoing decay and metabolism of the deep root-mat, trapped beneath its dense surface weave and bolstered by warm thermal drafts that climb the sheer outer cliffs from the valleys far below. The light is clear, bright, and unfiltered, casting sharp shadows across the pale, silver-green turf. Moisture arrives not in rivers or deep lakes, but in the slow, damp breath of those rising updrafts. In the early hours, warm mist curls over the plateau’s rim and condenses in beads along the waxy hairs of the sedges. Occasional afternoon rains sweep across the flat expanse in brief, heavy curtains, vanishing almost instantly into the porous turf, leaving behind only shallow, tea-colored pools in the rare stone basins before the sun and wind drink them dry.

Vertical features are sparse, separated by vast, empty stretches of wind-scoured air. Here and there, solitary stands of giant, hollow-stemmed reeds—pale as bleached bone and thirty feet tall—rise from the mat, spaced half a mile apart like lonely masts. In the wide spaces between them, there is nothing to break the wind.

Yet for all its openness, the plateau is neither quiet nor empty of life. The sound hits you first—a relentless, deafening roar that presses against your ears from every direction. High-altitude gales sweep unimpeded across the flat plain, booming through the hollow chambers beneath the turf and whistling across the fractured tops of the tall reeds. Layered over the wind is a staggering, uninterrupted wall of biological noise: the high, metallic rattle of millions of copper-shelled locusts, the shrill territorial whistles of burrowers, and the dry, continuous *chitter* of things moving just beneath the moss. To speak to someone standing an arm’s length away, you have to shout.

Every square foot of the living floor is teeming. The fauna is overwhelming—not in size, for large beasts are rare in this open, sparse-featured expanse, but in sheer, inescapable density. Part the sedge-blades with your boot and dozens of pale, long-legged beetles and slick-skinned lizards scatter into the weave. Small, long-eared grazers dart in nervous, erratic bursts between the hollow root-vents, vanishing into the dark spaces beneath the mat whenever the shadow of a raptor passes overhead. Vast, shifting ribbons of swifts and harrier-hawks wheel in the bright sky, riding the warm updrafts, while lean, rust-colored predators—all muscle and long stride—prowl the open distances, their ears twitching at the constant, deafening thrum of prey beneath their feet.

Scattered across this loud, vibrating plain are the faint, rare marks of people who have learned to live lightly on the skin of the world. There are no towns, no stone walls, and no deep foundations—to dig too far is to break through into the hollow dark below. Instead, you find occasional, low-slung shelters woven from split reed-cane and cured hide, shaped like overturned boats to shed the wind and pegged directly into the tough root-mat with carved bone. Near the rare stone domes where rainwater collects, simple, hand-worked cairns and shallow catch-basins sit beside racks of drying locust-meat and bundles of harvested fiber.

Thin, springy paths—worn down only by generations of bare feet and padded boots—connect these scattered camps, marked by tall poles hung with hammered-copper clappers and bleached skulls that clatter faintly against the greater roar of the wind. Life here is manual, sparse, and constantly in motion: mending the woven roofs, tapping the hollow reed-towers for trapped condensation, and reading the subtle changes in the ground’s vibration—listening through the soles of one’s feet for the shift of a storm, or the rush of a hunting pack beneath the moss.
