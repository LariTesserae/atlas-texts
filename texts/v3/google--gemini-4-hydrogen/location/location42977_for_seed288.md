The first thing that meets you is not the sight of the place, but the weight of its noise. It hits like a physical wall—a continuous, deafening roar that vibrates in the teeth and sternum before you can even pick out its parts.

You are deep below the surrounding plain, inside a vast, sunken limestone basin. High overhead, the rim of the world narrows to ragged, distant slots of pale gray sky, letting down a cool, dim twilight that barely reaches the floor. The air is chilly and bone-dry. There is no dampness in it, no smell of moss or wet earth—only powdered chalk, dry guano, and the sharp, papery scent of desiccated leaves.

Spread across the wide, flat valley floor are the remains of a city, though it is laid out with generous, almost lonely spacing. Clusters of stepped stone terraces, squat towers, and multi-tiered galleries rise out of the dust, separated by broad, empty plazas and wide avenues of bare, scoured bedrock. Nothing here was built with metal or mortar. It is all dry-laid limestone and packed earth, stacked and fitted by hand long ago. Millennia of dry grit carried on slow drafts have worn every sharp angle away. Staircases have been ground down into smooth, undulating ramps; lintels have slumped into gentle curves; entire walls look less like cut masonry and more like river-smoothed bone. 

Despite the total absence of surface water—not a puddle, not a seep, not even a dark stain on the rock—the basin is surprisingly lush. Between the scattered stone quarters, thick islands of vegetation spill over the sunken walls and choke the dry avenues. These are tough, patient plants: gnarled dryland figs, wiry salt-cedars, and dense curtains of leathery, pale-green creepers that drape from the upper galleries like heavy tapestries. They survive by sending massive, pale taproots straight down through hairline fractures in the bedrock, drinking from unseen aquifers trapped far beneath the city’s foundations, leaving the surface above completely parched.

Every hollow in the ancient stone, and every dense thicket of dry foliage, is packed with life. The deafening roar comes from millions of creatures sharing the dim, cool shelter. Vast colonies of bats and cave-swiftlets carpet the vaulted ceilings of the abandoned towers, their constant, overlapping clicks, chirps, and dry wingbeats echoing off the smooth, curved masonry. Beneath them, in the dusty scrub and cracks of the plazas, countless crickets, cicadas, and beetles stridulate without pause. Rock-doves clatter through the upper arches; lizards and small, sandy-furred mammals dart through the root-tangles. Because the stone bowl is dry and hard-walled, no sound is absorbed. Every rasp, flutter, and shriek bounces endlessly across the open plazas, merging into a single, relentless acoustic pressure that never drops below a shout.

Time moves with an almost imperceptible slowness here. There are no storms, no rushing floods to carve new channels, no sudden shifts in the cool, stagnant air. Over decades, a terrace settles another millimeter; a root slowly pries apart a dry-laid joint; a fine layer of limestone dust drifts across an empty square. Under the dim, unchanging light, the city and its noisy, thriving tenants simply persist—settled into a final, deafening equilibrium that feels as old as the rock itself.
