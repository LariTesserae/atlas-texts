High above the cloud line, where a freshly thrust ridge of black basalt and raw volcanic glass splits the thin sky, there is a crease in the rock no wider than a doorway. From ten paces away it looks like nothing—just a jagged shadow beneath an overhang of unweathered stone, sharp-edged as a broken tooth.

Inside, the mountain opens into a single, cramped basin the size of a small room, and the world’s rules come quietly unpinned.

The first thing that hits is the heat. It is not the dry bake of high-altitude sun, nor the roaring blast of a volcanic vent, but a dense, suffocating, wet heat that clings to the skin like a scalding compress. Clear, ordinary daylight pours through the narrow slit of the entrance, illuminating everything without drama or shadow. Yet the air inside feels thick as oil. The rocks—young, glassy, and razor-edged, untouched by wind or centuries—sweat continuously. Water weeps from hairline fractures in the ceiling and walls, beading into fat, trembling droplets that are hot enough to blister.

They do not fall the way water should. Some beads detach and drift downward at the speed of settling dust, hanging in the stifling air as trembling lenses that magnify the stone behind them. Others slide *upward* along the sheer, slick faces of obsidian before pooling in shallow, scalding saucers near the rim of the ceiling. There is no hiss of steam, no patter of drips, no roar of boiling. The pocket is almost completely silent. Even when a heavy drop finally breaks and splashes into a lower pool, the sound arrives like a dull tap through thick felt, swallowed instantly by the air.

No moss softens these edges. No lichen stains the stone, no root pries at the cracks. The only non-mineral growth is a thin, slick film of pale sulfur-slime that coats the wettest ledges, shimmering faintly like grease on hot broth.

And every inch of that slick, scalding stone is crawling.

The basin is packed—layered, crowded, near-touching—with thousands of small, heat-gorged creatures. Most are pale, wet-skinned things no larger than a human hand, shaped somewhere between a salamander and a blind, multi-jointed prawn, with translucent carapaces that show the slow pulse of dark organs beneath. Others are flatter, plated in dull bronze-colored scales, clinging to the weeping ceiling like ticks. Tiny, thread-thin parasites weave through the shallow pools in knots of hundreds. The sheer volume of life presses against the walls; when one creature shifts, a ripple of movement passes through thirty others.

Despite the crowding, there is no chittering, no scrabbling roar, no wet slap of bodies. Their frantic, ceaseless motion happens in near-silence. You hear only the faintest dry *tick* of shell brushing shell, or the soft, almost imperceptible *shhh* of a wet belly sliding over glass.

Nothing here is built. There are no burrows, no stacked pebbles, no woven nests or altered stone—the rock remains raw and untouched as the day it tore out of the earth. Yet the creatures are not without craft. Scattered across the wet ledges are thousands of naturally cleaved splinters of obsidian and sharp mineral flakes, and the fauna use them with a brisk, unsentimental precision. A pale, six-limbed crawler will snatch up a curved sliver of black glass, wedge it under the plated rim of a ceiling-clinger, and pry it loose with a silent twist of its body. Another holds a flat, palm-sized flake of basalt above itself like an umbrella to deflect a slow-falling bead of scalding water, then drops the stone the instant the danger passes. Smaller scavengers use needle-thin mineral shards to crack open the shed carapaces of their neighbors and scrape out the soft residue inside, discarding the tool in the same motion. Everything is improvised, used once, and abandoned on the slick floor.

If you watch them long enough, the wrongness of the pocket settles behind your eyes.

The creatures do not always move continuously from one point to the next. A predator lunging across a wet ledge will flicker, appearing two inches ahead of itself without crossing the space between, leaving a faint, wet smudge of a shadow that takes a second to catch up. In the shallow, hot pools, reflections do not obey the surface: a crawler perched on the rim looks down at its own reflection, and the reflection is already turned the other way, or multiplied into three overlapping shapes, or missing entirely.

The geometry of the basin itself refuses to hold still. Though you can see the far wall clearly in the normal daylight—just a few arm-lengths away—the space between the entrance and that wall feels stretched, or folded. A droplet falling from the ceiling takes too long to reach a pool that looks ankle-deep, and the swarms of creatures layered along the back face seem slightly smaller than they should be, as if viewed through the wrong end of a lens, even as they crowd right up against the front ledges.

Outside the slit, the wind of the high peaks scours the raw granite, cold and thin and loud. Inside this hidden, wet blister of stone, none of it reaches. The heat stays trapped, the water drifts and weeps in silence, and thousands of pale, tool-wielding things hunt, pry, molt, and flicker through their dense, scalding, impossible room.
