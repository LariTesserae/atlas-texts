At the top of a drowned mountain range, beneath a ceiling of iron-grey water, lies a valley built entirely of stone.

It is cold—near-freezing, heavy, and sharp against any exposed skin—and it is dim, lit only by the thin, bruised light that survives a hundred feet of storm-churned surface water. Above, the air is thin and bitter; down here, the pressure is modest compared to the ocean’s abyss, yet the world feels immense and sealed off. No natural bedrock shows anywhere. As far as the murky visibility allows, the terrain is paved, terraced, and retained: vast plazas of fitted slate, wide stepped causeways, sunken amphitheaters, and miles-long embankments that step upward toward unseen ridges.

Everything here was made, and everything here has been wearing down for so long that the making is almost forgotten. There is no metal, no glass, no pipe or gear or sign of tool-use—only dressed stone, petrified lime-mortar, and blocks of dark basalt laid dry without cement. Millennia of cold water have ground every right angle into a gentle curve. Stairs have melted into rippled ramps. The lintels of wide, roofless galleries are rounded like river pebbles, and whatever reliefs were once carved into the freestanding monoliths have been erased to faint, undulating ghosts. The architecture is open and sparse: huge, empty expanses of flagstone separate clusters of ruined colonnades and low, thick-walled cisterns.

Yet the place is never still. Though the water muffles almost everything into a heavy, pressure-thickened quiet, the valley is in a state of ceaseless, violent motion.

Fierce, erratic currents sweep across the terraces like invisible weather. Driven by freezing meltwater spilling from higher basins and the constant churning of storms overhead, the water shears in conflicting directions—an icy upwelling boiling out of a sunken stairwell, meeting a horizontal gale that tears across the plazas. Clouds of pale, chalky silt erupt from the floor without warning, billowing like smoke to swallow an entire colonnade, then thinning just as suddenly to reveal a different stretch of stone. Nothing settles for long. Every few hours, somewhere in the dim distance, an ancient retaining wall—its mortar finally dissolved after ten thousand years—gives way in a slow-motion avalanche of rounded boulders. No crash reaches the ear, only a dull, heavy thump felt in the sternum, followed by the soft, dry hiss of sliding gravel and a fresh bloom of silt.

No plants grow in this cold, churning dark. Not a shred of kelp, moss, or weed clings to the masonry. In the lee of broad pillars, where the current eddies into brief pockets of calm, a thin, pale film of cold-water bacteria coats the stone, making the worn flags treacherous and slick. That, and the mineral crusts wedged into the joints, is all the stillness gets to keep.

Life here belongs to the current. The fauna is abundant, though scattered across the wide emptiness in sudden, dense gatherings. Pale, eyeless crustaceans with flat, armored shells pack themselves by the hundreds into the eroded seams between flagstones, extending feathery fans into the passing grit to filter out microscopic scraps. Long, ribbon-bodied eels—translucent and grey-white—ride the violent shear-zones between opposing currents, undulating effortlessly in place before darting through the exact, eroded doorways of sunken halls as if following routes memorized before the valley flooded. 

Out in the open water above the plazas, vast shoals of flat, silver-bellied fish appear out of the silt-fog, hundreds strong, wheeling in tight, silent unison around a solitary pylon before vanishing back into the grey. Translucent jellies, torn and trailing pale tentacles, are dragged sideways by the undertow like scraps of wet cloth. Every creature here is built for cold, dimness, and impact—thick-skinned, low-slung, or supple enough to bend when the water slams them against the smoothed stone.

The ruins themselves have become the weather’s instrument. Narrow culverts and sunken channels accelerate the flow into silent, freezing jets that scour the flagstones bare; wide courtyards trap the currents into slow, grinding vortices that carve circular hollows into the ancient paving. You can drift for a mile over flat, featureless slate, seeing nothing but dim grey water and the occasional passing shoal, and then suddenly pass over the lip of a drowned aqueduct where the floor drops away into a churning, silt-choked trench—a landscape of pure geometry, endlessly battered by a silent, freezing storm that has lasted longer than memory.
