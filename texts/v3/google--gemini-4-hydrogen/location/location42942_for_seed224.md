Tucked into the windward shoulder of a high, dark ridge—so far up the air feels thin and brittle in the throat—there is a hollow no larger than a small, low-ceilinged room. From the outside it is invisible, masked by an overhang of sheared slate that has been worn smooth by centuries of dry, freezing gales. Stepping under the lip of the stone is like slipping into a pocket sewn into the lining of the mountain.

Inside, the wind stops completely. It does not howl past the entrance or eddy against the back wall; it simply fails to enter, as if the threshold were sealed by a pane of invisible, motionless air.

The light here is dim and flat, the color of tarnished pewter. A narrow slit where the overhang meets the cliff face lets in a dull, high-altitude twilight, though the illumination inside the pocket never quite matches the angle of the opening. Shadows pool in the wrong directions—short, blurry, and slightly detached from the stones that cast them. The cold is sharp and bone-dry, the kind of deep chill that draws moisture out of skin without leaving frost behind. There is no water here, not even a glaze of ice in the crevices; only bare, dark metamorphic rock and a fine, pale-gray mineral dust that has settled into the hollows of the floor.

At first glance, the chamber looks like any weathered cleft in a high peak. The walls are banded gneiss and slate, their sharp fractures long since blunted and rounded by time. Yet the longer you look at the corners where the walls meet the floor, the less they behave like rock. The angles do not add up. Three walls meet at a point that feels both acute and obtuse, and the mineral grain of the stone curves smoothly across the seam without breaking, as though the mountain itself had been folded like heavy cloth and then left to harden for ten thousand years.

Scattered sparsely across the floor are the only signs that anything was ever made here. Near the center, two knee-high pylons rise directly out of the bedrock—not bolted or cemented, but grown or extruded seamlessly from the stone. They are faceted, roughly hexagonal, and made of a matte, lightless material that refuses to catch the dim glow from the slit. Touching one yields no sensation of texture, and no transfer of heat; it feels neither cold nor warm, only absent, like pressing a palm against a gap in the world. Around the bases of the pylons, faint, hair-thin grooves have been scored into the rock in concentric, broken arcs, half-filled with dust and partially erased by slow exfoliation of the stone.

Nothing lives here. There are no droppings, no feathers, no insect husks tucked into the cracks. Even the faintest trace of animal passage is absent. In the lee of the smaller pylon, a dry, pale-ochre crust clings to the bedrock—a sparse, branching pattern of microscopic silicate needles that looks like dead lichen at a distance, though it is entirely mineral. It grows by drawing trace atoms out of the rock along the faint gradients left by the pylons, adding perhaps the width of a hair every few decades.

Sound dies almost instantly inside the pocket. A footstep makes a dull, flat *tap* with no echo, swallowed by the thin air and the deadened geometry of the walls. The silence is heavy and complete, broken only on a slow, patient cycle.

Once every hour or so—though time itself feels slightly viscous here, difficult to measure—a faint, sub-audible pressure shifts through the room. You do not hear it with your ears; you feel it as a brief, cold ache behind the teeth. For a few seconds, the fine mineral dust on the floor lifts half an inch into the air. It does not swirl or scatter. It hangs suspended in a rigid, three-dimensional lattice of invisible lines, briefly revealing the ghostly, geometric scaffolding of whatever machinery is still idling beneath the stone. 

Then the pressure eases. The dust drifts back down, settling into the worn grooves with no sound at all, and the pocket returns to its cold, dim stillness.
