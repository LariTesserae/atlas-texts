The whole place fits inside a single horseshoe hollow tucked high on a mountain shoulder—no wider than a long bowshot from one weeping limestone rim to the other, yet deep enough that its canopy forms a world of its own.

Everything here is cool and soaked. The air has the sharp, clean chill of high altitude, carrying the smell of crushed fern, wet wool, woodsmoke, and cold mineral runoff. Bright mountain daylight drops straight into the bowl, breaking through drifting ribbons of mist to strike wet leaves and slick timber in shifting, silver-green shafts. There is no stillness in the light, because nothing below it stops moving.

No bare earth shows anywhere in the hollow. The ground—if it can be called that—is a springy, multi-layered mattress of ancient roots, sodden peat, and waist-deep bracken, woven over centuries into a living floor that gives slightly underfoot. From this green, dripping base rise colossal, moss-furred cloud-oaks and dense stands of thick highland cane, their crowns interlocking overhead to roof the basin in a chaotic lattice of branches, hanging lichens, and broad, water-catching leaves. Water is everywhere: it sheets down the dark rock walls at the back of the hollow, pools in the cupped bases of giant ferns, drips steadily from every leaf-tip, and races in cold, clear threads through the root-tangles toward a narrow notch at the basin’s lip.

Into this wet tangle, a whole settlement has been wedged, lashed, and woven. It does not sit *on* the grove; it inhabits the volume of it, stacked three and four tiers deep between the trunks. Walkways of split cedar and braided cane wind around massive boughs, slick with green algae and scored with cross-cuts so boots can find purchase. Dwellings are tight, hunched shells of bent saplings, oiled bark shingles, and thick moss-thatch, tucked into branch-forks or cantilevered out over the damp abyss on lashed timber struts. Every joint is bound with hemp cord, rawhide, or boiled vine, reinforced here and there with crude, heavy nails and brackets of hammered bog-iron. There is no mortar, no cut stone, no straight line—only a dense, creaking geometry of wood, fiber, and living bark that flexes slightly when the wind hits the rim.

Because the wood rots fast in the perpetual damp, the settlement is in a state of constant, frantic repair. Men and women in oiled wool cloaks and greased leather aprons clamber along the upper struts with adzes, drawknives, and bundles of fresh cane, stripping away black, spongy planks and wedging in green replacements. Hollowed-out logs carry cliff-water from tier to tier—some feeding wicker fish-traps and eel-boxes, others spilling into wooden troughs where women slap and rinse heavy hides, still others diverted to turn small, splashing paddle-wheels that drive grinding stones or bellows. Thin threads of peat-and-alder smoke leak from clay braziers set on sand-boxes inside the huts, hanging low under the wet canopy before the mountain draft tears them apart.

What truly fills the space, though, is the sheer, overwhelming crush of animals. The grove is not merely inhabited; it is swarmed. Every eave, root-hollow, and wicker basket tucked under the walkways holds nesting cliff-pigeons, fat highland ducks, or chattering flocks of glossy mountain starlings that erupt in gray-and-iridescent clouds whenever a shutter slams. Sure-footed, shaggy goats are tethered to the thicker boughs, browsing on pruned branches and dropping pellets onto the compost-beds below, where bristled, short-legged pigs root noisily through kitchen scraps and rotting leaves.

Lower down, in the dim, wet gloom beneath the lowest boardwalks, frogs and slick orange-bellied salamanders pack every mossy crevice and water-butt. Thick-bodied beetles buzz lazily around the lantern-hooks; midges dance in tight columns wherever a sunbeam hits a steaming dung-heap; and in the rushing drainage channels, dark trout and freshwater crabs crowd against the wicker weirs. Even the people wear the fauna—feathers tucked into felt hats, dried squirrel-pelts hanging from belts, live hens carried upside-down by the legs through the narrow lanes.

The noise is a physical pressure, loud and continuous. Water roars down the back cliff and chatters through a hundred wooden sluices. Beneath that steady rush sits a deafening, layered racket: the clack of wooden clogs on wet slats, the bleating of goats, the shrill whistling of herders, the frantic wing-beats of startled birds, the rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of mallets driving wooden pegs, and a constant, wet chorus of croaking from the peat below. People have to lean close and shout to bargain over a basket of smoked eels or a coil of fresh hemp, their voices overlapping in the narrow, crowded passages where two loaded porters can barely pass without brushing shoulders.

Nothing stays put for five minutes. A boy slips on a mossy rung and catches himself on a goat-tether, sending a cascade of stored turnips bouncing down two tiers into the pig-runs; three women immediately scramble after them, shouting, while above them a rotted rope snaps and drops a bundle of wet thatch onto a duck-coop, setting off a fresh explosion of feathers and barking dogs. Every few minutes, a sudden squall sweeps over the limestone rim, dumping a cold curtain of rain that turns every roof-edge into a waterfall and sends people ducking under bark awnings without slowing their hands. Then the cloud passes, the sharp mountain sun breaks back through the leaves, steam rises off a hundred wool backs and mossy roofs, and the whole dripping, crowded, noisy hive surges right back into motion.
