High above the world’s weather—at an altitude where the outer air is too thin to carry sound or hold a cloud—a needle of black volcanic glass pierces the upper dark. It is a young peak, pushed up fast by some deep violence and not yet blunted by wind or time. Its flanks are razor-edged, shingled in fresh obsidian and pale, porous slag.

Nothing grows on the outside, and nothing lives there. Yet embedded inside the very crown of the spire, sealed behind a hundred meters of fractured basalt, lies a hidden pocket of space: a hollow blister no wider than a city square, and tall as a cathedral shaft.

Stepping into it—if there were a way in—is like stepping into the throat of a banked furnace. The air is stiflingly hot, radiating upward from the spire’s core far below, where the rock still remembers being liquid. Because the chamber is sealed and the heat has nowhere to go, it pools here in thick, shimmering layers. Near the outer shell of the blister, though, the extreme cold of the high altitude bleeds through the thin stone. Moisture trapped inside the rock is drawn out by the gradient, sweating onto the jagged inner walls and gathering along the ceiling in a perpetual, feverish damp.

It is a dim place. No sunlight reaches it, save for a few hairline fractures where the outer sky shows as slivers of bruised violet. Illumination comes instead from below—a dull, ember-orange glow rising through the shaft—and from the structures themselves.

For the blister is not empty, even though it is mostly void. Clinging to the raw, unweathered walls, and hanging across the hot emptiness between them, is a settlement.

It is built in sparse, layered tiers—terraces, cantilevered galleries, narrow transit-spines, and clusters of windowless chambers—organized with the dense, interlocking logic of an old town, yet occupying only a fraction of the space. Between one neighborhood of terraces and the next stretches a wide, dizzying gap of hot, still air. You could drop a stone from the upper tiers and wait several seconds before it vanished into the amber haze below.

The builders are absent, or were never the kind of thing that leaves footprints, and their engineering does not quite obey the rules of stone and steel. Whole blocks of the city are anchored to the sheer obsidian by nothing visible—no struts, no cables—hovering a finger’s breadth off the rock face, held in place by pale ceramic nodes that bend the dim light around them like heat-haze. Elsewhere, gossamer filaments, thinner than sewing thread yet taut as iron rods, span the void to carry slender walkways from one wall to the other. The materials are matte and strange: dark, velvety alloys that absorb the ember-glow, and pale membranes that pulse with a faint, cold-white luminescence, just bright enough to read a hand by.

There is no grass, no moss, no bird, no insect. The only things that "grow" here are mineral—delicate, dendritic blooms of sulfur and salt that crust the damp seams where the city’s outer skin meets the sweating rock.

And yet the place is restless. It is mostly quiet, wrapped in a heavy, muffled hush, but it is never still. Every few minutes, a bead of condensation grows heavy enough to detach from the upper dome and fall through the hot void, hissing briefly as it strikes a warm ceramic roof. The glass walls *tink* and pop as they expand and contract. Far below, a slow thermal cycle turns over; you can feel it as a sluggish updraft brushing your face, carrying the faint smell of wet slate and ozone.

As the heat shifts, the city shifts with it. Entire suspended balconies drift a few centimeters sideways on silent, invisible bearings, adjusting their angles to catch or shed the rising warmth. Near the pale ceramic nodes, stray droplets of water pause mid-fall, trembling in the air for a beat before sliding sideways along unseen field-lines and vanishing into thin, slotted intakes. The interiors of the small, stacked rooms look subtly wrong when glimpsed through their open thresholds—corridors that seem to angle slightly deeper than the thin shell of the building could possibly allow, as if the settlement has folded extra volume into the pocket to keep from crowding the void.

It feels less like an abandoned town and more like an organ tucked inside a needle of glass—a quiet, hot, self-regulating calculation running at the roof of the world, with no one left to read its answers.
