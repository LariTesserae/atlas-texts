The glare arrives before the heat registers—a white, high-altitude brightness that flattens shadows into sharp ink-strokes beneath every ledge and piling. You are high enough that the sky has thinned to a pale, washed cobalt, yet the air presses down like a wet wool blanket heated in an oven. It is a scalding, heavy stillness. Across miles of open, terraced plateau, nothing moves fast enough to make a sound.

The ground is ancient, settled into its own geometry: broad, stepped shelves of bone-white sinter and weathered black basalt, carved over millennia by mineral-laden water welling up from beneath the crust. There are no trees, no grasses, no moss. Where life clings to the stone without legs, it is only a thin, crusted stain—ochre, sulfur-yellow, and a bruised, metallic violet—coating the edges of the shallow basins. Everywhere, the plateau is wet. Water does not rush here; there are no cataracts or noisy creeks. Instead, it weeps from the porous faces of the low escarpments in slow, glistening curtains, and sheets outward across the flat pans in mirror-bright films no deeper than a wrist. Saturated with dissolved silica and salts, the water has a heavy, oily surface tension. When a drop falls from an overhang, it does not splash so much as strike with a dull, muted *thup* and skitter briefly like a bead of mercury before merging into the sheet.

Spread thinly across this vast, steaming plain is a city built for heat and distance. Its districts sit miles apart, connected by straight, elevated causeways of pale ceramic and long, sagging spans of thick-walled glass pipe. Because the space between the nodes is so wide and empty, the eye reads the plateau as wilderness at first—until you notice the ruler-straight dikes dividing the brine pans, the rhythmic spacing of the aqueduct pylons, and the way the layered, flat-roofed settlements are terraced directly into the basalt shelves.

The industry here is immense, mechanized, and eerily mute. There are no smokestacks, no clattering pistons, no grinding gears. Every engine in the valley runs on thermal gradients and silent current. Fields of parabolic mirrors, dull with a fine rime of dried salt, tilt in imperceptible increments to track the blazing sun, focusing heat into fluid-filled copper manifolds that hum at a frequency too low for the ear, felt only as a faint, greasy vibration in the soles of your boots. Tall thermoelectric spires—lattices of blackened bismuth and oxidized bronze—rise from the hottest steaming fissures, drawing electricity from the difference between the scalding subsurface brine and the thin, sun-baked air. Along the raised ceramic tracks between the refinery-nodes, flat, unmanned barges glide on magnetic cushions a hair’s breadth above the rail, ferrying cakes of precipitated mineral salt without a whisper of friction. Even the massive sluice-gates that regulate the evaporation pans open on slow hydraulic bladders, shifting tons of water with nothing more than a soft, wet sigh.

In the deep, layered under-structures of the urban nodes—built beneath thick canopies of porous, water-cooled stone—you can sometimes glimpse the maintenance corridors: damp, dim galleries lit by amber filaments, where condensation drips steadily into collection troughs. Whoever tends the spires and harvests the pans stays out of the upper glare, leaving the surface to the machines and the fauna.

And the surface is crowded with life, despite the absence of a single green leaf.

The heat and the mineral-rich brine have bred an abundant, entirely silent ecology that treats the city’s infrastructure as part of the geology. Through the shallow, scalding evaporation pans, thousands of pale, flat-shelled grazers—broad as dinner plates and nearly translucent—creep across the submerged sinter, scraping the microbial mineral-crust from the stone. Their movement leaves slow, cross-hatched trails in the sediment that vanish within an hour as new salt precipitates.

Above them wade the stilt-feeders: tall, impossibly slender creatures with bodies no bigger than a melon balanced on four jointed, keratinous legs. They step with agonizing deliberation through the steaming sheets, lifting each needle-thin foot cleanly so as not to ripple the surface, dipping long, flexible siphons into the brine to filter out the pan-grazers and dissolved nutrients. When a dozen of them stand motionless in the heat-shimmer near a mirror array, their reflections stretch downward into the water until it is impossible to tell where the creature ends and the mirage begins.

Up in the hot air around the thermoelectric spires, where the rising vapor carries a faint static charge, drift dense, soundless clouds of bladder-wings—hollow, papery organisms inflated with warm gas, trailing fine silver filaments that harvest the stray electricity and moisture from the air. They bump gently against the glass aqueducts and drift away like pale embers, never chirping, never buzzing. Even the predators—long, ribbon-like things that lurk in the cool, dark crevices beneath the ceramic causeways—hunt by thermal shadow and ambush, sliding into the warm water without a ripple.

The plateau runs on a slow, relentless cycle. By midday, the sun is so fierce that the wet stone steams evenly across the entire horizon, turning the distant urban towers into floating, disconnected silhouettes. The air grows thick with suspended mineral vapor, and the silence deepens until the loudest thing in the world is the faint, rhythmic *tick* of a cooling pipe or the dry *click* of a stilt-feeder’s joint. By late afternoon, the high-altitude air cools just enough for the rising steam to condense in mid-air—not as rain, which rarely falls here, but as a sudden, blinding white mist that settles straight onto every surface, re-wetting the mirrors, filling the collection troughs, and feeding the weeping rocks for another turn of the sun.
