The dark here is not cold. Stepping onto the plateau, the first thing you notice—after the sudden, vast absence of light—is the warmth rising through the soles of your boots, steady and dry, like the casing of an engine that has been running for a million years.

You are high up. The air is thin enough that every breath takes a little more intention, carrying a faint, metallic tang of ozone and warm grease. Above, there is no sky to speak of—only a perpetual, continent-wide ceiling of heavy vapor and fine carbon haze that swallows starlight and sun alike, holding the planet’s heat pressed down against the stone. The plateau stretches out beneath it for hundreds of miles in every direction, a high, settled tableland of black basalt, graphite seams, and iron-rich slate, worn smooth by eons of slow wind until every ridge has the rounded, patient profile of something that has finished changing.

It is mostly quiet. There is no wind to speak of, only slow, heavy barometric drifts that move across the vast flats like deep-water currents. In the stillness, you begin to hear the small, precise sounds of the place: a faint, sub-audible thrumming in the bedrock; the occasional soft *tick* of static jumping between two mineral spires; the slow, irregular *drip* of condensation falling from a stone overhang into a shallow basin.

Water does not flow here, and it rarely falls from the sky. Instead, it clings. The warm air holds just enough moisture to bead wherever the plateau’s shifting electrical fields cool a conductive surface. Thin films of dew form on the dark, glassy outcrops, gathering into narrow, oil-sheened pools in the hollows of ancient, dry riverbeds. Around these damp pockets, the only vegetation takes hold—sparse, scattered clusters of wire-sedge with stiff, copper-tinted stalks, and low mats of soot-black lichen that drink the mineral-heavy mist. Between these clusters lie miles of bare, warm, empty rock.

And yet, for all its emptiness and the complete absence of walls, roofs, roads, or any made thing, the plateau feels unmistakably like a factory floor.

The ground itself is a dynamo. Deep beneath the weathered crust, vast plates of piezoelectric quartz and magnetite grind against one another over geothermal vents, generating a silent, continuous direct current that threads through the graphite veins of the surface. You can feel it in the hair on your arms—a constant, gentle pull. Every few miles, where a fault line breaches the surface, the rock weeps a thick, dark fluid that smells like refined lubricant, pooling in natural sumps before seeping back into the stone.

Out across the dark flats, the fauna moves through this current—abundant, unhurried, and built on principles that belong as much to heavy industry as to biology.

Nothing here walks on soft paws or flaps feathered wings. The plateau’s creatures have evolved in the image of the crust that feeds them: silent, electrified, and mechanized. Half a mile away, a low, pale-lit shape glides across a basalt terrace—a refinery-grazer, the size of a flatbed truck, its carapace a dull, weathered bronze. It moves on three wide, segmented rollers that turn on fluid-lubricated biological axles, making no sound louder than a soft, sandy hiss. As it passes over an iron-rich seam, its underside drops a curtain of fine, glowing filaments—the only light for miles—that arc into the stone, electrolytically loosening the ore and drawing it up into internal acid-vats. Behind it, it leaves a neat, steaming ribbon of extruded slag and pure, cooling metal pellets, which immediately attract smaller things.

A dozen spark-mites—fist-sized, six-legged, with translucent quartz abdomens—scurry out from the crevices of a nearby sedge-patch. They swarm the warm pellets, their mandibles clicking softly like tiny relays as they strip the residual charge from the freshly smelted metal. Above them, drifting silently through the warm dark, a capacitor-ray passes—a wide, flat creature with a wing-like lattice of conductive bone, riding the plateau’s magnetic field lines without a single beat, its belly pulsing with a faint, rhythmic violet glow as it stores charge.

There is a fierce, quiet economy to the dark. Predators here hunt not by sight, but by thermal bloom and electrical leak. Near a damp condensation-basin, a siphon-hound—long-bodied, low-slung, covered in matte-black insulating plates—waits motionless beside a graphite vein. When a heavy, piston-legged ore-borer lumbers past, its internal furnace glowing a dull, buried cherry-red through the vents in its flank, the hound strikes without a roar. It drives two hollow, copper-tipped fangs into the borer’s main power-conduit, grounding the beast into the damp earth with a sharp, muffled *crack* and a brief flare of blue arc-light. Within seconds, the dark settles back in, and only the soft, rhythmic pumping of the hound’s fluid-siphons breaks the quiet.

On the horizon—so far away it seems to belong to another climate entirely—the plateau’s vast scale makes its own weather. A silent, slow-moving front of charged dust gathers over the far escarpments. There is no thunder, no rain; only wide, pale sheets of dry static discharging across the low clouds, briefly illuminating miles of ancient, eroded stone, the scattered tufts of wire-sedge, and the distant, crawling silhouettes of a hundred un-built machines, all humming quietly in the warm dark, refining the world into itself.
