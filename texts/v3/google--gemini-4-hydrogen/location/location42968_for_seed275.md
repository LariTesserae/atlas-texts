Far below the surface, a newly riven basin opens out—a subterranean valley miles across, its ceiling lost in a high, jagged lattice of raw, unweathered limestone and translucent selenite plates. Through these crystalline fissures, a cool, even daylight filters down, reinforced by long, high-strung lines of white industrial arc-lamps. The light is normal, crisp, and shadow-sharp, illuminating a landscape that has never known rain.

The air is bone-dry and briskly cool, carrying the scent of crushed chalk, ozone, and dry cedar. There is almost no water here—no puddles, no dampness on the stone, no mist in the air. Instead, the raw, angular geology—still sharp-edged and newly fractured, untouched by wind or water erosion—is completely swallowed by a living, dry-land mantle. Vegetation covers everything. It is not a wet, lush green, but a vast, interlocking architecture of pale cork, papery silver-grey fronds, and dense, springy carpets of fibrous lichen-felt. These plants do not drink water; they mine the mineral dust and the dry, circulating air, circulating a thick, cool, non-aqueous amber resin through their woody veins to keep their tissues supple. Where the rock splits beneath them, their roots immediately wedge into the fresh cracks, binding the raw stone into rolling, dry terraces.

Across this immense, quiet floor sits a city built into and out of the flora, yet laid out with jarring, deliberate sparsity. Organized urban infrastructure—straight, elevated causeways, stepped living quarters, and tall, skeletal factory-towers—is scattered across the basin with wide, empty expanses of living bark-plain between each district. From a distance, the settlement looks like a series of intricate wooden and copper machines left out on a vast, pale rug. Up close, the buildings are revealed to be grown rather than merely assembled: thick, hollow trunks of chalk-cane are trained around riveted iron frames and braced with tensioned steel cables, forming multi-story mills and resin-refineries.

The industry here is electrified and relentless, yet the valley is remarkably quiet. The thick, corky plant-mat blankets every surface, swallowing footsteps and machine-rumble like acoustic foam. Factories run on silent, high-voltage induction and slow, wax-lubricated flywheels. Overhead, tensioned silk-belts and copper busbars hum at a pitch just below hearing, carrying power from distant geothermal shafts to electrostatic looms and pneumatic dust-collectors. Everything is in active motion—air-drafts rustling the dry, papery canopies, sap-presses turning at a glacial pace, automated scrapers harvesting petrified white coral-wood from dead thickets to grind into dry cement—yet the loudest sound for miles is the soft, dry *shush* of leaves rubbing together or the faint *tick* of cooling metal.

Strange life thrives in the gaps between the machinery and the living walls. The plants themselves exhibit an eerie tropism toward electricity; their pale, woody tendrils slowly coil toward the high-voltage lines, thickening around the porcelain insulators as if feeding on the magnetic fields. Woven through this electro-botanical city is an abundant, hushed fauna adapted to the dry chill. Paper-winged chalk-moths, no thicker than mica flakes, drift in slow clouds around the arc-lamps, pollinating the resin-blossoms. Six-limbed, velvet-hided grazers cling silently to the undersides of the root-bridges, piercing the bark with hollow proboscises to sip the cool, oily sap, only to drop soundlessly into the lichen-carpet below when the factory walls emit a mild, defensive static pulse. Long-legged loom-spiders patrol the copper struts, spinning dry, conductive silk that the city’s workers periodically harvest for the generators.

It is a world just coming into its own—a cool, bright, and silent subterranean expanse where raw stone, dry-chemistry flora, and quiet, electrified industry are still figuring out how to grow into one another.
