High above the treeline, above even the last scoured aprons of snow, the mountain is still sharp enough to cut. It is a young uplift—jagged, black-veined basalt and raw slate thrust straight into the thin air, its edges unrounded by wind or ice. Near the crown of one such needle, a vertical split opens into the flank: a single, cathedral-tall cleft, no wider than a narrow nave, pinched almost shut at the top by an overhanging lip of fractured stone.

Inside, it is nearly pitch dark. Only a thin, bruised-indigo thread of light leaks down from the jagged slit forty meters overhead, and even that is swallowed before it reaches the floor. You know the shape of the place by the chill of its stone, the thinness of the air, and the overwhelming press of what lives in it.

The rock is cold and perpetually damp. Cloud-banks drifting past the summit shear across the upper slit, spilling pale tendrils of mist into the hollow. The mist condenses on the unweathered, glass-sharp facets of the walls, beading into silver threads that creep downward along hairline fractures and pool in shallow, angular pockets at the base. Nothing has smoothed these basins; the scree underfoot is a shifting bed of fresh, flat-sided shards that clink like broken pottery when disturbed.

Where the condensation threads run, sparse vegetation clings in pale, stubborn seams: rubbery, ash-colored liverworts, salt-crusted moss-hairs, and occasional leathery bladder-fronds wedged tight into the cracks, swelling slowly over months as they drink the mist.

But the stone itself is scarcely visible. Every ledge, undercut, and vertical rib is packed, layered, and crusted with living bodies.

In the thin, cool air, the metabolism of the hollow runs slow, yet the sheer density of fauna makes the darkness feel crowded and thick. Anchored directly to the sheer walls are thousands of heavy, flat-bellied creatures the size of hounds, with pleated, slate-grey hides and six long, stiff-jointed limbs whose chitinous tips wedge deep into the rock’s fresh fractures. They barely shift—moving perhaps a hand’s breadth in an hour—but they breathe in deep, wet, bellows-like sighs to draw enough oxygen from the extreme altitude. Because there are so many, packed flank to flank from the scree to the upper lip, their breathing overlaps into a continuous, heavy surf of sound.

Crawling slowly across the pleated backs of the sleepers, and threading between their wedged limbs, are smaller, pale, blind things with oversized, dish-shaped ears and long, dexterous forelimbs. They move with patient, deliberate care, avoiding the razor edges of the young stone. Many of them carry simple, unworked tools: naturally fractured splinters of basalt or curved ribs scavenged from the dead at the bottom of the cleft, bound at the grip with twisted fibers of bladder-frond. They use these picks to pry stubborn crusts of mineral-salt and liverwort from the damp seams, or to crack the hardened carapaces of fallen flyers.

Higher up, where the cleft narrows toward the slit, the ceiling is carpeted with translucent, thumb-sized flyers whose stiff, mica-like wings catch the faintest glint of moisture. They hang in shivering, near-touching sheets, occasionally detaching in slow, drifting spirals when a gust of high-altitude wind forces its way through the roof.

Despite the slowness of every creature here, the hollow is loud. The hard, unweathered walls act as a sounding board. The wet *shhh-ahhh* of thousands of bellows-lungs mixes with the dry, papery rattle of wings, the scrape of chitin against basalt, and the resonant *whoom* of wind shearing across the summit slit above. Cutting through it all is a slow, rhythmic *tock... tock... tock*—the sound of the blind scavengers tapping their stone splinters against hollow ribs of rock, testing the seams or signaling one another across the dark.

Nothing here is built. There are no woven nests, no stacked cairns, no deliberate shelters—only the raw crack in the mountain, the slow drip of condensed cloud, and a dense, patient, clamor of life wedged into the cold dark, waiting out the long, thin-aired cycles of the peak.
