The water here is so cold it feels brittle, and so clear that distance loses its meaning.

This is a high-altitude sea—an immense, continent-wide basin flooded to a depth of four hundred meters atop a freshly uplifted plateau. Above the surface, the air is thin and still; below it, the world is a single, unbroken lens of near-freezing freshwater, lit from above by a steady, neutral daylight that reaches all the way to the stone floor. There is no silt to cloud it, no plankton bloom to turn it green, no drifting detritus. Looking horizontally through the water column, you can see the jagged silhouettes of submerged mountain ridges twenty kilometers away, razor-sharp and blue-white in the refracted sun.

The rock beneath is young. Great stepped shelves of black basalt and pale, quartz-veined granite rise and drop in clean, unrounded fracture planes, as if the plateau were shattered and drowned only yesterday. No tide scours these edges. No kelp softens them. The stone is bare, save for the faintest mineral-gray crusts of chemotrophic film clinging to the leeward cracks—nothing that could be called a plant, nothing that reaches for the light.

And yet the silence you would expect from such emptiness does not exist.

The sea is deafening. It is not the crash of waves or the groan of shifting ice—the surface above is flat as glass, locked in a windless, unchanging equilibrium. The sound comes from everywhere at once, a physical pressure against the eardrums and a steady vibration in the marrow of the ribs. It is a vast, unchanging chord: a deep, subterranean bass thrumming at forty hertz, overlaid with a high, metallic whine and a rhythmic, hydraulic *thud-thud-thud* that never speeds up and never misses a beat.

Scattered across the enormous emptiness of the plateau, separated by tens or hundreds of kilometers of vacant water, sit the machines.

They are industrial monoliths—heavy, riveted steel housings, copper-wound stator rings the size of cathedrals, and tall, lattice-work electrolysis towers anchored directly into the sharp bedrock with poured-concrete footings. Thick, armored cables run straight across the empty abyssal plains between them, suspended a meter above the stone on stanchions, humming with high-voltage current. None of these stations have crews. There are no lights blinking in sequence, no exhaust plumes, no maintenance vessels. They are locked into a steady-state harvest, drawing power from the cold pressure gradients and mineral-rich fault lines below, pumping, separating, and refining in an endless, automated loop.

Because the water is dense, cold, and uninterrupted by soft mud or vegetation, the noise of these distant factories does not die. It bounces off the flat mirror of the water’s surface and the sharp, glassy faces of the drowned cliffs, turning the entire high-altitude ocean into a resonant chamber. Even when you drift in the middle of a hundred-kilometer void, with nothing visible in any direction except pale blue light and the faint, distant teeth of the seafloor far below, the industrial roar is as loud as standing beside a running turbine.

In that bright, roaring emptiness, life has arranged itself around the only things available: the rock, the current, and the vibration.

At first glance, the water seems sterile. You can hang suspended in the blue-white expanse for an hour and see nothing move. Then a ribbon of pale, translucent life glides past—thousands of slender, bone-white pelagic eels, each a meter long, swimming in a tight, ruler-straight formation along the magnetic field of a submerged power cable. They have no eyes, only broad, fluted lateral lines that read the deafening acoustic grid of the sea like a map.

Closer to the sharp basalt ridges and the concrete footings of the pumping stations, the fauna is surprisingly thick, though strange. Clusters of armored, pale-shelled crustaceans—no bigger than a thumbnail, with translucent carapaces that let the daylight through—coat the vibrating steel of the turbine intakes by the millions, grazing on the thin bacterial films that thrive in the warm, electrified boundary layers of the machinery. Translucent siphonophores, trailing fine stinging filaments like spun glass, drift in slow, vertical curtains through the open water, feeding on the dense clouds of microscopic mineral-grazers that rise from the fault vents.

Everywhere, the creatures are tuned to the machine-hum. Small, glassy shrimp click in exact rhythmic sympathy with the hydraulic hammers kilometers away. Larger, flat-bodied rays—pale as chalk and nearly invisible against the quartz-veined stone—glide effortlessly over the sharp scree, using the constant, unchanging roar as a kind of permanent sonar, reading the echoes of the industrial chord off every ridge and pylon.

Nothing here changes. There are no seasons in the water, no storms to stir the depths, no decay to cloud the view. The daylight holds steady overhead, refracting in pale, motionless shafts down to the fresh black rock. The scattered factories grind on at their fixed, eternal RPM. The pale ribbons of fauna thread their way through the vast, empty volume between them. And the water holds the sound of it all, bright and freezing and loud enough to shake your teeth, forever.
