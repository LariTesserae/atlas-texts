Three miles beneath a crust that has never known rain, the Hollows begin—not as a single cave, but as a continent-sized fracture that cooled too fast and never collapsed.

The space is vast enough to hold a sky, yet there is no room to look at it. From floor to unseen ceiling, the chasm is choked with young, unweathered stone: a dense, interlocking lattice of basalt blades, hexagonal pillars, and razor-edged curtains of volcanic glass that hang like frozen lightning. Nothing has been rounded here. Every edge is fresh from the break, sharp enough to score flesh or shave horn. Walking means threading a labyrinth of narrow, vertical slots and canted shelves, always within arm’s reach of mineral walls that press in from every side.

The air is warm—a steady, blood-heat stillness that never rises into a draft and never cools into a chill. There are no seasons, no weather, no gradients of temperature to stir the air. Whatever heat radiates from the deep mantle is held in perfect, stagnant equilibrium by the stone. It smells of dry slate, warm iron, and the faint, metallic tang of static electricity. There is no water—no mist, no drip, no dampness on the rock. The only moisture in the world is sealed inside the tough, waxy hides of the things that live here, or locked as microscopic brine inclusions deep inside quartz veins, unreachable and unmoving.

Light is almost absent. A few fissures in the lowest tiers glow with a dull, dying ember-red, too faint to cast shadows, only enough to silhouette the jagged ribs of rock against a slightly darker void. Higher up, the darkness is near-total, broken only by the occasional, slow-moving pinpoint of pale amber or bruised violet—the cold bioluminescence of creatures that use light not to see, but to warn or to bargain.

Despite the lack of plants—save for a brittle, soot-black crust of sulfur-crust that grows along the warm cracks, indistinguishable from mineral tarnish—the dark is crowded with life.

They are everywhere, packed into the crevices and clinging to the undersides of the obsidian shelves. Most are low-slung, armored, and built for wedging rather than running: segmented stone-borers the size of dogs with tungsten-hard mandibles; flat, leathery gliders that hang like tapestries from the vertical flutes of the basalt; colonies of blind, pale-shelled climbers that cluster in the warm pockets between pillars, their bodies pressed tight together to conserve every molecule of internal moisture. 

They do not eat plants, and few of them hunt. Instead, they graze on the stone itself, or on the strange energy of the place. Some press flat, copper-rich bellies against stressed quartz seams, harvesting the faint piezoelectric current generated by the weight of the continent overhead. Others rasp away at the sulfide crusts with crystalline tongues, digesting rock over weeks, their metabolisms tuned to the unchanging warmth. 

For all their numbers, the Hollows are mostly quiet. Without wind or water, there is no background roar—only a vast, muffled hush. Now and then, a dry *tick* of chitin against glass carries half a mile through the still air, or a low, rhythmic thrumming vibrates through the soles of your feet: two territorial borers drumming on a resonant pillar, arguing in infrasound. When they move, they move with a slow, economical patience, never rushing, never wasting heat.

Scattered through this dense, silent geometry are the marks of hands.

They are small, deliberate, and sparse—a bridge of lashed, calcified ribs spanning a three-foot drop; a series of shallow, chipped notches hammered into a sheer obsidian face to make a ladder; a cairn of balanced, triangular slate shards marking a junction where twelve identical corridors meet. Whoever made them worked with simple tools: flint hammers, bone awls, sinew, and resinous paste scraped from the hides of the gliders. There is no smelted metal here, no mortar, no grand architecture. Just small, practical accommodations carved into the young stone.

In the wider pockets—rare chambers the size of a room where the pillars part—you sometimes find their camps, though it is hard to tell if they were abandoned yesterday or a thousand years ago. Nothing rots in the dry heat, and nothing blows away. A circle of flat stones holds a shallow, carved basin smeared with a grease that still faintly glows, casting a dim, greasy halo over a pile of cracked borer shells and a handful of bone needles. A wall nearby is scored with neat, parallel tally marks, thousands of them, all the same depth, as if someone stood in the dark for a very long time, counting something that never changed.
