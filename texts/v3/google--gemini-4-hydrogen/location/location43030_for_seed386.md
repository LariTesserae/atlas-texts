Far below the rim of the basin, a flat white ocean of clouds hides the rest of the world. Up here, at an altitude where the sky should be thin and freezing, the air is instead a scalding, heavy blanket. Heat rises straight out of the stone—not the dry, brittle heat of a desert, but a wet, pressing warmth that turns every breath into a slow, thick draught.

The basin itself is wide and open, a broad saddle of land cradled between weathered, jagged ridges that have long since settled into their final shapes. Millions of years of runoff and thermal seepage have rounded the dark basalt shoulders and carved terraced pools into the pale mineral crust. Every rock face weeps. Water beads on the undersides of overhangs, drips steadily down curtains of pale rootlets, and gathers in shallow, braided channels that wind across the valley floor before vanishing into steam-wreathed sinkholes.

Light falls cleanly from above, bright and ordinary, though it breaks into a thousand soft glints across the wet surfaces. The valley is packed tight with growth. Giant, broad-leafed ferns and waxy, copper-tinted reeds crowd the terraces, their fronds overlapping in dense, dripping layers. Thick mats of moss and liverwort cushion every boulder, and epiphytes hang in heavy, saturated garlands from the gnarled limbs of low, heat-loving trees. Nothing here is sparse; every square foot of wet stone is claimed, layered, and steaming.

Despite the sheer scale of the basin and the constant motion of water and mist, the place is remarkably quiet. There is no wind up here—only the slow, upward drift of warm vapor—and the lush foliage swallows whatever small sounds arise. You hear the steady *tock, tock, tock* of condensation falling onto broad leaves, the soft hiss of steam escaping through fissures in the moss, and, if you stand still long enough, a faint, continuous hum that seems to come from the soles of your feet rather than the air.

That hum is the valley’s real work. There is not a single wall, pipe, roof, or hammered rivet anywhere in sight—the landscape is entirely untouched by hands—yet the entire basin operates with the rhythmic, electrified precision of a vast, silent factory.

Deep beneath the weathered basalt, superheated aquifers press against chambers of hollowed rock, driving natural geothermal pistons. Every forty seconds, with clockwork regularity, a muffled, subterranean *thump* vibrates through the valley floor, followed by a measured, pressurized sigh from the vents along the terraces. Each pulse forces mineral-rich water up through the porous stone and out across the terraced pools. The water is a warm, stinging electrolyte, heavy with dissolved copper, zinc, and sulfur.

The plants have adapted to this charged bath—and in doing so, they have mechanized it. The waxy reeds and swollen, bladder-podded shrubs lining the pools act as living capacitors and electrodes. Their metallic-sheathed roots draw a steady, low-voltage current directly from the flowing brine, using the energy to strip dissolved ores from the water. Along the submerged root-mats, pure, gleaming nodules of copper and pale ingots of crystallized sulfur grow like fruit, accumulating in quiet, scaled production before breaking free and rolling down into the gravel beds. Now and then, a swollen seed-pod along the bank reaches capacity and discharges a tiny, sharp blue snap of static into the wet air, resetting its cycle.

Life moves through this charged, steaming garden in abundant, unhurried silence. Heavy-shelled snails and flat, armored beetles with dull, metallic carapaces crawl along the warm shallows, grazing on the mineral-crusted algae and using the precipitated copper to reinforce their own shells. Pale, electro-sensitive amphibians—smooth-skinned and nearly translucent—glide through the terraced pools, reading the subtle shifts in the water’s current to hunt smaller, darting larvae. Above the fern-brakes, large, silent dragonflies with broad, oil-slick wings patrol the warm updrafts, snatching slow-moving moths from the mist without a sound.

Nothing here rushes, and nothing breaks. The geothermal pulses keep time, the electrified pools refine their quiet harvest, the ferns drink the hot mist, and the armored grazers tend the beds. Perched miles above the cloud-sea, the valley sits in its own ancient, self-contained balance—an unbuilt, sun-warmed engine humming softly to itself at the roof of the world.
