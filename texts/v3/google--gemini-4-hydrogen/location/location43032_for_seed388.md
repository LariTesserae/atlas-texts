The basin is no wider than a city block, sunken into low, waterlogged ground and walled in by four tiers of brick arches, riveted iron walkways, and weeping limestone. Overhead, a sagging lattice of blackened trusses and wired glass holds back the sky, admitting only a bruised, twilight-colored glow. Everything inside is warm, close, and saturated. The air tastes of boiled iron, wet mortar, and crushed greenery.

There is no horizon here—only the opposite wall of the pit, visible through a drifting veil of steam and mist, stacked high with boiler housings, tiled roofs, and cantilevered catwalks that nearly touch across the narrowest gaps. Because the space is bounded and roofed, every sound stays inside it. The place is loud with a heavy, unhurried racket: the steady *shhh* of water sheeting down corrugated tin, the hollow *plink-thunk* of heavy condensation falling into flooded sumps, and beneath it all, the immense, patient rhythm of the engines.

The machinery here does not race. Great cast-iron beam engines, slick with black grease and mineral crust, rock back and forth on strokes that take half a minute to complete. A piston sinks; a sluice gate groans open; a column of warm groundwater surges into a copper-lined cistern with a deep, chest-rattling *thoom* that vibrates through the perforated iron grates underfoot. Then comes a long, wet hiss of venting steam, a pause where only the dripping water speaks, and the slow, inevitable return of the counterweight. Dim amber bulbs in wire cages hang at the junctions of the walkways, fed by humming, oil-cooled transformers strapped to the brick piers. Their light barely reaches the next stanchion, turning the rising vapor into a series of glowing, jaundiced halos.

Time has settled the whole apparatus into a single, weathered crust. Brick corners have softened under centuries of damp; iron stair-treads are dished smooth in the center from vibration and constant runoff. Copper pipes, thick as a torso, have turned a velvety, uneven verdigris, sweating warm droplets onto the walkways below. Nothing looks newly built, yet nothing looks broken—only worn into exact balance with the water that feeds it.

In the absence of any animal life, the plants have taken over every available surface. There are no flies circling the amber bulbs, no spiders in the corners of the trusses, no rats in the drains, no birds nesting in the rafters. The silence of anything that crawls or flies is absolute, leaving the mechanical roar strangely clean. Instead, a lush, strange flora thrives on the warmth, the dimness, and the mineral-rich mist. Broad-leafed ferns and thick, waxy vines choke the lower brickwork, their fronds slick and dark-emerald. Where copper pipes leak at the flanges, pale, fibrous root-mats have wrapped themselves tightly around the joints, swelling with moisture until they form living gaskets that stanch the drip. Along the warm housings of the electrical dynamos, clusters of translucent, copper-tinted fungi grow in neat rings, drinking the stray heat.

The cycle of the basin is geological in its patience. Water seeps in through the surrounding stone, warms in the deep sumps, is lifted by the slow-turning iron wheels, condenses on the iron roof-ribs, and rains steadily back down through the tiers of walkways and leaves. The engines turn, the moss drinks, the brick slowly softens, and the warm mist never quite clears.
