It sits in a high, narrow notch of young dark slate—a cleft barely three hundred paces across, pinched between sharp, unweathered crags that have not yet learned to round themselves. Below the lip of the notch, the mountain drops away into a sea of pewter cloud. Above, the sky is a flat, cold lid, low enough to touch.

From a distance, it looks like a tumor of black-green moss wedged into the stone. Up close, it is a city.

There is no bare rock inside the boundary, and no soil that isn’t actively alive. The entire cleft is packed—wall to wall, floor to eaves—with a dense, multi-tiered architecture of living plants. Dwarf highland junipers, iron-tough sedges, and thick, rubbery cushion-mosses have been braided, pleached, and fused into a single continuous settlement of corridors, buttresses, vaulted galleries, and stacked chambers. Not a single nail, rope, or cut stone exists here. Where two beams meet, a living branch has been bent and grafted into its neighbor until the bark healed over the join in a smooth, pale knuckle. Where a retaining wall holds back the slope, it is woven from living root-cables as thick as a forearm, locked so tightly together that not even a finger can wedge between them.

The scale is small and claustrophobic—a hamlet compressed into the volume of a cathedral. The "streets" are narrow, shoulder-wide alleys winding between bulging walls of wet moss and gnarled timber. Above them, second and third stories bridge the gap, their undersides ribbed with tangled, dripping roots. Light barely reaches the lower levels; it filters down through layers of waxy needles and translucent, water-logged lichen as a dim, slate-green twilight, just bright enough to see the gleam of wet bark and the pale, frost-bitten tips of new shoots.

It is bitterly cold, hovering right on the edge of freezing. Along the windward rim of the notch, where the mountain updraft hits the outer wall of the city, everything is armored in rime—needles stiffened with white ice, sedge-blades glazed like blown glass. Deeper inside the packed mass, however, the sheer density of living tissue and the slow, silent fermentation of the dead peat beneath keep the air a fraction of a degree warmer. It is not warmth—it is a damp, bone-aching chill that numbs the hands—but it is just enough to keep the water moving.

And the water is everywhere. The city sits inside the cloud-belt, combing moisture continuously out of the freezing fog. Every needle holds a bead of condensation. Every moss-cushion is saturated like a cold sponge, weeping a steady, slow drip onto the tier below. The architecture is built around this wetness: sloped roofs of overlapping, shingled juniper boughs shed sleet into living gutters—troughs of hollowed, still-growing bark—that channel the icy runoff down through the levels into deep, root-lined cisterns. At the bottom of the alleys, shallow runnels of black, freezing water slide over mats of drowned moss, skinned here and there with thin plates of clear ice that crackle underfoot.

Because of the thickness of the wet vegetation, the city is almost silent. The wind that howls across the sharp slate crags outside is muffled to a low, distant sigh. Inside, the soundscape is small and intimate: the irregular *tock-tock* of heavy drops falling from an overhead root-bridge into a puddle, the faint, leather-like creak of frozen boughs settling under the weight of sleet, the soft *shhh* of mist moving through a million needles.

Animal life is scarce. Now and then, a pale, heavy-bodied bird—the color of wet ash and lichen—steps silently along a mossy sill, plucking tiny, slow-moving frost-beetles from the crevices of the bark. Beneath the lower tiers, in the dark, slightly warmer peat-vaults, pale springtails swarm in the damp rot. Nothing large lives here; there are no hearths, no middens, no voices. If something once trained these plants into alleys, cisterns, and vaulted rooms, it is gone, or it moves so rarely and quietly that it leaves no mark beyond the geometry itself.

Yet the place is restless. It is an ecosystem locked in slow, relentless motion. Every few hours, a denser bank of freezing fog rolls up from the valley, saturating the alleys until visibility drops to an arm's length, followed by a brief, hissing squall of sleet that coats the upper roofs in slush. Beneath the surface, the city is quietly rebuilding itself: roots wedge deeper into the fresh, hairline fractures of the young slate below, prying the rock apart millimeter by millimeter; lower chambers, crushed by the weight of the tiers above, slowly die and compress into dark, steaming peat; and at the top, where the dim light is strongest, green shoots keep reaching outward, braiding themselves into the cold air, adding another tight, wet, living roof over the old.
