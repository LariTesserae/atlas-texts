Fifty feet beneath the seabed, the intake vault of the old tidal works sits entirely drowned, a sunken cathedral of poured concrete and riveted iron filled to its ribbed ceiling with cold, green-black water.

The chamber is not vast—perhaps sixty paces from the arched sluice-wall at its north end to the sump pit at its south, and tall enough that a diver hovering near the grated floor can barely make out the crown of the vaulting above. Every boundary is man-made and unmistakable. There is no natural stone here, no mud bottom; even the sediment that collects in the corners is ground concrete dust, flakes of black iron oxide, and crushed shell. Yet centuries of moving water have worked the room into something almost geological. The sharp chamfers of the concrete buttresses have been rounded down to smooth, pitted curves, exposing the river-pebble aggregate beneath. Cast-iron flanged pipes have lost their crispness, their bolt heads fused under thick, knobbly crusts of rust and calcium carbonate that have themselves been scoured satin-smooth by the current.

Water enters through a barred vertical shaft high in the north wall, falling—though underwater, it is more a heavy, silent-looking lunge—down toward the central floor. There, set into a recessed masonry well, the great bronze-and-iron turbine still turns. It is an ancient, over-engineered thing, thirty feet across, driven by the relentless head of the ocean above draining into some deeper fault or lower conduit beyond the grate. Most of the room around it is empty water. The designers left wide, open bays between the structural piers for maintenance cranes and haulage, and now those spaces hang vacant and dark, crossed only by the occasional slack steel cable or a vertical iron ladder descending into nothing.

A single overhead gantry crane spans the upper third of the vault, frozen on its corroded rails. Beneath it, suspended from rusted chain-falls, hang three heavy-walled brass cage-lamps. Two are dead, their thick glass globes filmed with pale mineral scale. The third still burns—fed by a surviving, tar-wrapped umbilical from a small auxiliary dynamo coupled to the main turbine shaft. Its light is dim, a murky, bruised amber that reaches perhaps twenty feet before the suspended silt swallows it. A faint, cold ghost of daylight sometimes filters down from the high intake shaft when the sun stands directly over the sea above, never enough to illuminate the floor, only enough to turn the upper water column a deep, bruised slate.

Despite the dimness, the chamber is loud—the kind of underwater loudness that is felt in the sternum and the jawbone before it is heard. The turbine turns at a slow, heavy cadence, maybe twelve revolutions a minute, and with every turn a warped bronze vane grazes its scoured housing with a deep, resonant *thrum-scrape* that vibrates through the floor grates. Behind it lies the continuous, roaring hiss of pressurized water forced through the silt-choked intake bars, a sound like distant surf trapped in a drum. Every forty seconds or so, as the tidal surge shifts outside, a massive, counterweighted check-valve on the east manifold seats and unseats with a dull, metallic *boom* that sends a visible shudder through the silt on the floor. Over all of it lies a dry, rapid-fire crackling—thousands of tiny pistol shrimp and barnacles snapping inside the pipe joints.

Because the light is poor and the water cool—a steady, numbing eight degrees—almost nothing green grows here. Only around the glowing brass cage-lamp do thin, ragged tufts of dark red filamentous algae cling to the ironwork, swaying in the lamp’s slight thermal plume. Elsewhere, the surfaces are bare of plants, slick only with a thin, colorless biofilm and patches of pale, branching bryozoans that look like frost on the concrete.

Animals, however, have claimed the machine completely. The constant through-flow of seawater acts as a conveyor belt of plankton and detritus, and the chamber's sparse geometry gives them endless ledges, eddies, and pipe-interiors to hold. Clouds of translucent mysid shrimp orbit the amber lamp-cone, darting inward and snapping back as small, silver-grey rockfish dash through the periphery to feed. In the dead-water eddies behind the concrete piers, dozens of plump, pale pollock hang motionless, their noses pointed into the spill, waiting for anything stunned by the turbine blades to wash out of the sump.

Along the grated catwalks and the rungs of the vertical ladders, large brown crabs pick their way sideways over mats of gooseneck barnacles, their shells encrusted with tube-worms. Thick-bodied conger eels have threaded themselves into the open junction boxes and the hollow spaces behind the turbine’s bearing-housings, only their blunt heads and rhythmic gills visible in the gloom. When the tidal surge peaks and the turbine accelerates for an hour—groaning louder, shaking loose flakes of rust from the overhead gantry—the schools of fish tighten and drop lower toward the floor grates, while the anemones lining the non-moving pipe flanges swell open, blooming white and translucent in the roaring dark.
