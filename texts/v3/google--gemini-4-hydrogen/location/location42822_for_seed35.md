The chamber is no larger than a railway terminus, vaulted and sealed beneath an unknown thickness of dry overburden, yet it feels cramped—not from lack of space, but because every cubic meter is spoken for.

Its walls are young, unweathered fracture planes of dark anhydrite and pale, razor-edged gypsum. Nothing here has been rounded by water or wind. The rock meets itself in sharp, crystalline steps, jagged overhangs, and fresh cleavage faces that catch the dim light like broken glass. There is no moisture anywhere; the air is warm, heavy, and bone-dry, tasting faintly of hot copper, ozone, and pulverized salt. Dust does not drift down to settle on the floor—it hangs suspended in dense, stratified bands, kept aloft by the constant, rhythmic convection of the room.

Light comes from two sources, neither of them generous. Scattered along the lower ledges and bolted directly into the raw rock are heavy, cast-iron industrial housings—five or six of them in total, spaced irregularly around the perimeter like shrines to an abandoned utility. Each machine bears a thick, caged glass bulb glowing a steady, bruised amber, and between them run thick bundles of braided copper conduit, ceramic insulators, and riveted brass pressure pipes that vanish straight into the uncarved stone. The only other illumination is a faint, dusty violet sheen clinging to the sharpest mineral edges, where the dry air discharges static in tiny, continuous sparks.

The machines are running, and they are loud. One drives a set of exposed, reciprocating pistons that slam into dry-lubricated sleeves with a flat, metallic *clack* sixty times a minute; another spins a flywheel shrouded in perforated sheet metal, producing a deep, resonant turbine howl that never rises or falls in pitch. A third vents bursts of hot, dry gas through a reed-like brass valve, adding a harsh, rhythmic hiss. Because the chamber is small and its stone faces are hard and angular, the sound does not dissipate. It folds back onto itself, layering into a dense, physical pressure that vibrates in the teeth and sternum.

In the crevices between the conduits and the salt-crusted ledges grows the only vegetation: sparse, brittle thickets of what look like oxidized wire. These are dry-land scrub-filaments, leafless and rootless in the usual sense, anchoring themselves by dissolving microscopic pits into the gypsum. Their stems are segmented, matte-black, and stiff as horsehair, topped with small, papery pods that rattle softly when the piston-shock passes through the stone. They do not grow toward the amber bulbs; they lean instead toward the copper conduits, drinking the stray electromagnetic hum and shedding fine, metallic chaff that joins the suspended dust.

Everything else in the room is alive, and it is packed shoulder to shoulder.

The fauna here has no use for water, and it has arranged itself in strict, near-touching layers according to vibration and heat. Clinging to the warm iron casings of the engines are thousands of flat, hexagonal creatures the size of dinner plates, their shells fused from shed mineral grit and hardened, waxy chitin. They do not crawl so much as *shudder* in place, pressing their ribbed undersides against the humming metal to feed on the kinetic energy, while their upper plates act as acoustic baffles—tilting minutely to catch the turbine howl and convert it into a dry, high-pitched stridulation that fills the upper frequencies of the room.

Above them, crowding the jagged ceiling and hanging in dense, overlapping curtains from every sharp overhang, are pale, blind, bellows-bodied things. They have no wings, only pleated, parchment-thin membranes stretched between long, jointed limbs. They hang upside down, packed so tightly their membranes brush and scrape with every pulse of the air, opening and closing in silent, synchronized waves—except they are not silent; each compression forces a dry, clicking chirp through a spiracle at their throat. Beneath them, weaving endlessly through the tangled copper conduits and across the sharp gypsum shelves, are segmented, ribbon-like scavengers with translucent, amber-tinted carapaces. They are thick as wrists and yards long, braided around one another in slow, dry knots, grazing on the metallic chaff of the wire-scrub and the shed husks of the plate-backs.

What makes the place unsettling is not its noise or its crowding, nor even the strange, waterless biology packed into its ribs—it is that nothing here is going anywhere. There are no rusted-out hulks beside the working engines, no piles of corpses beneath the ceiling curtains, no fresh fractures on the floor. The machines do not seem to be pumping anything out or refining anything in; the pipes enter the rock and the conduits loop back into the housings in closed, self-canceling circuits. The heat never climbs high enough to cook the bellows-hangers, nor drops enough to slow the engines. Every flake of dust that falls is lifted again by the exact same draft; every husk shed by a ribbon-crawler is consumed before it hits the anhydrite floor. The whole loud, dim, densely packed cathedral is locked in a state of roaring equilibrium—a dry, warm, mechanical terrarium that has already reached its final shape, humming and clicking in place without a single gradient left to spend.
