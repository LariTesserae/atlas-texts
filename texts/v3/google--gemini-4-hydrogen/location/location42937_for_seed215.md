Up here, the world feels newly cracked open. 

The plateau stretches on a continental scale, high enough that the air is thin, sharp, and persistently cold, yet bright under a clear, ordinary sun. Beneath that broad sky, the ground is raw and unweathered—vast shelves of dark basalt, pale granite, and glassy seams that look as though they were pushed up yesterday. Nothing has had time to round the edges. Cliff faces drop in crisp, geometric steps; boulders lie in angular splinters; the stone has never known the slow grinding of glaciers or eons of wind.

Because the rock is so young, there is almost no deep soil, and water has not yet carved proper riverbeds. Instead, it lingers everywhere on the surface. Thin sheets of meltwater and condensation slide across the flat stone terraces. Horizontal fractures in the cliffs weep steadily, feeding shallow, cold-rimmed pools and marshy basins where dark silt collects in the hollows. At night and in the permanent shadows of the ridges, the dampness freezes into delicate lace; by midday, the sun loosens it into a quiet, glittering trickle. Mist forms directly against the cold stone, rolling across the flats in low, shifting banks before tearing apart in the breeze.

Vegetation clings only where it can find purchase. Wide expanses of bare, wet rock are broken by copper-colored crusts of lichen, tight cushions of silver-green moss drinking from the weeping seams, and sparse, tough sedges bristling along the edges of the shallow pools.

Across this vast, damp emptiness sits a city—not packed into a single dense core, but scattered in distinct, organized districts separated by miles of open highland. From a distance, the settlements look almost like natural extensions of the raw geology: layered, terraced structures of dark sintered stone and seamless glass, rising in clean, angular tiers that mirror the surrounding cliffs. Between the districts, slender causeways thread above the wet flats, suspended a few yards off the rock with no visible pillars or cables.

The engineering here belongs to a physics far beyond the modern, yet it draws no attention to itself. There is no hum of generators, no exhaust, no glowing clutter of screens or warning lights. Power and water are drawn silently from ambient thermal gradients and atmospheric moisture through conduits woven invisibly into the rock itself. Transit platforms glide along the suspended ribbons on quiet repulsion fields, carrying passengers between residential terraces, research halls, and agricultural basins without a whisper of friction. Even the massive atmospheric regulators—smooth, dark monoliths spaced across the outer flats—stand as still and mute as boulders, betraying their work only by a faint, heatless shimmer in the cold air above them. To anyone walking the plazas, it feels entirely matter-of-fact, as unremarkable as gravity.

Despite the cold and the sparse plants, the plateau is thick with animal life. The mineral-rich waters and the subtle warmth bleeding from the urban terraces support a dense, restless ecosystem. 

Along the wet flats and marsh-edges, thousands of pale, cold-tolerant amphibians and segmented filter-feeders churn the shallow silt, grazing on dense mats of algae and mineral bacteria. Above them, wide herds of long-legged, thick-fleeced grazers pick their way across the sharp stone with sure, cushioned hooves, cropping the lichens and sedges. Slate-gray predators—low-slung, lean, and silent—shadow the herds from the fractured ridges, while broad-winged birds wheel in huge, synchronized flocks, riding the cold updrafts that spill off the cliffs.

Everything here is in constant, unhurried motion, yet the whole vast expanse remains remarkably quiet. There is only the dry rustle of wind over sharp stone, the soft patter of rain squalls condensing and passing, the occasional splash of a wading bird, and the distant, muffled crunch of hooves on wet gravel. A weather front builds on the far horizon, sweeps across the scattered city tiers in a veil of cold drizzle, and clears again into crisp sunlight—leaving the wet rock gleaming, the herds moving on, and the silent causeways gliding above it all.
