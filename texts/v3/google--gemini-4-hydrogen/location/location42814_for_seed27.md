The ground here is not stone, and it is not soil. It is a single, unbroken hide of living, bone-dry plant.

Three miles above the lower atmosphere, on the planed-off crown of a mountain range so old its peaks have been ground down to a gently rolling tableland, the world is entirely covered in a miles-thick mat of desiccated flora. It feels like tightly compressed cork or ancient, sun-bleached wasp-paper underfoot—pale pewter, dried lavender, and chalky ochre, worn smooth by eons of gritty wind. There is no mud, no dew, no wet sap anywhere in sight. Moisture is a rumor from another epoch. If you drive a spike into the ground, it does not bleed; it hisses softly, venting a dry puff of pressurized nitrogen and silvery mineral dust from the honeycomb of hollow vascular chambers just beneath the crust.

Because the air is thin and cool, the cold here is sharp and clean, smelling of ozone, dry cedar, and warm chalk. Above the plateau, the sky is a dim, bruised slate-amber, as if the sun is small and far away, or permanently filtered through a high haze of suspended pollen. Everything is cast in a low, even twilight that stretches shadows out into long, soft-edged smears.

Across this vast, open expanse, the architecture of a scattered city rises—not crowded together, but widely spaced, leaving miles of empty, undulating plant-floor between one district and the next. Yet the city is unmistakably a city, organized and infrastructural, grown directly out of the living substrate and coaxed into impossible shapes by engineering that treats gravity and solidity as mere suggestions.

The towers look like fluted, hollow seed-pods or enormous, paper-thin locust husks, thirty stories tall yet built of dry lignin no thicker than an eggshell. They should collapse in the first gust of wind, or under their own weight, but they do not. Running through and around them are faint, shimmering *stasis-threads*—hairline fractures in the local geometry, anchored to matte-black knots of dense, light-swallowing machinery that hover silently near the bases of the buildings. Where those black anchors sit, the air tastes faintly of copper, and fine dust motes do not fall; they orbit in slow, horizontal rings, or rest on invisible shelves of folded space.

Between the sparse districts run elevated transit-ribbons: long, sagging bridges of woven plant-chitin suspended a hundred feet in the air with no pillars beneath them, held taut only by the quiet, impossible tension at either end. Some of the larger structures are not even whole; the upper third of a spire might hang six feet to the left of its stalk, disconnected in open air, bridged only by a roaring, translucent column of compressed atmosphere and pale blue static.

Despite how open and sparse the plateau looks—miles of flat, pale-grey felt broken only by the occasional cluster of impossible towers—the place is deafening.

The sound comes from everywhere at once. Because the ground is a dry, hollow lattice of ancient plant-pipes, the steady, cool wind sweeping across the plateau turns the entire landscape into a colossal wind-instrument. A deep, resonant groaning rises directly through the soles of your boots, layered with a thousand higher-pitched whistles wherever the crust is perforated by breathing-pores. Over that base note sits the city’s own voice: the eggshell towers vibrate continuously at audible frequencies to keep their dry-matter walls rigid and shed mineral grit, filling the dim air with a brassy, overlapping hum that shifts pitch whenever the wind changes.

And then there are the animals, which are loud in a different way. 

Nothing here has wet skin or heavy, water-laden muscle. Flocks of *clackers*—kite-sized creatures with wings of stretched, translucent parchment and heads like articulated seed-husks—ride the updrafts between the widely spaced spires. In the dim light, they navigate by snapping their chitinous mandibles in rapid, machine-gun bursts, filling the canyons between the towers with a dry, rattling hailstorm of clicks. Down on the flat, empty expanses of the plant-mat, long-legged, segmented *static-grazers* trot in wide, nervous herds. Every time their hard, keratinous hooves strike the dry felt, they discharge tiny, snapping sparks of blue triboelectric light that ripple outward through the ground-fibers like miniature lightning. Beneath the surface, unseen *borers* chew steadily through the dead lower strata of the substrate, their rhythmic *scritch-thump, scritch-thump* amplified by the hollow crust like a drumhead.

The plateau has no rainstorms, but it has weather. Every few hours, the barometric pressure drops, and the entire living floor exhales at once. A miles-wide mist of dry, silvery spores rises from the ground like smoke, catching the dim amber light in glowing, ghostly sheets. Before the cloud can drift off the edge of the plateau, the city’s tall, skeletal collector-spires wake up. Their tips flare with pale, soundless distortion, and they begin to drink the air—screaming like boiling kettles as they pull the spore-clouds inward in tight, twisting vortices, feeding the dry harvest down into the dark, humming roots of the city.

When the squall passes, the spires quiet back to their steady brassy drone. The wind goes back to booming through the hollow earth, the parchment-winged flocks rattle overhead in the gloom, and the vast, dry, ancient plain settles once more into its restless, vibrating equilibrium.
