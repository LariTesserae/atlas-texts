The air here has no water in it—not even the memory of dampness. It tastes of warm iron, chalk, and ozone, and it moves with a violent, tireless impatience.

This is a high, enclosed bowl of dark stone, scarcely a hundred paces from rim to rim, tucked into the shoulder of a wind-scoured peak. Above, a permanent ceiling of high-altitude dust and slate-gray cloud chokes the sky, holding the basin in a dim, bruised twilight. Now and then a pale, dusty shaft of light breaks through and sweeps across the floor like a searchlight, only to be swallowed again by the swirling haze. Despite the thinness of the high air, the bowl is warm; deep, dry fissures in the basalt floor breathe steady plumes of geothermal heat, which collide with the cold crosswinds spilling over the jagged rim.

The result is a state of perpetual, roaring turbulence.

Sound hits you before you can make out the shapes in the gloom. The basin's walls—weathered over millennia into smooth, fluted ribs and honeycombed alcoves—act like the pipes of a vast, broken organ. Every gust sets the stone humming in a deep, vibrating bass. Layered over that is a relentless, dry *clatter-clack* and the shrill, rhythmic *thrum* of millions of moving parts.

At first glance, the floor of the bowl looks mostly empty: wide, swept-clean expanses of polished ironstone where miniature dust-devils spin, collide, and tear themselves apart. Between these bare plazas, however, life erupts in dense, roaring islands.

Because there is no water, the vegetation has invented another way to be lush. Anchored in the lee of boulders and draped in thick, tangled curtains from the upper ledges, the flora is entirely dry, fibrous, and hyperactive. Tall thickets of *rattle-reeds*—jointed stalks of calcified silica—thrash violently in the crosswinds, their translucent, mica-thin leaves clicking together like thousands of wooden chimes. Beside them sprawl dense, springy mats of copper-colored *wire-grass* that feed on friction; as the wind whips their brittle stems against the stone and one another, they build up a fierce electrostatic charge, snapping with tiny, pale-blue sparks in the dimness and smelling sharply of burnt dust.

Clinging to the warm fissures are *bellows-blooms*—leathery, veined bladders that inflate and deflate in rapid, chaotic cycles with every shift in air pressure. Each time a gust compresses them, they puff out clouds of dry, golden spores that are instantly snatched up by the updrafts and braided into the swirling dust.

The fauna thrives on this ceaseless motion. The air is thick with *skimmers*—flat, six-winged insects with chitin like smoked glass—that ride the thermal columns in dense, chittering swarms, snatching spores out of the gale. Their wings beat so fast they add a high, metallic whine to the basin’s roar. Down in the wire-grass thickets, pale, armored *drum-crabs* scuttle over one another in anthill frenzy, scraping their serrated claws against the hollow silica stalks to signal mates over the wind. Everything here is in a hurry; nothing rests on the bare stone for long before a shifting eddy tumbles it into a thicket or lifts it back into the dim air.

Scattered sparingly through the chaos are the marks of someone who comes here, or once lived here, working entirely by hand. Tucked into the deepest, quietest alcoves of the eastern wall are three low, dome-shaped shelters built of unmortared slate, chinked with dry ash and roofed with lashed bundles of rattle-reed. Outside one, a length of braided plant-fiber is stretched taut between two rock spurs, hung with notched bits of bone and hollow crab-shells that clack frantically in the wind—perhaps a charm, perhaps a crude gauge for when the gusts turn lethal.

A simple copper scraper and a half-finished basket of woven wire-grass sit wedged beneath a flat stone, coated in a fine layer of golden spore-dust. Whoever made them understood the rhythm of this place: you build low, you lash everything down, and you learn to read the noise. When the wind shifts and the organ-notes in the stone drop an octave, the bellows-blooms all exhale at once, the sparks in the grass flare brighter, and the whole small, loud, waterless world spins a little faster.
