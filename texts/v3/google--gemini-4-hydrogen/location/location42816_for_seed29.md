The place is a pocket no larger than a low cupboard, wedged deep inside a high-altitude ridge of dark gneiss and iron-rich basalt. From the outside of the mountain, there is no sign of it—only a hairline fault pinched shut by scree and dry wind. Inside, the rock has split apart recently enough that nothing has had time to round it off. Every ledge, overhang, and floor-plate is raw, freshly cleaved stone: razor-sharp crystal facets, splintered quartz veins, and angular shelves jutting inward until there is barely room for a hand to pass between ceiling and floor.

There is no water here, not even as a film on the stone. The air is thin with altitude and baked to the dry, parching heat of a brick kiln. It does not swirl or draft; it sits trapped in the narrow cleft, tasting of hot iron, pulverized silica, and the faint, sharp tang of ozone. Because nothing has ever washed through this cavity, the finest dust from the rock’s original fracturing still lies where it fell, coating every horizontal sliver in a flour-soft layer of grey mineral powder.

Nothing has been built within these walls—no timber, no mortar, no pipe or wire—yet the pocket is saturated with the presence of heavy industry. High on the ridge above and behind the rock face, unseen engines, crushers, and high-voltage transformers run without pause, and the mountain’s iron-bearing veins conduct their excess into this hidden hollow. A steady stray current tingles through the basalt. Along the sharper ridges of magnetite, the rock flour stands up in tiny, rigid bristles, aligned to invisible magnetic fields. When the dry dust shifts, static snaps in brief, pinpoint sparks of blue-white light—the only illumination in a darkness otherwise total, save for the dullest, barely perceptible red-grey warmth radiating from deep within the bedrock.

The noise is immense and relentless. Trapped in so small a chamber, the bedrock acts as a sounding board for the machinery beyond it, filling the cavity with a bone-deep, continuous bass roar and a high, metallic whine that never drops in pitch. Layered directly over the mechanical drone is a dry, rasping hiss: the sound of chitin scraping against unweathered stone.

Despite the heat, the thin air, and the lack of moisture, the pocket is packed full of life. There is almost no empty volume left between the jagged rock protrusions. Every crevice, undercut, and flat shelf is carpeted by thousands of small, dry-adapted arthropods—flat-bodied thermophilic beetles, blind crevice-scorpions, bristling silverfish-like runners, and armored mites. They are pressed carapace to carapace in dense, layered mats, occupying every millimeter of shelter away from the sharpest static arcs.

Everything here operates on a slow, patient rhythm. The heat does not cycle with day or night; it holds steady, bleeding upward from the rock and outward from the distant furnaces. The machinery outside runs on unbroken shifts, turning the roar into a permanent, unchanging wall of sound. Within the crowd of bodies, movement is minimal and deliberate. A beetle shifts a fraction of an inch over the course of an hour, nudging its neighbor aside to reach a cooler hairline crack or a flake of shed exoskeleton. To move fast in this cramped, dry dark would be to spend moisture that cannot be replaced and to scrape soft joints against edges of raw quartz.

The only things that change with any speed are the tiny static discharges snapping between antennae and charged dust, brief as blinking eyes. Otherwise, the pocket simply endures: a hidden, airless-feeling knot of hot stone, stray electricity, deafening vibration, and crowded, patient life pressed tight into the dark.
