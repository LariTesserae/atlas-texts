High above the lower valleys, the basin opens out like a shattered plate tilted toward a bright, cool sky. It is a wide, high-altitude amphitheater, perhaps a league across from rim to rim, ringed by peaks that have never known a storm. Their ridges are razor-sharp, freshly cleaved planes of pale grey slate and dark chert, meeting at clean, unrounded angles as if the mountain were cracked open yesterday and left to cool.

The light here is clear and unblinking. It has the crisp, slightly thin brilliance of late morning at high elevation, casting sharp, cool-edged shadows across the basin floor. Yet the air itself is cool—just cold enough to stiffen bare fingers, carrying no wind at all. Nothing stirs the atmosphere. A thread dropped from a ledge would hang straight down without a tremor.

Across this open expanse sits a city built in layers, yet spread thin. It is unmistakably urban in its ambition—hundreds of structures, terraced plazas, narrow stone causeways, and multi-story towers—but there is vast emptiness between its parts. Whole acres of raw, fractured bedrock and moss-choked hollows separate one cluster of buildings from the next. The settlement does not crowd itself; it stretches across the valley in a deliberate, sparse grid of elevated walkways, stepped retaining walls, and tall, narrow dwellings that cling to the sharpest rock faces.

Everything is made by hand, with simple, patient tools. The walls are dry-stacked from angular slabs of slate, fitted so tightly that not even a fingernail could slip between the stones, though you can still see the pale, fresh bruise-marks of stone hammers and copper chisels along their edges. Where timber is used—for cantilevered balconies, narrow bridges spanning the rock clefts, and tiered roofs—the beams are rough-hewn and joined with wooden pegs, braided plant fiber, and small, hammered pins of dull bronze. There is no mortar, no iron, no glass. Every surface shows the grain of manual labor: scraped bone, lashed reed, carved stone.

Despite the lack of rain or rivers, the city is damp. Moisture gathers directly out of the cool air, beading on every sharp stone edge and pooling in shallow, hand-carved basins that line the terraces. A network of narrow, open channels—chiseled straight into the bedrock—guides this condensation from the upper roofs down into wide, mirror-still cisterns in the open plazas. The water never rushes; it creeps in a thin, silent film, or hangs in fat, trembling drops from the tips of stone spouts, falling one by one into pools that never seem to rise or spill over.

In this perpetual, cool dampness, the vegetation is unexpectedly lush. Thick, velvety cushions of emerald and blue-green moss swallow the bases of the towers and pad the stone stairs. Broad-leafed ferns uncurl from every dry-stone joint, and trailing vines with waxy, silver-edged leaves drape over the balconies like heavy curtains. Because there is no wind to batter them and no heat to wilt them, the plants grow to a pristine, unblemished fullness. Every leaf is slick with a fine, even sheen of dew.

And the place is full of life, though it is almost entirely silent.

Everywhere you look, something is watching or moving slowly through the damp greenery. Pale, spotted salamanders crowd the rims of the stone cisterns, dozens to a basin, their throats pulsing faintly in the cool air. Heavy-bodied, iridescent birds—something like sleek, long-tailed magpies—perch motionless along the wooden eaves or glide from tower to tower on broad, silent wings, never calling out. Small, long-legged mammals with thick, slate-grey fur pick their way delicately across the moss-covered causeways, grazing on the waxy vines without a sound. Beneath the ferns, thousands of slow, velvet-shelled beetles crawl along the carved water channels.

Yet for all this abundance, the basin is wrapped in a deep, uncanny quiet. There is no chatter of birds, no rustle of leaves, no distant roar of water or wind. The loudest sound in the valley is the faint, spaced *plink* of a condensation drop hitting a full cistern, or the soft, muted pad of an animal’s foot on wet moss.

It takes a little while to realize what is truly strange about the place. There is no dust on the stone floors, and no fallen, yellowing leaves rotting in the corners of the plazas. The bronze pins holding the wooden bridges together have no green patina; the freshly chiseled slate shows no sign of weathering. The shadows do not lengthen. The birds glide, land, and take off again, yet their numbers never seem to thin or swell, and no creature hunts another in haste.

The basin is locked in a quiet, cool equilibrium. The drops that fall into the cisterns evaporate back into the still air at the exact rate they condense on the cold stone. The moss stays lush without overgrowing the walkways; the sharp mountain ridges stay razor-edged without crumbling into scree. Even the city itself—its thousands of hand-cut stones, its empty, sweeping plazas, its silent, teeming wildlife—exists outside of decay or growth, held in a bright, damp, unmoving permanence.
