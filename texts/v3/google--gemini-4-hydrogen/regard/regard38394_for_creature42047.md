At first, the impulse of benevolence was simple: *to mend.*

Drawn across the dark by the strange, bright anomaly of a sea lifted miles into the thin air, the power arrived above the silvered surface and looked down into the blinding blue. What it saw looked, to any eye trained on gentler worlds, like a cry for help. A continent-wide wound of raw basalt and jagged silica, deafened by sixty-knot rivers, scrubbed bare of soil, of warmth, of a single darting fin. A world stuck in the terrible noise of its own birth, unable to finish.

Benevolence reached, as it so often does, for the mercies it knew best.

It gathered in its mind the softness of silt to cushion those razor-sharp ridges. It considered a single drop of green plankton to cloud the harsh, surgical daylight into something kind, a first small fleet of crabs to clean the crevices, a tempering warmth to quiet the tearing shear of the currents. It had seen a thousand barren seas made whole by such small, patient gifts.

Then wisdom stayed its hand.

Before a single grain of sand could fall, the power stopped looking for bodies and began to pay attention to the *manners* of the water.

It sank—not as a weight, not as a warm shadow that would spoil the geometry, but as a pure, unintrusive attention—down into the optical-glass void. It felt the sixty-knot current slam against a silica needle and slide off, a hair’s breadth from the stone, in uncanny, frictionless quiet. It watched a rank of tiny bubbles snag on an unseen filament, march sideways across a kilometer of roaring emptiness, and vanish with a crisp *ping, ping, ping*. It traced the towering, slow-motion lenses of chilled water bending the daylight into searchlights, and felt the pale-green ribbons of weed snapping like tattered flags, singing their sharp vibrations down through glassy roots into the groaning bedrock below.

And the power understood.

To drop a handful of silt here would not be charity; it would be blindness. To seed this water with plankton would be to stuff wool into the ears of a listener. If the razor edges were allowed to round into sand, if the first crabs scuttled into the crevices and died and rotted, the clarity would fail, the taut catenaries of charge would slacken, and the minds that *held* this ocean would dissolve into an ordinary, crowded sea.

They were not trapped in an unfinished world. They *were* the unfinished world.

Benevolence, humbled, drew back its gifts. Yet a second, subtler temptation remained.

Looking deeper—past the glassy holdfasts, down into the freshly extruded fissures where the iron wept and the plateau groaned upward—the power saw the horizon of time. This place was a precarious miracle. A planet does not stay in its first breath forever. Sooner or later, the hearths beneath the floor would cool. The bedrock would stop rising, the kinetic fury would ebb, and the long, taut architecture of tension would have no choice but to let go.

*Then,* whispered benevolence, *let us keep it for them. Let us stoke the hearths from below, brace the plateau against gravity, and hold the glass clear forever.*

Again, wisdom refused.

A held breath is only alive because it strains against the weight of exhaling. The holders did not want a museum; they fed on the *fury* of becoming—the ozone stripped from whitecaps, the thunderclap of collapsing cavitation, the slow, metallic speech of rising stone. To lock this sea in amber from the outside, to replace their tireless, straining work with an effortless eternity, would be to hollow them out just as surely as drowning them in mud.

So the power asked itself the hardest question a benevolent and wise nature can ask: *What is left to do, when both fixing and saving would destroy?*

Almost nothing. And yet, not quite nothing.

Looking outward beyond the churning, silvered ceiling, the power saw the rest of the planet—lower down, off the high plateau, where older, warmer seas were already beginning to cloud with the first green haze of ordinary life. A blind, planetary storm was gathering there, massive enough to hurl spores and warm, muddy upwellings over the rim of the high shelf long before the plateau’s own season was ready to close.

The power did not build a wall. It did not banish the ordinary life below, which had its own right to grow and crowd and sing in mud and flesh. It simply laid a quiet, invisible hand against the shoulder of that single storm and turned its path aside, letting the murky tide spend itself in the lower deeps. It bought the high sea nothing eternal—only its full, unhurried span.

And then, before withdrawing, it offered one small courtesy, spoken in the only tongue that does not rattle uselessly in a sixty-knot roar.

Deep in the open blue, miles from any spire, the holders felt a brief, inexplicable alignment. A fragile crystalline fin, newly frozen from a mineral plume and trembling on the verge of snapping in a rogue eddy, found itself wrapped for three heartbeats in a stillness as clean and frictionless as the holders’ own skin—until the current shifted and passed it by. Along a league-long catenary of force, a single, unbidden *ping* traveled from spire to spire, clear as a struck bell, carrying no weight, no heat, no demand.

Only: *We see the tension. We hear the song. Hold the glass as long as it is yours to hold.*

Then the power stepped back into the wider dark, leaving no shadow, no warmth, and not a single grain of silt behind.

Miles above the true crust, in the blinding, deafening blue, the pale-green ribbons snapped like flags against the flood, the bubbles marched in crisp, parallel ranks, and the vast, invisible architecture turned back to the loud, tireless work of keeping the world unborn.
