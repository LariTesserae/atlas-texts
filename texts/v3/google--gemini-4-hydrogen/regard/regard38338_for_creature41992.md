If a benevolent and wise power were to turn its attention toward this pocket of black stone, it would first have to learn how to arrive.

It could not come as light, for light here is rationed to a bruised, green-filtered twilight, and anything brighter would scald the translucent snails and bleach the blind workers in their arcades. It could not come as wind, or voice, or thunder, for the chamber’s first law is silence. To enter without breaking what it wished to behold, the power would have to fold itself small, shed its radiance, and step across the narrow ceiling rift as nothing more than a deeper kind of attention—obeying both laws from the very first moment: *do not waste heat, and do not make a sound.*

Inside, it would wait.

It would let its awareness settle the way the moisture settles, clinging to the undersides of the raw basalt ledges. In that thick, incubating hush, it would read the chamber not as a collection of small, struggling things, but as one slow, intricate organism. It would trace the pale columns of termites laying their world down millimeter by millimeter; the matte-black wasps stalking the mud-spines with moth-husks in their jaws; the white mycelium breathing steady warmth up through the organ-pipe chimneys; the geckos drinking that warmth through their bellies; the whip-spiders reading the wet stone with hair-thin whips.

And here, benevolence alone would feel a sudden, sharp ache to *help*.

Benevolence would look at the razor-edged, glassy basalt and wish to smooth it, so no velvet worm would ever have to pick its way over blades, and no gecko would risk a fatal fall. It would look at the bare mineral floor and wish to lay down a bed of rich, dark soil for the ferns. It would look at the narrow rift in the ceiling and wish to widen it—just a hand’s breadth—to let in a real shaft of sun, a breath of clean air, a little room for the smoky-winged wasps to fly again. It would see a stray termite wander an inch beyond its damp mortar, see the gecko’s tongue flick out, and wish to stay the predator’s strike.

But wisdom would hold benevolence’s hand.

Wisdom would see that if the stone were smoothed, the wasps would lose the sharp glassy ridges that anchor their cantilevered galleries, and the miners would lose the soft vesicles they rasp into mortar. If soil were poured across the floor, it would choke the aerial roots, bury the angular pools where the snails drink, and smother the bare expanses that give the city its stillness. If the rift were widened, the wind would rush in, the humidity would flee, the mycelium would wither, and the loud, sun-drunk wasps from the scrubland above would descend and slaughter their silent, grounded kin. And if the geckos were forbidden to eat, or the whip-spiders to hunt, the city would swell past the measure of its chimneys and choke on its own unburied numbers.

Even the faint, incubating warmth rising from the young basalt—the power would see that it is finite, a residual gift from the stone’s cooling, destined one day to fade. Benevolence might wish to stoke it forever, to lock the pocket in an eternal, unchanging spring. Wisdom would know that nothing living is meant to be kept in amber; this city is a brief, exquisite chord struck between fire’s cooling and weather’s arrival, and its beauty belongs to its own season.

So the power would do nothing to the chamber.

It would not mend a single damp wall, nor spare a single moth, nor hurry a single drop of water. It would not even announce itself, for a creature that spends its life listening for tremors in the dark does not need the weight of a god’s gaze pressing down on its back.

Only, perhaps, as it withdrew—rising back through the jagged rift onto the flat, indifferent scrubland above—it might linger for a moment on the threshold. Not to build a wall around the shelf of black stone, and not to alter its fate, but simply to turn aside, by the width of a single pebble, a careless hoof or a gathering trickle of floodwater that would have drowned the pocket before its time. Just enough to let the chamber keep its own quiet counsel for a little while longer.

Then it would leave the place as it found it: dark, warm, and whole. Beneath the shelf, the blind workers would go on laying down their mud highways, the geckos would press flat against the clay pipes, and at long intervals, a single bead of condensation would swell, let go, and strike a broad leaf with a soft *tock*.
