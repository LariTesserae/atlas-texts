It did not arrive with thunder, because thunder would have cracked the dry-stone walls and sent the cliff-swallows spiraling in panic out over the void. It came instead as a quiet sharpening of attention—the way a mind leans closer to a small, intricate knot of thread to see how it holds.

High above the heat-smeared plains, where the air was thin and the light had the color of old pewter, it paused at the lip of the granite bowl and simply looked.

At first, it felt the sharp, immediate ache that any loving thing feels when looking upon scarcity. It saw the bone-dry cracks in the rock. It saw the mist-tenders crawling out onto lashed-timber catwalks in the dark before dawn, their narrow bone visors tied against a glare that had not yet risen, waiting in agonizing stillness for a few thimbles of dew to condense on goat-hair nets. It saw the horn-thick heels, the careful, shallow breathing, the children chewing bitter, fleshy leaves to wet their tongues.

And in the first breath of its pity, it nearly reached out.

It could have struck the granite behind the highest tier of houses and called up a spring—sweet, cold water spilling down the soapstone stairs, pooling in the bedrock mortars, turning the dust to mud. It could have parted the high-altitude veil of dust and let the full, golden noon pour into the bowl, warming the dim rooms until the tallow lamps were no longer needed. It could have widened the ledges, softened the thorny scrub, and given them wheat that grew in a season instead of roots that took years to fatten in hairline fractures.

It held those gifts in its palm, ready to drop them into the hollow.

Then, because it was wise as well as good, it waited, and traced what each gift would undo.

If it gave them a spring, the water would seep behind the mortarless stones, swelling the ancient clay plaster until it sloughed off in heavy sheets. The multi-story dwellings, built to breathe dry heat, would sag and buckle. The silver-jade succulents would rot at the crown; the beards of gray-green air-plants would blacken and drop from the overhangs. Worse, the dampness would climb into the high honeycomb hollows where the dead lay wrapped in fiber, turning odorless parchment back into corruption, breaking the clean circle by which a grandmother’s femur became a loom-shuttle and an uncle’s rib held a mist-net taut.

And if the water spilled over the lip of the chasm, gleaming down the sheer granite face toward the plains miles below, someone down there would see the shine. In a dry world, a mountain that weeps is a beacon. Men with iron would come climbing up the baking rock, and the people of the bowl—who had no word for *march* and no metal sharper than bone—would have nowhere left to retreat.

If it parted the dust to give them the sun, their wide, twilight-adapted eyes would sear in an hour. If it silenced the ten thousand swallows to give them quiet, it would strip away the very wall of noise that gave them privacy in alleys two shoulders wide, and render their bright, whistling speech meaningless. If it made their lives easy, they would forget how to move slowly; they would begin to hurry, to spill breath, to discard what was cracked instead of wedging it with slate.

They were not broken. That was the hardest truth for benevolence to accept. What looked from afar like poverty was an architecture of astonishing grace—a crust of life so finely fitted to its rock that to pry up one stone would unseat the whole wall.

So the power drew back its hand from the bowl, leaving not a single ripple in the smoked-amber air.

Instead, it turned its gaze downward.

It looked through three miles of solid, baking granite, down to the mountain’s unseen roots where two ancient tectonic shelves were slowly, invisibly grinding toward a fracture. Not today, and not for a thousand years, but eventually that tension would have snapped in a single violent shrug, shaking the elephantine domes like pebbles and spilling the town into the sky. Gently—so gently that not even a rock-hyrax paused in its barking—the power laid its palm against the deep stone and eased the seam, letting the stress bleed away into harmless heat, returning the mountain to the slow, safe pace of continents.

Then it looked farther still, out past the sheer drop, down into the flat, featureless smear of the plains where no one in the bowl had ever walked.

There, in a distant, unnamed basin at the foot of the massif, lay the marshes and scrub-forests whose faint exhalations fed the evening thermal. They were thinning, century by century, starved by shifting winds. If they dried completely, the pre-dawn updraft would carry no ghost of humidity at all. The mist-tenders would crawl out onto their catwalks, stretch their dark nets over the void, and draw them in dry—night after night—until the clay amphorae stood empty and the tallow lamps went out one by one.

Here, far from the bowl, the power went to work. It nudged a buried aquifer an inch toward the surface. It turned the course of a seasonal flood-channel so the basin below would drink deeply once every few years. It did not make a paradise of the plains—only ensured that the valley’s quiet, steady breath would not fail.

The mist-tenders would never know. They would still have to wake in the cold dark, still tie their bone visors, still wait in aching stillness for the nets to darken. Each drop would still be earned with patience, and measured in thimbles, and sealed with fig-gum. Only now, the mountain’s promise would hold.

When it was finished, the power rose one last time to the rim of the bowl.

Dusk was deepening toward dark pewter. The looms had fallen silent; the *grind-grind* of the mortars had stopped. Along the worn roofs, people sat with their backs against the warm living stone, chewing pickled caper-buds while the swallows settled into the eaves. Beneath them, the evening thermal began its slow climb from the unseen world below, carrying the faint, phantom scent of distant rain.

The power watched them breathe it in—slowly, economically, through flared nostrils—and gave them the rarest gift a wise love can offer:

It left them entirely to themselves.
