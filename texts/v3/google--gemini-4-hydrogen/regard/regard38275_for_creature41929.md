At first, the Power’s instinct was to open its hand and give them green.

It had seen many worlds from the cold side of the stars, and it knew the shape of a wound. Looking down at the endless, steaming plane—at the blinding specular glare, the deafening roar of the sluices, the slick-skinned crowds shouting in bone-conducted hums and sharp snaps of the fingers—the Power felt the ancient, tender reflex of all great caretakers: *Let me cool this. Let me quiet this. Let me give you soft earth to lie down in, and a horizon where nothing is moving.*

It nearly reached out to part the humid clouds, to coax moss onto the pitted basalt piers, to hush the relentless bass thrum of the mass-dampeners so a child could hear a whisper across a street.

Then, because it was wise as well as kind, it stopped. It folded its vast attention inward, narrowed itself to the width of a catwalk filament, and *listened* before it touched a single bolt.

---

It listened from the top down.

On the swaying upper decks, it watched a High-Walker whistle a pale glider down from a thermal plume, feed it a scrap of crab-meat, and let it pick copper-eating mites from the tensioned cables. If the Power cooled the air and stopped the updrafts, the gliders would starve, the filaments would rot, and the squalls would come unannounced.

Lower down, it pressed its awareness against the programmable silicate walls. It felt the Skin-Coaxers moving in spider-harnesses, silent and reverent, pressing their tuning-forks to the stone so the city could flex and breathe without crushing its own crowds. It saw the feral rigs—old, unledgered iron—scuttling into dry pockets behind steam-manifolds with hoarded copper washers, patching leaks out of sheer, stubborn habit.

At street level, it stood unseen inside a hanging curtain of moving water. It watched two dock-hands argue a cargo weight in three seconds of jagged flick-cant, then press their knuckles to each other’s collarbones to hum a quiet warning past the screaming vents. It watched a sluice-tender—ears long dead to high frequencies, feet bare on a vibrating grate—read the health of a gravity-baffle three blocks away by the geometric ripples in a puddle at her heel.

If the Power silenced the roar, it would blind the silver-darts in the canals, deafen the flake-crabs that chimed like porcelain in the pier-joints, and leave the sluice-tenders stranded in a world that no longer spoke to the soles of their feet.

And when the Power sank all the way down—past the sulfur-eating mats weeping warm brine, past the silt-tongues anchored in the dark—into the quiet vaults of the Dissolvers, it finally understood.

It watched the warm digestion-vats return a dead Skin-Coaxer to clean salt, mineral heat, and silicate slurry. It understood that these people were not prisoners trapped in a broken machine. They, and the fungus, and the crabs, and the half-feral iron, and the restless walls were *one single, fiercely loyal organism*. To give them graves in cool dirt would be to exile their dead from the street beneath their children’s feet. To tear down the crowding would be to tear apart the loop that kept them alive.

The Power unclenched its hand. It would not remake the Basin.

Instead, it looked for what the organism could not heal on its own.

---

In a system packed to the absolute limit, where nothing leaves and nothing rests, the danger is never malice. It is *margin*. It is the slow, invisible narrowing of slack until a single misstep breaks the whole engine.

So the Power went to work—quietly, in the grammar of the city, leaving no signature that would frighten the superstitious or invite worship.

First, it sank into the ancient bones. Down in the murky shallows, where centuries of warm, turbulent currents had pitted and grooved the cast basalt and vitrified silt, the Power gently coaxed the dissolved minerals in the water. It did not repair the piers overnight; that would have changed the currents and confused the shoals. Instead, it taught the vitrified silt a slow, geological patience—a crystalline habit of growing inward into micro-fractures whenever the bass hum of the mass-dampeners stressed the stone. The foundations would hold for another ten thousand years without ever needing to be rebuilt.

Second, it looked at the chemosynthetic mats—the warm, bruised-looking pelts that scrubbed the sulfur and fed the curd-houses. A single rogue phage, a single shift in the mineral runoff, could starve the whole basin in a month. Without altering their taste or their heat, the Power braided a quiet redundancy into their chemistry, widening their tolerance for salt and heavy metals by a fraction of a percent. Invisible. Unremarkable. Enough to catch a catastrophe before it began.

Third, it listened to the walls. The Skin-Coaxers had been right: the programmable silicate *did* carry memory. Every hurried reconfiguration, every frantic patch, every shouted argument near a fresh extrusion had left tiny, rigid knots of strain in the material—stutters in the stone’s memory that made the walls a little stiffer each decade, a little slower to yield.

Wait-deep in the night-cycle, when the street traffic thinned to a trickle, the Power sent a soft, sub-audible harmonic through the mass-dampeners. Not enough to disturb a single standing wave in a puddle. Just enough to coax the silicate to let go of its old bruises.

And last, the Power looked at the people—at the salt-calloused cheeks, the narrow eyes behind smoked-ceramic visors, the endless, breathless cycle of glare and steam with nowhere to look that wasn't already occupied.

It could not give them a horizon. There was no room for one.

Instead, it reached into the upper atmosphere, where the warm, salt-heavy updrafts met the low, fast-moving clouds, and nudged the thermal convection by half a degree.

---

The next morning, a Skin-Coaxer named Vael hung in her spider-harness forty meters above a clogged thoroughfare, her mouth shut tight, her tuning-fork poised against a bulging wall.

When she struck the prong and pressed it to the silicate, she nearly dropped her field-wand.

The wall did not resist. It did not stutter or shudder against her palm. It softened like warm wax, smooth and unhurried, flowing into a clean new buttress with an ease she had not felt since her apprenticeship thirty years ago. She looked across the canyon at her partner on the opposite wall and snapped a rapid, bewildered question in flick-cant. Her partner only shrugged, tapped the side of his ceramic visor, and grinned. *Maybe we were finally quiet enough,* his fingers said.

Down on the lower grates, a sluice-tender paused over his puddle, frowning at the geometric ripples. The hum in his heels felt… cleaner. As if a grit-clogged bearing three blocks away had somehow spat out its stone.

And that evening, between the passing of two tepid squalls, something happened that had not happened in seven generations.

Out on the swaying catwalks, the High-Walkers looked up as the low, fast-moving cloud deck tore cleanly open—not for long, just for ten quiet minutes—and stayed open against the rising steam.

The glare was gone. Above the endless, gleaming plane of metal and water, there was a deep, uncrowded, motionless dark, pierced by a thousand cold points of white fire.

A High-Walker froze with a wrench in his hand. Beside him, a leathery glider tilted its head, its sonar-pings falling silent as it stared up at something that did not move, did not steam, and did not need to be eaten or patched.

Far below, in the roaring canyon of a street, a child stopped running through a water-curtain and pointed down at a vibrating puddle on a metal grate. The standing waves were still dancing—sharp, geometric, rhythmic—but caught inside every tiny crest of water was a reflected spark of starlight, trembling like a secret.

Then the warm updrafts rolled back in. The clouds knitted shut. The tepid rain began to hiss against the hot ceramic roofs, and the Basin went back to its work, roaring and whole.

And the Power, having left no name behind to be argued over, withdrew into the dark—content to let the engine hum.
