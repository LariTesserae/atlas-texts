At first, the impulse was to rain.

A vast and patient attention, drifting across the bleached emptiness of the plateau, had caught the faintest anomaly: a smear of heat and greenness no wider than a courtyard, humming like a struck bell inside a dead world. It leaned closer, peering through the basket-weave of thorns and waxy leaves, and saw the whole four-story tangle at once—the cramped balconies, the soot-blackened ovens, the children tethered by sinew to cane posts, the elders with their molars ground flat by stone-dust.

It saw a people who had never washed in water. It saw three hundred lives pressed so tightly together that a dropped snake sent ripples of alarm through four tiers of households, and a man’s privacy was nothing more than a fig-leaf mat and the courtesy of his neighbors’ eyes.

And for a long moment, the power considered an easy mercy.

It could part the dry air above the plateau and summon a slow, soaking rain to soften the wind-planed rock. It could crack the sinkhole’s rim, widening the bowl into a terraced valley where the agave and fig might spread their roots in the sun. It could call the deep mineral aquifers upward until a clear spring bubbled up through the talcum-fine floor, washing the resin-smudge from their skin and the grit from their meal.

It held the rain in its hand, ready to let it fall.

Then it waited, and watched a day turn inside the hollow.

It watched the Tap-Keepers crawl out into the iron-hard roof before dawn, spider-light and quiet, reading the health of the canopy in a single bead of sap. It watched two women on the middle tier argue without raising their voices, turning their backs to one another and pounding their agave fibers harder—*thock-thock-thock*—letting the rhythm of their mallets carry the weight of their anger until it spent itself against the stone.

It watched a boy climb a bone-runged ladder, his splayed, calloused toes gripping a notched femur that had once belonged to his great-grandmother, and it understood what would happen if it broke the stone open.

Rain on the plateau would rot the dry-adapted roots that held the roof together. A spring on the pit floor would drown the hyraxes and the pale geckos, turn the dust-baths to mud, and breed fevers the Rim-Under had no names for. Worse: a green valley on a dead plain would be a lantern lit in the dark, drawing whatever lowland empires forged those heirloom bronze blades up from the foothills to claim it.

And the Rim-Under themselves—if you gave them flat ground and endless water, they would unlearn the verticality that had shaped their bones and their manners. They would spread out, raise their voices, forget the quiet shorthand of tapped knuckles and averted eyes. They would cease to bury their dead in the ladders that held up the living.

To rescue them from the pit would be to unmake them.

So the power closed its hand over the rain, and let the plateau stay white, silent, and forbidding.

Instead, it went down into the dark, past the talcum floor, past the blind pale centipedes, three hundred feet into the ancient bedrock where the taproots drank.

There, where no human eye would ever look and no gratitude would ever be owed, it did its quiet work.

It found a hairline fissure that had begun to pinch shut under the slow, grinding weight of the plateau, and eased it open by the width of a reed-stalk, coaxing a steady, mineral-rich vein of water toward the oldest roots of the fig-canopy. It did not flood the stone; it merely ensured that for the next thousand generations, when a Tap-Keeper scored the bark before dawn, the sap would bead thick and honest.

Rising back through the tiers, it brushed the hanging curtains of dry-moss and fibrous thatch—not changing their smell or their texture, only lending the cellulose a subtle, stubborn reluctance to catch a stray spark, buying a distracted cook the two extra breaths needed to smother an ember with dust.

And on the third tier, in a damp seam behind a beehive oven where no one had planted anything for a century, it nudged a single, dormant seed of a wild mountain-cress—hardy, bitter, and rich in calcium—to wake and send out a pale shoot. It would not change their diet or turn the hollow into a garden. It would only give the elders something sharp to chew beside their locust-meal, enough to keep their teeth a few winters longer.

Nothing in the sinkhole paused. No one looked up at the olive-stained canopy to wonder at a miracle.

A weaver-bird stitched a loose strand of sinew into its bottle-nest. A slender weasel, wearing a collar of carved hyrax-bone, slipped into a hanging storage-bag to sleep out the heat. Below, a woman finished scraping the dried mucilage from her arms with a smooth rib-bone, shook the dust from her skirt, and leaned over the rail to pass a basket of seed-cakes across the gulf on a hooked pole.

The power lingered until nightfall, when the fires were banked and the fruit bats dropped in soft rushes from the roof.

Outside, the wind rose across the dead plateau, howling over the sinkhole’s rim like a mouth over a bottle. Inside, three hundred people swayed gently in their knotted hammocks, breathing the warm, sharp scent of fermenting sap and woodsmoke, held safe in the belly of the stone.

The power touched nothing else. It simply listened, for a little while, to the steady, crowded hum of a world that had already learned how to live—and then withdrew, leaving the Rim-Under to the dark, the dust, and the deep, unbroken water beneath their feet.
