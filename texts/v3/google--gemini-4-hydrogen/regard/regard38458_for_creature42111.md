It would not arrive with thunder, or a parting of the thin highland clouds. A power that has grown wise enough to be truly gentle knows that the loudest thing in the sky is rarely the most welcome.

It would simply notice, the way an old gardener notices a single green shoot forcing its way between two flagstones at the far end of a ruined courtyard.

From the height where such attentions usually travel, the primordial mountain range is a finished thing—its peaks long since ground down to a flat, sun-bleached tableland of iron-stained gneiss, empty of everything but wind and quartz dust. Yet there, tucked below the level of the wind, sits a forty-pace anomaly: a small, furious knot of heat and noise and water, holding out against the plateau.

The power leans closer, not to intervene, not yet, but simply to *see*.

At first, it looks with the eyes of the sky, and what it sees is a place that seems almost cruel in its confinement. A deafening parabolic dish where thousands of cicadas and frogs scream into a stone echo chamber; birds diving like knives into a space no wider than a courtyard; and on the floor, two slow, slate-gray creatures that cannot hear the chorus or see the emerald shock of the ferns, spending their entire lives crawling across bare stone to sweep up the dead husks of louder things.

A lesser benevolence—one that was merely kind, without being wise—would be moved to pity. And pity, armed with great power, is a dangerous thing.

Pity would look at the forty-pace bowl and think: *How cramped.* It would want to widen the hollow, carve a gentle valley down to the lowlands, let the creatures wander free.

Pity would listen to the saw-toothed wall of cicadas and think: *How exhausting.* It would want to quiet the static, soften the high-altitude glare, and bring a real, generous rain to fill a proper pond among the moss.

Pity would look at the sweeper—deaf beneath its fused bone plates, squinting through milky slits of silver, eating the dampness off the rock and burying beetle wings in the sphagnum—and think: *How lonely, and how blind.* It would want to open the creature’s ears, sharpen its eyes to the sun, or lift it out of its basin of dust and set it somewhere lush and wide.

The wise power considers each of these gifts, and sets every one of them down, untouched.

Because it looks longer. It waits the twenty minutes it takes for the pattern of the bare rock to make sense.

It sees that if you widen the hollow, the plateau wind drops in and scours the bath-warm humidity away in a single afternoon. It sees that if you bring ordinary rain, you drown the delicate mineral chemistry of the structured dew, starving the silver-backed mites and the translucent grazers that wait beneath the moss. It sees that if you give the sweeper ears, you condemn it to madness inside a stone amplifier; if you give it sharp eyes, the high-altitude sun will burn them blind, and it will no longer be able to read the faint, oily shimmer of phase-state water hanging above the buried disks.

Most of all, it sees that the sweeper is not a prisoner of the hollow, nor a servant left behind by whoever built the seven machines. It is the hollow’s own memory of how to stay clean. Without its slow, sweeping flanks and its habit of tucking compacted chitin into the sphagnum roots, the bare lanes would choke on dead shells, the moss would starve, and the forty-pace world would suffocate in its own abundance.

To "improve" any of it would be to break a sentence that took ten thousand years to write.

So the power does nothing to the air, and nothing to the light, and nothing to the creatures. Instead, it lowers its attention all the way down to the warm gneiss, matching the posture of the two sweepers, and waits to hear what they hear.

Time passes. A high cloud dims the sun for three breaths, and the translucent grazers slip out from the moss to rasp at the stone. A swift snaps its beak inches above a fern.

Then comes the *thrum*.

It rises from beneath the floor, sub-audible and slow, making the fat droplets on the maidenhair fronds shiver and hop upward. Across the bowl, both sweepers freeze—the larger one on the sunlit floor, the smaller, paler one clinging to the wall inside the impossible shadow of the slotted ceramic ring. They flatten their resonant breastbones to the rock and draw water taut beneath their pleated hides, turning themselves into living drumheads to drink the pulse.

And the power, listening through the stone alongside them, feels what they cannot name.

Deep beneath the pale ceramic ring, locked inside the bedrock where no blunt finger can reach and no moss root can grow, the ancient gneiss has shifted by the thickness of a fingernail over the last few millennia. It is pinching the buried flank of the artifact. The *thrum* is still strong, still drawing water out of the dry highland air—but deep in the stone, there is a faint, gritty dissonance in the note. A hairline fatigue in the ceramic that has not cracked yet, not quite, but will, in another century or two. When it goes, the ring will fall silent, the humidity in the western half of the bowl will drop, and the margin of life in this forty-pace room will narrow by a fraction it cannot afford to lose.

The sweepers tend the surface. They keep the lanes clear and the moss fed. They have no way to tend the roots of the machines.

So the wise power does the one thing the hollow cannot do for itself—and does it at the speed of geology, so nothing is startled.

Without a sound, without a tremor the frogs would notice, it eases the pinch of the bedrock by a hair’s breadth. It clears a century’s worth of stray mineral scale from the hidden seam of the ceramic ring, and lets the ancient artifact settle back into its true, unstressed geometry. It does not make the machine stronger, or brighter, or newer. It only gives it back the stillness it had before the mountain shifted.

The *thrum* finishes its four-second swell.

For the last half-beat of that vibration, the note in the stone rings clean and whole—warm, seamless, and without the faint, tired rasp that has lived in it for generations.

Both sweepers hold still a fraction of a second longer than usual, their milky eyes half-closed, feeling the clear resonance pass through their cartilage keels and the taut water beneath their skin. They do not know a power has visited them. They only know that the stone is good today, and the deep pulse is steady.

The fern droplets settle back into gravity. The smaller sweeper unhooks its broad, calloused fingers from the wall and resumes its slow descent toward the floor, while the larger one turns back into the glare, gathering a fallen beetle wing into the soft pouch beneath its jaw.

Above the rim, the wind skims across the vast, empty tableland, carrying nothing. And the power withdraws as quietly as it came, leaving the roaring teaspoon of jungle to keep itself—whole, unmeddled with, and enough.
