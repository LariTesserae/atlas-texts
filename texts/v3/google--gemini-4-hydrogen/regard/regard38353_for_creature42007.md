At first, looking down from a height where even the cloud-deck seems small, the notch looks like an injury—a jagged tooth of raw rock, weeping steam and hung with rusting iron, where two or three human things have been caught in the teeth of a machine and forgotten.

Benevolence, when it first arrives, is almost always tempted to rescue.

It sees the thin, water-logged air and thinks of wide, dry valleys where breath comes without effort. It sees the deafening hiss of the copper relief stack and thinks of quiet orchards, of bird-song, of voices that do not have to be beaten into iron pipes with a wrench-handle. It sees the verdigris ground into the knuckles, the salt-rime in the creases of pale necks, the smoked mica strapped against a blinding glare, and it feels the old, sharp urge to reach down a gentle hand, lift these keepers out of the cauldron, and set them somewhere soft.

Wisdom, however, does not act at the speed of pity. To understand a place where a flywheel turns twice a minute, it has to slow down.

So the power waits.

It lingers in the humid haze, unnoticed by the keepers and the pale salamanders alike, and lets the rhythm of the shelf take its measure. It stays for ten turns of the great wheel. Then a hundred. It listens not to the roar of the steam—which is only the surface of the place—but beneath it, to the *shhh-thunk* of the pistons and the dull, patient *boom* of the counterweight dropping in the dark.

It watches a keeper stand motionless for twenty minutes, palm flat against a fogged sight-gauge, waiting for a tremor too fine for any eye to catch. It watches the slow, economical *tink, tink* of a brass-headed hammer flaking pale mineral scale from a valve-stem, and feels how that small vibration travels through the wet stone into the soles of another keeper’s boots twenty feet away—not a cry for help, not even labor in the way the lowland world knows labor, but a quiet conversation between two parts of the same body.

And when high noon arrives, turning the shelf into a white, blinding well of light and wet green, the power watches them sit with their backs against the humming transformer bank, knees drawn up, chests rising and falling in exact time with the wheel’s turn.

It sees then what pity would have missed.

These are not prisoners. They are not broken tools left behind by some vanished master. They have achieved something rarer and harder than comfort: they have achieved *fit*. Their barrel-ribbed chests belong to this thin, warm air; if it carried them down to a lowland meadow, the thick, dry atmosphere would feel like wool in their throats, and the silence—empty of the transformer’s steady hum—would leave them deaf and unanchored, listening in vain to dirt that does not answer back. Their hands are stained green because they touch what keeps them alive. Their eyes are still because there is nothing here that needs chasing.

To save them from this place would be to unmake them.

So the power’s first act is restraint. It puts away the lowland valleys. It does not quiet the relief stack, or dry the weeping cliff, or soften the raw, glassy edges of the stone. It does not scrub the salt from their skin or give them back the use of their throats. It does not even announce itself—for what would a revelation be in a place where everything is already blindingly lit, and where the only language is weight and vibration?

Instead, it looks where the keepers cannot.

They have hands for the vines, the insulators, the brass valves, the fogged glass. They have the salamanders to warn them when the current surges or the pressure climbs. But three hundred feet below the boiler, down inside the young, unweathered core of the peak, the rock is still new—thrust up too fast, cooled too unevenly, resting on sharp, precarious angles where no brass hammer can reach. There, in the dark heat beneath the shelf, a deep seam of fractured stone is bearing too much weight, creeping fraction by fraction toward a shear that would drop the whole cathedral notch—iron, ferns, salamanders, and keepers alike—into the white void below.

Quietly, with no more disturbance than a settling ember, the power reaches into the deep stone and eases the tension. It does not make the mountain eternal—nothing made of rock and iron should be forced to last forever—but it seats the seam cleanly against its bed, so the floor will stay faithful to the feet that stand on it.

Above, nothing changes. The counterweight drops with its dull iron *boom*. A drop of warm condensation falls from the flywheel’s oiled rim onto a broad fern leaf. One of the keepers shifts slightly against the humming transformer bank, breathes in—a vast, slow draw down to the heels—and breathes out.

The power watches for one more turn of the wheel, asks for nothing, and withdraws, leaving them to the steam, the green, and the long, unhurried noon.
