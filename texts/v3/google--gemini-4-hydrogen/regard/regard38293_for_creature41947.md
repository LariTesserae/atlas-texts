A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive in a burst of light. Light, here, would only scatter uselessly off the anvil clouds, startle the gliding reptiles from the chimneys, and blind the crane-operators on the viaducts.

Instead, it arrived the way the afternoon rain arrives: by slowing down until it matched the tempo of the plateau.

At first, from a great height, it saw what any outsider would see. It saw a world three thousand meters up that had no trees, no grass, no dry stone to sit upon. It saw air so thick with water and midges that no one could run or shout without choking. It saw iron that pitted, mortar that softened, and people whose hands had to be greased against rot, living in the perpetual bass-hum of turbines and the *scritch-scritch* of shelled beasts scraping slime from the cobbles.

Pity, which is the first and least careful form of kindness, stirred in it. It began to measure what it might give.

*I could give them green,* it thought. A single spore of moss, a tough, salt-tolerant grass tucked into the lee of a retaining wall. Within a century, the mesas would be carpeted. The air would smell of crushed leaves instead of warm lime and beetle-meal.

Then it traced the gift forward, and stayed its hand. Grass would drink the thin sheet of runoff before it reached the basins. Roots would pry apart the soot-darkened masonry that held the terraces together. Worse, green would starve the grey and rust-colored slime-fungi—and without the fungi, the great shelled grazers would stall and die on the cobbles, the blind crustaceans would vanish from their skirts, the silk-spinning hives would empty, and the people would be left with grass they could not eat and timber they had no dry ground to season. The plateau did not lack life; it had simply built its forest out of chitin, steam, and bone.

*I could give them clear skies,* it thought next. It could part the anvil clouds, dry the cobblestones for a season, and let them see the stars.

Again, it traced the gift. Without the warm, patient weeping of the air, the settling ponds would shrink. The turbines would whine dry in their arched halls. The amphibians’ pale hides would crack, the gliding reptiles would ground themselves in the dust, and the oiled silk coats of the workers would stiffen like dead husks. The mist was not a prison; it was the lung of the city.

*Then let me give them permanence,* it thought, looking down at a foundry-crew prying a rusted boiler plate from a viaduct. *Iron that never pits. Mortar that never softens. Hands that never need oiling against the mineral damp.*

And here, watching a worker pause to clear lime-grit from the underside of a passing grazer with a bone pick, the power finally understood. If the iron did not rust, the foundries would cool. If the mortar did not yield, the masons would have nothing to mend. The slow, steady casting of new rail-ties to replace the old was not a burden laid upon these people; it was the pulse that kept them woven into the stone. Make the city imperishable, and it would cease to be alive. It would become a monument, and the people inside it would become strangers to their own walls.

So the power withdrew every grand miracle, one by one, before a single drop of water had been turned aside.

It did only two things, and neither of them broke the rhythm of the plateau.

First, it reached three thousand meters down, beneath the dark basalt and the pale karst, into the blind, sunless rock where no grazer crept and no pipe was plumbed. There, in the deep silence below the city’s bass-hum, it found the ancient fault-lines and the hollow limestone vaults that held the plateau’s weight above the distant lowlands. It did not freeze the rock—stone must still weep and dissolve, or the fungi would starve—but it steadied the deep shelves against sudden shear, ensuring that no blind geological collapse would ever drain the milky basins or crack the mesas in half. It kept the stage true, so the play might continue.

Second, at the rim of the plateau, where the smooth-shouldered ridges met the pale, curving haze, it thickened the clouds by just a fraction. Not enough to darken the daylight or chill the warm rain—only enough to make the three-thousand-meter climb look, from the distant lowlands, like nothing more than empty stone lost in perpetual fog. If there were hurried empires below, with axes and dry-land ambitions and a desire to improve what they did not understand, they would look up, see only mist, and leave the high stone to itself.

Then the power did nothing else.

It did not speak, because its voice would have clashed with the brass reed-whistles and the chest-deep humming of the people. It did not reveal itself, because gratitude would have interrupted the crane-operators’ hand-signs and slowed the steady work of the silk-harvesters.

Instead, it simply stayed for a while—which, to a power of that kind, meant a few quiet centuries. It rested its attention on the warm, wet stone, listening to the drop-forges, the *scritch-scritch* of the grazers, and the soft clicking of the reptiles cooling on the chimneys at dusk, content to witness a world that had already found how to breathe.
