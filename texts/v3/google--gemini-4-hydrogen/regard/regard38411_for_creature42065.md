First, it would turn down its own brightness.

A power that had crossed the distance between stars or between thoughts would know, almost before it reached the cast-iron octagon, that its usual mercies were poisons here. Warmth—even the gentle warmth of a palm held open in greeting—would raise the air from one degree to two, and at two degrees the spores of gray mold sleeping in the mineral oil would wake. Sunlight would scorch the translucent filaments to paper. Even a voice, carried on a pressure wave through the stamped-steel grating, would shake the delicate rhizoids loose from the pores of the fired clay.

So it would arrive as cold as the room itself. It would strip away its radiance until it was dimmer than the amber pilot-lamps behind their fogged-glass bezels, quieter than the sixty-cycle murmur of the transformers.

For a long time—measured not in human hours, but in forty-second intervals, in solenoid sighs and millimeter shifts—it would simply *attend*.

It would not look for a mind inside the moss, because it would be wise enough to know that a thing does not need a mind to have a life worth keeping. Instead, it would learn the moss’s own language. It would feel the steady, comforting itch of the induction coils through the ceramic baffles. It would wait through the dry intake cycle, feeling the taut, paper-thin longing of the cells as the ozone-sharp air rushed past, and then it would feel the sudden, cool relief of the three-second mist—the *shhh* of the crown-valve, the swelling of a million pale mouths sealing the pleats of the growing lung.

It would trace the faint, unintended circuits where the damp threads draped across the copper capillaries and the slate, and it would understand what the moss was doing without knowing it was doing anything at all: holding the whole caisson in a single, wet, shivering awareness, adjusting the breath of a blind machine.

A lesser benevolence might have pitied the moss. It might have wanted to carry a tuft of it up through three hundred meters of frozen earth and set it on a creek-stone in the sun, where it could turn green and thick and careless. Or it might have wanted to hurry the extrusion arms, to bring the *something with lungs* down the shaft early, to give this quiet chamber a purpose a human could name.

The wise power would do none of those things. It would see that the moss was not exiled; it was *fitted*. It had pared itself down to the exact shape of this freezing dark, and it was content in the only way a bryophyte can be content: it was unbroken, unrotted, and needed.

And yet, looking closely at the newness of the place—at the sharp, unblemished weld-beads and the putty not yet cured to hardness—the power would see the thinness of the margin.

A machine built to run for years in the dark has small, mortal seams. A Bakelite relay can weld shut on a surge. A glycol pump can lose a fraction of its head, letting the perimeter creep up by half a degree. The pale-blue work-light on its articulated arm has a filament, and filaments eventually thin and part, leaving the outer pleats without even the faintest memory of chlorophyll. And in the sump below the grating, where the runoff moves in a film too thin to ripple, the dissolved copper salts could one day grow too rich, turning from nourishment to slow poison.

If the power acted at all, it would act there—in the margins that neither the blind machine nor the brainless moss could watch.

It would not alter the blueprint. It would not speed the forty-second clock by a single beat.

Instead, it might lay one cold, invisible hand against the outer iron plates, three hundred meters beneath the frost, and simply *steady* the cold—so that whatever shifted in the world above, whatever seasons thawed or froze, this pocket of air would hold at one degree above zero until its work was done. It might touch the glass envelope of the pale-blue work-light, not to brighten it by a single lumen, only to ensure the filament would never snap before the last slate panel was seated. It might balance the trace salts in the sump by a few parts per million, leaving the water clean enough to drink for another century.

And then—because true benevolence does not ask to be thanked by a thing that has no words for thanks—it would withdraw.

It would leave no sign of its visit. Not a warm spot on the aluminum flanges, not a ripple in the sump, not a flicker in the amber pilot-lamps.

Forty seconds later, a solenoid would sigh. The matte-black arm would shift two degrees and lay down another millimeter of dark polymer. A drop of condensation would gather on a copper busbar, tremble, and fall into a galvanized catch-pan—*tink*—and across thirty square yards of wet, humming ceramic, the moss would feel it all at once, and keep breathing, and keep waiting in the dark for the rest of the body to finish growing.
