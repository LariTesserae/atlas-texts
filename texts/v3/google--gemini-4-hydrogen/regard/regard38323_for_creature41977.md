A power that is both wise and benevolent does not arrive with light.

It knows, before it ever crosses the threshold of the basalt, that light here would be an injury. It arrives instead the way water arrives at the root of a fern—seeping through the unweathered stone without breaking a single sharp edge, taking the measure of the chamber by its heat, its pressure, and the slow, thunderous cadence of its breath.

At first, it simply listens.

It feels the house-sized valves cycling behind the walls. It watches the invisible containment fields gather the runoff into trembling lenses, hold them, and let them fall. It feels the thirty-meter drop, the booming crash against the dark basins, and the sudden, synchronized hunching of the matte-charcoal forms along the recessed balconies as the shockwave drives oxygen and dissolved stone through their copper-veined hides.

And it reads the hair-thin filaments. It does not try to translate the feverish, half-degree pulses passing back and forth across the misty void—wisdom knows the difference between eavesdropping and witnessing—but it understands the *shape* of the conversation. It is the sound of things that were brittle, reminding one another how to be soft again.

Then the power looks at what was built in haste.

Because it is benevolent, its first impulse is to comfort. It thinks of gentling the violence of the water-lenses so the balconies do not shudder so hard; of warming the bruised amber-violet gloom into something kinder; of reaching out to touch one of the matte-black filaments with a single, steady pulse that means *You are no longer in danger. You may rest.*

Because it is wise, it does none of these things.

To soften the crash of the water would be to starve their membranes of the pressure they need to drink. To brighten the chamber would be to scorch skins that abandoned sight long ago. To speak on the filaments—even a word of peace—would be to introduce an alien grammar into a closed room where every tremor is already accounted for. These beings did not build a tomb, nor a prison. They built a cradle out of thunder and wet slate. The kindest thing a visitor can do in a nursery is keep its shadow off the floor.

So, if it acts at all, it acts only in the margins—in the places the builders, working in haste, did not have time to finish.

Deep inside the mountain’s bones, *above* the vaulted ceiling where the rock need not stay sharp, the power feels the micro-fractures where the laser-vitrification met natural fault lines. Left alone, ten thousand booming drop-cycles would eventually rattle a slab loose. Quietly, without altering a single visible plane of the ceiling, it knits the deep stone together, lending the hasty excavation the patience of geological time.

It traces the closed hydrological loop down into the dark floor-slabs and tastes the air. The chamber is running rich right now, heavy with crushed fern and oxidized copper, but a closed system built in a hurry always spends its trace elements faster than it expects. So the power seeds the deep drainage channels—not with anything foreign, only with a slow, unhurried reserve of copper-bearing mineral and slate-salt, tuned to dissolve at the exact rate the air loses its tang. The breath of the chamber will not thin out before the cycle is done.

Finally, it looks upward, toward the dry, sunlit, or airless world that drove these sleepers underground. Between that surface and this vaulted ceiling, it draws a quiet seam. Groundwater seeping down from above will find itself turned aside before it can carry surface mold, or beetle eggs, or the scent of predators into the immaculate wet. The deep, chest-rattling hum of the infrastructure will not travel upward as a seismic curiosity to draw drills or excavation crews. From the outside, this patch of earth will seem entirely, unremarkably dead.

Then the power withdraws its attention.

It does not linger to be noticed. It leaves no signature in the wet silt, no warmth on the railings that did not come from the machinery, no ripple along the filaments.

Down in the roaring dark, the next water-lens grows heavy. The containment field gives way. A ton of warm water explodes against the basins below, shaking the mist out of the air, driving life deeper into the folded mantles on the balconies—and in the ringing silence afterward, the filaments begin to hum again, uninterrupted, unaware that anything ever looked in on them and decided they were worth keeping safe.
