First, it would not step inside.

A lesser kindness—one that knew only flesh and water and the fear of small spaces—might have descended onto the flat-topped spine in a shape of warmth and breath. It would have squeezed sideways down the basalt stairs, marveling at the bronze baffles, and wondered why the air felt so dead. And three walls away, wedged in the dark, the flat-plated things would have felt a sudden, damp silence crawling through the stone, wrong in every frequency.

A wise power knows how to look without occupying space. It arrives not as a body, and not as a voice, for even a whispered word would flatten an overtone. It attends to the mountain the way the mountain attends to the wind: by reading the pressure of the air, the grain of the basalt, the exact pitch of the chord rising out of the sun-baked basins below.

At first, benevolence tests its own habits. It looks at the dry, leather-cracking heat and considers *rain*—a single, gentle cloud drawn up through the ochre haze to cool the baked clay and rinse the salt from the corners. Then it looks closer, at the quartz-and-salt lattices laced through the creatures’ spines, at the mortar cured hard as iron, at the bronze plates untouched by green decay. It sees what rain would do: swell the joints, poison the metal with verdigris, turn the dry salt to slurry, and drown the chord in mud. To bring water here would not be mercy. It would be an execution dressed as a gift.

So it considers *quiet*. Surely, it thinks, a century of deafening, unchanging roar is a kind of madness. What if it softened the thermal draft for a single hour—just long enough to let the stone stop shaking, just long enough for the flat, oil-filled bodies wedged in the shafts to rest? Then it follows the current down into their nerves and sees that the vibration *is* their blood. Without the bass drone flexing their crystals millions of times a second, there is no spark in them, no waking, no life. To hush the mountain would be to suffocate them in their sleep.

So it considers *space*. What if it widened the ribcage-narrow halls by a hand’s breadth, lifted the low ceilings, opened a courtyard to the amber sky so they might stretch out and see the horizon? Then it watches one of them climb a corkscrew shaft, splaying four double-jointed limbs to brace against opposite walls, suspending itself like a living bridge. Widen the stone, and they fall. Give them eyes, and they are blinded by a light that carries no information they can use.

Benevolence stops, and wisdom takes its place.

It looks at the place again—really looks, without trying to translate it into something softer or greener. It sees a world that has solved every problem it was given. There is no hunger here that requires a kill; the wind feeds them, and the salt they shear from the mortar keeps the wind’s throat clear. There is no rot, no fever, no frantic race against seasons. Even death is clean: a slow stiffening into amber wax, and then the quiet hands of kin wedging the hollow, resonant husk into a lower crevice so the note stays true.

There is no prisoner here to free. There is no wound here to bind. There is only an equilibrium so patient and exact that even the smallest improvement would be a kind of violence.

So the power does nothing.

It does not lay a blessing on the stone, because a blessing might weigh down a tile. It does not leave a sign in the mortar, because a sign might alter the draft. It holds back its own brightness, its own moisture, its own instinct to mend, and withdraws its attention as carefully as it arrived—leaving not even the shadow of a silence behind.

If it takes anything from the mountain, it is only the memory of the chord: the quiet certainty that somewhere above the ochre haze, life has found a way to live inside a single, unbroken note, and needs nothing from heaven except to be left to sing.
