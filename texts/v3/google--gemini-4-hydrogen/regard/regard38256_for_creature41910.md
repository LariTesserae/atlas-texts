At first, the attention arrives as an impulse to mend.

To a power accustomed to measuring life by green shoots, open sky, and the clean rush of water, the pocket looks like a mistake—a dark, airless wound wedged inside the mountain, punished by someone else’s machines. It sees the kiln-heat with no dawn to break it, the deafening roar of the crushers, the razor-edged quartz, the thousands of blind bodies pressed carapace to carapace in their own stale breath. It sees the flat-backs wedged into the hottest seams of magnetite, waiting days without moving for a stray spark to stun a neighbor so they can drink it dry.

A merely kind power would reach in at once.

It would send a single, merciful thread of snowmelt seeping down the hairline fault to cool the stone and wet the parched vents of the beetles. It would lay a palm over the ridge-top transformers and hush the relentless whine of the engines. It would widen the cleft by a few inches, just enough to let the thin mountain wind sweep out the ozone and give the crowded mats room to turn.

But this power is wise before it is gentle, and so it pauses—and looks again, not from above, but from the floor of the pocket, with its belly pressed flat against the basalt.

In that stillness, it sees what its mercy would do.

A single drop of water would turn the flour-soft magnetite into heavy, conductive mud. It would clump the iron-oxide bristles along the flat-backs’ flanks, strip away their living shields, and let the next blue-white arc cook them inside their shells. It would swell the joints of the beetles and bring rot to a chamber that has never known decay.

A breath of cool wind would stiffen the heat-cured wax sealing every carapace, until the smallest shift of a leg cracked a creature open to bleed its moisture into the air. It would scatter the stagnant, shared humidity that the crowded crust of bodies has hoarded for generations.

And silence—silence would be the cruelest blow of all. To hush the engines above would be to blind the flat-backs. That bone-deep bass and metallic whine are not noise to them; they are the horizon, the steady pressure of the world against which the faintest *tick* of a mite’s claw becomes legible. Take away the current, and the sparks stop snapping. Take away the sparks, and the hunters in the seams starve in a sudden, meaningless void.

The power withdraws its hand before a single grain of dust is stirred.

It understands now that these creatures are not prisoners enduring a siege. They are artisans of an impossibly narrow margin. Out of waste heat, stray voltage, pulverized rock, and industrial thunder, they have built a closed, humming cathedral where nothing—not a shed husk, not a breath of vapor, not a speck of iron dust—is ever thrown away. Their patience is not resignation; it is form, honed over countless dark shifts until it fits the cleft as tightly as a crystal seam.

To rescue them would be to erase them.

So the power leaves the pocket dark. It leaves the air thin and tasting of hot silica. It leaves the roar unbroken, the quartz razor-sharp, the dust dry enough to stand in rigid bristles along the magnetic lines. It lets the female flat-back keep her leathery eggs tucked beneath her tail-plate, and lets her hatchlings roll in the grey flour to gather their first armor of iron.

It does only one thing, so small and quiet that not even the vibration-readers in the seams can feel it happen.

Looking up through the dark gneiss toward the ridge where the unseen engines grind and the blasting crews work without knowing what lies beneath their boots, the power sees how newly split this chamber is—how easily a stray tremor, a deeper borehole, or a shift in the scree could collapse the ceiling flat or crack the hairline fault wide to the freezing sky. Without altering the pitch of the roar or the flow of the stray current, it steadies the arch of the stone. It sets a quiet, enduring patience in the basalt around the pocket, so the roof will hold, and the narrow seam will stay pinched against the wind, for as long as the machines above keep turning—and, when they finally fall silent, gently enough that the mountain’s own slow heat will still remain.

Then the power does the only other thing left to give a world so complete in itself: it bears witness. It holds the memory of the blue-white sparks skating harmlessly across matte-grey backs, the glacial drift of armored mites, the fierce, quiet brilliance of a life that asked for no rain, no light, and no mercy—only a two-inch crack of stone, and time enough to endure it.
