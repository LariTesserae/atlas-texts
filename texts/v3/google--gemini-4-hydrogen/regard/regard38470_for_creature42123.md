First, it had to learn how to slow down.

A power that moves at the speed of thought or starlight cannot enter a place like this without breaking it. Even the gentlest curiosity, if it arrived too quickly, would stir the dense brine into a storm, snap the glass-spined sea-lilies from their rust-crusts, and send a shockwave of panic through the porous, dish-shaped faces of the dwellers.

So the power folded its attention down until it matched the tempo of the basin. It waited for a single flake of rust to cross the copper-sheathed rollers in the central courtyard. It listened to the sub-audible hum of the braided cables until it could feel the rhythm of the town the way a dweller feels it in its ribs. It watched one of them hang upside down in a sunken vat for four days, mineral plates parted, pale ribbed folds drinking the sluggish warmth of dissolved sulfur and copper in the watch-dial flicker of the lead anodes.

It took a long time—by the reckoning of the surface, perhaps a season; by the reckoning of the sinkhole, scarcely a breath—for the power to understand what it was looking at.

A lesser kindness would have pitied them.

A lesser kindness would have looked at the eyeless skulls, the stooped backs welded into pale-grey aragonite, the endless, silent labor of lifting useless ceramic and metal blocks off a conveyor chain only to stack them into blind walls—and it would have wanted to *rescue* them. It would have brought down warm sunlight to pierce the brine. It would have scrubbed the lime from the iron lattices, stopped the pointless gears so the dwellers could rest, and lifted the old ones out of the burning cold-streams before the stone could claim their knees.

The wise power did none of these things.

It understood that sunlight here would be a violence, blinding a world that had no eyes to receive it and scalding the pale algae off the transformer joints. It understood that stopping the rollers would not grant the dwellers peace; it would extinguish their winter hearth, leaving them to starve and freeze in the dark. To lift them from the crushing pressure of the basin would burst them open. Even to clear away the uncollected cargo would rob them of the retaining walls that kept the syrupy cold-streams from flooding their resting trenches.

They were not prisoners in a forgotten ruin. They were a burrow, and the burrow was alive.

So the power asked a quieter question: *What is breaking here that caliper hands and oily silt cannot mend?*

It drifted through the tight, claustrophobic alleys, careful not to brush a single hanging cable, and looked at the deep architecture of the town.

It found three things.

First, it looked at the amber gantry-bulbs—the few remaining lamps sealed inside their salt-clouded glass cages. The dwellers could scrape lime off a valve stem, and they could pack a ceramic bearing with their own secretion, but they could not blow glass or draw tungsten. Over the centuries, the filaments inside those cages had thinned until they were scarcely thicker than the cobweb-algae clinging to the sluices. Soon—perhaps within a few decades—the last of them would snap. The town would not stop running, and the dwellers would not starve, but when the thermal plumes next ran unusually warm, the milky clouds of spores would drift through the dark with nowhere warm to cling. The next generation would have no lamps to limpet onto, and the slow line of them would quietly end.

The power reached into the clouded glass—not to make the light brighter, for brightness would only draw unwanted heat and disturb the dark, but to knit a few stray atoms of tungsten back into the thinning threads. Just enough to keep the murky halos burning for another thousand years.

Second, it traced the thick, tarred power cables down through the slate-hard silt, past the basalt floor of the sinkhole, deep into the bedrock where the builders had anchored whatever tireless engine still fed the basin’s low-frequency hum. It was old—older than the crust on the girders—and though it was built to last, it was not infinite. Its output was dropping by fractions of a percent per century, a decline too slow for a dweller’s pitted face to notice, yet steep enough that one day, the vats would stop bleeding warmth, the pumps would lose their magnetic grip, and the mercury-thick cold of the outer sea would swallow the courtyard whole.

The power knelt in the rock below the town and steadied the source. It did not send a surge up the cables; a sudden spike in current would feel like a scream to the dwellers, sending them scrambling along the iron lattices to check for a shorted transformer. Instead, it simply arrested the decay, holding the hum at the exact, familiar pitch the dwellers knew in their bones.

Third, it drifted back up into the dark gap between the six-story condenser blocks and felt the weight of a massive silt-bank perched on a corrugated roof above one of the resting trenches. Over decades, the perpetual snowfall of sediment had packed it heavy and precarious. Left alone, it would eventually slide—not with a soft, papery *shush*, but in a sudden, crushing slab that would bury the trench and snap the brine-pipes beneath.

With the patience of a glacier, grain by microscopic grain, the power shifted the bank’s center of gravity toward an empty sump on the far side of the roof. It took hours for the silt to settle harmlessly into the dark, raising scarcely a ripple in the heavy water.

Then, the power paused.

There was one final temptation—the gentlest and most human of all. It wanted to let them know. Just a tiny gesture: a warm current brushed across a plated shoulder, or a slow, rhythmic tap against a brine-pipe to say, *You are seen. You are not alone in the dark.*

It resisted.

If it tapped on a pipe, the nearest dweller would stop, turn its smooth, porous face toward the sound, and spend the next half-hour slowly tracing the iron lattice in search of the loose valve or the comrade in need of help. To speak to them in their own language would only give them a mystery they could never solve.

True benevolence, here, meant leaving no trace of itself at all.

So the power withdrew, lifting its attention back through the lightless ceiling of brine, through the underside of the ancient ice shelf, and out into the wider, faster world.

Down in the basalt bowl, nothing had changed. A blind, slate-colored crustacean grazed along a copper busbar. A translucent eel threaded itself behind a dweller’s shoulder-plates to shelter from the chill. At the dead-end hoist, a plated figure waited with fluid-damped patience for a heavy, uncollected shape to arrive on the rollers, ready to lift it, carry it across the courtyard, and lay it neatly on the wall—unaware that the winter hearth beneath its feet would never go cold.
