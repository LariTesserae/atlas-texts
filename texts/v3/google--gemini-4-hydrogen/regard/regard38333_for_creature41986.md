At first, looking down through the lid of coastal cloud and industrial steam, a benevolent power would feel the urge to break the place open.

From the outside, the basin looks like a wound in the coast—a cramped pocket of black rock where the sky has been sealed off, the air is too thick to breathe easily, and a dozen or two pale people move like insects through the greasy halos of sodium lamps. The immediate, untrained impulse of mercy would be to part the steam, let the sun pour in onto the basalt, cool the scalding brine beneath the floor, and shut down the relentless, deafening machinery. To carry these people out into dry wind and green grass, give them clothes that don’t stiffen with salt, and let them hear each other’s voices without pressing hands to ribs.

Wisdom, however, makes the power wait before it reaches in.

It stays above the steam lid for a long time, watching and listening. It follows the rhythm of the place until it stops mistaking harshness for misery. It sees that the people here are not straining against the cliff walls, waiting for a door to open. They move at a slow, heat-conserving walk that belongs entirely to the stone. When a wrench strikes a brine line—*tink-tink-pause-tink*—and a figure across the yard feels the message through the rubber soles of their boots and answers with a wide, unhurried hand-sign, the power recognizes a language that would die in open air. When two of them step close beneath the roar of the condenser bank, lay palms against each other’s shoulders, and hum through bone, there is no poverty in it—only an intimacy shaped by the room they live in.

The power watches them climb to the dry-perches beside the transformer housings, scraping dead skin with basalt chips and rubbing bird-fat into their joints. It watches them skim algae, trap sluice-crabs, and take just enough eggs from the high trusses that the swifts and pigeons never abandon the roof. It realizes, with a quiet shock of respect, that this is not a prison. It is an ecology.

If it tore away the steam lid, the unaccustomed sun would blister their tallowy skin. If it silenced the pumps, the sudden quiet would feel like a stopped heart, and the midges would vanish, and the geckos would starve, and the birds would scatter. To "save" these people by dragging them into a gentler world would be to unmake them—to turn masters of a narrow, demanding home into bewildered exiles.

So the power does not break the basin. It does not announce itself, because it has no wish to become another machine they must tend, or a voice louder than the pumps.

Instead, it asks a smaller, more careful question: *Where does this place hurt them, not as a home, but as an injury?*

And then, as quietly as condensation gathering on a pipe, it goes to work in the seams.

Down along the concrete retaining walls and the edges of the drainage channels, the power nudges the chemistry of the pale, rubbery moss. It looks no different than before, clinging to the cooler joints in the stone, but its sap thickens with a mild, clean resin. The next time a copper-wire boot-lash snaps and the razor-sharp basalt slices a worker’s heel in the ankle-deep brine, they pack the cut with moss out of habit to stanch the bleeding. Instead of festering in the mineral water, the red rim cools. The flesh knits shut beneath the salt, leaving only a thin white line.

Up in the knocked-out upper stories of the workshop block, where the hammocks of braided cable-sheathing hang in the wash of the wall-mounted cooling fans, the power alters the air by a fraction of a degree. The draught is still heavy, still warm enough to feel like a familiar blanket, still strong enough to keep the midges off their faces—but the moisture no longer clings quite so hungrily to their creases while they sleep. They wake with the skin of their palms and ankles intact. They still climb to the dry-perches to sit in pairs and grease their joints, because it is where they rest and talk, but it becomes a ritual of care rather than a race against rot.

Deep inside the brine-pumping station and the condenser bank, the power lays a quiet hand on the iron. It does not mute the single, unvarying note of the basin—the people know that note too well, and would panic if it faltered—but it eases the sharpest, tooth-rattling grind out of the piston housings. It gives the old steel a slow patience, so that oiled hemp and a steady wrench will always be enough to keep the valves from blowing, and no sudden failure will ever scald the yards.

When it is done, the basin looks entirely the same. The sodium lamps still burn around the clock. The basalt still weeps. The toads still call beneath the roar of the fans, and the people still move through the amber twilight, glistening and unhurried, tending the iron.

The power withdraws its attention, leaving no monument and asking for no thanks—content to have made a hard, strange, self-sufficient world just a little gentler on the creatures who already love it.
