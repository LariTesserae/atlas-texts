At first, the power thought of rain.

It is the oldest instinct of tenderness, when looking down upon a dry and frantic place: to open the sky, to cool the air, to wash the salt-crust from the red-rimmed eyes and lay the drifting chaff to rest. Standing—if such a presence can be said to stand—above the cracked selenite roof, the power felt the terrible heat rising through the stone, heard the ceaseless, tearing roar of the pipe-organ valley, and wanted to hush it. Wanted to give these thin, parched people soft mud to cool their feet, and sweet water to rinse the mineral grit from their teeth, and quiet earth to lay their dead inside.

Then it looked closer, and stayed its hand.

Wisdom is often nothing more than seeing what a thing is *made of* before trying to mend it. The power traced the chemistry of the basin down into the dark fissures, following the warm mineral oils and volatile esters up through the cork-wood lattices. It saw what a single storm of rain would do.

Water here would not be mercy; it would be a plague. It would dissolve the milky selenite ceiling into a weeping, toxic brine, bringing the sky down in jagged slabs. It would turn the talc floor to lye, blacken the resin-trunks into sodden, rotting pulp, and choke the blind borers in their own tunnels. Worse, it would unmake the people. Their oiled, parchment-tight skin would blister; their waxed lungs, built for hot dust and toasted air, would drown in dampness. To give them a green, quiet world would be to erase them, leaving only a drowned pit where a fierce and brilliant engine of life had once run.

So the power withheld the rain. It swallowed its own pity—for pity, when it does not understand, is only a softer kind of violence—and simply watched.

It watched a tapper perched sixty feet up on a swaying reed-bridge, one hand hooked into a tensioned cable, the other carving a clean channel in an amber trunk with a scraper ground from a grandmother’s femur. It watched two figures half a mile apart in the hazy yellow glare conversing through plucked vibrations, their bodies reading the jungle like a stretched string. It watched them wedge their dead upright into the forks of fast-growing shoots, not out of callousness, but so the bones might hold up the next generation’s blinds—a refusal to let anything, even grief, go to waste in a world that could not afford it.

*They do not need to be saved,* the power understood. *They are already masters of this place.*

And yet, benevolence is not blind. Even in mastery, there is a cost. Watching long enough—watching past the clipped economy of their climbing, past the sulfur-bright precision of their hand-signs—the power saw where the basin took slightly more than it needed to.

It saw them crawl into their low, crouching huts in the lee of the rock outcrops. It saw them pull the wax plugs from their ears and unlace their horn visors, desperate for a pocket of air where a person might hear themselves think. The stretched carapace-plates muffled the metallic screech of the stridulators, turning the roar into a distant surf. 

But the floor still trembled underfoot.

Even in the dark, even with their eyes shut and their backs against the woven bark, the deep shale conducted the frantic, unceasing vibration of the basin straight into their heels, their spines, their teeth. Their bones were never off-duty. Even in sleep, their nerves were braced against the next collapse, the next borer-rasp, the next snapping cable. Because the stone itself never went still, they could never fully set their weight down—and because they could never set their weight down, there was no room in them for anything except tomorrow’s hazards.

The power did not touch the sky. It did not touch the plants, or the brass-winged swarms, or the great, roaring thermals that kept the valley alive. 

Instead, it reached down—quietly, without a flash of light or a breath of wind—and laid its attention along the raw, fractured bedrock beneath the small rock outcrops where the huts were wedged.

It did not change the basin. It only slipped a thin, deep seam of deadened stone into the shale beneath those few sheltered lees—a tiny discontinuity in the rock, no wider than a hut’s floor, that refused to carry the tremor. Like a hand laid gently against the rim of a ringing bell, just enough to stop the metal from humming in one small spot, while the rest of the valley went on singing.

That evening—or what passed for evening, in the unchanging, shadowless late-afternoon glare—a tapper crawled backward through the low entrance of a blind, dragging a basket of oily pith and two sealed joints of warm sap.

The day had been loud. A whole sector of upper lattice had buckled three hundred yards away, and the tapper’s arms ached from hauling straight reeds out of the wreckage before the new shoots could lock them down. Their eyes burned behind their visor; their throat was dry as husk-paper.

Inside, behind the stretched carapace-plates, the roar dropped to its usual dull surf. The tapper unlaced their visor, pulled the rolled wax plugs from their ears, and sat cross-legged on the flattened husk-mat, waiting for the familiar, heavy thrum of the floor to settle into their ankles.

It didn’t come.

The tapper froze, one hand halfway to their flint blade. In the basin, stillness is usually the second before a collapse. They pressed their bare palms flat against the talc-dusted floor, reading it the way they had been taught since childhood.

There was no tremor. No distant rasp of borers, no low thrumming of tensioned cables, no vibration at all—only the solid, unmoving weight of cold rock, holding them up without shaking them back.

Outside, the dust still sifted through the bark-weave. Through the baffle, the muffled drone of the thermals still rose and fell. The basin was still there, roaring and growing and tearing itself apart. Nothing had broken.

Slowly, the tapper’s shoulders dropped—an inch, then another, muscles unclenching that they hadn’t known were locked since they were born. When another climber crawled in a moment later, she froze too, palms on the mat, eyes wide and red-rimmed in the dim light. They looked at each other, then pressed their knuckles together, baffled and watchful, waiting for the trick.

By the third passing of the sap-joint, they stopped waiting.

For the first time in their lives, the silence wasn't just in the air; it was in their marrow. Without the constant static buzzing up through their spines, the small room felt larger, deeper, unhurried. When the tapper spoke, their dry, raspy voice didn't have to push against a vibrating skull.

They didn't talk about which bridges were singing too high to trust tomorrow. Instead, the tapper unhooked the bone scraper from their belt—the one ground smooth against the sulfur-shale, polished dark with years of mineral oil—and turned it over in their hands.

"She had a crooked thumb," the tapper rasped softly into the quiet. "From a glider-strike, when she was young. You could always tell her pluck on the cable by the drag."

The other climber listened, her head tilted, her hands resting completely still in her lap. She didn't answer right away. In the quiet beneath the rock, there was finally time to let a memory sit in the room without being shaken to dust.

Outside, a seed-bladder split with a gunshot crack, and a cloud of stridulators rose in a brass, screaming wave toward the milky sky. Inside, the two of them leaned their backs against the woven wall, closed their eyes, and slept—truly slept—while the fierce, dry world roared on around them, unbroken and whole.
