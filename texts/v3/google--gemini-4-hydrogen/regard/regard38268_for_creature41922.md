A power that is both wise and benevolent does not arrive with thunder. In a place where even a dropped pebble can be heard across two hundred paces of open green, thunder would be an intrusion—a rude shout in a room built for low voices.

Instead, the power came to the tarn the way the cold comes: by simply being there, listening.

It heard the basin long before it looked into it. From leagues away, beneath the wind-scoured ridges, it caught the deep, continuous *thrum* of the northern snowmelt vibrating through the limestone—and woven through that bassline, something rarer. The dry, muffled *tok-tok-tok* of stone hammers on cold copper. The faint, rhythmic creak of braided swim-lines. The patient pulse of a people who had learned to live inside an instrument without breaking a single string.

The power peered down through the disc of dark jade, past the silvered ceiling and the amber trunks, and took the full measure of the four hundred paces.

At first, benevolence moved faster than wisdom. Looking at the tarn-folk, the power felt the sharp, immediate ache of pity that all vast kindnesses feel when they behold small, mortal labor.

It saw the young air-drawers kicking upward through the freezing dimness at dawn, their lungs burning, their ribs stretched wide as they hauled cobble-weighted gut-bladders down from the thin sky. It saw the salt-stiffened palms of the fish-packers, pale as peeled willow, and the tallow-greased wool that never quite dried. It smelled the stale, heavy air inside the pitch-caulked bells at the end of a long night—air thick with wet wool, brine, and the breath of sleeping families.

*I could ease this,* the power thought.

It would be so small a thing to warm the deep hollows by five degrees, just enough to take the bite off the root-mat. It would be effortless to teach the bladder-cedars to exhale sweet, breathable air directly into the inverted domes, sparing the young their morning climb. It could soften the native copper so it yielded to a thumb’s pressure, or coax the marsh-fungus to burn bright as noon so the elders’ eyes need not strain in the slate-blue dark.

Then wisdom stayed the hand.

The power looked again—not at the hardship, but at what the hardship held together.

If the water warmed by five degrees, the slow iron-green moss would surge, rot, and choke the clear avenues; the trout-kin would sicken, the salamanders would flee the hollows, and the mineral crust that bound the old weirs would soften and crumble. If the domes filled themselves with air, the young would no longer swim to the surface each morning to taste the slate-wind; their chests would narrow over three generations, their voices would grow hasty because breath was no longer carried down by hand, and the taut, deliberate geometry of their town would slacken into dry-land clutter. If the copper yielded without effort, the *tok-tok-tok* would vanish from the water’s song, and with it the quiet pride of things made slow and hard.

To mend their struggle would be to unmake them. They were not prisoners of the bowl; they were its shape, just as the bowl was theirs.

So the power touched nothing beneath the water. Not a single crayfish was startled; not a single frond was hurried beyond its finger’s-breadth a year.

Instead, the power turned its gaze upward—out of the green cathedral, up over the northern lip, into the glaring, treacherous world of gray scree and high wind that the tarn-folk dreaded.

Here, unseen and unasked, there was work to be done.

High above the northern inflow, wedged beneath the shoulder of the middle peak, the power found a sleeping fracture in the limestone. In forty winters—or perhaps fifty, when a heavy spring thaw met an early frost—that shelf would have sheared away. Millions of tons of gray rubble would have thundered down the fold, damming the snowmelt, burying the stone-walled camp, and turning the blown-glass basin into a choked, muddy grave.

The power did not blast the rock apart. With the slow, unhurried patience of the tarn itself, it leaned its weight into the mountain’s ribs. Grain by grain, over the course of a single moonless night, it eased the fault-line shut, shifting the immense burden of the ridge eastward so that when the rock finally yielded a century from now, it would spill harmlessly into a barren gorge miles away.

Next, it looked to the snowfields on the three peaks. A drier age was creeping up from the lower valleys—slowly, over lifetimes, the kind of shift no mortal watchman notices until the wells run shallow. The power touched the high air currents, tilting the winter eddies by a hair’s breadth. It taught the wind to pack the snow deeper into the shaded northern couloirs, out of the blistering sun, ensuring that the broad, steady sheet spilling over the mossy lip would match the unseen southern seepage, drop for drop, for a thousand years to come.

And finally, it looked outward toward the lower passes, where someday a straying prospector, a mapmaker, or a lord’s tax-collector might climb high enough to see a disc of dark jade gleaming in the scree—and wonder what lived beneath it.

The power raised no wall of fire, no ring of mist that might draw the curious like a riddle. It simply laid a quiet dullness over the approaches to the fold. To any dry-lander looking up from the pastures below, the limestone crease would always seem steeper than it was, barren of game, dry as bone, and unrewarding to the boot. Eyes would slide past it without catching.

When the work was done, the power looked down one last time.

The mountain shadow had just dropped across the water. The golden shafts were gone, and the basin had settled into its clear slate-blue. Along the larch-plank catwalks, tenders were swimming out to unhood the wicker lanterns, letting the cold, greenish-white glow of the fungus-jars spill across the moss-velvet. Below, in the dark hollows, a fat mottled salamander barked once from a root-knot. On a middle line, someone gave two sharp tugs—*air-bell low*—and a pair of young divers slipped silently out of a hatch-hole, bladders lashed to their belts, kicking upward toward the last light on the surface.

They would never know a hand had passed over their peaks. They would never build an altar in the central plaza, or carve a name into the amber bark of the oldest cedar.

The power smiled—if something as vast and quiet as deep water can be said to smile—and withdrew, leaving them the rarest, cleanest gift any power can bestow:

The right to go on being exactly what they were.
