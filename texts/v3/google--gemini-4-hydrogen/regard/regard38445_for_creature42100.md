First, it does nothing at all.

A lesser mercy would have acted within the hour. It would have looked at the jagged, unweathered lip of the basin and seen a wound that needed closing; it would have felt the air just above freezing and brought warmth; it would have seen the matte-gray bridge passing through the hexagonal pylon and gently pulled the two solids apart so each could have its own honest volume of space. It might have opened eyes in those smooth, canted heads, or given them mouths to tell it what they wanted.

Wisdom knows that haste is a kind of violence, especially in a place that builds at the speed of stalagmites. So the power slows itself down—not to human time, which is frantic as a bird’s heart, but to the pace of mineral extrusion. It stays through an overcast noon that lasts a season. It watches a suspended walkway rotate three inches. It waits while a slate-colored beetle crosses a threshold, climbs a chalk-white ankle, and grazes its way down again.

By watching, it learns the grammar of the place.

It sees that the cold is not deprivation; it is the precise temperature at which the air can bear the weight of these geometries without dissolving. It sees that the overlapping walls are not a collision, any more than two notes played together are a collision; they are a chord held across centuries. And it sees that the inhabitants are neither trapped nor lonely. When one of them stands for three days with its fingers buried in the nerve-like filaments of an alley wall, it is not enduring captivity—it is listening to the slow, subterranean music of a city settling into itself.

Only when it has understood what must not be touched does the power begin to act, and its actions are so quiet they look like weather.

First, it tends the shelf of flat ground beyond the rim. The basin sweats its own water from pinched space, yet its roots are still anchored in the ordinary earth; the shallow troughs depend on the deep, cold water table beneath the plain. The power reaches down through the bedrock and steadies that unseen reservoir, ensuring that whatever droughts, poisons, or shifting seasons cross the flatlands in the coming centuries, the vein feeding the basin will remain clear and cold.

Second, it looks at the jagged boundary where two kinds of physics meet. The outer world is brisk, curious, and heavy-handed; left unguarded, a pit of impossible stone no wider than a cathedral nave will eventually draw surveyors, fences, drills, and men who measure value by what can be pried loose. Yet sealing the basin under a dome would starve it of the flat, ordinary sky—and of the solitary, pale-plumed birds that drop down now and then to drink from the unreflecting troughs, reminding the tenants of the brisk air above.

So the power does not build a wall. Instead, it lays a gentle, unforced obscurity over the lip of the quarry. Eyes scanning the flat horizon from a distance find nothing to snag on; footpaths bend around the shelf as naturally as water bends around a buried stone. Only things that travel lightly and mean no rearrangement—the wind, the overcast light, a stray bird descending to drink—find the way open.

Finally, the power considers the inhabitants themselves. They need no rescue, no instruction, no improvement. Yet benevolence is not merely protection; it is also recognition.

Near the center of the city, where the three thick conduits converge around the sunken cistern and gravity tilts politely uphill, several of the chalk-cased figures are gathered as usual, watching their mirror-bright alloy discs migrate steadily toward the lip of the tank.

The power does not speak to them, for the cold air carries no gossip and they have no ears to catch it. Instead, it steps into the basin without displacing a single droplet of condensation, lowers its presence until it weighs no more than a slight rise in barometric pressure, and stands still beside the cistern.

For a long time—perhaps a month, perhaps a year—it simply keeps them company at their own speed. Its stillness occupies the same volume as the damp air, overlapping them without intrusion, as patient as the slate.

And once, before it withdraws to watch over the rim from afar, it lays no gift on the flagstones and alters no stone. It only touches a single bone-white lattice with the lightest possible attention—producing one clear, tempered-steel note that travels the length of the narrow, folded alleys, arriving at the far edge of the basin a fraction of a second before the touch itself, as if to say:

*I hear the schedule you are keeping. Take your time.*
