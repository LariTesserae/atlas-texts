If a wise and benevolent power came to this altitude, it would not arrive as a pillar of light. Light here is a scarce, bruised thing, and sudden brilliance would only blind the pale-eyed swallows, scatter the translucent moths from the amber lamps, and send the Shadow-Sitters shrinking deeper into the boiler-house corners.

Instead, it would arrive as an act of patience. It would slow itself down to match the fifty-foot flywheels. It would wait in the damp mist until it stopped flinching when the *thunk-hiss* of a drop-forge arrived across the chasm before the hammer fell.

At first, benevolence alone might tempt it to mend everything.

Looking at the razor-edged quartzite that never dulls, at the permanent chill, at the staircases that miscount their steps and the clocks that lose their minds near the transformers, a merely kind power would feel the urge to set things right. It would want to part the upper haze and pour down a warm sun. It would want to smooth the raw stone, knit the frayed seams of space and sequence back together, and make the water fall downward like water is meant to do.

Wisdom would stay its hand.

Because if you watch a viaduct for ten minutes, and then ten hours, and then ten seasons, you begin to see what holds this place together.

If the power made gravity ordinary, the Ceiling-Eels would drop from their inverted puddles onto the iron grates and dry into nothing. If it brought down a sun, the pale-eyed swallows would burn their sight, the velvet moss would crisp, and the six-legged ungulates would overheat in their dense, oil-rich coats. If it forced shadows to fall away from the floodlights instead of pooling toward the heavy iron, the Shadow-Sitters might starve of whatever quiet resonance they drink from the humming beam-engines.

And the Tenders—the power would look longest at the Tenders.

It would watch a Tender spend an entire afternoon oiling a single row of cable-pulleys, waiting calmly between each stroke of the grease-gun. It would watch them drop a brass plumb-bob before stepping onto an unfamiliar catwalk, leaning into a slant with the unhurried grace of sailors. It would listen to the crisp, metallic tapping of their wrenches along the steel rails, and see them sleep level in canvas hammocks while the seven-story tenements tilted gently against the cliff.

It would realize that these people are not trapped in a broken world. They are the ones keeping it tethered. Their patience, their heavy iron-shod boots, their quiet truce with the beasts and the breathing dark—all of it was shaped by a mountain that hasn't finished rounding its corners. To "fix" the altitude would be to make the Tenders strangers in their own home, and to strip them of the very craft that gives their lives weight.

So the power would fold its brilliance away. It would not smooth a single shard of basalt. It would not straighten a single staircase.

Instead, it would do three small, quiet things—so quiet they would look like nothing at all.

First, it would reach far beneath the city, down into the unlit roots of the peaks where no crane can reach and no Tender can climb. There, where the mountain's frayed rules threaten not merely to bend but to tear loose altogether, it would steady the deep fractures. It would not make the rock soft or level; it would simply hold the foundation fast, taking a share of the strain so the stone pilings and iron tie-rods would always have something true to bite into.

Second, it would stand watch at the outer edges of the mist. Around the wide, barren shelves of slate, it would weave no wall—only a subtle, unhurried distance. Anyone climbing from the lower world in haste, carrying bright lamps, straightedges, and the urge to conquer or improve, would find the ledges slipping quietly out of reach, until they turned back. Only those willing to move at the speed of a glacier would ever find the viaducts.

And third, within the city itself, it would leave the machines, the moss, and the broken clocks alone. It would only lend a quiet mercy to the things the Tenders already trust: seeing to it that the tallow-pitch and moss-stained linen heal the white nicks on their knuckles clean against the damp, and ensuring that whenever a Tender lets a brass plumb-bob dangle over an unfamiliar floor, the weight—no matter how strangely the room leans or how fiercely the wind pulls—always tells them the honest truth of the slant.

Then, having done all that wisdom requires, the power would do nothing else. It would settle into the bruised twilight beside the heavy iron, keeping the machines company alongside the Shadow-Sitters, content to let the flywheels turn.
