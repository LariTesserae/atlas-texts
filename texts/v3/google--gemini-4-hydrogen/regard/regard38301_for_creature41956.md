A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive with thunder. In air this thin, thunder would only shatter against the flutes of the stone and die twenty paces from its own making.

Instead, its attention settles over the plateau the way the dust settles at dusk: without weight, without sound, taking the exact shape of the basins and the wind-planed rock.

At first, looking down through the fierce, unsoftened light, benevolence alone feels the old, familiar ache to mend.

It sees a world that appears, by every wet-land measure, to be starving. It sees thirty-foot spires drinking acid from stone, creatures with no moisture to spare for a voice, and a tall, gaunt figure wrapped in dead leaves and shed membrane, walking fifteen miles of blinding chalk just to clear husks from a hollow. It sees the sharp drop of temperature at sunset, when everything alive must fold itself shut or seal its joints in wax to survive the dark.

For the span of a single breath, the power considers what a mercy would look like here.

A single cloud, drawn up from some distant, forgotten sea—just enough rain to wash the talc from the air and soften the edges of the gypsum. A thickening of the atmosphere, so that a call might carry from one mesa to the next, and the tenders would not have to walk for years to touch palms. A gentle warmth held in the stones after sunset, so no one would have to sit beside a smokeless coal, hoarding heat under a cloak.

Then wisdom looks closer, and stays the hand.

It watches the afternoon thermals rise off the white pans, lifting curtains of glittering mineral dust into the cobalt sky, and sees how the groves drink them—how the microscopic hairs on the suede leaves crackle with static, snagging nitrogen and sulfur from the dry wind. It watches a single spark-mite leap on a repelled charge, and a glider hook its hollow ribs onto an invisible pillar of rising air, circling for six hours without spending a beat.

If rain fell here, the gypsum pans would turn to sour mire. The waxy felts of the fan-spires would rot at the crown; the bract-borers would drown in their papery tunnels. Thicker air would weigh the gliders down until they starved on the stone, and dampness would short-circuit the quiet, tingling language that passes between two tenders when their chalky palms meet. Even warmth at night would be a kind of theft—if the chill never came, the spires would never fold, never rest, never gather the resin in their cores.

Every gift the power knows how to give from other worlds would be a poison here. To soften this place would be to erase it.

So the power watches a little longer, learning the grammar of a world that has no lowlands left.

It watches a tender kneel at the lip of a sunken basin, using a sliver of rhyolite to pry dead, silica-stiffened bracts away from a young spiral. It watches the tender unseal a small pouch, press a pinch of golden-grey spore-powder into a fresh hairline fracture in the gypsum, and smooth the dust over it with a dry thumb. There is no haste in the gesture, and no despair. The tender is not enduring a punishment; the tender is simply in tune with the plateau, as finished and inevitable as the yardangs.

Benevolence, schooled by wisdom, realizes that the hardest and highest kindness is sometimes to leave a masterpiece alone.

It does not bring rain. It does not thicken the air, or widen the groves, or place a second tender on the horizon to shorten the years between meetings. It does not even whisper in the tender’s mind to say *you are seen*, for a voice in a silent world is a disruption, and the tender has no use for a witness.

If the power does anything at all, it is something so small and so quiet that nothing on the plateau will ever know it happened.

It turns outward, toward the deep dark beyond the bruised cobalt sky, and draws a quiet boundary around the roof of the world—a promise of continuance. No stray comet of ice will fall here to flood the pans. No careless, heavy-handed traveler from wetter stars will ever land to pity the striders or "save" the groves with rivers.

Then the power withdraws its attention, leaving no footprint in the talc and no ripple in the wind.

Below, the sun drops toward the dusty horizon, turning the white stone to piercing amber. The dust settles. The groves fold their heavy leaves inward, locking the day’s warmth into their hearts. And in the sheltered lee of a mesa, beside a coal of resin that glows without flame or smoke, the tender sits wrapped in felt and membrane—untouched, unburdened by a miracle, waiting for the sky to brighten again.
