If a power both benevolent and wise turned its attention to the terrace, it would know, before it even arrived, not to bring a footstep.

A footstep would send a false pulse across the wet slate. A breath of wind, misplaced, would muddy the bass note rising from the chimneys. Even a sudden shadow—though nothing here has eyes to see it—would cool the sunlit stone too quickly and alter the slow, patient sweat of the back wall.

So it would come as pure attention, weightless as the midday glare, and settle into the amphitheater without displacing a single bead of mist.

At first, it would simply listen.

It would feel the deep, continuous organ-hum of the flooded cavities vibrating through the limestone, and trace the geometry of the hollow by sound alone, the way the dwellers do. It would hear the forty-foot drops strike the basins like bronze bowls, and feel the faint, glassy overtone as a travertine mound tucked among the roots of the oldest tree equalized its chambers and lifted an inch from the slate. It would follow the slow, hydraulic glide of three translucent pillars across a mirror-puddle, and the quiet suction of fleshy lobes climbing spongy, moss-wrapped bark into the cool afternoon mist.

Benevolence, when it first looks upon a small and hidden thing, always feels the urge to mend.

The power would see the dwellers’ blindness—the smooth, eyeless domes pressed flat against the rock—and might, for a heartbeat, consider the gift of sight. How vast the gulf beyond the cliff’s lip is; how bright the halos in the rising mist; how extraordinary the epiphytic orchids look when the sun catches their pale throats.

Then wisdom would look a little longer, and stay its hand. Sight is a loud, hungry sense. To give eyes to creatures whose entire world is a map of pressure and resonance would not widen their terrace; it would crowd it with glare and distraction, pulling their attention away from the subtle, rising tension of the leaf-bladders above. They do not miss the sky. They feel it in the warmth of the puddles and the ring of the rain-drops.

Benevolence might next look at the pale, heavy crust of travertine building up along their backs, threatening over decades to fuse them to the floor like the lower trunks of the trees. It might consider softening the mineral water, or sparing them the shock of the melon-sized bladders dumping their gallons onto the hollow stone.

And again, wisdom would see the hinge on which the whole cathedral turns. Without the lime, there is no armor against the midday heat; without the thunderous concussion of the booms, the oldest plates would never crack away, and the orchid pollen would never drift down from the high boughs to be carried from one lonely tree-island to the next. What looks like violence from the outside is the terrace’s own way of keeping its promise to itself.

It would look at the single pale salamander hanging motionless in the darkest basin, and the dozen dwellers who never crowd one another, who pause at the water’s rim only long enough to hum a brief, glassy harmony before gliding apart. It would notice the healed notch in the calcified bark, and the unhafted wedge of knapped flint resting dry behind the roots—the trace of a single visitor who took a gourd of mineral-sweet sap and had the grace to leave the rest untouched.

And so the power would understand that there is nothing here to fix, nothing to speed up, nothing to explain. The amphitheater is not waiting to become something else. It is already complete—a slow, booming equilibrium that has no need of rescue.

What, then, would it do?

Almost nothing.

Perhaps, as a quiet courtesy, it would incline the path of the high-altitude winds just enough that no rockslide from the upper massif ever chokes the vertical chimneys, and no careless fire or iron-shod crowd from the lowlands ever finds the crevice that leads up through the mist—leaving the cliff-face steep, and the occasional climber rare and gentle-handed. Not a wall, and not a miracle; only a slight, unnoticeable favoring of the odds, indistinguishable from the mountain’s own patience.

And before it withdrew, it might do one thing more, though it would leave no mark at all: it would simply remain until the next leaf-bladder reached its tipping point, and let the boom roll through its own vast stillness, bearing witness to a song that was never meant to have an audience.

Then it would let go, cleanly, leaving the terrace to the mist, the bronze-bowl drops, and the slow, hydraulic creatures carrying dust from tree to tree across the humming stone.
