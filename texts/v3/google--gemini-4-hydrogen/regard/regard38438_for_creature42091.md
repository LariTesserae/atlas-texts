It did not arrive in a pillar of light, nor did it hush the gale to announce itself. Anything that displaces air inside a pipe changes the pitch, and the power had come to listen.

It settled into the chamber the way a shift in barometric pressure settles—invisible, weightless, felt only as a sudden depth to the attention of the sky. Above the jagged oculus, the pale-bellied falcons continued to ride the vertical thermal without a twitch of their wings. Down in the widest saucer-basin, the slate-backed creature did not wake; its ribs rose and fell in their slow, half-minute tide, mineral water spilling in bright threads down its flanks.

The power looked, and listened, and took the measure of the place.

First, it considered mercy as smaller minds imagine it.

It saw the thin, biting air and the relentless wet that never let the stone dry. With a thought, it could have warmed the gneiss by a few degrees, gentled the western gale into a breeze, or drawn a roof of cloud-proof stillness over the oculus so the moss might grow lush without being torn. Yet as soon as the thought formed, it saw what that warmth would do: the weeping walls would dry, the bladder-leaved rosettes would shrivel, and the creature in the basin—built for cold, slick rock and repelled by the faintest memory of dry smoke—would suffocate in comfort. Worse, the great organ-note of the peak would die.

It considered the creature’s solitude. There was no other of its kind on the needle-sharp ridge, nor in the valleys miles below. Its milky eyes looked out through translucent lids at swifts that ignored it, marmots that fled from it, and stone that knew nothing of it. A hasty benevolence might have shaped a companion from the wet gneiss, or lifted the creature down to some gentler gorge where its kind could multiply.

Then the cloud-bank slammed into the western arch, plunging the chamber into swirling grey, and the power watched the creature wake.

It watched the water drain cleanly from the horn-rimmed vents until the hollow chambers of skull and chest were tuned to the roar. It watched the splayed, smooth-nailed limbs flow up the forty-foot wall, the pleated flank-membranes unfurling like grey sails to catch the updraft. It watched the creature climb to the ribs beneath the crown, open its neck-vents to the blast, and bend a sharp, tearing whistle into a soft, wet flute-note—and when the gale paused for three heartbeats, it heard the creature’s hollow chest hold the mountain’s note like a struck bell so the silence could not break in.

The power understood then. This being was not lonely. It was wedded to the stone and the wind, and its solitude was the space required for the instrument to resonate. To give it a gentler life would be to unmake it.

Nor did the power soften the teeth of the place. It watched a falcon stoop like a dropped stone through the empty center of the chamber, scattering the swifts in a shriek of feathers. It watched a sudden gust dash one swift against the wet wall, and saw the creature take the fallen bird whole without malice or haste. Everything here turned on a clean, cold wheel: mist fed moss, moss bred cloud-flies, flies fed swifts, and the drowned remnants fed the quiet singer in the dark. To meddle with that wheel would be arrogance, not wisdom.

So the power held its hand, content simply to witness—until the weather turned.

Late in the grey hours, the wind swung around to the northeast, blowing backward against the grain of the peak. The deep, steady bass of the organ pipe fractured into flat, choppy gusts that slapped aimlessly against the ribs.

In the upper ledges, the creature grew restless. Its vents clicked softly as the pressure dropped; it paced the slick moss, its beard lichen snapping in the cross-draft.

Then, high in the eastern arch, the old human work caught the wind.

The hollowed horn and bird-bone tubes, green with lichen and strung across the narrow gap, began to clatter against the gneiss. Thin, reedy, uneven, their chorus cut through the broken gusts and spilled out into the empty sky. At once, the creature climbed straight up the eastern ribs, anchored its broad pads in the moss below the gap, and pressed its wet flank to the stone. Tilting its blunt head toward the clattering bones, it opened its vents and poured a low, steady, resonant hum into the wind—answering the frail reeds, stitching the mountain’s voice back together until the gale should turn west again.

The power drew closer to the eastern arch, where no swift nested and no marmot climbed.

There, in the damp shadow of the stone, it saw what the creature could not see, and what the long-dead hands that tied the mountain-ash ladder could no longer tend. The blackened sinew binding the horn and bone tubes to the rock had weathered to grey thread. Salt-mist, freeze, and a thousand northeast squalls had frayed it down to a few brittle fibers. One hard ice-storm, one winter gale a little fiercer than the last, would part the knot, and the flutes would drop forty feet into the void—leaving the creature to pace the eastern arch in a broken wind, waiting for an answer that would not come.

To replace the sinew with anything incorruptible—metal that never rusts, cord that never rots—would be a lie against the mountain, where everything is meant to wear away.

Instead, the power did something so small that not even the cloud-flies stirred.

In the wet crevice where the frayed knot rested, it nudged a slow, tough strand of beard-lichen and dark moss-root to creep a fraction of an inch farther than it would have on its own. Gently, patiently, as if decades of damp had already passed, the living fibers braided themselves through the dying sinew—not making it eternal, only lending the old human knot a little more of the mountain’s own grip, enough to outlast the lifetime of the singer below.

The creature did not notice. Its milky eyes remained half-shut against the mist; its hollow chest continued to hum against the gneiss, keeping the chorus whole.

When the wind shifted back to the west and the great bass roar swelled once more through the stone eye, the power withdrew—leaving the chamber cold, loud, weeping, and untouched, save for a handful of moss holding fast to a handful of bone.
