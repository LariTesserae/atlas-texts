It would not arrive with warmth, or thunder, or a sudden flowering of color. To bring those things to the plateau would be like shouting in a room built for listening—and worse, it would be a kind of violence. Warmth would rot the iron-dark cushion-mosses that know only how to drink from cold fog; a warmer sun would dry the glassy hairs on the ferns before they could comb the mist.

So the power would arrive the way a fog bank arrives: without sound, without edges, simply by being present in the thin air.

At first, looking down at the endless shelf of frost-shattered slate, the impulse of benevolence would be to mend. A lesser kindness—one that knew how to heal, but did not yet know how to look—would see a world that had come unstrung. It would want to pin the orphaned shadows back to their clouds. It would want to level the aqueducts so the black water obeyed the slope again, stitch the peeled-up sheets of bedrock back into the plaza, and smooth the distances between cities so five miles remained five miles. It would look at the Tenders in their folded alleys, at their smooth, faceless hoods and their joints snapping like dry seed pods, and it would ache to give them back their breath, their voices, their blood. It would want to call birds into the huge, pale sky.

A wise power does not act in the first hour.

It would stay past the second fog bank. It would kneel beside a square of cold, forgotten shade on the herringbone basalt and watch the soot-colored grazers peel themselves up from the stone to feed on bronze dust. It would see that if it pinned the shadows back to the clouds, those small, flat lives would be pressed into the pores of the rock forever.

It would look beneath the translucent, curled-up sheets of bedrock, down into the damp dark between the inverted cobblestones, and see the pale, quartz-veined membranes pulsing in rhythm with the morning fungi, filtering the uphill water. If it pressed the stone flat again, it would crush them.

And then it would take the wrong distance. It would walk toward the neighboring city, pass through the outer kilns twice, and step into the thick, mineral air of the folded seam where the Tenders work.

It would stand quietly in the dark beside a workshop bench, watching a slate-gray hand grip the oak crank of a windlass so that, in the bright, empty daylight far above, a wet hemp rope rose from a canal without anyone touching it. It would watch a reed-hooded figure pause between motions to let the cold mist settle on its shoulders. It would walk the older districts, where the retaining walls soften into moss, and see the ones who have already knelt down for good—their bent backs and shoulders half-braided with root-mat and green-patinated copper wire, indistinguishable from the basin they spent centuries keeping.

And the power would understand: there is no grief here. There is no plea for rescue.

The Tenders are not trapped in the seams; they *are* the seams. Whatever language they once spoke did not get stolen from them—it simply closed up over the eons, replaced by something more durable: the *clack* of a sluice-gate, the *thud* of a counterweight, the mortarless fit of stone against stone. They have outlasted blood and breath and arrived at a mineral patience. To give them back their mouths would be to unmake them.

So the first thing a wise and benevolent power would do is nothing.

It would withhold its hand from the sky, leaving it pale and birdless. It would leave the water running uphill, the distances unreliable, the shadows lagging behind their clouds. It would not wake the sleepers in the retaining walls, nor call the Tenders out into a daylight too thin to hold them.

And yet—it would also see that the surface *has* worn thin. It would see how fragile the delaminating rock has become, like the pages of a wet book left out in a high wind, and how even the deepest folds of the cities are only a few layers away from wearing all the way through.

So if it did anything at all, it would do one small thing, in the only language the plateau still speaks.

It would not glue the book shut, and it would not press the curled stone flat. Instead, it would lend a quiet, imperceptible weight to the underside of the world—just enough density in the deep seams to keep the pages from tearing loose in the wind, just enough steadiness in the bedrock so the folded centuries do not unspool into nothing before their work is done. No more than a master mason’s hand laid briefly against a dry-stacked wall to test its plumb, and then lifted away.

Then the power would withdraw, careful not to leave a shadow behind on the basalt, careful not to stir the dry stone dust beside the bronze chisel.

In the basins, the fog would roll in from the vast emptiness. The glassy hairs on the ferns would begin to weep. Somewhere down a terraced alley, a wooden sluice-gate would drop into place with a wet *clack*, redirecting the uphill water toward a freshly sprouted bed of reeds—and the quiet would settle back in, undisturbed.
