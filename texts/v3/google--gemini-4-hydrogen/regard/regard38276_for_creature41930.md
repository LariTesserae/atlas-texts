It would not arrive in light. The chamber already has too much of that—a blinding, pale-blue glare bouncing endlessly off wet quartz and unstained slate. And it would not arrive in voice, for a voice in this water would only be another roar added to the cavitation, or worse, a vibration that cracked the dwellers’ heavy jawbones.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward this high, drowned bell, it would arrive the way the dwellers themselves know the world: by listening through the stone.

From miles away, across the jagged, unweathered spines of the rising range, it would feel the mountain’s slow, grinding ascent—and then, tucked inside the throat of the peak, it would notice a small, frantic, rhythmic clatter that did not belong to geology. Hollowed slate striking hollowed slate. A tempo that changed by a fraction when the rock tilted.

Someone was measuring.

So the power would draw its attention inward, slipping past the wind-packed ice and the translucent quartz, and hold itself still in the center of the eighty-pace void—right where the shear-zones tear at anything made of flesh—and it would watch.

At first, the impulse of simple kindness would be almost overwhelming. 

To look at four compact, scarred beings wedged behind cairns of razor-edged rock, tethered by leather to bone hooks, weighted down with raw copper so the earth’s own breath does not dash them against the ceiling—to watch them eat rubbery moss in the only scraps of shadow they could build with their own hands, and descend miles of freezing scree in the blackest winter nights just to scavenge sinew from dead goats—a lesser mercy would reach out immediately.

It would want to quiet the roar. It would want to warm the biting meltwater, calm the colliding currents, smooth the glassy basalt edges, and lay down a bed of soft, forgiving silt. It would want to widen the chamber and fill it with easy, slow-moving prey.

A wise power would stop its own hand before a single ripple changed.

It would look at the gill-pleats along the dwellers’ flanks, opening like wet books to drink the silver-white micro-bubbles beaten into the freezing churn—and it would understand that calm, warm water would suffocate them.

It would look at the smooth cartilage plates where ears should be, at the thick skulls and wide jaws pressed against the stone—and it would understand that silence would be blindness. Without the thunder of cavitation and the drum-crack of ceiling pockets, they would lose the shape of their home.

It would trace the taut root-fiber cords whipping in the wash, the shed fibers drifting down to feed the blind, armored crawlers, the pale-green moss clinging only because the rock is bare and scoured—and it would see that softness would starve the whole precarious ladder of life in a single season.

Most of all, it would watch one of the dwellers unbuckle their copper tether, wait with milky lids drawn shut as a ceiling fissure detonates, and kick off across the blinding void in that brief, three-heartbeat lull—limbs tucked, copper dragging them down toward a distant seam just before the shear-zones snap shut. And the power would recognize that these beings are not prisoners of the mountain’s violence. They are its partners. To rescue them from the edge would be to unmake them.

So it would do nothing to the water. Nothing to the cold. Nothing to the deafening, endless collision that is the silence of home.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and benevolence rarely leaves a place without looking a little further ahead than the dwellers can see.

Pressing its attention deeper, down through the grinding floor of the blister into the unformed strata of the peak, the power would read the mountain’s future. This peak has not finished rising. The same tectonic hunger that split this bell open and filled it with storm-water is still pushing upward from below. In thirty winters, or a hundred, a fault-line beneath the floor will not merely vent—it will cleave. The pale-blue lens will drain in an afternoon, leaving four water-breathing beings stranded on dry, razor-sharp stone beneath a sky they cannot bear.

The power would not stop the mountain from rising. It would not reinforce the floor to make this eighty-pace courtyard eternal; nothing inside a young mountain is meant to be eternal.

Instead, far below the floor—in the dark, solid rock where no current has ever reached—it would lay the weight of a single thought against a deep seam of slate. Just enough to coax the coming fracture, when its century arrives, to angle sideways instead of straight down. To guide the future split toward a buried vein of translucent quartz and an ancient pocket of trapped meltwater.

So that when the old bell finally tilts too far, and the outer slate bells begin to clack with a new, terrible urgency, and the floor at last gives way—the water will not vanish into the abyss. It will pour downward into a newly hollowed chamber, eighty paces wide, cold and bright and roaring, waiting to be tethered and learned all over again.

Then the power would withdraw, leaving no sign that it had ever been there. No warmth in the water, no hush in the stone, no sudden gift of copper or bone.

Behind the blade of quartz, a dweller presses their chin to the wet rock, listening to the frantic clatter of the bells, and hears only the mountain. Which is as it should be.
