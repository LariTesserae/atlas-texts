A power that is merely strong would look at the basin and want to fix it. It would see humans greasing their faces with dogfish oil and sleeping lashed to wet steel, and it would reach down to drain the water, shatter the roof, and set them back upon dry grass under an open sky.

A power that is also wise would look longer before it touched a single valve.

It would hang invisibly in the midwater, feeling the twin heartbeats of the pumps and the herring. It would watch a child—whose eyes have never known a horizon, whose pupils are pinned to black dots—ride a warm cooling-return thermocline between *Forty-four-North* and *Twelve-East* with the effortless grace of a pelagic jack. It would listen to the steady *tink-tink-tink* of a wrench against a tie-rod socket, carrying news two kilometers through the rebar bones of the world, and it would recognize that something fierce and legitimate is already happening here.

To drain this place would be a second catastrophe. It would slaughter the baitballs, strand the seals, and turn the younger ones—who have already begun to belong to the current—into exiles in a dry world they do not know how to love. To shut off the turbines would be worse: without the pumps, the nine-degree water would go still, thin of oxygen, and choke.

So the wise power would not undo the basin. It would *tend* it. It would ask what hurts unnecessarily, what will fail in twenty years, and what small mercies would let these people not merely survive the machine, but live inside it.

---

First, it would give them night.

Not total darkness—total darkness in a chasm of spinning impellers and thirty-meter concrete columns is a way to drown. Instead, over the course of weeks, the power would teach the overhead floodlamps to breathe.

At first, the divers would think the grid was failing. When the blazing electric cyan softened by a fraction, then a fraction more, wrenches would ring frantically against the pylons from one end of the basin to the other. Gleaners would kick hard for the trusses, fearing the pumps were next.

But the pumps would keep their steady, reassuring thrum. Only the glare would ease, warming slowly from sterile white-blue to a deep, bruised indigo, and then to a quiet twilight that held for eight hours before brightening again. Along the intake cages, the bubble-plumes, and the maintenance trusses, a few dim, amber marker-lights would remain—just enough to keep a swimmer from blundering into a screen or a column.

In that first true dusk, the old ones would float in the midwater and weep, feeling their pupils dilate for the first time in years until the black dots opened wide and soft. On the trusses above, people would untie the black rubber strips from their eyes and look down at the water, discovering that the moon jellies had begun to catch the amber light like drifting lanterns.

---

Second, it would soften the resting places, without stealing the sea.

A human being should not have to tie themselves to a cold steel beam to keep from drowning in their sleep, nor fight a barking sea lion for six inches of dry catwalk. Yet the power would not build sterile towers or lift them out of reach of the water they know.

Instead, tucked into the maintenance air-pockets beneath the roof, broad, gentle ledges would begin to appear—grown or shaped seamlessly onto the trusses like wide shelves of warm, porous stone. They would stay dry above the splash, holding a mild, steady warmth—not hot like the lamp-housings, just warm enough to take the ache out of nine-degree bones. Shallow troughs along the inner walls would catch the condensation, filtering out the ozone and the tang of oiled machinery, offering clean, sweet freshwater without anyone having to lick wet steel.

The sea lions would get their own broader haul-outs a little further down the beams, keeping the old truce intact without the crowding. And on the human ledges, there would be low lips at the edges so a sleeping child could never roll off into the dark water below.

---

Third, it would quiet the sharpest violence of the machine.

The power would not silence the basin—the divers navigate by the pitch of the turbines and tell time by the *boom-clank* of the eastern sluice gates. Silence would blind them. But it would tune the diffusers and the intake manifolds, dampening the tearing, chest-rattling frequencies that bruise the inner ear and turn the bubble-plumes into walls of bone-breaking static.

Around the intake cages on the gridded floor, the suction would be gentled just enough—not so much that the currents falter, only enough that a tired gleaner can pry a stunned jack from the mesh without feeling the brass impellers trying to pull the fins off their feet. Sturdy, wide-runged handholds would appear along the concrete curbs and the sharp corners of the columns, placed so quietly that the divers would assume they had simply missed them before in the glare.

---

Fourth, it would look ahead to the years the humans cannot yet see.

The concrete is young and sharp now, and the sacrificial zinc anodes still gleam dull silver. But zinc pits away. Steel rusts. Pumps wear down. In three generations, an untended machine becomes a tomb.

So the power would mend the slow rot invisibly, strengthening the alloys and the seals so the roof does not fall on their grandchildren. And at the same time, it would help the basin outgrow its own sterility.

Along the bare, vertical faces of the octagonal pylons, it would seed what already wants to live there: cold-water holdfasts, dense ribbons of kelp, sponges, anemones, and slow-growing corals suited to the chill. It would introduce subtle strains of bioluminescent plankton that bloom softly during the new twilight hours, turning the wake of a kicking fin into a trail of pale green sparks.

Over decades, the razor-sharp formwork seams and batch numbers would soften under living coats of mussels and kelp. The thirty-meter columns would cease to be mere pillars of poured stone and become vertical reefs, humming with shelter, hiding places, and food. The divers would still know *Forty-four-North* from *Twelve-East*, not only by the stamped numbers, but by the shape of the kelp forests and the creatures that nested there.

---

And finally, for the oldest ones—the ones who still stop in the midwater when a cold front sweeps through, remembering dirt and clouds—the power might leave one small, quiet gift.

In a single, sheltered alcove high beneath the roof, where a warm lamp shines down onto a wide stone ledge, there would be a patch of dark, living moss, and a thin mist of soft water falling steadily from a vent overhead. It would smell of wet earth instead of concrete and fish oil. It would not be enough to go back to—there is no going back—but it would be enough to sit beside, to let a child press their palm into something soft and green, and to know that the world did not forget what they came from.

---

Then, having done all this, the benevolent power would step back and say nothing.

It would not carve laws into the concrete, nor demand gratitude, nor reveal a face above the silver surface of the water. Wisdom knows that a gift given too loudly turns the receiver into a pet.

Down in the basin, the lights would dim toward their first true evening. A diver clinging to *Forty-four-North* would feel the strange new warmth rising from a ledge overhead, notice the faint, unfamiliar softening of the turbine’s roar, and reach for the wrench at their belt—right beside the dull silver chunk of zinc.

They would tap three slow notes into the concrete, listening as the rebar carried the question across two kilometers of cold, clear water:

*Did you feel that? The weather is changing.*

And somewhere in the twilight, another wrench would tap back, steady and unafraid.
