At first, the power was drawn by the sound.

From the high, flat emptiness of the plain, the sunken pit looked like nothing more than a scatter of whitewashed rims—chimney-mouths flush with the dry grass, humming in the wind like forgotten jugs. But beneath that bass note was a second, subtler vibration: not the chaotic flutter of forty thousand wings, nor the hiss of rain on terracotta, but a deliberate, measured tapping that traveled through timber and taut hemp. Someone was speaking in the dark.

When the power leaned closer and looked down through the slanted columns of dust, its first impulse was pity.

It saw ribs sharp beneath lime-streaked skin. It saw ears scarred flat by a lifetime of echoing racket, feet calloused into dark leather, bodies smeared with grey weep-hole mud to suffocate mites. It saw them crouching like featherless birds on splintered cedar, eating raw snake-meat and unhatched eggs, drinking from gourds in the brief curtains of rain, tucking their dead into crevices beside the owls.

A lesser kindness would have reached down immediately. It would have lifted them out into the clean grass of the plain, washed the droppings from their matted hair, mended their eardrums, given them woven wool, cooked bread, and wide, quiet rooms with windows.

That is what pity would have done.

Instead, the power waited. It did what the dwellers themselves did when the wind shifted: it pressed its attention flat against the masonry and listened with its bones.

And as it listened, the pit unfolded itself—not as a prison, nor a ruin, but as a single, intricate instrument tuned over generations.

The power saw that if it healed their scarred ears, the ceaseless roar of the doves and starlings would become an agony. If it gave them fire, the dry reed-matting and weathered cedar would turn their home into a kiln within an hour. If it lifted them onto the plain above, the flat, endless horizon would feel to them like falling forever; their light-boned frames, trained to spread weight across three struts at once, would grow heavy and clumsy on solid ground; and their language—made of knuckle-raps and plucked hoist-ropes—would go mute in the open air, where there is no lattice to carry a name.

They were not broken versions of the loud, thick-booted people who had left the copper scrapers behind. They were finished for this place, as finished as the swifts and the skinks. To rescue them would be to unmake them.

So the first thing the benevolent and wise power did was *nothing*. It stayed its hand from all grand mercies. It left the dust in the air, the mud on their skin, the owls in the roof-shadows, and the bones in the swifts’ nests.

Yet wisdom is not indifference. Looking closely—with the slow, patient gaze that sees the grain inside a beam and the salt inside a stone—the power noticed where time was quietly thinning the edges of their world.

The builders were gone. No fresh cedar grew beneath the domes, and no hemp grew in the guano-crusted dark. The counterweight ropes were brittle with lime; the planks were splitting along their old nails. In another generation or two, the middle tiers would begin to give way faster than reed-matting could lash them back together. And in the dry years on the plain above, when no rain fell straight down the light-shafts, the dwellers had only the foul, ammonia-heavy condensation of the bottom pans.

So the power acted, in ways so small they could never be mistaken for a miracle—only for the pit itself being true.

High in the third tier, well above the damp saltpeter crusts and the clinging mist of the floor, it eased a hairline seam in the earth behind the retaining wall. It did not break the brick; it only coaxed a buried vein of limestone to lean a fraction closer to an old weep-hole. By the next morning, a single, steady bead of water—filtered through thirty feet of dry earth, cool and free of guano—began to form at the lip of the mortar, dropping once every six breaths into the shadow. Not enough to rot the timber, or wake the ferns where they shouldn't grow. Just enough to fill a hung gourd between rains.

Next, it touched the lattice. It did not replace the weathered cedar with new, unblemished wood—that would have erased the map of flexes and creaks that their toes knew in the dark. Instead, it slowed the drying of the heartwood and the stiffening of the lime-soaked hemp, lending the old fibers the quiet endurance of living roots. The ropes would still fray, and the planks would still groan when three dwellers crossed a span at once, but they would hold for another ten generations. And when the next autumn gale swept across the plain, the wind happened to carry a scatter of supple, storm-torn willow-withes and long marsh-reeds straight over the whitewashed rims, spilling them down the light-shafts onto the upper walkways like ordinary wrack.

Finally, the power steadied the vaults overhead against the slow shifting of the plain, ensuring no dome would crack and no chimney would choke.

Then it withdrew—leaving no voice in the air, no strange tremor in the wall, nothing that might startle a sleeper into losing their grip.

Down in the middle tiers, a thin, lime-dusted figure paused on a cross-beam. They pressed one broad palm against the warm brick, drinking in the low, familiar hum of the wind across the chimneys. Finding the pitch steady and the wood true beneath their splayed toes, they tapped twice on the cedar—*I am here, the way is clear*—and glided silently back into the shade.
