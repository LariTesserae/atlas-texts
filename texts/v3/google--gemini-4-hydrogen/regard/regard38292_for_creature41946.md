It would not arrive with light, or with a voice in the air. A power that is wise as well as kind knows better than to break the pitch of a room before it has heard what is being played.

At first, from far above, the basin would look like an omission. Across a green and restless planet, here was a scar of bleached stone miles wide, stripped of soil, scoured of trees, pinned beneath an upper-atmosphere river that never paused to draw breath. A lesser benevolence—the kind that measures mercy by comfort—might have felt an immediate urge to mend it. To warm the thin air. To hush the relentless shriek of the ridge-flutes so that something soft might grow, and someone might stand upright without bracing against the west.

Before acting, however, the power would wait. It would lay its attention flat against the pale quartzite, matching the sub-bass hum of the bedrock until the roar stopped feeling like an assault and began to resolve into structure.

And then it would see.

It would feel the miles-wide drumhead come alive: the crisp, deliberate tap of a knuckle three hundred yards away; the sudden, clean drop in a flute’s pitch as a flat shoulder pressed against a ridge-vent; the panic of deaf rodents bolting through dark limestone mazes. It would watch the saucer-shaped pools beneath the overhangs, where the standing waves shifted from tight squares to six-sided crystal lattices—entire sentences written in trembling light across the stone.

It would look into the undercut ribs at dusk, when the glare died to ash, and see them curled together in the dry dark, padded with moss, sharing heat without a wasted motion. It would see a young one held chest-to-chest against a parent’s broad, hollow sternum, learning the grammar of the mountain long before its second eyelids could bear the sun. It would watch an old one, stiff and unable to brace, crawl patiently into a windward crevice to be stripped back down to quartzite.

Understanding would come all at once, and with it, a quiet, sobering awe.

To quiet the wind here would be to blind them. To soften the stone with soil would be to cut the strings of their instrument and leave them deaf in the dark. To give them fire, or roofs, or voices carried on the air, would be to teach them a clumsy, fragile way of living that the first true winter would tear apart—or worse, to make them long for the wet, suffocating silence below the timberline.

They were not enduring this place. They *were* this place, tuned to it down to the marrow of their jawbones.

So the power would stay its hand. It would leave the cold. It would leave the blinding white of the terraces, the sharp black shadows, the thinness of the air, and the steady, unyielding pressure of the west. It would not move a single pebble from a flute, nor smooth a single undercut rib, nor spare them the necessity of their own precision. It would not even tap the stone to say *I am here*, knowing how unsettling an unplaceable note would be to a people who read every fracture in the hum.

Yet a wise power does not walk away empty-handed when it sees something rare and whole. If it touches nothing *inside* the basin, it turns its attention to the *edges*—to the slow, indifferent drift of time and the world below.

From that moment on, the basin would have a quiet counterweight at its rim.

As centuries turned and the planet’s great currents wandered, the power would subtly steady the upper-atmosphere river, keeping it laminar and true so the mountain’s pitch would not suddenly fall silent or break into violent, erratic gusts. When the slow grind of frost and heat threatened to collapse a vital honeycomb of limestone arches all at once, the power would let the stone wear only at the pace the basin could absorb, preserving the instrument’s resonance across ages.

And down below the timberline, where the air was thick and the restless, noisy peoples of the valleys were always building, climbing, and wanting to fix what they did not understand, the power would do very little—only enough. When travelers climbed the ridge for water or meat, it would let them come, drink, set their snares, and leave. But if ever a larger expedition looked up toward the saddle with plans to quarry the white stone, or bridge the overhangs, or bring down the strange, silent shadows they glimpsed beneath the ledges, the power would simply let the mountain be itself a little more fiercely. A little more glare off the quartzite. A slightly sharper edge to the windward flutes. Just enough to turn the trespassers back toward the trees, convinced there was nothing up there worth taking.

Then, having set its watch at the threshold, the power would withdraw its weight from the rock, leaving not a single ripple of dissonance behind.

Below it, in the dark under the stars, a knuckle would tap twice against a limestone shelf. Half a mile away, in a saucer of ice-cold water, a lattice of light would tighten from squares into hexagons, and hold steady.
