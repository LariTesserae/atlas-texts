It would not announce itself with thunder, or a shadow across the floor, or a sudden cooling of the air. A power that is truly wise knows better than to step heavy-footed into a room where the inhabitants measure time by the turning of stone.

At first, it would only listen.

High above the timberline, where the air is thin and most things that live have fur or feathers or roots desperate for meltwater, it would notice the anomaly: a faint, steady sixty-cycle hum rising out of a nameless crease in the ridge. No wind makes that sound. No water makes it. Curiosity—patient and unhurried—would draw the power’s attention down into the sunlit fold.

And then it would wait.

If it stayed for only a minute, even a wise power might mistake the pocket for a barren scar, an unfinished kiln where the mountain had cooled badly. So it would stay longer than a minute. It would match its attention to the glacial drift of the magnetite shelf, watching the shadows creep a finger’s width across the glassy basalt.

It would see the bismuth beads crawl out of the ore beds and stack themselves in tidy, magnetic rows. It would see the copper-veined fronds unfurl toward the midday glare, taut with hot dielectric oil. And finally, it would see the low, stepped mound of quartz and bismuth glide across the stone on its humming cushion of copper bristles, pause over a drift of carbonized spores, and later nudge a thick stem to siphon a single drop of oil—leaving a clean, polite spark behind.

A lesser benevolence—one that only knew the green, wet valleys below—might feel a sudden urge to *help*.

It might look at the blistered, razor-edged stone and wish to soften it with moss. It might look at the bone-dry air and think of sending a gentle rain to wash the dust from the quartz plates. It might look at the blind, tin-beaded rim of the creature and wish to give it eyes, so it could know the bright, ordinary sky above it.

Wisdom would stop that hand before it moved.

For the power would understand, in the slow, quiet way that true understanding arrives, that water here is poison. A single afternoon of rain would drown the oiled bristles, short the clean arcs in the roots, shatter the hot quartz with thermal shock, and turn this crisp, humming engine into mud and rust. Eyes would be a cruelty in a place built for the blinding noon; speed would tear the delicate gaskets against the raw basalt.

Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is asking to be saved. This is not a wasteland waiting for a garden; it is a garden the mountain dreamed in a different language—written in resin, voltage, and patient stone.

So the power would touch nothing inside the crease. It would not adjust the torque of the magnetite rotor, nor add a single wafer to the neat stacks, nor spare the creature the slow, honest work of mending its own shell.

If it did anything at all, it would do its work at the lip of the ridge, where the pocket meets the rest of the world.

High-altitude weather is fickle. Sooner or later, a stray thunderhead, heavy with sleet from the wet valleys, would drift toward that nameless fold. And when it did, the power would simply lean against the wind—not with a wall or a roof, which would steal the sharp sunlight the plants drink, but with the lightest possible deflection. A warm updraft here, a nudged current of air there, just enough to coax the cloud an inch to the left, so its rain would fall on the thirsty pines a mile below and leave this floor untouched.

And if, centuries from now, a surveyor or a wanderer should climb the ridge with boots and a hammer, looking for ore, the power would let their eyes slide easily past the unweathered crease. To them, it would look like nothing at all—just another dry, jagged crack in the rock, too hot and too sharp to be worth the climb.

Beyond that, the power would only watch, the way one watches a small, steady flame in an open field—cupping a hand near it not to change its light, only to keep the wind from blowing it out.

Down in the hollow, the magnetite shelf would keep turning. Two of the quartz-shelled creatures would meet along a magnetic line, press their tin-beaded rims together, snap a tiny blue-white spark like a grain of salt, and glide apart into the bright, dry silence—never knowing they had been seen, and never needing to know.
