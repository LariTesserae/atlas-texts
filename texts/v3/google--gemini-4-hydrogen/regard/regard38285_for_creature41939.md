At first, the power only listened, which was not easy to do. There was so much of it—the steady *shhh-roar* of the back cliff, the clatter of wooden clogs on wet cedar, the *thwack* of mallets, the barking of herders, the frantic eruption of starlings every time a shutter banged. It took a long moment of stillness, tucked invisibly into the mist above the limestone rim, to hear that all of it was one sound, the way a lung is one sound even when it wheezes and sighs and coughs.

The first impulse of kindness was to mend it.

Any power with a heart would feel that pull, looking down into the bowl. Everything there was fighting the cold and the wet, and losing by inches every day. A single breath of warmth could have dried the unbleached linen against their ribs. A word spoken into the grain of the cedar could have turned every walkway and cantilever to timber that never blackened, never went spongy, never snapped under a porter’s heel. A gentle hand at the back cliff could have slowed the waterfall to a polite trickle, giving the elders back their hearing and letting two neighbors speak without shouting into each other’s ear-rims.

It would have been so easy to give them stone. To widen the rim, drain the peat, and let them build for their grandchildren.

So the power waited, holding its gifts in an open palm, and watched for a turn of the weather.

It watched a rotted plank come tearing out under a woman’s drawknife, and watched her toss the black, crumbling wood straight down into the gloom, where three bristled pigs fell on it like a feast, churning it into the steaming mulch that kept the whole floor alive. It watched two men wedged chest-to-shoulder on a dripping catwalk, screaming curses over a frayed goat-tether while their hands—without either man looking—checked the lashing on the other’s load and hooked a thumb toward a loose peg overhead. It watched an old woman carefully packing orange-bellied salamanders into damp moss, and saw how the hide-tanner’s whole craft, and the dryness of every boot-seam in the bowl, hung on those cold, slick bellies.

It listened to their songs, timed to the scrape of adzes, and heard the old story they told around the banked clay braziers: of the terrible summer forty years gone, when the back cliff dried up for three days and the hollow fell so quiet that nobody could sleep for the dread of it.

And the power closed its hand around the gifts it had meant to give, and put them away.

To dry the hollow would be to kill it. To make the wood unrotting would starve the beetles, the frogs, the ducks, the pigs, and the very urgency that bound four tiers of people together like hemp cord around a mast. If a house lasted a hundred years, a man would not need his neighbor’s hand on his roof-beam before dawn. If the floor never gave an inch, they would forget how to walk with their knees unlocked, ready to catch each other when the mountain moved.

They were not a people trapped in a hard place. They were part of a single, dripping, noisy creature—oak and goat, starling and trout, peat and bog-iron—and the rot was only its appetite, the rain only its blood.

Still, a wise power does not walk away entirely when it loves what it sees. It simply looks for where the seams are—the places too deep or too high for an adze to reach.

So it did not touch the dead wood. It left the cedar to blacken and the hemp to fray, so the rope-makers would keep their trade and the young would keep their nimble grip on the upper boughs. It did not touch the rain, or the cold, or the squalls that flattened the awnings and sent turnips bouncing into the pig-runs.

Instead, it reached down beneath the springy peat, past the cold springs and the root-tangles where the trout hid, and laid a quiet, steadying weight against the deep limestone of the mountain shoulder. A fault had been creeping there for three centuries—a slow, blind crack where winter ice wedged deeper every year, waiting for the spring thaw that would shear half the rim away and bury the bowl in a single roar of stone. The power did not seal the rock, for the weeping cracks fed the moss and the cliff-water; it only knit the deep bone of the mountain back to itself, so the bowl would hold.

Then it moved among the ancient cloud-oaks—not the planks cut from them, never the planks, but the living anchor-trees themselves, whose crowns held up three tiers of huts and walkways. It did not make them immortal, for old trees must eventually fall and make room for cane and sapling, but it washed a stubborn blight out of their sap before the first leaf ever curled, and coaxed their taproots a fathom deeper into the dark beneath the peat, so no wind could tip them while the people slept.

And last, on the nights when a true killing gale gathered beyond the mountain—not an ordinary squall to rattle the shutters and make a good story, but the kind of storm that strips a hillside bare—the power stood unseen at the horseshoe rim and leaned a shoulder into the wind. Not enough to make it calm. Never enough to make them careless. Just enough to turn a ruinous tempest into a hard, cold blow that sent them scrambling up the rungs with their adzes and green cane, shouting over the roar, cheerfully building for next Tuesday.

It left no sign of itself. A sign would have made them look up at the sky, and in the hollow, anyone who takes their eyes off the slick slats ends up in the pig-wallow.

By morning, the mist parted in silver-green shafts. A boy slipped on a mossy rung, caught a goat-tether, and sent a basket of smoked eels clattering down two tiers into a duck-coop. Three voices immediately began shouting at once, a cloud of starlings erupted from the eaves, and the power—satisfied, and entirely unnoticed—let the noise wash over it, and went on its way.
