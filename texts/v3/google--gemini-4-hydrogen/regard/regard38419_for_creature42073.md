If a power that was both wise and benevolent turned its attention toward this drowned range, it would not arrive as light from above, or as a sudden, unnatural calm. There is already enough light strobing through the torn ceiling of the sea, and a calm here would be a kind of death.

First, it would simply listen—not through the water, where the roar of collapsing bubbles and grinding cobbles would drown out any nuance, but the way the eldest matriarchs listen: pressed flat against the ancient gneiss, feeling the long, slow grammar of the mountain’s bones.

It would feel the cold that aches, the forty-knot rips tearing around the saddles, the sting of glacial grit. It would see the hides scarred nearly white with old barnacle-scrapes, the crooked jaws worn down by decades of prying mussels from iron-hard stone, the young ones lost in the early years to the bronze impellers.

A lesser kindness—one that had compassion without wisdom—might be tempted to reach down and soothe the place. It might warm the currents, smooth the swells, dissolve the rusted brackets and silence the thrumming turbines, turning the cordillera into a quiet, sunlit garden where nothing had to wedge its fingers into granite just to sleep.

And in doing so, it would unmake the people of the smooth stone.

A wise power would see this at once. It would understand that the storm is not an affliction laid upon these creatures; it is the medium they are written in. Take away the surge, and the cobbles in the potholes stop grinding, leaving the thick-shelled crabs uncracked. Calm the saddles, and the kelp hangs limp, no longer slinging them across the ridges like stones from a sling. Silence the iron entirely, and you strip away the warm cables where they rest their bellies in the dark, and the amber floodlights that draw the shrimp within easy reach.

You do not pity a mountain for being hard, or a creature for having grown to fit the mountain.

So the power would stay its hand from the great, obvious miracles. It would leave the cold sharp, the currents fierce, and the stone unforgiving. Instead, its work would be small, quiet, and tucked into the grain of things—so subtle that neither the stone-people nor the surface-tenders would ever know a hand had been laid on the scales.

It would look first at the one true wound the machines had brought: not their presence, which the mountain had already folded into its geology, but the way their bronze *thrum* masked the distant, low-frequency groan of building surface gales. Without changing a single watt of power or muting the hum the inhabitants now used to navigate, it would tune the resonance of the bedrock—just slightly, along the deep fault-lines—so that the warning tremor of an approaching storm carried a cleaner, lower pitch, distinct from the chatter of iron. Even a young one, pressing its jaw to a shelf for the first time, would feel the difference between the engine and the sky.

Then, it would turn its attention to the other, stranger thing happening in the blue-black dimness around the intake station.

It would see the gifts wedged into the rusted brackets by the door: the marble-smooth cobbles, the snapped fishing wire stripped carefully from the turbine shrouds, the hollow carapaces of giant crabs. And it would look through the thick glass of the station, and up the taut steel hawser to the world above the silver ceiling, where fragile, air-breathing things in canvas and brass were trying to keep the lights on without knowing they had neighbors.

Here, wisdom would see the real danger—not the forty-knot current, which the stone-people could always outlast, but the possibility that the surface-dwellers might one day grow careless, or greedy, or frightened of what moved just beyond their lanterns. A sprawling expansion of concrete, a heavier dredge, a panicked shot fired into the gloom—any of these could break a truce that had taken decades of silence to build.

So the power would touch the fragile ones, too, in the only way that respects human minds: not by commanding them, but by clearing a little silt from their attention.

Up in the dry, cramped drafting rooms where engineers sketched future grids for the coast, a quiet, stubborn conviction would take root—a sense that this particular range was already at its limit, that the single intake cylinder and the scattered turbines in the saddles were enough, and that anything larger would tear the bedrock loose. When a designer looked at the blueprints for a replacement shroud, his pencil would pause, and he would add a simple, flared grating over the intake mouth—cheap to weld, no loss to the current, just narrow enough to keep a curious young swimmer out—and think nothing of it beyond a sudden bit of good sense.

And down in the cold, inside the swaying maintenance bell, when a tender peered out through a scratched faceplate at the polished cobble wedged into the door-bracket, the power would simply keep him from brushing it off as current-drift. It would let the weight of the sphere settle in his gloved palm for an extra second, until he felt the deliberate, careful balance of it—until the loneliness of the forty-knot dark shifted, just a fraction, into something like company.

Nothing in the drowned range would look different afterward.

The swells would still thunder against the shallow domes. The kelp would still whip flat against the gneiss and spring upright again. The eldest matriarchs would still sleep hooked around the warm, barnacle-crusted cables, reading the health of the bearings through their ribs.

Only, the next time the maintenance bell descended on its singing hawser and a diver stepped clumsily out onto the ledge in his lead boots, the slate-colored shadow that glided past the lantern’s edge would not find him flinching away, or reaching blindly for a wrench. It would feel the air hose hum differently as the diver held perfectly still, letting the current wash over them both—and perhaps, wedged neatly beside the iron bracket when the bell went back up, the stone-people would find something small and bright left behind in the warm light, before the storm took the mountain back.
