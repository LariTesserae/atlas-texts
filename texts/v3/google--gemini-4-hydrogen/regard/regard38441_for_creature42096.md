At first, from a great distance, it sounded like suffering.

A power that watches worlds—one old enough to know the difference between a wound and a callous, and gentle enough to care about both—is drawn often to loud places. Here, miles above the lowlands, the noise rose like a column of brass: a twenty-mile drumhead beaten endlessly by high-altitude gales, layered with the metallic rasp of millions of jaws and the shrill alarms of small, hunted things.

So the power leaned close, and looked.

It saw the people first, because it is always easiest to pity what resembles oneself. It saw them crouched into the wind, their hair bound with copper so it would not blind them, their shins armored in insect-shell against grass that cut like glass. It saw them unable to speak a loving word beneath the open sky without shredding their throats. It saw them sleeping with their ears pressed to a living floor that hid pale, eyeless predators inches below their ribs. It saw them drinking morning sweat from the throats of giant reeds, and laying their dead on bare stone to be unmade by hawks.

A lesser mercy would have moved at once.

A hasty kindness would have raised a wall of mountains around the rim to break the gale. It would have poured rich, heavy loam into the dark voids beneath the turf, smothering the pale packs and giving the people solid ground to plow. It would have hushed the locusts, softened the sedge, brought rivers winding across the flat, and given the people back their voices so they could sing in the open air.

The power held its hand above the plateau, and felt the shape of that mercy—and then, before acting, it did what wisdom requires. It knelt, metaphorically, and pressed its palm to the moss.

It listened.

Through the springy weave, it felt the slow, steady fever of the root-mat’s decay—the furnace that kept the high-altitude frost from turning the world to iron. It felt the updrafts climbing the sheer outer cliffs, carrying the only moisture the sky would ever give, feeding the mist that beaded on the waxy sedge. It followed the blind gleaners chewing away dead fiber so the mat would not choke on its own history, and the under-packs culling the grazers so the green skin would not be stripped to bare limestone.

And it saw what would happen if it broke the wind.

Without the gale, the thermal updrafts would stall. The mist would never curl over the rim. The locusts would drop and rot; the reeds would dry to brittle bone; the living skin would parch, crack, and blow away, leaving only twenty miles of dead, blinding rock. Fill the hollows beneath, and the furnace would suffocate; the people would grow heavy-heeled, would dig and build and tear the weave, until the whole tableland slid off its ribs into the valleys below. Give them back loud speech, and they would forget the crisp geometry of blade-talk against the bright sky; they would stop pressing their chins to the moss to hear a companion’s staff a hundred yards away.

This was not a prison. It was an instrument—strung so tight that every creature on it had become a note. To loosen the string out of pity was to destroy the music.

So the first thing the benevolent power did was nothing.

It withheld the wall of mountains. It withheld the rivers, the deep soil, the silence. It let the wind roar, and let the copper-shelled locusts rattle like hail, and let the people keep their crouch, their horny soles, their quiet, practical reverence for a floor that must never be cut deeper than a finger’s length.

Yet wisdom is not cold, and restraint is not the whole of love. Having decided not to remake the drum, the power looked more closely at the edges where time, rather than balance, was wearing things thin.

It moved beneath the skin, into the dark, wind-scoured chambers where no sun had ever fallen. There, far out toward the eastern rim, it found a place where the limestone had eroded too wide over ten thousand years, and the ancient root-pillars holding up the ceiling had grown as thin as stretched tendons. Above it, a band had pitched their boat-shaped shelters, unaware that the next heavy rain would weight the turf past what those tired fibers could bear.

The power did not fill the void. It did not turn the roots to stone. Instead, it breathed the smallest patience into the oldest fibers—coaxing them to braid a little tighter, to draw a fraction more silica from the dust, just enough to hold until the gleaners and the new shoots could catch up. A quiet mending, indistinguishable from luck.

Then it rose to the glass-polished stone domes, where the people came with bladder-skins after the afternoon storms to ladle tea-colored water before the wind drank it dry. It touched the shallow basins—not to deepen them into lakes, which would breed rot and draw too many bodies to one fragile place, but only to roughen the microscopic grain of the stone at the bottom, imperceptibly, so the water would cling to the rock for half an hour longer against the sun. Half an hour—just enough for a runner from a distant camp to arrive with an empty skin and not find dust.

And last, it lingered beside a path-pole at dusk.

An old woman had stopped there, kneeling in the silver-green sedge with her shin-greaves scuffed and her staff laid flat beside her. Above her head, a bleached skull and three hammered-copper clappers swung in the gale, clattering their dry rhythm against the roar. She was waiting for her band to signal from the mist, her chin resting lightly on the moss to feel the beat of their staves.

The power could not speak to her without breaking the silence of the ground, and would not if it could. Instead, as the warm mist curled over the rim and settled in beads along her braided hair, it steadied the air inside a single hollow reed-stand nearby—just for three heartbeats—so that the wind across the fractured crown did not shriek, but hummed a low, clear fifth, like a bow drawn once across a vast string.

The woman did not look up, for there was nothing to see. She only pressed her palm a little firmer against the living skin beneath her, feeling the warmth of the mat, the distant *thrum* of her people’s feet, and—for one inexplicable, unrepeatable moment—the sense that the drum was not merely being beaten.

It was being heard.

Then the power withdrew, leaving the wind to take back the reed, leaving the world sharp, loud, perilous, and whole.
