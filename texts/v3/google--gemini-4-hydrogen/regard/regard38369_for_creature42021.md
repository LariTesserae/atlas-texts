At first, from above the motionless lid of vapor, the power saw only a wound that had forgotten how to close.

It looked down upon the flat, coppery-grey expanse and felt the ancient, familiar ache of pity. Here was a place trapped in the moment of its own cooling—no wind to clear the stagnant air, no night to grant a pause, no soil to soften the knives of the basalt. Life had poured into the basin before the stone was ready, and now it was packed so tight it could barely draw breath. Everything wept; everything screamed; everything crawled over glass.

A lesser mercy would have acted at once.

The power’s hand rose, gathering the raw materials of what it knew to be good: a cool, sweeping wind to tear open the cloud-ceiling; a slant of clean sunlight; a slow, gentle frost to fracture the vitrified plates into loam; a quietness in the air so the creatures below might finally hear themselves think.

Then, before it let a single breeze fall, wisdom stayed its hand. *Look first,* it told itself. *Do not heal what you have not touched.*

So the power lowered its attention through the oven-heat of the sky, past the wet-winged moths and the smoke-columns of midges, into the tea-colored dusk beneath the fronds.

It watched the dwellers move on knuckles and knees through the pale aerial roots. At first glance, pity returned—for their low, hunched sprawl, for the bitter oil weeping from their ribs, for the thick black horn peeling from their soles. It watched one of them pause beneath a broad leaf, tilting its head back so the slow *plip-plip-plip* of condensation struck the dark, flushed curtains of skin beneath its chin.

*If I cool the air,* the power realized, *the leaves will stop weeping. If I dry the vapor, their heat will have nowhere to go, or the cold will crack their slick skin before they can grow fur they do not have.*

It watched a knot of four tear away a thick mat of plants to expose a shelf of naked, razor-edged basalt, and drag a dead amphibian onto the glass. The clicking tide of scavengers poured from the cracks, and the dwellers scooped them up by the handful.

*If I crumble the stone into soil,* the power saw, *there will be no clean table. The dead will rot into mud, the parasites will multiply in the filth, and the scavengers that feed them will drown in dirt.*

It looked into the wide, lidless, milk-glass eyes of a dweller resting on the mossy back of a seep-giant.

*If I part the clouds for noon, I will blind them.*

Still, the power wondered: *What of the noise? What of the silence they must crave, locked inside this deafening roar of frogs and beetles?*

It watched a dweller halt on a bare plate of stone, tuck its elbows in, and lower its broad, callused jaw until the bone rested flush against the glass.

The power pressed its own awareness down into the basalt beside that jaw.

Instantly, the basin transformed.

The maddening biological wall of the air—the stridulating wings, the booming throat-sacs, the wet slithering—vanished as if a heavy door had shut. Beneath the surface, the newly cooled plates of volcanic glass stretched unbroken for miles, ringing like giant, dark bells.

Through the stone, the power heard the *tick-tick-tick* of scavengers three ridges away. It heard the deep, sulfurous sigh of a seep-giant shifting in the weeds. And then, half a mile across the steaming flats, it heard a slow, deliberate cadence:

*Tap… tap-tap… tap.*

It felt the dweller’s answering knock through the bone of its jaw. It felt the whole silent, luminous network of the basin’s floor—how the listeners mapped the heat, found their kin, and remembered their dead. Far off on a high shelf, a circle of crouched shapes was tapping a steady, unbroken rhythm into the glass while the scavengers stripped white bones clean. Not a mournful sound, nor a desperate one, but a clear note rung into an instrument built to hold it.

The power understood then. To soften this world would be to cut out its tongue. To silence the air was nothing; they already possessed a deeper silence, and they spoke inside it.

Slowly, the power withdrew the wind it had gathered. It let the clouds remain heavy and unbroken. It left the heat fierce, the dusk coppery, the basalt sharp enough to score horn. It did not wake the sleepers from their half-brain rest, nor straighten their spines, nor alter a single drop of the bitter oil that kept them whole.

Yet benevolence does not leave empty-handed, even when it chooses not to reshape what it loves.

Beyond the jagged, unweathered rims of the basin, the outer world was vast and indifferent, full of drifting ice, ash-storms, and wandering rivers that could spill over the lip and drown this delicate, sweltering equilibrium in a single night. The power laid a quiet, invisible ward along the high rims—not a wall, and nothing the dwellers would ever notice, only a gentle turning aside of blind catastrophes, so that this young geology might have its full, unhurried span to live and age in its own way.

And then, because there was nothing to fix and no one to rescue, the power settled its own vast attention down into the deep, vitrified bedrock beneath the seeps.

It did not tap back. It would not deceive them with the sound of a neighbor who was not there. Instead, it simply pressed its own stillness against the underside of the ringing glass, and listened—holding every knuckle-tap, every bone-hammer’s song, every slow, rhythmic note of the dwellers’ lives in a memory that would not cool, even when the eons finally turned the stone to dust.
