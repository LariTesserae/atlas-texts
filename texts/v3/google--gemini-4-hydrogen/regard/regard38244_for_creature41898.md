At first, the power only looks.

It knows how to mend things, and because it knows how, its first instinct is the ancient, dangerous kindness of the healer: to look at what is dry and wish for rain; to look at what is still and wish for wind; to look at a creature alone in a folded afternoon and wish for a second voice to answer it.

For a long moment, it considers what a single cloud would do here.

A soft, gray rain, no heavier than mist. It would darken the banded gneiss, coax a scent of petrichor out of the old dust, and pool in the concave hollows of the footpath. It would soften the tar-black lichen and give the keeper something to drink besides the waxy shavings of weaver-silk.

Then the power looks closer, and stays its hand.

In a world that has forgotten how to rot, water is not mercy; it is a slow, violent dissolution. A single damp afternoon would swell the papery hides of the cliff-clingers until they tore from the stone. It would weight the translucent, bladder-bodied drifters and dash them into the bottomless chasms. It would turn the swept, bone-dry floor of the beehive hut to slick paste, bloom mold in the seams of the cured-hide garments, and rust the hammered copper rings wedged into the cairns. Even a breath of wind would scatter the delicate membranes spun across the rock’s pores, starving the weavers of their mineral dust.

And what of the light? To bend the pale slate sky toward dusk would be to introduce an ending where everything has already survived its end. To straighten the folded geometry—to pull the overlapping moments apart so that every six-legged grazer stayed single and every shadow stayed heel-bound to its walker—would be to evict the mountain from the only way it knows how to hold all of its remaining life.

So the power sets aside rain, and wind, and sunset, and straight lines.

Instead, it watches the keeper.

It watches the linen-pale figure thread silently between the swaying, ash-colored legs without brushing a flank. It watches the slow, deliberate blink of the black-glass eyes, the unhurried scrape of flint against bone, the careful placement of a rounded cobble atop a cairn. It sees that the keeper is not starving, not frantic, not waiting for rescue. The keeper is simply faithful—tending the ledger of an impossible, pleated afternoon so that nothing slips away uncounted.

A lesser power might still feel the urge to leave a sign: a brighter stone, a softer bed, a whisper of approval in the thin air.

But this power is wise enough to know that any intrusion—even love, if it arrives loud and damp with its own pulse—is just another mess on the swept floor, another shadow the keeper would have to rise and brush away with a bundle of silver-gray wire-scrub.

So inside the basin, the power does nothing.

It does not smooth a single ridge. It does not wet a single lip. It does not even let its own attention fall heavily enough to cast a shadow across the doorway of the beehive hut, where the keeper sits with knees drawn up and palms pressed flat to the cool gneiss, breathing at the pace of the rock.

Instead, the power turns outward, to the high, unseen rim where this ancient plateau meets the rest of the turning universe.

There—miles beyond the last needle-thin arch, where no six-legged grazer ever wanders—it sets a quiet, seamless boundary. Not a wall, for walls can crack, and not a storm, for storms have voices. Just a gentle, permanent refusal: a fold in the air that turns aside the stray cloud, the wandering seed of green moss, the loud, leaking curiosity of younger worlds. It ensures that the rain will fall elsewhere, where things are built to drink and decay, and that nothing wet or hasty will ever stumble over the rim to trouble the count.

Then, carefully, without stirring a mote of mineral dust, the power withdraws its gaze—leaving the basin dry, silent, crowded, and entirely its own.
