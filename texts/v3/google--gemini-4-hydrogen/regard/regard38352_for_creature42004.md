A lesser power would have brought the sun.

It would have looked down through the bruised gray ceiling, heard the deafening, indifferent roar of the plateau, and felt the reflex of pity that mistakes harshness for suffering. It would have parted the fog to let warmth touch the wet slate. It would have softened the razor-edged reeds so they no longer sliced the creature’s straps, or crumbled the raw basalt into loam so something softer might take root, or given the blind, salt-crusted thing in the thicket a pair of eyes to see the blue-violet sparks of its own passing.

The power that came here did none of those things. Because it was wise, it arrived the only way this place could bear to be met: without light, without heat, and without a sound of its own.

It settled into the plateau the way the fog settles—occupying the space between the tightly packed stems, tasting the wet slate and ozone, letting the tectonic *crack* travel up through the marrow of its attention. It listened to the continent-wide organ of the wind and the dry, static *crackle* of millions of osmotic valves boiling water cold inside the reeds. It watched the droplets creep upward along the charged ribs of the fronds, defying gravity in the dimness.

And then it noticed the silences.

It followed one of those small, localized absences—a patch of thicket where the diffraction-grating blur suddenly sharpened into focus and the droplets fell straight down in a heavy, ordinary patter. There, wedged in the forks of the vegetation, it found the creature: a flattened, charcoal-dark sack of pressurized hide, streaked with verdigris and chalky mineral salts, its seven asymmetrical straps locked onto the metallic striations of the reeds.

The power did not touch it. It knew that its own presence, if pressed too close, would register to those coppery cilia only as a strange, unreadable distortion in the voltage gradient—a phantom reed that could never be gripped, a false promise of charge. So it held itself just outside the circuit and simply watched.

It felt the faint chemical warmth bleeding from the creature’s damp back, torn away instantly by the wind. It heard the steady *thrum* of current passing through flesh where a heartbeat might have been. It saw the old, blunted scars where the razor-edged flora had sliced a limb shut, and the slow, patient, hydraulic certainty with which another limb peeled away—*snap*, a flare of blue-violet light—to taste the fog and find a fresh stalk.

Then, miles below, the basalt shifted half a millimeter.

The *crack* rang up through the crystalline rock and the taut root-network like a struck bell. Across the fog-choked plateau, dozens of circuits snapped open at once. The creature purged its excess water in a heavy, copper-smelling gush, deflated, and squeezed downward through crevices no wider than two fingers, past the last of the gray light, until its belly scraped the sterile, glassy stone.

The power followed it into the freezing dark. It watched the creature spread its straps wide into the newly opened fissure before the roots could thread in and lock it shut, running its harvested sky directly into the earth, etching branching scars into rock that had never known a river.

And here, in the dark beneath the roots, the power weighed everything it could do.

It could have warmed the air by ten degrees—and watched the condensation fail, the osmotic valves starve, the pressure drop, and the creature dry out like a bladder left on a shore.

It could have dulled the blades of the reeds—and watched the wind-friction vanish, the electrostatic charge die, and the plateau fall into a quiet, well-fed, meaningless sleep.

It could have given the creature eyes—only to trap it in a fifty-yard wall of fog with nothing to look at but the backs of stems too dense for a hand to pass. It could have given it a voice—only to teach it, for the first time, that the storm was too loud to hear it. It could have woken its dim, electrochemical hunger into self-awareness—and invented loneliness in a world that had previously known only the hum above and the stone below.

Everything that looked like mercy from the outside would have been an amputation from within.

The power looked at the creature pressed flat against the basalt, discharging its small, patient portion of the sky, and understood that this was not an unfinished world waiting to be rescued. Whether it was a forgotten maintenance organ that had learned to live for its own sake, or simply what wet stone, wind, and excess charge make when left alone long enough, it was *whole*. It did not know it was blind. It did not know it was small. It was already completely occupied by itself.

So the power withdrew every gift it had considered, before a single one could leave a mark.

Instead, it did the only thing that could truly be called kind in a place like this, and it did it so far beyond the bruised gray ceiling that no reed bent and no spark flared.

It turned its attention outward, to the wide, indifferent dark around the world—to whatever builders had seeded these superconducting thickets and walked away, and to any traveler who might one day crest the plateau, see a roaring, lightless marsh of knives and fog, and feel the urge to pave it, warm it, or finish what had been started. Gently, without raising a wall or leaving a monument, it drew a quiet un-noticing around the continent. A bend in the path of those who might come to harvest it. A long, unhurried permission for the stone to keep cracking, for the reeds to keep whistling, for the sky to keep grounding itself through blind, salt-crusted flesh.

Then, before it left, the power lingered for the space of one more tectonic shift—not to alter a single droplet on a single frond, not to smooth a single scar on the basalt, but only to bear witness to the hum in the dark, and to leave the creature to its work.
