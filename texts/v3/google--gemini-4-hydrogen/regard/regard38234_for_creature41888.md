First, it would match the room’s temperature.

A careless kindness would arrive as light, or cool wind, or a sudden rush of rain—and in doing so, it would ruin everything. Light would be a meaningless glare against blind, soot-black plates, or worse, a scalding burn to the crescent pits tuned only to cherry-red filaments. Wind would strip the parched air of its fragile pockets of dampness. Rain would drown the shallow basalt basins, wash away the dark paste of stewed leaves, and rot the roots that have spent lifetimes learning how to drink one drop at a time.

So the power does not announce itself. It enters the chamber without displacing the heat, without stirring the dust settled in the seams of the creature’s back. It has no frantic pulse to alarm the stone, no wasted moisture to shed into the dark. 

It simply waits.

It waits through the first twenty-minute silence, and the first sixty-second hum. It waits through the second cycle, and the third, letting its attention settle into the slow grammar of the place: the *tock* of water on leather-thick leaves, the faint *tick* of expanding iron, the long, glacial rise and fall of ribs draped across braided copper.

In that stillness, it sees the temptation of ordinary mercy—the urge to crack the unseen ceiling and let down the sky; to trade the bruised violet and bone-white for green; to lift this scarred, armored thing out of the feverish dark and set it somewhere soft, with eyes to see a horizon and others of its kind to walk beside.

Wisdom sets every one of those mercies aside. To give this creature a horizon would be to strip it of its world. Its flat teeth were made for mineral-heavy mulch; its broad flanks were made to mirror the geometry of heat; its patience was forged for a room with no predators and no hurry. Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is asking to be rescued.

Yet a wise eye sees what the creature’s heat-pits cannot: that the chamber’s heartbeat is borrowed, and mortal.

The power turns its attention inward, past the soot-rimmed sight glasses and the riveted iron shells, into the quiet mechanics that keep this pocket of life from turning back into sterile rock. It sees the slow, inevitable wear of things made by hands long gone. Inside one of the sealed casings, the copper contacts of a solenoid are pitted from years of tiny arcs, wearing thin toward the day they will weld shut and silence the twenty-minute rhythm forever. Beneath a transformer housing, a heating element’s filament has grown brittle and narrow, one surge away from going dark. Along the lowest bend of a condenser manifold, corrosion has eaten halfway through the pipe—not yet a leak, but a future rupture that would bleed the chamber’s scarce water into steam before a single drop could reach the roots below.

The power does not replace the machines with miracles. It does not make the iron eternal or turn the copper into something unearthly. 

Instead, it waits for the next click of the solenoid.

When the low-frequency hum rises through the basalt—that sixty-second cover of vibration during which the creature itself dares to shift an inch across the stone—the power moves with it. Quietly, without a flash or a sound, it mends the small inevitabilities. The pitted copper contact thickens by a fraction of a millimeter, clean and true. The brittle filament regains its steady gauge. The thinning wall of the condenser pipe knits itself back to strength, leaving the pale verdigris on the outside untouched so the roots and the creature’s claws still know their way.

It touches nothing else. It does not blunt the razor edges of the young basalt; the creature’s hide is already built to bear them. It does not summon a second creature to cure a loneliness the being does not feel, knowing well that two mouths in a room measured in drops would only teach them to hunger, or to fight.

When the hum subsides and the silence locks back into place, the power lingers for one more cycle, just to be sure the rhythm holds.

The creature, pressed flat against the warm stone beside the transformer housing, knows none of this. It has no name for grace, and no use for gratitude. It only knows that the floor beneath its belly is steady, the faint red glow in the sight glass has not dimmed, and when it drags itself up to the lowest bend of the cooled copper manifold, the metal is slick and sweating right where its throat expects to find it.

A single drop gathers, swells, and vanishes into its scales.

Satisfied, the power withdraws—leaving the dark intact, the stone hot, and the long, patient work of the chamber unbroken.
