When the power arrived at the edge of the basin, it did the wisest thing it had ever learned to do: it left its light outside.

It was a power accustomed to making dawns. In other places, its mere attention arrived as a golden meridian, a sudden clearing of fog, a straightening of crooked lines. Had it brought even a fraction of that habit here, the valley would have screamed without knowing why. Light would have struck the velvet-winged flyers like a physical blow, scrambling the static corridors they rode. It would have dried the bacterial film on the basalt, withered the pale fungal felt, and turned the dark—which was not an emptiness, but a medium, thick and supportive as water—into an exposure.

So the power stepped into the ankle-deep brine as a presence without radiance. It took no shape that needed to be seen. It simply made itself heavy enough to displace the tepid water, and quiet enough to hear the *drip-drip* from the stanchions.

For a long time—three turns of the nearest flywheel, perhaps an hour—it did nothing at all. It only listened, tasting the ozone and the boiled salt, letting the low, sub-audible hum of the transformers settle into its ribs.

It felt the first temptation almost immediately, as any benevolent thing would. Beneath its attention, the black flint and vitreous slate lay razor-sharp and cruel. A stilt-legged wader was stepping slowly through the dark twenty yards away—*plip… plip…*—and the power saw how easily it could smooth the basin floor. One gesture, and the cruel edges would round into river-pebbles.

Then it waited, and watched the wader’s knee twitch three seconds *before* its whisker-fringed pad descended toward a submerged blade of flint. The creature stepped neatly sideways, feeling the sting of the cut ahead of the cut itself, and moved on unharmed. Around the wader’s ankles, dozens of leathery filter-feeders clung to those same sharp edges, using the angles of the fractured stone to anchor themselves against the oily current.

The power withdrew its hand from the stone. To soften the floor would be to unmoor the things that lived on it, and to make useless the very gift by which the waders walked.

Next, it considered the frayed seam of time. Here, a splash arrived before the stone left the hand; a breaker’s ozone bloomed twenty minutes before the iron fell. A power built for order naturally wished to comb the threads straight, to put cause cleanly ahead of effect.

Instead, it watched a pale, rubbery ambusher uncoil from a stanchion and snap its jaws into empty air—and three seconds later, a flyer swerved straight into the waiting teeth. It watched a tender, massive and silent, materialize beside a brine pipe with a wad of greasy oakum in its blunt, calloused palm, three seconds before the iron split under pressure.

*Ah,* the power thought, without words. *This is not a wound. This is how they survive the dark.*

It turned its attention sideways, slipping gently across the grain of the wet vellum into the quieter, drier pages of the valley. Here it found the other halves of the tenders: broad, eyeless faces with rhythmic gill-slits and crowns of fine, swaying filaments, resting peacefully in the dry dark while their pillar-like legs stood planted in the brine next door.

For a moment, the power felt a deep, aching pity—the kind that makes lesser gods ruin what they love. It saw the salt crusting on the pump rods, the heavy tallow buckets, the endless, patient labor of smearing grease onto barn-sized gears that turned only once every twenty minutes. It thought of lifting the tenders out of the marsh, of gathering all their scattered parts into a single, whole room, of letting them rest.

Then it leaned closer to one of the tenders—not touching, only sharing the same cubic meters of humid air—and felt what the tender felt.

There was no misery in the creature. There was only the vast, tidal comfort of the flywheel’s *shhhk*, the steady lung-beat of the beam pumps, the sharp, clean vocabulary of a shorted busbar singing through its jawbones from a mile away. The dry room next door was not a prison; it was a sanctuary where its heart stayed safe from the voltage and the stone. And when the power drifted past an elder—one who had grown too heavy with absorbed salt to shift anymore, its shins calcifying in the brine while fungal felt climbed up to drink its last stray warmth—it felt no terror in the cooling flesh. Only the slow, dignified settling of a creature becoming part of the architecture that had held it.

A foolish benevolence would have woken the elder. A wise one let it sleep.

In the end, the power understood that nothing here was broken. The valley was strange, and sharp, and blind, and crowded to bursting—and it was entirely whole. It did not need a savior. It did not need a sun.

So what did the power do?

Almost nothing.

It lingered for one more cycle of the great flywheel. In the quiet dark, it reached out a hand—not to mend, not to alter, not to bless in any way the world could name—and laid its palm flat against a crooked iron stanchion. It felt the slow, peristaltic pulse of the ribbon-bodied colonists milking the stray voltage, and let a single bead of warm, waxy tallow drip across its knuckles. It stood still as a ribbon of velvet-winged flyers streamed past, brushing its forehead with the tickle of damp fur.

And then, before it withdrew, it did the only thing a power from outside could rightly offer such a place.

At the far, unseen rim of the basin—where the wet vellum thinned out toward the wider, sunlit universe—it drew a quiet, unbreakable seam. A mercy of pure concealment. It made sure that no stray dawn would ever leak into the valley, no clumsy explorer with a lantern would ever stumble down the basalt shelves, and no younger, prouder god would ever look upon this dark, warm, patient marsh and decide to fix it.

Then the power stepped back, left the dark intact, and let the relay click shut behind it—*tock*—leaving the valley to wait in peace for the next slow cycle to turn.
