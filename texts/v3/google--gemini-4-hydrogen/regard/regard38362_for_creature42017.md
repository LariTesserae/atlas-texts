At first, the power saw only a wound.

From high above the white-gold glare, where the thin air gives way to colder silences, the hanging basin looked like a place where the world’s loom had slipped a stitch. A benevolent gaze, accustomed to the long, clean arcs of rivers finding the sea and stones resting true upon their beds, recognized the strain at once. Here, weight pulled sideways. Time tripped over itself, letting the *crack* of a breaking cliff outrun the fall. Beneath the wet shale and fractured obsidian, the bedrock hummed with the stubborn, sub-audible ache of a bell struck too hard to ever quite stop ringing.

It would have been the simplest thing in the world to mend it.

A single, gentle pressure—no louder than mist settling on slate—and the sheared seam in the air would close. The western cataract would drop straight and clean to the basin floor, sparing the notched pillar another ten thousand years of chewing water. The shadows would snap back to their obsidian spires. The bronze-ringed plumb lines would hang true, pointing faithfully toward the center of the earth, and the mountain’s deep, teeth-aching hum would finally subside into quiet sleep.

Benevolence, by its nature, reaches toward what is broken. The power gathered itself above the saw-toothed rim, preparing the small, merciful correction that would set the valley right.

Then wisdom asked it to wait until noon.

So the power held its hand and watched through the fierce morning glare. For hours, nothing moved that was not water or falling shale. It noticed the small, stubborn marks left by earlier visitors—the unmortared slate windbreaks, the square catch-basins, the sinew plumb lines trembling at impossible angles—and felt a faint curiosity. Whoever had climbed up from the green world below had not tried to straighten the stones. They had chipped footholds into the slick quartz, taken their measure of the crooked down, and gone away again.

At midday, the mist thickened, swallowing the rim in white light. The wind dropped. Water beaded on the black basalt and began to creep upward along the pale quartz veins in glittering threads.

And the valley woke.

The power watched the sinew plumb lines twitch toward the dark basalt. It watched the salt-and-ochre crusts peel away from the fluted slate, lifting to reveal the pale, quartz-clear lattices beneath—the silica ribbing, the slow pulse of copper-green and iron-red, the spun-glass filaments tasting the wet air for ozone.

It saw how heavy they were. Without the sheared gravity of the fault lines, those iron-rich bodies would be crushed flat against the scree, pinned immobile beneath the furnace of the sun. It watched one of the smaller ones cling inside the horizontal flutes of the slate pillar, drinking the violent, vibrating pressure of the sideways river. It watched another on the lower scree ripple and slide smoothly aside a full second *before* a frost-split slab peeled from the cliff above—saved by a warning echo that had arrived ahead of the falling stone.

In an unbroken valley, they would have starved, burned, and been broken. Everything that looked like an injury to the world’s order—the crooked weight, the staggered time, the ringing bedrock—was the very architecture of their breath. Ten thousand years of sideways water had worn the stone smooth to fit them.

The power understood then that healing is not always a kindness, and that a crooked room can still be a cradle.

Slowly, it drew back the hand that had meant to mend the seam. It did not quiet the mountain’s bell, nor straighten the cataract, nor stitch the trembling shadows back to their spires. To leave the valley broken was the hardest mercy it had ever practiced, and the truest.

Yet it did not depart quite empty-handed.

As the midday mist thinned and the beings settled back into the wet hollows of the shale, becoming stone once more, the power lingered just long enough to leave a single, weightless grace upon the high rim—not in the basin itself, where even a gentle miracle might bruise a resonant membrane or drown out the hum of the quartz, but in the thin air above the passes leading up from below.

It laid no wall there, and no barrier. Those who carried the patient humility of the cairn-builders—who knew how to hang a plumb line, listen before stepping, and leave a strange house as they found it—would still find the footholds chipped into the slick quartz.

Only, for any who climbed the mountain with heavier intentions—the ones who come to straighten what bends, to mine what hums, or to heal what they have not waited until noon to understand—the mist would seem a little thicker, the glare a little harder on the eyes, and the hanging basin easy to pass beneath without ever looking up.

Then the power withdrew, leaving behind no footprint, no echo out of place, and no sign that it had ever been there—except that the valley went on ringing, undisturbed, in its own cracked, faithful key.
