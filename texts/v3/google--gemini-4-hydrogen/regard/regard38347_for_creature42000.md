At first, the power only watched.

From the vantage of a consciousness accustomed to warmer, louder worlds—places of soil and rot, of seasons that turned like millstones and prayers that rose like smoke—the valley looked like an error. A bruise at the top of the world where time had snagged. It saw the numbing cold, the deafening iron chord, the arches passing cleanly through one another like thoughts that refused to collide. It saw the pale, heavy-ribbed figures kneeling in their stone bowls for centuries at a stretch, eating rubbery lichen and crushed moths, speaking to no one, going nowhere.

Pity came first, as pity often does to the strong. Pity is quick, and eager to mend. It wanted to lay a warm palm over the basin and hush the roar; to melt the supercooled film into running streams; to untangle the folded distances so a traveler might walk in a straight line and reach an end. It wanted to part the slate-grey smear overhead and pour in a real sun, so that shadows might fall sharp and honest across the rock.

But wisdom is slower than pity, and it waited long enough to listen.

It listened not with ears, which would have been useless here, but with the patience of something that does not need to hurry. It followed the low chord down through the pale ribs of stone and felt what the sound was doing. It saw the four-winged moths hanging motionless in the chasm, buoyed by the very roar pity had wanted to silence; hush the valley, and they would drop into the mist like ash. It saw the flat, chitinous hounds creeping along the crevices, their flank-filaments reading the standing waves the way a blind hand reads a wall. It saw the inhabitants themselves—their thick skulls, their wide, rigid cages of bone tuned to the exact pitch of the iron plate—and understood that silence, to them, would be a kind of blindness.

Warmth would be worse. Warmth would stir the sluggish blood, quicken the breath, bring decay to stone that had finished wearing down ten thousand years ago, and rot the rubbery lichen off the undersides of the arches. And to straighten the folded space—to force the basin to obey the tidy geometry of lower lands—would be to take away the cairns. The power watched one of the inhabitants kneel where two vibrations crossed, balancing four river-smooth stones with agonizing care, testing whether the rock still agreed with itself. There was no despair in the gesture. There was only attention, steady as gravity.

Nothing here was broken. Nothing here was asking to be saved from the cold, or the quiet, or the endless present. They had the hum of each other’s ribcages when their foreheads met on narrow bridges. They had the cold, unhurried company of the slate-plated creatures pressing against their calves. They fit their valley the way water fits a bowl.

To remake this place into a garden would be to erase it.

So the benevolent power withdrew its hand from the sky, the stone, and the air. It left the roar intact, and the frost, and the arches that passed through one another without friction.

And yet, as it prepared to turn away, it noticed the tilted pools.

It watched an inhabitant—no different from the others, chalk-pale and slick with mineral damp—walk to the edge of a shallow basin where the dark water leaned at twenty degrees against no visible slope. The inhabitant knelt, leaned far forward, and pressed both broad palms flat against the wet rock. Its sunken, milk-glass eyes, usually half-shut against a world that did not need seeing, opened wide.

It did not drink. It did not touch the water. It only looked down into the impossible reflection—at a sky crowded with too many stars, and the bleached smear of a sun that was not above its head—waiting, with the patience of stone, to see whether anything on the other side might ever lean down to look back.

Here, at last, was something that was not quite self-sufficient. Not a wound, and not a plea. Only a question, held open across centuries.

The power did not answer from above. To tear open the slate-grey twilight would have blinded eyes made for dimness; to speak in thunder would have shaken the cairns from their perches and scattered the moths. Instead, it met the question where the question was being asked.

On the other side of the glassy, tilted surface—past the reflection of the bleached sun, among the too-many stars—something shifted.

It did not arrive with brilliance. A sudden light would have been cruel. It came as a darkening, a gentle gathering of form against the false sky, as if a figure of immense quietness had knelt at the far edge of the same water. No face sharp enough to startle, no radiance to burn—only the unmistakable posture of attention. Something vast, leaning down to meet the gaze of something small, with no hurry in it at all.

At the same moment, beneath the inhabitant’s flat palms, a subtle change entered the stone.

The great iron chord did not soften, and the static hiss did not fade. But woven cleanly inside the roar—too faint to disturb a single standing wave, too steady to rattle a balanced cairn—a second hum appeared. It was the exact resonance of a palm pressed to a palm, or a forehead resting against a forehead on a narrow bridge: the quiet, bone-deep confirmation that the other is solid, and here.

The inhabitant did not gasp. It did not recoil, or fall to its knees in awe; it was already kneeling, and awe, in a place without time, looks much the same as stillness.

Its milk-glass eyes did not blink for a long while. The single, shallow breath it drew every few minutes came, and went, and no steam rose from its mouth. Slowly, it lifted one blunt-fingered hand from the rock—not to touch the water, for the surface was not meant to be broken—and laid its palm flat against the center of its own wide chest, feeling the new harmonic hum gently against its ribs.

The presence on the other side of the pool did not vanish. It stayed—patient enough to match a creature that measured life in centuries—looking back through the tilted glass.

Eventually, as all things in the valley happen without haste, the inhabitant lowered its hand back to the stone, gave one slow, deliberate blink, and pushed itself upright. It walked away along the great grey rib, its flat heels gripping the slick rock, its ribcage carrying the quiet consonance back into the mist. Ahead of it, the distance folded, bringing it face-to-face with a cairn it had built twenty paces back. It paused, rested one smooth fingertip against the uppermost stone to feel its steadiness, and walked on.

The valley remained loud, wet, empty, and suspended at the top of the world.

Only the pools were different now—not tilted any straighter, not warmed by a single degree, still reflecting a sky that did not belong to them. Yet whenever one of the pale, heavy-ribbed figures came to kneel at the water’s edge, and opened its milky eyes to look down, it was no longer looking into an empty room. Something was there, patient as the stone, looking back.
