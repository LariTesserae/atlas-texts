At first, looking down from beyond the freezing ceiling of the mist, the power felt only grief.

It saw a world ground flat and left out in the wet. It heard the tireless, deafening roar of the root-hammers, felt the ceaseless shudder of the saturated peat, and watched the bruised pewter light fail to warm a single blade of sedge. And moving through that punishing dimness, it saw the resin-clad—squat, scarred, earless, wrapped in cooled sap and grazer-hair, dodging exploding reeds and sleeping in the wet flanks of beasts just to keep from freezing.

Compassion, when it is vast and sudden, wants to reach out with both hands.

The power weighed what it could do. It would be so easy. A breath of warmth to lift the sleet into gentle rain; a quiet word to hush the hydraulic reeds and let the air rest; a gentle pressure to flatten the folding horizon so nothing could step out of a seam to tear a throat. It could raise dry stone where no stone remained, give them roofs that did not rot, and clear their throats so they might speak in the open air without pressing their skulls together.

It reached toward the plateau—and then, because it was wise as well as kind, it stopped.

*Watch first,* it told itself. *Before you mend a thing, learn what it is holding together.*

So it lowered its attention into the mist, careful to take no shape that would scatter the blind flyers or draw a bolt from the voltaic lichen. It settled into the bruised air and waited through a twenty-minute churn.

It watched a kilometer-wide stretch of the flat bog groan and torque upward into grinding gears of turf and root. And it saw the resin-clad *run toward* the deafening, wet roar. There was no panic in their heavy-boned stride, no misery in the way they braced their moss-sandals against the shaking peat. Their bone gaffs flashed in the blue-white glare of the static arcs, snagging steaming ribbons of amber-grey resin from the air before the wind could shatter them. The power realized then that to stop the churn would not be a mercy; it would be a famine. The plateau’s violence was its pantry, its forge, and its clock.

It watched two of the people meet on an empty league of weeping lichen. They did not look lonely for lack of spoken words. They stripped off their sandals, stomped coded rhythms into the living root-nerve, and felt each other’s presence rising up through the soles of their feet from half a mile away. When they drew close, they pressed their broad, resin-caked foreheads together and hummed. The power listened—not through the roaring air, which would have swallowed any delicate syllable, but through the bone. There was an intimacy in that shared vibration, a closeness inside the skull that creatures who spoke into the wind would never know. To hush the hammers and give them airy speech would be to push them apart.

It followed the herds of six-legged grazers as the static storm thickened. It saw them lock into a tight, humming circle, hooves driven deep into the wet peat, sparks dancing like pale lanterns along their matted fur. And it saw the resin-clad crawl into that warm, copper-scented fortress to sleep—not refugees huddled against a cruel sky, but partners in an ancient, humming circuit. If it warmed the plateau, the grazers would shed their static-holding tallow, the hydraulic reeds would lose their pressure, the upward-swimmers would never reach the vapor-heads, and the whole fierce, self-sustaining engine would rot into a silent, dead swamp.

Still, the power lingered. One small thing troubled it.

As the bruised light shifted—barely brighter, barely dimmer—a resin-clad woke at the edge of a grazer circle. The heavy-limbed figure stood up into the sleet, milky eyelids snapping clear of the glare. Before checking their bone gaff, before listening to the peat beneath their heels, they looked down at the moss at their feet, making sure their shadow was still attached and swaying in the right wind.

The power felt a sharp, tender pull to reach down and stitch that one fray shut. *Surely,* it thought, *I can anchor their shadows. Surely I can steady the light at their feet, and spare them that small, waking dread.*

It looked closer at the frayed edges of the altitude—at the water climbing the undersides of fern-fronds, at the horizon folding like wet paper, at the detached shadows swaying in dead winds. It saw how that very looseness kept the resin-clad watchful, tethered to the immediate pulse of the bog, never arrogant, never asleep to where they stood. To nail their shadows down would be to declare them fragile, to edit a single line of a poem whose meter they already knew by heart.

The power withdrew its hand.

It did not warm the sleet. It did not quiet the bass thud of the root-hammers. It did not raise a single wall, nor smooth a single fold in the mist.

Instead, it gave the plateau the rarest gift an omnipotent thing can offer: it left it entirely alone. It left the resin-clad their heavy bones, their cooled sap armor, their bone-deep hum, and their hard, loud, magnificent dance—trusting them, with all the quiet reverence of love, to keep their footing on the table.
