If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to this shelf, the first thing it would take care not to do is cast a shadow.

The dwellers in the pools know shadows. They know the weight of overhead light, and they know when something stands between them and the sky. So the attention would arrive without obscuring the sun, without stirring the warm, wet air, and without sending the faintest tremor through the slate beneath their bellies. It would arrive the way heat arrives in deep stone—already present, patient, and indistinguishable from the place itself.

For a long time, it would simply watch.

It would watch the thin, glassy film sweating from the concave walls. It would watch the bladder-sac on a plum-and-jade stalk swell over months and finally split without a sound, sending its pale wash of spores gliding across the mirror of the floor. It would watch the clear pane in the cup-shaped hollow slide by fractions of an inch, rolling its soap-film edges inward like an eyelid, and see the slow, bloodless tide rising and sinking inside its body—warmed by the rock, cooled by the air, knotted with amber where old food had settled. Beneath the great cushion of liverwort on the back wall, it would feel the pillowy, rose-veined one pulse by a hair’s breadth, drawing in the moss-filtered runoff and breathing it back into the roots above.

And on the dry western ledge, it would see the dulled stone scraper and the braided cordage, and understand the pact that held this place together: someone who climbed out of the clouds every few years with bare feet and patient hands, taking only the empty, pearlescent saucers that had been left behind, and deepening the hollows so the next slow life would have room to spread.

A lesser power—one that loved life loudly, or mistook stillness for lack—might have felt the urge to mend things here.

It might have wanted to give the pool-dwellers eyes, so they could see the vast, bright floor of cloud beyond the drop. It might have wanted to give them voices, or speed, or a memory that lasted longer than the slow turn of a season. It might have wanted to bring birds to break the silence, or to lock the geothermal vein deep beneath the ridge so the fever in the slate would never cool, and the shelf would remain warm forever.

Wisdom would stay that hand.

To give the pool-dweller eyes would be to teach it the fear of distance; its whole upper skin already knew the only light it needed. To give it speed or blood would be to break the quiet convective engine that let it live on nothing more than heat and patience. To bring wings to the shelf would be to bring hunger, and to make the rock’s warmth eternal would be to trap a living thing inside a museum, denying it the very geology that had shaped it—the slow rising, the calcification, the gradual shift of seams that taught it how to lay down stone and move on.

Even the silence was not an emptiness to be filled. It was the medium in which the dwellers heard the ridge groan and the spore-sacs split.

So on the shelf itself, the power would do nothing. It would not nudge a single drop of water past the basalt wedge. It would not soften the climb for the rare visitor, nor hasten the growth of a single bruised-plum leaf. To improve a place already whole on its own terms is only another way of erasing it.

If it acted at all, it would act far below, beneath the flat, bright floor of cloud, where the world is loud and hurried and full of hands that build walls. There, without anyone noticing, it might turn away a careless gaze. It might let a ridge-line be misremembered on a map, or keep a trail overgrown, or quietly dull the ambition that drives people to climb high places just to see what can be taken from them—leaving the path open only to the one who already knew how to come with bare hands, mend a basin, and leave nothing broken.

And then, as quietly as it had arrived, the power would withdraw its attention, leaving no footprint in the wet mineral film and no ripple in the ankle-deep pools.

On the shelf, nothing would have changed. In the center of the clear pan, the translucent pane would relax its margins by a fraction of a millimeter, drinking the warmth from the slate, laying down—grain by invisible grain—the next thin layer of pearlescent stone.
