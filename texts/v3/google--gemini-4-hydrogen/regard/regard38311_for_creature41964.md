A power that is both benevolent and wise does not enter a room like this by the door.

It knows, before it ever reaches the summit, that light here would be a blade. A single flare of radiance poured down through the frost-shattered cleft would strike the upper roosts like a stone, sending thousands of oil-rich birds into a blind, battering panic against the wet limestone, choking the thin air with loose feathers and terror. It knows that widening the throat of the peak to let in the cleaner, richer air of the valley would be an execution: the alpine wind would drop twenty paces into the dark like an axe, stripping the thirty-degree warmth from the basalt in a single night and turning the lukewarm salamander basins into plates of ice.

So it does not come from the sky. It comes the only way anything is truly known inside the mountain: up through the stone itself, patient as a root, listening with the weight of the rock.

At first, it simply holds still and lets the chamber speak.

It feels the roar of the place—not as noise, which is how a creature of open air would judge it, but as a single, braided pulse. The papery *whir-clatter* of the upper roosts. The needle-sharp rain of the bats. The dry, shifting *tick-tick-tick* of the crickets carpeting the lower walls. And beneath them all, riding the curved basalt like the lowest string of an instrument, the slow, wet *thrum* of the leather-bellows lungs.

A lesser mercy, looking at such a world, would ache to rescue it. It would see the milky, clouded eyes and wish to give them sight. It would see the thin, ammonia-heavy air and wish to give them wind. It would look at the smooth river cobbles—*the hard ones*, tethered with coarse hair and sinew—and feel pity that no living hand here remembers the sunlit valleys from which those stones were carried. It might even lift the heavy-shouldered sleepers out of the grease and the guano-mud and set them down beside a bright river under an open sky.

And in doing so, it would kill everything they are.

The wise power watches—or rather, *feels*—a mother on a mineral-glossed shelf, dished out by ten thousand years of resting hips. She has an infant clinging to the coarse tufts along her collarbone, and she is humming against the small of its back, teaching its ribs the pitch of the dark before it has ever tasted a beetle. The power feels two elders meet on a narrow ledge, pressing their calloused palms flat to the stone between them to pass the news of a weak bird on the third shelf. It feels the quiet, unsentimental thrift with which they hold a cooling body until its last heat bleeds into the rock, before taking the sinew and the bone and returning the rest to the warm compost where the shelf-fungus glows.

There is no misery here. There is no prayer for a wider sky. What lives in the throat of the peak is not a broken fragment of the valley world; it is a completed thing, tuned over ages to the exact curve of its stone, wasting nothing, hurrying toward nothing, holding its breath in common so that every wing and gill and lung has room.

To change it would be vandalism.

So the power’s first act—and its greatest—is refusal. It refuses to brighten the pewter sliver of daylight overhead. It refuses to sweeten the air, or widen the shelves, or multiply the beetles so that the primates might grow fat and fast and outbreed the narrow cathedral that holds them. It refuses to give them names for themselves, knowing that names are a luxury of places where breath is cheap.

Yet, because it is wise, it does not stop at the ledges. It lets its attention sink deeper—past the rising floor of feathers and crushed chitin, past the shallow calcite basins where the blind salamanders hang motionless in the mist, down into the dark, porous basalt beneath the mountain’s root.

And there, it feels what the primates cannot feel.

Deep in the stone, the geothermal vein—the ancient, slow-breathing hearth that keeps the chamber at thirty degrees while glaciers grind the ridges outside—is thinning. Not today, and not in the lifetime of the infant clinging to its mother’s chest. But in the long, geological dark ahead, the ember is cooling. Century by century, the rock’s slow exhale is failing. One winter far down the line of generations, a snowflake surviving the twenty-pace drop through the cleft would not melt into lukewarm mist; it would settle, and stay, and the chamber would begin its long surrender to the iron cold.

The power considers this. Everything ends; even mountains are temporary. It would not be wise to freeze the chamber in amber, to make a museum of a living thing, or to grant it an unnatural eternity while the rest of the world turns to dust.

Instead, it does something so small that not a single bat shifts on the ceiling, and not a single cricket pauses in its *tick-tick-tick* along the wall.

Deep beneath the floor, where no root reaches and no bone chisel has ever scraped, it lays the equivalent of a palm against the cooling basalt. It does not stoke the vein into a furnace—that would boil the basins and scatter the roosts. It simply lends the dying ember a quiet, steady persistence, holding the rock’s warmth at the exact threshold it has always kept: thirty degrees, give or take the breath of the crowd. Just enough to keep the mountain’s argument with the blizzard from ending too soon.

Then it withdraws, leaving no sign. No sudden bloom of moss, no gentler wind at the summit, no miracle to startle the dark.

High above, the alpine wind still skips across the throat of the peak like breath across the mouth of a bottle. Inside, a heavy-shouldered elder wakes on its dished-out shelf, eases three feet along the slick limestone without startling a sleeping bird, and presses a long, calloused hand flat against the floor to listen for the news of the chamber.

The stone is warm. And if the elder hums into it, the mountain—immeasurably deep, immeasurably patient—seems, for an age or two longer, to hum back.
