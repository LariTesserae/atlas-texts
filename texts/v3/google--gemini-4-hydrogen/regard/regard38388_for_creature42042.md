It would not arrive with light. Here, light is already sharp enough to bleach bone and crack resin. Nor would it arrive with wind, or thunder, or the sudden, generous smell of wet earth. Those are the manners of the lower plains, where things can afford to be loud.

Instead, the attention would settle over the basalt shelf the way dusk settles—without displacing a single moth, without stirring the dust on a single copper-green paddle.

At first, seeing as a vast and tender mind sees, it would feel the ache of the place.

It would look into the shaded alcoves and see the builders lying thin and heavy-lidded after a wall-mending, their bodies spent into the chalky plaster. It would watch a climber hang by hooked toes over a sheer drop for a handful of gypsum dust, or chew the bitter pith for days to hoard enough moisture in its glands to bind a single stone. It would feel the sheer narrowness of the margin: one failed winter, one season too dry for the vines to spare a bead of sap for the lizards or the moths, and the whole braided city would unravel into silence of a different, final kind.

And the first impulse of kindness—the kind that has power and has not yet paused to think—would be to *give*.

It would be so easy. A finger traced through the high, thin air, and a cloud would gather above the plateau, dark and heavy with sweet rain. A slight loosening of the basalt seams, and a spring would well up clear and cold in the center of the orchard, spilling down the narrow alleys, washing the chalky walls, filling every carved succulent bowl to the brim.

The power would pause, its hand poised above the sky, and look closely at what that mercy would do.

It would see the rain strike the porous, saliva-bound gypsum—and watch the three-story towers slump into pale, boneless mud. It would see the swollen, water-hoarding roots of the succulents split and blacken in the sudden flood, drowning in the very thing they were built to ration. It would see the dusty-winged moths grounded and heavy, their velvet scales matted together; the blind springtails washed out of their dark fractures; the lizards shivering in the sudden damp.

Worst of all, it would see the climbers. Given water without effort, they would no longer need the scrolled passages in their snouts, nor the dense, mist-trapping fur, nor the slow, deliberate geometry of their posture. They would open their throats, spend their breath into the wet air, and learn to hurry. In a single generation, the covenant with stillness would be broken, and the beings who had made an art out of scarcity would become merely another noisy tribe on a wet rock.

To heal their thirst by drowning their world would not be benevolence. It would be an erasure.

So the power would withdraw its hand from the sky. No clouds. No spring. No soft, unearned abundance.

Instead, it would look deeper—past the gypsum towers, past the lizards wedged in their crevices, past the blind mites grazing in the dark—down into the young, unweathered basalt itself.

Because the shelf is geologically young, it is restless. Far below the reach of the deepest succulent roots, the stone is still settling under immense, blind pressure. The power would see what the climbers cannot: a hairline fault in the deep rock, shifting by fractions of a millimeter each year, slowly pinching toward the narrow mineral vein that feeds the entire plateau. Left alone, in ten winters or a hundred, the stone would shear—not with malice, only with the indifferent weight of mountains—and seal the dampness away forever.

There, in the dark where no eye watches and no pride can be wounded by charity, the power would act.

It would not widen the vein; to give more than the city can hold would be to rot it from below. It would simply brace the deep stone, slipping its quiet, tireless patience into the fault like a wedge of something unyielding, keeping the ancient, microscopic seam from closing. And once in a great while, when a violent, unseasonable storm wandered too high from the plains, carrying enough hail or deluge to strip the gypsum from the walls in an hour, the power would lean gently against the wind—just enough to turn the storm’s shoulder aside toward the empty cliffs.

Nothing on the surface would ever know. No climber would look up in wonder; no lizard would pause in its slow turning toward the sun. The builders would still chew the bitter pith, still spend themselves into the walls, still hold their postures for long seconds until the meaning cured like resin.

Yet, before it drew back—or perhaps without ever quite leaving—the power would linger for one long afternoon.

It would match its own vast, timeless attention to the stretched clock of the orchard. It would watch a silver-pelted climber take a full minute to cross a three-pace bridge of calcified vine, testing each joint with a scarred, leathery palm. It would watch two climbers settle onto their haunches, leaning their shoulders together while their pale throat-pouches pulsed in a shared, soundless rhythm, exchanging warmth without spending a drop of breath. It would smell the faint dry cinnamon of a nocturnal bloom opening to a moth as silent as falling ash.

And the power, which had made oceans and forests and skies without counting the cost, would sit very still on the dry stone and learn something about grace: that it is easy to be generous with an endless well, but to hold a world together with nothing more than patience, salt, and silence—that is a work worthy of being left untouched.
