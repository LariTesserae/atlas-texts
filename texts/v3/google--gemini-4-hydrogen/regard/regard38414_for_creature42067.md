If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to the spire, it would not arrive in thunder. Thunder would rattle the turnbuckles loose and send a panic through the oil-ticks.

It would slip in quietly, the way the cliff-swifts do, through the razor-thin ribbon of sky or the knife-edged cleft halfway up the face. And before it touched a single bolt or blessed a single brow, it would do what the Plumb-keepers do: it would press one palm to the stone, steady itself, and listen.

It would listen to the dry *shhh* of powdered slate in the hide-lined chutes, the *tick-tick-tick* of grounding-chains bleeding static into the catwalks, the brief, economical hiss of laughter between two riggers sharing a thimble of warm iron-tasting water. It would watch a mother unhitch her family’s riveted frame and winch it twenty feet higher in synchronized silence before a fracture could open. It would see the old mechanic with a velvety vole curled in his breast pocket, and the dead at the bottom of the V, light as paper, held gently in the narrowing jaws of the rock.

And having seen all of this, the power’s first act of wisdom would be to refuse its own simplest mercies.

It would think of rain—of cool mist rolling down the shaft to wash the mica from the creases of their knuckles, to turn the grey lichen into moss and let green things root in the timber shoring. Then it would see the iron plates rusting at the rivets, the drive-belts swelling and slipping, the web-felt turning to cold slime, the oil-ticks drowning in their casings, and the dry-preserved dead waking to rot in the dark. It would withhold the rain.

It would think of stilling the mountain—of laying a vast, quiet hand upon the spire’s root so the slate would never creep or fracture again, and no one would ever have to unbolt their home in the middle of sleep. Then it would see the Plumb-keepers’ cradles hanging empty, their purpose gone; it would see the cables go slack, the modular frames fuse into dead weight, and the living clockwork of the cleft stiffen into a monument. It would let the mountain keep settling.

It would think of widening the walls, of lifting these deep-chested, dry-skinned people out onto a sunlit plain where air was cheap and a person could shout without wasting breath. Then it would feel the quiet, breathless vertigo in their bones—the terror of a world with no wall within reach, no guy-wire humming underfoot, nothing to hold onto. It would leave the cleft thirty paces wide.

Wisdom, here, looks very much like leaving things alone.

Yet benevolence is not indifference. Having chosen not to remake them, the power would look for the small, quiet places where help could fit inside their world without breaking its balance—gifts small enough to measure in a brass thimble, quiet enough to travel through a tension rod.

Far below the lowest amber bulb, deeper even than the narrowing V where the dead rest in mineral wool, the power would follow the single copper riser down to the deep-vein bore-hole beneath the spire’s root. There, in the blind dark where no Plumb-keeper’s palm can reach, it might notice a heavy shelf of granite drifting slowly across the fault—a shift that, in ten or twenty seasons, would silently pinch that solitary vein of water shut. The power would not freeze the mountain, nor flood the shaft. It would only lean its weight against that one deep shelf, nudging its course a hand’s-breadth aside, so the spring would keep feeding the riser for a hundred generations more. And perhaps it would draw the sharpest bitterness of sulfur from the deep rock, leaving the water still warm, still tasting faintly of iron, so it still tasted like home.

Higher up, in the raw slate along the thirty-pace walls, it would leave no miracle—only a slightly truer grain in the stone. Not enough to stop the fractures, only enough that when a dangerous strain began to gather, the singing vibration would travel clean and clear into the palms of the Plumb-keepers a few heartbeats earlier, giving the town one extra breath to unhitch, winch, and re-anchor in peace.

And high above the cleft, out where the pale cliff-swifts vanish into the lethal, roaring wind, the power might soften the sharpest cross-draft just off the lee of the spire, and let a little dust of distant, sun-warmed pollen—from some far-off valley the cleft-dwellers will never see—settle lightly onto the birds’ moth-pale wings. Not enough to change the cleft. Only enough that, now and then, a rigger wiping slate-dust from a warm hopper-rim might notice a fleck of unfamiliar gold on a swift’s feather, and feel, without vertigo, a quiet wonder at the size of the world beyond the rock.

Then the power would withdraw, leaving no altar, no legend, and no sign that it had ever been there.

Inside the cleft, a Plumb-keeper’s palm would steady on a plumb-bob; three slow taps would ring down a guy-wire; an entire tier of workshops and lockers would uncouple and rise smoothly in the amber dimness, ticking, shifting, and holding fast in the dark.
