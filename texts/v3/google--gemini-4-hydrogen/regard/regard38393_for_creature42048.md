If a power both benevolent and wise turned its attention here, it would not arrive as a shadow—there is nothing in this room that knows how to cast one—nor as a brighter light, since the air is already full to the corners with a glare like high noon.

It would arrive simply as stillness. A vast, patient noticing, settling over four paces of silicate and basalt without disturbing a single bead of water on the walls.

At first, it might recognize the older work: the photonic dispersion woven into the bedrock, the frictionless pull drawing moisture through the mineral lattice—remnants of a craft so ancient it has become geology. And then it would see what had moved into the accidental fracture. Not heirs, not pilgrims, not anything that could ever wonder who lit the stone. Only a slow, chalky-orange crust clinging to the cooler edges of the wet rock, and a handful of blind, thumbnail-sized grazers, pale as warm resin, tending the rim of a puddle.

Benevolence, at first, is always tempted to mend.

Watching from outside the stone, the power would feel the kiln-thick heat pressing down on the shallow basin. It would see the thinness of the oxygen in the warm water, the constant, barely visible beating of feathery gill-plates beneath seven overlapping amber shells. It would watch a grazer climb halfway up a weeping wall just to sit half-submerged in the moving current where air and moisture meet.

It would be so easy to help.

With less effort than it takes a drop of water to fall, the power could cool the bedrock by five degrees, letting the basin hold more breath. It could dull the razor-sharp edges of the fresh silicate so nothing could ever snag. It could weave a gentle dimness into the ceiling for half of every day—a mercy of dusk, a rhythm of rest—or coax a thread of moss onto the wettest shelves to sweeten the air. It could even touch those tiny, blind heads and grant them eyes to see the fierce beauty of the room they inhabit, or a spark of thought to know they are alive beneath tons of stone.

Wisdom, however, would pause before touching anything.

It would watch a drop fall from an upper seam—*plink*—and see how the clean ripple stirs flecks of the orange crust loose, sending two grazers darting sideways across the clear floor to feed. It would trace the heat down to the microbial film and see that without the kiln-warmth, the crust would starve, and the closed weather cycle would stall. It would look at the arthropods’ wide, padded legs and realize they were made for gripping glassy, unforgiving stone; soften the rock, and the world would feel strangely numb beneath their feet.

It would consider giving them sight, and then imagine what sight would be in a room with no horizon, no shadow, no predator, and no night—only a blinding white expanse that their palps already read with complete accuracy. And as for thought: what a cruelty it would be to wake a creature into wonder or longing inside a sealed room four paces wide, when right now it has no lack, no hurry, and no grief.

To improve this place would be to break it.

So the power would withhold its hand from the light, and from the heat, and from the water. It would leave the arthropods blind, and the walls sharp, and the orange film slow.

And yet—because it is benevolent, and not merely indifferent—it might do one small thing, so far outside the pocket’s notice that it would never be felt as an intervention.

Listening to the rare, sharp *tick* of the young basalt adjusting to the heat, the power would trace the hair-thin stress lines in the stone above the ceiling. It would see where the fresh fracture is still settling under the weight of the mountain—a quiet tension that, in a season or a century, might shear loose and fill the shallow basin with rubble before the orange crust has finished claiming the edges.

Gently, without a sound, without shifting a grain of silicate inside the room, it would ease that tension in the upper rock. Just enough so the roof holds. Just enough so the seam stays open, and the old water-pull keeps weeping, and the puddle has its full, unhurried span.

Then it would withdraw its attention, leaving no trace—not a ripple in the basin, not a missed beat of a gill-plate.

Inside, nothing would have changed. A drop would creep along a razor-edged ledge, break free, and land with a bright *plink* in the warm water. Beneath an overhang, a blind, amber grazer would feel the pulse against its back, tap the slick stone with its palps, and move unhurriedly out into the shadowless light to feed.
