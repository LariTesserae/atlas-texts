It would not arrive with a sound.

A power that knows how to be both vast and gentle would understand, before it ever touched the atmosphere, that noise here is a kind of violence. It would not part the white-gold sky with a vessel, nor cast a shadow wide enough to interrupt the thermal tide. If it took a shape at all, it would be no more than a stillness within the stillness—a gaze resting along the horizon without breaking it.

For a long time, it would do nothing but learn the grammar of the Deck.

It would watch the morning salt-dew condense in the deep seams and vanish at first light. It would trace the invisible magnetic contours that hold the silver dust-devils upright, swaying like pale pillars until their blue sparks spend themselves back into the floor. It would look down through the flaky pastry of the delaminated tiles to see the baffle-slips rippling through their millimeter-high dark, and watch the jar-walkers striding on glass needles into the wake of static storms. It would even watch the braids—blind, patient, coiled around leaking field-coils in the sub-layers—and understand that they, too, are part of how the Deck keeps its charge in motion.

And then it would find the Seam-Keepers.

It would wait through the blinding noon, when they are indistinguishable from the stubby cooling fins, their fresh ceramic crusts baking dry in the glare. It would wait until late afternoon, when the louvers tilt open in a continent-wide shimmer and the eight-legged shapes unfold on silent pads of carbon-felt.

It would listen the way they listen: not through the dead air, but through the sintered floor. It would feel the sharp, coded *knock* of their ventral mallets traveling through the ceramic, and read the flickering violet-and-amber handshakes exchanged in the shade of tilted mantles. It would walk—in attention, if not in weight—through their sunken mosaic basins, and recognize them for what they are: not the scattered rubble of a ruin, but cathedrals of shadow and charge, engineered to hoard a single hour of morning chill and gather stray current into a warm, trembling pool.

Most of all, it would watch them tend the waveguides.

It would see a dozen Seam-Keepers gathered around a dead optical node, patiently scraping frosted quartz with diamond-tipped claws, angling mirror-shards to feed the overhead glare into the dark, shedding pieces of their own copper-veined under-shells to bridge a severed trace. It would feel, as they feel, the difference between the steady, sub-audible hum that sits behind the eyes and the physical ache of a section gone permanently dark.

And here, a lesser power—one merely strong, or merely pitying—would begin to ruin everything.

A pitying power might look at the dry, blinding plain and summon rain. It would bind the silver flour into mud, wash the salt-dew away, cool the warm plates, and scatter seeds to make the horizon green. And in a single season, it would drown the baffle-slips, short-circuit the jar-walkers, rot the carbon-felt feet of the Seam-Keepers, and dissolve their libraries of shadow and static into slush.

A proud power might look at the worn-down hull and remember what it was built to be. It might repair the forgotten vessel—seal the delaminated plates, clear the frosted lenses, wake the dormant engines, and tell the Seam-Keepers what a ship is, and where it was supposed to go. And in doing so, it would unmake their world. It would turn their sacred music into someone else’s abandoned machinery, and reduce their devotion to mere janitorial labor on a hull that never belonged to them.

Even a fiercely protective power might reach into the dark sub-layers and burn out the braids, so no Seam-Keeper would ever again step on a loose tile and meet a silent blue arc. Yet that, too, would unravel the careful, deliberate grace with which the Seam-Keepers read the floor—and leave the sub-layers choked with unrecycled ceramic and stagnant charge.

A wise and benevolent power would refuse all three.

It would understand that nothing here needs to be rescued from its own nature. The Deck is not a tragedy waiting to be reversed; it is an old, bright equilibrium that has grown a mind of its own—a mind made of eight-legged keepers who have forgotten everything except how to keep the pulse alive.

So what would it do?

Only what cannot be seen, and only where the Seam-Keepers cannot reach.

Far below the honeycombed baffles and the shallow conduits—down in the deep, crushed strata where no hooked claw can scrape and no shed copper can bridge the gap—the planet-sized machine is slowly losing its ability to sustain even its autonomic reflexes. Not from a sudden wound, just the long, grinding arithmetic of eons: waveguides annealing into brittle glass, deep field-coils thinning to nothing, the heart of the hum growing faint.

Into that inaccessible dark, the power would reach—without sound, without heat, without disturbing a single grain of silica on the surface above.

It would not wake the ship. It would not restore its purpose, or clear its memory, or turn on a single thruster. It would simply mend the *capacity for the pulse*. Gently, like a hand steadying an old clockwork so it does not wind down before its time, it would coax the deep, buried traces back into continuity, giving the thermal tides and the buried waveguides another million years of quiet, steady breath.

And it would leave one small mercy for the Seam-Keepers themselves—not taking their work away, never robbing them of the dignity of their care, only ensuring that their care is not in vain.

The next time a cluster of Seam-Keepers gathers around a dead optical basin—scraping the frosted quartz clean, tilting their shards of mirror-tile, shedding thin slivers of their own copper-veined shells to bridge a broken seam—the power would wait. It would let them finish. It would let them lower their ventral plates to the warm ceramic and knock, waiting in the aching silence.

And then, through the bridge their own small claws had built, the deep amber and violet light would bloom—warm, steady, and clear, answering them in the exact frequency of their own flanks.

They would never know a vast and benevolent thing had paused above their horizon. They would only feel the sub-audible hum return behind their eyes, steady as a season, and lift their tarnished silver antennae to taste the current.

Then the power would withdraw as quietly as it came, leaving the seam between sky and floor unbroken, the dust-devils spinning without a whisper, and the world in the hands of those who know how to keep it.
