At first, the power mistook the city for a trick of the geology.

From the height of its attention, the continental spine looked like nothing more than newly broken rock—sharp, unweathered, and cold as iron. It was only when the sun reached its zenith, and the ink-blue shadows drew back into the canyons, that the power noticed the movement: not the slow heave of tectonic plates, but the tiny, deliberate shifting of leather buckets along braided ropes, and the glint of meltwater running down channels too straight to be the work of frost.

It leaned closer, folding its vast awareness down into the thin, bright air, until it could hear the *clack-clack* of split hooves on slate and the muffled *thud* of wooden mallets three tiers deep.

It saw the column of life: the great, patient beasts standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark, warming the floorboards with their breath; the compact, walnut-skinned people rubbing tallow into their knuckles before stepping into the freeze; the small, round-eared rodents listening to the groans of the unmortared walls; the glossy birds tucking themselves into the leeward niches before a gale. It saw a grandmother chipping a basalt cistern a handful of dust at a time, knowing she would be laid beneath a cairn on the high ridge long before the basin ever held a drop of water.

The power was benevolent, and its first impulse was to mend.

It felt the sting of the dry freeze on exposed skin, the ache of lungs drawing thin air, the sheer, grinding weight of every drop of water measured and carried by hand. It could have reached down with effortless grace and softened the world. It could have warmed the winds blowing up from the lowlands, turned the morning cloud-banks into gentle, soaking rain, and sent clear springs bubbling up through the fractured basalt. It could have raised forests of straight-grained timber in the leeward canyons, laid down veins of soft iron, and taught the builders the secret of lime and mortar, so that a wall might stand in a season rather than three lifetimes.

It held those gifts in its hand, ready to let them fall like snow.

Then, because it was also wise, it paused and looked again.

It traced the path of a sudden spring of water through the city, and saw it freeze at mid-afternoon, expanding in the dry-laid chinks until ten tiers of stone heaved outward and crashed into the talus. It imagined warm rain falling on roofs weighted only with boulders against the wind, turning the dung fuel to sodden ash and rotting the thick, grease-cured pelts. It saw what mortar would do to a mountain that still grew by a fraction of an inch each year: how rigid walls would not groan and settle, as dry stone did, but snap all at once in the night, burying the stables and the sleeping platforms alike.

And it looked at the people themselves. If it gave them abundance, and timber to burn without counting, and walls that went up in a week, what would become of the silence? What would become of the slow, flat-footed step that tested every stone, or the bond between the child who cleared the runnel and the grandfather who had carved it? To remove the cold would be to unmake the thick-ribbed chests that held it at bay, to scatter the beasts from beneath the floorboards, to make the birds' warning unnecessary and the rodents' vigilance meaningless.

A lesser kindness would have remade the city in its own image of comfort. Wisdom stayed its hand.

Yet benevolence is not indifference. The power could not simply turn away and leave them untouched; it had come to love the stubborn, quiet geometry of their endurance. So it chose to act the only way a wise neighbor acts in a house built without mortar: by fitting itself into the existing seams, without disturbing the weight of the stones above.

It did not change the cold, nor the thinness of the air, nor the pace of the chisels.

Instead, in the bitterest years—when drought in the distant lowlands starved the sky, and the morning cloud-banks threatened to fail the crags altogether—the power laid a quiet palm against the upper winds. It did not summon storms. It only nudged the drifting mist a league or two higher up the continental spine, just enough that the windward faces still woke furred in white rime, and the midday sun still had something to coax into the cisterns.

In the deepest, most sheltered clefts of the lower canyons, where the wind could not reach and a rare seep of moisture lingered, it warmed a handful of dust just enough for a single, slow-growing seed of gnarled alpine wood to take root—a tree that would grow no faster than the city’s own retaining walls, so that three generations from now, a builder scavenging the talus might find a fallen branch as dense and smooth as horn, and mend a broken ladder rung with gratitude.

And sometimes, in the dead of night, when the mountain shifted its fraction of an inch and a heavy lintel groaned above a sleeping family—too suddenly, perhaps, for the rodents to scatter in time—the power placed the weight of a single feather against the slipping slate. It did not lock the stone in place, for stone must be allowed to move. It only turned the angle of the slip by a hair’s breadth inward, so the arch wedged tighter rather than spilling, giving the builders until midday to read the new crack by the white light of the sun.

Then the power drew back into the vast, brilliant sky.

Nothing in the city changed. The mallets still struck with their small, dry *thud*; the hooves still clicked on the frozen roofs; the thin plumes of dung-smoke still rose straight into the freeze. No one looked up to offer a prayer of thanks, for there was no miracle to point to—only a good season for frost, a lucky find in the canyon, and a wall that held.

And to the power watching from the silence, that was enough.
