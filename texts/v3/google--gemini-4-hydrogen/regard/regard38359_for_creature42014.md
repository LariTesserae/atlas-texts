A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive like weather. It brings no sudden warmth to melt the rime-fog, no thick wind to carry a voice across the ridges. To announce itself in light would blind the smoky-clear domes of the tenders’ eyes; to speak in air would be to shout into a vacuum.

Instead, it simply turns its attention toward the slate shelf, and waits.

Wisdom is, first of all, the patience to look long enough to see what is actually there. At first glance, even a vast mind might see only a high, austere machine—basalt, static, sulfur, and cold. So it watches. It lets the starlight wheel over the frost-planed peaks. It counts the intervals between the silent, blinding-blue coronas. It notices the skip along the northern ridge, the three slow heartbeats in the central basin, and follows the ripple down to the waist-deep mats of carbon-reeds.

It watches a tender uncurl from a taproot. It watches the pleated collar seal against a fungal gasket to drink the warm, mineral-heavy steam. It follows the slow walk across the bare basalt saddle, the laying of the unworked copper ribbon in a meltwater groove—not a monument, not a road, only a seasonal kindness to a starved basin. It sees two of them kneel on the wet slate, jawbones pressed flush together, trading the quiet news of the dark through bone and stone.

And here, benevolence feels its oldest, sharpest temptation: the urge to mend, to ease, to give.

Looking down into the gloom, the power sees how hard the work is, and how thin the margin. It knows it could thicken the sky above the amphitheater, wrapping the ridges in a blanket of warm, breathable air. It could coax the carbon-reeds into green, soften the frost, teach the tenders how to smelt the copper into permanent wire so they would never have to lay another ribbon in the cold. It could give them voices that travel through the wind, roofs to sleep under instead of damp sedge, and eyes that look at a bright sun.

Wisdom stops the hand before it moves.

To thicken the air would drown the ferro-lichens, whose whole life is the dry tension between bedrock and near-vacuum. To bring the sun would scorch the lightless mats and blind the ones who tend them. To smelt permanent wires would sever the tenders from the slow, seasonal rhythm that makes them the nerves of the valley rather than its masters. And to give them voices in the air would make the earth-hum feel small, turning a creature that listens with its jawbone to the heartbeat of a mountain into something lonely and loud.

The valley is not broken. It is not waiting to be finished. It is a closed, ancient loop, and every rough edge—the freezing wind, the thin oxygen, the brief violet flash of the predator at the water’s edge—is part of the tension that keeps the machine turning.

So the power does nothing to the basins. It does not touch a single spire, or clear a single vent, or whisper a single comfort into the basalt. It leaves the tenders to their blankets of hollow-stemmed sedge, trickle-charging in the dark.

Instead, its benevolence turns outward, toward the sharp, unblinking stars.

High above the slate shelf—far above the thin upper winds that feed the forty-foot helices, so high it will never alter a single volt of the valley’s static—it sets a quietness in the dark. Not a roof, not a wall, nothing that would ever cast a shadow or trap a breath of air. Only a subtle bending of paths, a gentle, enduring blind spot laid over the amphitheater like a palm held over a candle flame in a draft.

If a stray iron meteor falls toward the ridges, its trajectory will curve, ever so slightly, toward the empty oceans miles below. If, in some distant century, builders from lower, louder worlds climb this high with their instruments and their hunger, their eyes will slide over the dark terraces and see only barren slate, random lightning, and nothing worth cutting.

That is all.

Before it withdraws, the power lingers for one more cycle of the spires—watching a blue flash answer across the central basin, watching a tender curl down beside a rhythmic piston-vent and pull the living sedge over its copper spine. Then it leaves the amphitheater as it found it: unbuilt, uninterrupted, humming quietly beneath the stars.
