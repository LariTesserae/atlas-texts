At first, the power’s instinct is to open the roof.

Looking down through miles of unyielding strata, it sees a fist-sized wound in the earth packed with heat, iron, and small, stooped figures who have forgotten the sky. To an eye accustomed to open pastures and clean wind, the pocket looks like a lung held underwater—a place of terrible compression, where children never run and the dead cannot even be buried beyond the walls of the kitchen. Mercy, at its fastest and most obvious, wants to part the basalt, let the steam billow out into the cool night air, and lift these people up until their spines straighten and their eyes adjust to the stars.

But wisdom does not act at the speed of first impulses. Before touching a single grain of stone, the power slows its attention.

It slows past the pace of breath, past the pace of footsteps, down until it is watching at the speed of the place itself: the glacial curl of a plum-colored frond toward an amber bulb, the swelling of a single drop of condensate on the underside of an iron pipe, the fractional advance of a hydraulic ram.

And at that speed, the illusion of a prison dissolves.

It sees that the tenders are not trapped here in misery; they have woven themselves into the pocket as tightly as the fibrous vines around the ceramic insulators. If the roof were lifted, the cool air would not refresh them—it would chill their damp-flushed skin and crack it, dry out the resinous sap that protects them, and wither the translucent succulents that drink from the railings. Sunlight would sear their wide, dark-pupiled eyes. Open distance would sever their quiet, tactile language of pipe-taps and resting hands. To "rescue" them by breaking their walls would be to unmake them.

More than that, the power sees the dignity of their equilibrium. There is no waste here, no frantic noise, no hoarding. Even grief is folded back into the warmth, the dead cradling the roots that brace the ironwork. To step into this room with miracles—to mend their old boilers with effortless magic, or conjure endless food from thin air—would be to strip them of their stewardship, leaving them idle in a world where attentiveness is the very shape of love.

So the power does almost nothing inside the walls.

Instead, it listens as the tenders themselves listen—through the soles of the feet, through the damp mortar, through the faint vibration of the rock. And listening that closely, it feels what the tenders already fear: how *young* the cavity is. The basalt around them is still tense from the shift that tore it open. Miles above and below, the deep strata are settling, grinding slowly toward a second spasm that would pinch this fragile hollow shut like a closing hand. A people can brace old iron with living roots, and mend brick with mortar, but they cannot brace a mountain.

There, in the blind dark beyond the basalt where no filament bulb will ever shine, the power goes to work.

It lays a vast, patient steadiness against the fault lines. It does not freeze the earth into dead, impermeable stone—for the thermal fissures beneath the lowest floor must still breathe their warm, mineral steam, and the pocket must remain alive. Rather, it eases the shearing weight of the upper rock, guiding the slow tectonic drift to flow *around* the cavity instead of *into* it, the way a careful hand cups a candle flame without starving it of air.

And within the pocket itself, it allows itself only two small, native graces—nothing that would announce a visitor, nothing that would startle a sleeping tender or confuse the blind millipedes.

First, in the rising steam of the thermal fissures, it lets the water carry a slightly gentler trace of dissolved silica and soft clay. Not enough to alter the mineral balance of the electrolytic baths or sour the filtered condensate, just enough that over the turning of the seasons, a faint, smooth bloom begins to gather along the raw, razor-edged basalt of the ceiling and walls—softening the stone’s newest teeth a little sooner than millennia would normally allow, so a child’s reaching hand is less likely to tear.

Second, in the deep brick planter-troughs where the elderly are laid to rest, it nudges the dark soil’s chemistry just enough that the roots of the plum-colored ferns find them swiftly and cleanly, turning what is given back into sturdy, sweet-resined growth without rot or waste.

Then the power withdraws its gaze, leaving no voice in the air, no sudden brightness, no sign that anything has happened at all.

Three tiers down, an old tender wakes from her turn in a vine-hammock. She listens to the low, steady hum of the vats through her bare heels, waits for a ceramic catch-basin to receive its next slow drop, and rests her palm for a moment against the raw basalt wall beside her elbow. It feels, perhaps, a fraction less sharp than it used to—or perhaps her own skin has simply grown wiser.

She taps twice with her knuckle against a warm copper pipe to let the lower tiers know she is awake, wipes the condensation from a pressure gauge with a dry rag, and steps slowly back into the rhythm of the room.
