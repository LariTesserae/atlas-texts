At first, it almost made the mistake of pity.

A lesser kindness—one that had not yet learned to look twice—would have parted the pewter ceiling. It would have sent a clean, dry wind across the flats to lift the stench of roasting shellfish and stagnant mud. It would have quieted the million frogs in the drainage channels, softened the razor-edged basalt into loam, planted grass along the causeways, and given these people a sun to wake to and a night to rest in.

The power held that wind in its hand, ready to loose it. Then it paused, and looked closer.

It traced the thread of the breeze before it ever blew. It saw the mist tear away from the outer sumps; it saw the wet, rust-orange biofilm crust over and crack into dead flakes under a sudden sky. Without the biofilm, the larvae died. Within a season, the slate-backed frogs fell silent in the channels, their pale bellies shriveled against dry stone. The shorebirds, finding no frogs and no fish-scraps, shrieked and scattered beyond the jagged rim of the ridges, leaving the roofs bare of guano. The slow fires went out. The crabs desiccated in the chinks of the walls, and for the first time in a thousand generations, the alleys began to rot.

And the people—the power looked at the people under that hypothetical sun. It saw the fish-oil and mineral silt baking into a cracked rind on their shoulders. It saw them squinting, half-blinded, against a glare their eyes had never been built to bear. It saw the relay break: everyone waking at once in a sudden morning, everyone sleeping at once in a sudden dark, the warm, tangled heaps on the sinew mats scattering into separate, lonelier silences.

Worse than the hunger, it saw what a sun would steal from them.

Here, in the roaring gray damp, a mother sat on a stone plinth with a shard of split shale, rubbing it hour after hour against wet hide and silt until its edges rounded into something soft enough to press against a child’s cheek. That gesture took years. It was a measure of love precisely because the world around them was sharp enough to draw blood at a careless touch. If the power softened the basalt into soil, it would unmake the meaning of every smooth stone in the city.

It looked at an elder squatting by a drying rack—wholly deafened by eighty years of knapping and frog-song, his skin mottled like wet slate, his thick fingers flashing in the dim light as he read a cousin’s intent from the slightest twitch of a shoulder across the terrace. In a quiet, sunlit world, he would have been an old man left behind by speech. Here, he was one of the sharpest voices in the basin.

The power lowered its hand. The wind did not blow.

To interfere here—even with mercy—would be to strike the basin like a hammer strikes a basalt core: leaving a clean, bright, terrible edge where there had only been a weave. A miracle, the power realized, is just another sharp thing in a world that spends its whole life trying to rub edges down.

So it chose restraint.

Yet it did not turn away entirely. Instead, it sank its attention down beneath the raw, unweathered shelves of rock, past the chinked foundations and the warm seepage flats, into the deep mineral plumbing of the valley. It listened to the slow, subterranean heat that kept the air saturated, and the steady, hidden springs that fed the pale mud without ever flooding the causeways. It checked the seam of that equilibrium the way a careful builder checks a lintel—not to alter it, not to improve it, only to make sure the balance would not slip by accident and starve the frogs or drown the roofs.

It touched nothing the people could see. It left the midges hanging in their stagnant columns, and the crabs waiting in the dark chinks for a careless hand, and the blind giants wallowing out in the mist-choked sumps.

Then, before withdrawing, it lingered for a little while on one of the lower terraces, invisible in the wet heat, simply watching.

A low doorway opened. A woman crawled out from the muffled quiet of the sleeping-room, blinking into the perpetual dusk, her shoulders still marked with the weave of the sinew mat. Without a word, she walked to where her brother squatted beneath a hide awning. He handed her the stone mallet—warm from his grip, its handle worn smooth by three generations of palms—and touched two knuckles to his jaw. She flicked her fingers near her ear in answer, took his place on the plinth, and struck a fresh flake from the basalt core.

*Clack.*

The sound bounced off the wet wall, stacked itself onto the roar of a million throats in the channel below, and vanished into the warm, humming dark.

The power smiled—a quiet, unhurried thing—and left them to their peace.
