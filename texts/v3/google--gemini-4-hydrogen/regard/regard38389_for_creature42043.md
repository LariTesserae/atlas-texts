The attention did not arrive as a beam of light. The chamber already had more light than it could spend.

It came instead as a quietness *around* the noise—a vast, patient awareness filtering down through forty meters of unbroken basalt, drawn by the oddity of a room that refused to go dark.

At first, the power looked upon the vault with the sharp ache of simple pity. It saw a sealed stone box, cramped and scalding, where a forgotten engine of white-hot geometry tore the air apart every few seconds. It saw four or five eyeless creatures welded to the walls by sheer necessity, battered by sideways waves, blistering in a glare so absolute that the only shadow left in the world was the narrow overhang of their own skulls.

The first impulse of kindness was to break the roof.

It would have been so easy. A gentle parting of the basalt to let in the cool, star-pricked night; a quiet hand laid upon the shifting lattice to still its filaments and cool its fever; a widening of the basin so the surf could settle into a calm, dark tide-pool. The creatures could have been given soft silt to rest their pale bellies upon, and quiet water for their eggs, and eyes to see the sky.

But wisdom is slower than pity, and it waits to see what a thing is before it tries to mend it.

So the power held still, and listened.

It matched its perception to the cavern’s teeth-vibrating thrum. It watched the lattice fold through an impossible angle, and felt the pitch of the room drop by a fraction of a tone—so slight a change that only something made of stone and hollow bone could hear it. It watched a sheet of water tear upward from the floor, hanging like shattered glass, and saw a dark plate detach from the scalloped wall.

In that two-second pocket of dead calm, the power saw grace.

It saw the fluid, effortless glide of dozens of pale feet across the bared slate. It saw the delicate sensory frills unfurl into the private shadow beneath the wedge-shaped brow, tasting the heat and pressure of the world with exquisite precision. It saw the creature flatten into the polished channel just as the water collapsed, shedding the blow of a breaking wave off its curved back as cleanly as a cliff sheds rain.

And then the power understood what its mercy would have done.

If it stilled the lattice, the warm, mineral-heavy spray would vanish, and the white young hiding high among the mosses would never grow their roofs of stone. If it cooled the bathhouse air, the slow, steady metabolism of the room would gutter out. If it silenced the roar and the low, physical thrumming, these beings would not find peace—they would go deaf, and mute, and lost, cut off from the only voice they knew. To give them eyes under a real sky would be to burn away the only darkness they had ever owned: the small, safe shadow they carried beneath their own brows.

They were not prisoners of the storm. They were its dancers. The lattice, the water, the slate, and the armored things in the flutes had spent centuries carving one another into a single, unbroken instrument.

To "save" them from the chamber would be to unmake them.

Yet the power did not turn away entirely. Wisdom sees the difference between a harsh home and a fragile one, and there were things here that no creature with mineral stones in its skull could ever sense or mend.

Far above the vault, in the blind, silent miles of dark earth, the rock was not as still as it seemed. Slowly, over millennia, the weight of the continent was shifting along an ancient hairline seam. In another thousand years—or ten thousand—the basalt shelf would groan, crack, and drop the dark ceiling straight into the blinding noon.

And at the center of the room, twelve meters above the basin, the white-hot lattice was old. Whoever had hung it there had built it to last, yet its invisible anchors were drifting by the width of a thought, century by century. Left entirely alone, its rhythm would one day stutter—not stopping cleanly, but snagging into a chaotic seizure that would boil the basin dry in an afternoon.

The power reached out, careful not to brush the air inside the chamber, careful not to stir a single drop of mist or ripple the fluid in a single skull.

Outside the walls, deep within the crushing dark of the basalt, it wove a quiet steadiness into the stone. It eased the creeping tension of the fault, setting the arch of the vault at peace so the roof would hold its shape against the weight of the world.

Then, delicately, it traced the geometry of the core. It did not dim the light. It did not soften the gales. It did not smooth away the stray steam-cracks that pitted the old patriarch’s shell, for those scars were the shape of his long life, and his dignity. It only trued the ancient tolerances by a hair’s breadth, mending the slow drift in the machinery so that the song would not sour, and the two-second lulls would keep their exact, faithful measure.

Inside the room, nothing changed.

Up on the glass-smooth stub of the broken walkway, the massive patriarch hung half-off in mid-air, letting the upward rain wash over his scarred back. Around the matte-grey pylons, the water sheeted away from the bone-dry halos where the translucent egg-sacs rested in blinding peace. High in the diffused mist, white hatchlings grazed on pale-green moss, waiting for the air to build them armor.

Down in the polished channels, during a brief, roaring lull, two adults crossed paths. They halted, pressed their broad, mineral brow-plates together, and let the cavern’s endless thrumming pass from one skull to the next—sharing the rhythm of a storm that would keep its promise, held safe in the dark, for a very long time to come.

And the power withdrew, leaving behind only a silent watch—a promise that when the distant age arrived in which even this deep equilibrium must finally end, it would not end in a sudden, senseless dark.
