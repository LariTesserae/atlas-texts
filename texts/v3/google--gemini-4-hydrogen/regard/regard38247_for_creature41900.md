It was not a cry that drew the power’s attention—nothing like a cry could survive the concussive roar of that drowned nave—but a discrepancy. High above the timberline, where the world ought to have been clean stone and thin, freezing air, a small seam of reality had frayed. Gravity was pooling in the wrong corners. Time was stuttering along a fracture that could not finish breaking. And inside that roaring knot of broken physics, something small and stubborn was stacking stones.

The power leaned close, looking down through the bruised ceiling where storm-cloud and water churned without a surface.

At first, benevolence moved faster than wisdom.

Seeing the razor-edged obsidian, the scalding silt, the dim amber murk, and the scarred, ash-white creature clinging to a wedged bone like a climber in a vertical gale, the power’s first impulse was to mend. It gathered a gentle, quiet will—enough to seal the geothermal cracks, to still the shearing currents into a clear mountain tarn, to round the lethal glass into river-smooth stone. Enough to part the taut, unbroken skin over the creature’s face and give it eyes to see the sun, and to lift it out of the deafening dark into the clean air above.

Before that mercy could touch the water, wisdom asked it to wait. To look not from the sky, but from the floor.

So the power lowered its attention into the boiling murk, past the lagging shadows and the screaming pockets of trapped air, and pressed itself against the obsidian as the creature did—listening past the water’s chaos to the deeper groans of the mountain’s crown.

It felt the creature’s heavy, ballast-dense bones vibrating like tuning forks in the thunder. It understood, all at once, what mercy would have destroyed.

Still the currents, and the creature would sink like lead to the rubble, deafened by silence, unable to ride the updrafts to the surface it had never seen. Seal the vents, and the high-altitude cold would lock this warm room in ice within an hour. Give it eyes, and the lying, murky light and detached shadows would bewilder a mind built to read truth through its sternum. Smooth the obsidian, and its leathery palms, its bone-ladders, and the half-moon cradle it had spent years grinding with a broken shell would lose their meaning. Lift it out into the open air, and its heavy frame would be a prison on dry land, with nothing left to tend.

The creature was not a victim trapped in a wound. It was the wound’s caretaker, and the wound was its home.

So the power drew back its healing hand, leaving the glass sharp and the water violent.

Yet it lingered a little longer in the copper-lit dark, watching as the blind climber unhooked itself from a bone-grip, intercepted a boulder-sized bubble of storm-air before the currents could tear it apart, and rode the scalding backwash down to a quiet eddy beneath an overhang. There, with slow, meticulous respect, the climber balanced another razor-edged shard of basalt onto a trembling cairn, listening with its skeleton to the hum of the current until the pebbles around the peak settled into their slow, obedient orbit.

Then the climber clung to the wall, lifted a smooth stone, and struck the obsidian twice.

*Tap-tap.*

The sound raced cleanly through the solid glass of the mountain’s crown, bypassing the deafening water. And the climber pressed its chin to the stone, waiting.

From the far side of the sunken nave, a moment later, a faint *tap-tap* answered back—either another climber in its own half-moon hollow, or only the mountain stuttering its own knock back late from a fracture that had not yet finished breaking.

Once more, the power felt the ache of benevolence: the urge to reach into the stone and send back a clear, unmistakable *tap-tap* of its own—a warm, un-stuttered greeting across the dark, so the climber might know for certain that it was heard.

And once more, wisdom stayed its hand. A power that could not stay as a neighbor would not pretend to be one, nor leave a solitary keeper waiting against the stone for a voice that would not come again.

In the end, the power did almost nothing.

It did not calm the storm. It did not clear the silt. It did not touch the climber’s scarred hide or mend the fraying gravity. Only, deep beneath the talus—far below the vents, in the unseen root of the mountain where the heat was born—it laid a quiet, imperceptible steadiness upon the stone, ensuring only that the fissure would not go cold while the climber still lived to tend it.

Then it withdrew into the thin air above the timberline, leaving the drowned room intact: warm, deafening, unbroken in its breaking, and faithfully kept.
