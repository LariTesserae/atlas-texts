When the attention arrived, it did not come as light.

A lesser power might have sent a beam of gold down through the jagged slot in the roof, thinking to warm the marrow-deep chill of the limestone. A merely generous one might have parted the cloud-bank pressed against the mountain’s flank, just for an hour, to show the dwellers what a sky looked like.

But this power was old enough to know that light can be a blade, and it was wise enough to look before it touched.

So it entered the hollow the only way anything ever entered it: like mist seeping through a crack. It had no weight to press upon the moss, no heat to stir the air, no voice to trouble the wet stone. It simply became present in the bruised-indigo gloom, narrowing its vastness until it could fit inside a space no wider than a small nave, and slowing its thought until it matched the *plip, plip* of the ceiling.

For a long time, it only listened.

It felt the mild, damp warmth rising an inch above the shelves—the shared, banked-coal heat of a hundred slow hearts pressed shoulder to hip. It watched a dweller’s long, pale-palmed hand lift a rib-bone pick lashed with fern-fiber and part the silver-grey coat of its neighbor, teasing out a bead of lime-water with infinite, unhurried care. It saw the pale salamander wedged behind a folded knee, unbothered and unbothering. It watched the feathery-gilled sleepers hanging like smoke in the mirror-still pools, and the newborn curled inside the curtain of its mother’s chest, learning the shape of the world by the rise and fall of her ribs.

And as the power watched, it felt the ancient, aching reflex of benevolence: the urge to *mend*, to *add*, to *give*.

It held its own gifts in its mind and weighed each one against the hollow.

*I could give them room,* the power thought. *I could widen the limestone throat, carve them dry alcoves, give each one a space of its own.*
And at once it saw how the chill of the stone would rush into the gaps between their bodies. It saw them shivering in the sudden, terrible distance between one shoulder and another, no longer knowing by touch alone whether their neighbor still breathed.

*I could give them abundance,* it thought. *I could warm the water, quicken the moss, multiply the snails until no one ever waited for hunger.*
And it saw their slow hearts begin to race, their metabolisms flaring like dry brush, outstripping the glacial patience of the stone. It saw the pools cloud with silt, the shelves overflow, the delicate equation of drip and seepage breaking apart into rot and scarcity.

*I could give them a voice,* it thought. *Names. Stories. Memory of the mountain beyond the ridge.*
And it heard how even a single spoken word would ricochet off the wet flutes like a falling stone—a violence of sound shattering the grammar of weight and pressure, teaching them to want what could not fit inside the well.

The power stopped. It looked again at the rib-bone pick in the dweller’s wrinkled fingers. It understood what it was seeing: not a prison, not a poverty, not a people waiting to be rescued from the dark. It was a finished thing. A chord held so cleanly that any added note would only make it flat.

Nothing here was wasted. No one here was alone. Even the dead did not leave; they only sank into the moss until they were clean enough to groom the living.

So the power folded its gifts back into itself, untouched.

It did not widen the slot. It did not warm the stone. It did not wake the sleepers in the pool or whisper a single name into the dark.

Only once, as it began to withdraw—rising slowly up through the pewter-grey dilution of the mist, careful not to leave a wake or stir a single frond of the translucent ferns—did it pause on the outer flank of the ridge, where the wind and frost were forever chewing at the mountain’s teeth. There, high above the overhang, a heavy slab of limestone had worked loose over centuries of ice, leaning quietly toward the crease that hid the hollow.

With no more force than a root shifting in deep soil, the power eased the slab’s balance a fraction of an inch the other way, so that when it finally fell, ten winters or a hundred from now, it would thunder down the outer scree and leave the brow intact.

Then it let go.

Down in the bruised-indigo gloom, a fat drop of water gathered on the underside of a stone lip and let go—*plip*—into the pool below. On the shelf, a dweller shifted its hip an inch to make room for a neighbor’s elbow, and laid one long, damp palm flat against a breathing spine.

Neither of them ever knew they had been seen. Which, the power knew, was the kindest thing of all.
