If the power were truly wise, it would not begin by speaking. A voice from the sky would shatter the acoustic law of the basin, bouncing off the four-story slate in a terrifying clutter of echoes, drowning out the tipping-troughs and leaving every household scrambling to check its gutters.

So it would begin by listening.

It would learn the town the way the townspeople do: by cadence. It would hear the soft, continuous hiss of springwater sheeting over glazed roofs, the dull *clack* and dry knock of the counterweighted troughs, the *shhhk-shhhk* of copper-edged paddles lifting brittle needles from the settling-pans. It would look into the dark cisterns and see the blind *clear-backs* grazing the bacterial film off the stone. It would follow the chalky bloom in the creases of broad, flat-nailed hands, the slight crouch of knees on wet stairs, the quiet economy of two people passing on a shoulder-wide landing without a word. And it would climb to the dry attic-boxes beneath the rim, where the elders sit wrapped in oiled hides, braiding hampers while the dampness rattles in their chests.

A merely powerful force, moved by simple pity, might be tempted to rescue them. It might push back the jagged cliffs to let the noon sun flood the lower tiers, dry out the weeping seams, lift the basin above the marsh, and lay down dark soil where wire-grass and sedge could grow into grain.

Wisdom would stay its hand.

To widen the cleft would be to blind a people who squint at the open sky and feel their voices go dangerously exposed in the wind. To stop the springs would kill the *clear-backs*, choke the mineral harvest that buys their eels and tubers and copper, and turn their intricate hydraulic engine into a silent ruin of dry-stacked stone. A rescue that erases the world a people have shaped themselves to fit is only another kind of destruction.

And yet, benevolence does not look away from needless pain. The power would see the difference between the rigor of a chosen life and the slow attrition of the body—the peat-smoke thickening in low rooms, the cold creeping into the bone, the young, unforgiving rock that could shift in a single night and bring four stories of mortarless slate down into the cisterns.

So if it acted at all, it would work at the same scale as the mineral glaze: a fraction of a millimeter at a time, leaving no seam or signature for anyone to worship or fear.

Deep beneath the lowest runnels, where the slate is still raw and restless from whatever uplift opened the hollow, it might lay a quiet stillness into the fault-lines. Not smoothing the sharp cleavage planes the builders rely on for pegs and wedges, nor sealing the horizontal seams that feed the springs—only knitting the deepest fractures together, so the basin would hold steady against the slow settling of the marsh and the sudden shudder of the earth.

In the water itself, it might alter nothing the eye could measure. The *clear-backs* would still need their cold dark; the mineral would still crust the pans in brittle, profitable needles. Perhaps only the sharpest edge of the salt’s bite on living skin would ease, so the cuticles of the scrapers cracked less often in the winter mist.

And in the sleeping-platforms slung above the runnels, and the tiny attic-boxes wedged under the upper rim, it might coax a faint, dry warmth up through the stone—not enough to heat the air of the basin or disturb the chill of the cisterns, only enough to take the wet edge off the slate where bodies rest. With less need for smoldering peat in the braziers, the earthy haze in the low rooms would thin. The elders’ knees would still stiffen with age, and the dampness of the lowlands would remain what it is, but the wet, rattling weight in their lungs might loosen its grip, giving them a few more quiet seasons to knot rawhide and carve bone scrapers while listening to the town below.

Then the power would withdraw.

In the morning, the mist would hang over the cleft as it always had. The scrapers would climb the stairs with hampers strapped to their backs, soft-soled feet finding the familiar hollows in the glazed stone. A tipping-trough would fill, nod forward with a dull *clack*, and swing back into place. Nobody would look up at the rim, because nothing had broken the rhythm—only, here and there, a sleeping shelf would feel a little drier against a tired back, and the stone beneath their feet would hold a little longer than it otherwise might have.
