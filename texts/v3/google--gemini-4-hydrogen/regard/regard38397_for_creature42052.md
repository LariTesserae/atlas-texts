It would not arrive with light.

A power that is only benevolent might have brought a lantern, or parted the greyness above the rim to let the sun pour down onto the wet shale. It might have seen the stooped backs, the milky, half-shut eyes, the raw, tallow-pale skin scraped against sharp masonry, and felt a sharp, urgent pity. It might have sent rain to fill the sumps, warmth to dry the cellar-chill out of the wool, and a strong, enduring mortar to keep the walls from ever sliding down again.

And in a single afternoon, it would have destroyed them.

A sun would have baked the ash-brick dry and silenced the runnels. Rain would have drowned the culverts, choked the sump-dwellers with silt, and washed the unmortared cairns into shapeless heaps. Clear sight would have made the dark an enemy instead of a medium, and permanent walls would have turned a living, breathing instrument into a dead monument.

Because it is wise as well as kind, the power does nothing at first. It simply stops at the rim of the valley and listens.

It listens for a long time—not in days, which mean nothing where there is no sun, but in the slow cycle of green cedar warping and shale settling. It learns the grammar of the *clack-tip-splash-boom*. It follows a single bead of condensation from the cold air onto the underside of a lintel, down a chisel-cut runnel, into a tipping-trough, and hears a mason three hundred paces away tilt their head at the sound, satisfied that the tier is sweating as it should.

It watches a mason kneel at a brimming sump and tap three times on the cobble lip. It watches the broad, silt-colored head surface with a wet *slurp*, nudge the grease-stained fingers, and dive back into the dark culvert. It watches a mason lift a blind, hound-sized crawler by its leathery scruff and set it gently three stones over from a bed of tacky mortar, neither creature startling at the touch.

And slowly, the power understands. This is not a ruin. These are not prisoners. The valley is a song played by ear in the dark, and the masons are not suffering the noise—they are *inside* it, as a lung is inside a chest. To "fix" the valley would be to cut the strings of a harp so they would never go out of tune again.

So the power withholds its grander mercies. It leaves the dark bruised and grey. It leaves the air cold enough to settle in the bone. It leaves the walls raw, jagged, and unfinished.

Instead, it offers only the smallest, quietest courtesies—things so native to the valley’s own logic that no one who lives there will ever recognize them as a miracle.

Up along the high, unseen rim, the power gently bends the upper weather. If a scorching summer wind or a sudden, torrential storm sweeps across the outer world, it is turned aside before it can spill over the lip of the basin. The air that drops into the tiers remains steady, cool, and cellar-damp, never parched and never flooded.

Down in the crowded dark, it does not stop the walls from falling—because the masons need the rhythm of sorting rubble, and the fresh, weeping faces of newly split stone. But when a green timber strut is overloaded and about to snap, the power lets the wood *sing* its warning a few heartbeats sooner: a slow, unmistakable *groan-creak* that travels through the stone and gives a crouching mason time to roll clear before the slate comes down.

It does not make the water plentiful, which would make the tipping-troughs redundant. It merely lets the cold air carry a fraction more moisture in the leanest seasons—just enough that the lowest sumps never quite crust over with salt, and the grey fungus on the hemp-beds stays thick and velvety. It lets the mineral in the water be a little kinder to old joints, and the shed belly-plates of the wall-crawlers cure a little tougher against the sharp shale.

And once—only once—it leaves something behind.

Not a crown, not a book, not a source of fire. Simply, in an unquarried pocket near the middle tiers, a seam of dark, fine-grained shale that splits cleanly under the chisel, and rings, when struck by a wooden hammer, with a note so clear and true that it can be heard three terraces away through the thickest mist of noise.

The masons find it by touch, as they find everything. They do not kneel to it or wonder where it came from. One of them simply trims a wedge from it, lashes it above a tipping-trough with a length of hemp, and waits for the water to fill.

*Tip. Splash. Boom.*

The new note settles into the valley’s hum—clean, steady, and wet—and somewhere up in the cold, bruised greyness beyond the rim, the power listens for a moment longer, nods once in the dark, and leaves them to their work.
