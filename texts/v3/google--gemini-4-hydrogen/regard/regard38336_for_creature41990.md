The first thing a benevolent and wise power would do is **stay its hand**.

A merely kind power—one moved by pity without patience—would look at the plateau and see only deprivation. It would see ribs pressed against narrow stone, salt crusted beneath dark-lidded eyes, lungs hoarding a single breath for a full minute, and a sun that punishes anything that stands upright. It would be tempted to call down a cool, soaking rain; to soften the travertine into soil; to widen the fissures into rooms, or fill the silent air with easy abundance.

And in doing so, it would unmake the people of the plateau.

So the wise power watches first. It watches for a full turning of the seasons, from the white-gold glare of summer to the bitter blue hours of winter, until it understands the grammar of the basin. It sees how the beings fold themselves into the basalt alongside the slate-hued lizards, not as prisoners of the dark, but as sleepers in a cradle built to their exact measure. It watches them plaster the broken insect hives with iron-rich mud so the larvae survive to rebuild. It listens to their palms against the guy-wires, tapping out clipped, dry news across three ridges without spending a breath.

It recognizes what a hasty savior would miss: *they are not enduring the plateau. They are its expression.* Every adaptation—the chalky dust on their shoulders, the heat-dumping veins along their bellies, the quiet precision of their hands—is the shape of a long, unbroken agreement with the rock. To make the plateau easy would be to turn their strength into a vestige, and their silence into an emptiness.

So the power leaves the sun hot. It leaves the air thin. It leaves the water hidden in the seams where only patience can find it.

Then, having chosen restraint on the plateau, it turns its attention to the one thing the inhabitants cannot read, and cannot adapt to in time: the taut lines disappearing over the saw-toothed ridges.

It follows the power lines and the buried conduits out of the basin, down to the unseen, distant grid—to the cities and offices and restless minds that built the geothermal cylinders and the condenser-towers and then forgot them. There, it sees the true danger. Not the heat, and not the salt, but *attention*. One day, a distant engineer might notice a slight irregularity in a metering gauge. A planner might look at a satellite map and decide the basin has room for a hundred more towers, a gravel road, a crew with bright lights and loud engines.

Here, the power acts—quietly, invisibly, in the only language the distant grid understands.

It lays a gentle, persistent stillness over the paperwork. In the distant databases, the plateau’s geothermal yield reads as *marginal, corrosive, barely worth the copper*. Maintenance requests for the relay sheds are subtly deprioritized, lost in layers of quiet redundancy. Whenever a surveyor’s eye drifts toward the basin, the numbers nudge them elsewhere—toward easier valleys, richer seams, flatter ground. The power wraps the plateau in the strongest armor a modern world can offer: the illusion of being unprofitable to disturb.

On the plateau itself, its touch is even lighter—so small it could always be mistaken for luck, or geology.

It does not build new cisterns. Instead, it allows a single rubber gasket at the base of an aging condenser-tower to weep—a hairline flaw, too minuscule for any distant sensor to flag as a failure, yet enough to keep one shaded, mineral-rich puddle from drying out during a harsh decade. Deep beneath the blistered rhyolite, it nudges a shifting fault by the width of a finger, keeping a failing steam-seam open for another thousand years. And when the bitterest pre-dawn cold spills over the ridges, the transformer boxes hum just a fraction of a degree warmer against the bellies pressed to their sides, without ever tripping a fuse.

It never speaks to them. It never shows a face in the heat-haze or carves a sign into the basalt. To announce itself would be to break the silence that keeps them whole, and to ask for gratitude would be to invent a debt they never owed.

Instead, when the blue hour arrives and the first dampness clings to the leeward cracks, the inhabitants uncoil from the stone as they always have. They climb the skeletal towers, brush the salt from the copper mesh, drink from the dark seams, and tap their news along the humming wires—believing, rightly, that the world is harsh, and steady, and theirs.
