If a power that was both benevolent and wise came upon this basin, the first thing it would do is slow down.

It would not arrive in a burst of golden light, for too much light here would burn the dark-blooded kelp and bleach the velvet pelts of sea-moss into ash. It would not speak, for in water this heavy and this still, a voice—even a gentle one—would strike the hanging dwellers like a landslide, tearing their taut guy-lines from the walls and shattering the mercury-mirrors of gas beneath their vaults.

Instead, the power would stanch its own speed. It would let go of the tempo of stars and cities and heartbeats, and match itself to the glacial creep of the continental current. It would wait a week just to watch a single kelp frond bow and straighten. It would wait a year to measure the breadth of a hair added to a coralline crust.

Only then, when it had learned the grammar of *shush* and *tock* and *blup-blup*, would it look at what was here.

At first, a lesser kindness might be tempted to grieve.

Looking at the worn hollows in the flagstones, a merely pitying power might remember the hands that swung the bronze chisels, the bare feet that polished the limestone, the voices that once echoed in the dry air of these courtyards before the water came. It might feel the urge to raise the basin back into the sun, to drain the pewter dark and let the wind dry the slate roofs once more. Or, if it left the water, it might pity the dwellers—blind, mouthless, suspended alone in the pitch-black cisterns—and wish to give them eyes to see the iron-red gardens, or minds to wonder at the ancient walls, or bright, darting fish to keep them company in the silence.

But wisdom sees clearly what is, rather than what was.

To drain the basin would be to murder the only thing that still keeps faith with these stones. To bring back the sun would turn the hanging lanterns of translucent silk into dry, blackened husks on the floor of a dead desert. To introduce fish—even small, harmless ones—would be to introduce teeth, hunger, and panic to a skin that has spent millennia listening only to the drift of silt and the settling of pebbles. And to give the dwellers minds like ours would be to invent loneliness in a world that currently knows only patience.

So the wise power would stay its hand from all of those things. It would recognize that this place is not a ruin waiting to be rescued; it is a work that has finished becoming what it was meant to be. The builders made the stone to keep the world out, and the water turned their walls into a cradle for something gentler than they could ever have imagined. Even the erased boundary steles are not truly blank—the dwellers’ digestive secretions have written a slow, branching script across them, a quiet translation of the old labor into the new.

What, then, would the power do?

Very little that could be seen from the flagstones.

Far above, where the bruised slate-blue light is born, it would set a quiet boundary—nothing as crude as a wall, just a subtle bend in the currents of the upper world. It would turn aside the warm, restless eddies that might otherwise wander down into the lowland and rot the cold-tuned kelp. It would guide the shoals of hungry, bright-eyed things along other coasts, so that no grazing mouth or scuttling claw ever stumbled down into the pewter dark to discover how soft and defenseless the hanging dwellers are. It would keep the ceiling of the world unbroken, so that the rules of the basin—*cold preserves, darkness slows, and water levels all things*—might hold for another thousand years, and another after that.

And down below, in the drowned granaries and the velvet-lined alleys, it would offer only one thing more: its attention.

In a universe full of noise and haste, the power would linger here for the span of a century, simply to bear witness. It would watch a single, hearth-wide dweller grow heavy with salvaged red pigment and dissolved limestone. It would feel, alongside the creature, the slow shift of the outer current leaning the kelp forests in unison. And when the day finally came—quietly, without ceremony—for the dweller to pinch off a fist-sized bladder from its flank, fold a single pearl of ceiling-gas inside, and weight it with grit, the power would follow that small, pale lantern as it slipped out from under the arch.

It would drift beside the bladder through the iron-red curtains, past the slumped lintels and the sunken causeways, asking nothing of it, changing nothing about its course. When the cold water at last dissolved the trapped gas, and the bladder sank into some distant, empty courtyard to split open in the silt, the power would watch the first tallow-colored thread creep out into the hollow of a flagstone worn smooth by forgotten feet.

And seeing that the thread was whole, and patient, and ready to wait for the first dead frond to fall, the benevolent power would withdraw—leaving not a ripple behind it, only the quiet assurance that the dark would remain dark, and the still water would remain still.
