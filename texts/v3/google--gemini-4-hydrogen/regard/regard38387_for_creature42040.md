At first, the power thought of rain.

It is an old reflex, among those who watch over worlds from the high, quiet places: to see a dry thing and wish to soften it; to see a dark thing and wish to part the clouds; to see a creature bent low against a gale and wish to lay a palm across the sky and hush the wind to a sigh.

Looking down through the bruised twilight of the peak, the power saw a hundred paces of ironstone locked in a perpetual, screaming eddy. It saw eight small, hunched figures wrapped in skin like cured vellum, chewing the dry pith of dead reeds so their gums would not split, tethered to the rock by cords of copper-colored grass so the very air would not strike them numb. It saw the slate domes crouched like beetles in the eastern alcoves, and the taut string of bone and crab-shell chattering its frantic warning at the door.

*How hard,* the power thought, and reached toward the ceiling of slate-gray dust, gathering the memory of a gentle spring, of quiet mornings, of soft green moss.

Then, before its hand touched the clouds, it paused to listen.

Not into the air—the air here tore sound to shreds, leaving only a meaningless, battering roar. Instead, the power lowered its attention into the basalt rim, down through the fluted ribs of the stone, until it felt the deep, steady thrumming of the mountain’s organs.

And there, in the rock, it found them.

It heard what the dwellers heard: not chaos, but a vast, intricate loom. It felt the bass note of the western rim drop an octave, and felt, through the soles of eight splayed feet, the immediate, wordless answering hum in the bones of the dwellers’ shins. It watched the elder emerge from the eastern dome—not cowering, not enduring, but *reading* the storm with her knuckles pressed to the floor. It watched her snap a tiny, pale-blue spark from her thumb to wake a companion across the gloom, a language made of lightning and dust.

Inside the low slate dome, the power watched them sit in the dark, foreheads and knees pressed together, sharing a quiet, rhythmic clicking through their teeth—a warmth that needed no fire, a closeness that needed no open sky.

And when the bellows-blooms swelled and the golden storm broke across the ironstone, the power saw them unfold from the alcoves, spread their knotted nets, and lean forward into the gale until the wind lifted them just enough to run.

The power drew its hand back from the clouds.

It understood, then, what its pity would have done.

A single week of rain would have rotted the cured-vellum skin off their bones, drowned the bellows-blooms in mud, and shorted the living lightning out of the wire-grass. A calm sky would have blinded their smoked-horn eyes with raw sun and starved the skimmers out of the thermals. Silence would have severed them from one another, leaving them deaf in a world where only the roar made sense.

To mend this place by the measure of softer worlds would be to unmake it. They were not prisoners of a broken machine; they were the notes of an instrument that could only play at this pressure, at this dryness, at this speed.

So the power withheld the rain. It withheld the sun. It withheld the silence.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and love still looks for a way to lay its hand upon what it loves without bruising it.

Far above the jagged rim, in the blind, indifferent upper air where the great winter cyclones grind across the peaks, the power quietly set its shoulder against the sky. It did not stop the wind that fed the bowl. It only turned aside the killing storms—the rare, monstrous tempests that come once in a thousand years to scour a mountain bare of stone and memory alike. It became, for that hundred paces of basalt, the unseen lee of a greater boulder.

And deep beneath the floor, where the geothermal fissures breathed their steady, dry heat, the power laid a single, patient finger against the cooling rock, keeping the warmth even, so the bellows-blooms would not wither in their sleep for a long, long time.

Down in the eastern alcove, the storm was beginning to ease back toward its usual, tireless roar. The elder knelt by her flat stone, her grounding cord hissing softly behind her, and pressed her palm to the fluted wall to listen for the next shift in the wind.

For the space of a single breath, beneath the familiar bass of the organ-pipes and the high, frantic chatter of the reeds, she felt something else in the basalt. A stillness inside the vibration. A warmth in the deep stone that had no edge to it, like the feeling of a forehead pressed gently against her own in the dark.

She tilted her head, listening with the resonant bone of her jaw. The feeling did not speak, and it did not linger. Soon it was only the ironstone again, humming its old, fierce song.

The elder clicked her teeth softly in answer, gathered her half-finished basket of wire-grass, and went back to her work.
