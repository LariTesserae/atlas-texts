It did not come down through the oculus like sunlight, nor split the dome with a voice. Either would have scattered the swifts, blinded the milky-slate eyes along the upper step, and broken the standing lattice on the water.

Instead, the power arrived the way the seepage arrived: through the pores of the brick, patient and cool, listening first through the stone before it ever looked.

It had been drawn by the sound—not the high, sharp *tink-tink-tink* of the birds, which stayed inside the vault, but the deeper note underneath it. Traveling outward through the wet limestone and the roots of the earth, the booming of a thousand clay jars carried like a slow, steady pulse in the dark. A living thing, buried and humming.

So it gathered its attention in the empty bowl of the rotunda, careful not to displace a single curtain of mist.

It took the measure of the room as the room itself took measure: eighty paces of thumb-pressed brick, the mineral ribs bleeding white down the ochre curve, the ankle-deep mirror of black water turning slowly toward the central bone-grate. It watched the blind amphibians inflate their translucent throats in staggered waves, trailing silver mold, and felt how each note struck the water and wove itself into a shifting geometry of ripples. It watched the eels spiral. It watched a swift drop like a thrown slate, skim the black sheet, and vanish upward into the damp.

And then, after a long moment of stillness, it saw the two humps against the outermost step breathe.

The power looked at them for a long time. It saw the elder, whose shoulders and brow were ridged with brittle salt like old mortar, and the younger one, crouched a few paces away with its chin tucked to its collarbone. It felt the deep percussion traveling up through their paddle-flat feet, humming in the hollows of their chests, vibrating in their teeth. It understood, all at once, that they were not prisoners in a cistern, nor castaways in the dark. They were the room’s hands.

Yet benevolence, by its nature, aches when it looks at effort and age.

The power felt the stiffness in the elder’s salt-crusted knuckles. It saw the raw edges where the braided water-mold belt chafed wet skin, and it knew that within a few turns of the seasons, the elder’s ribs would be cleaned by the eels and lashed into the central grate with swift-sinew.

For a breath, it considered what a gentle hand might do.

It could widen the slatted oculus and let in a shaft of warm, golden afternoon—enough to dry the chill from their backs and turn the mirror-black water blue.
*No,* wisdom answered. *Warmth would bake the liverwort, parch the mist-drinking mold, and crack the lime-scale seals around the jars. And the light would burn their eyes.*

It could quiet the deafening, relentless roar of the drums, giving them a pocket of soft silence where they might rest their bones and hear each other’s breath.
*No. To silence the jars is to blind them. They do not hear the roar as noise; they read it as the health of the world. A quiet room would feel to them like a dead one.*

It could mend the central grate with something that would never rot—bronze, perhaps, or stone fused seamless—so their knees need never kneel in the cold deepest water to re-lash the bones. It could leave them fire, or woven cloth, or a spring of sweeter food.
*No. Fire would choke the swifts. Metal would bleed into the water and poison the eels. And if the grate never clogged and the jars never loosened, what would their hands do? To take away the tending would be to hollow them out, leaving them nothing to be but ghosts waiting in a machine that no longer needed them.*

Even a small, secret mercy—a touch to un-stiffen the elder’s joints, a sudden smoothing of the worn slate—would alter the weight of a footstep, the rhythm of a crouch, the quiet apprenticeship of the younger one watching how an elder endures the damp.

So the power held its hand completely still inside the rotunda. It did not touch a single brick, or steady a single loose jar, or lift a single drowned midge from the notched stone.

Instead, it turned its attention outward, beyond the wet liver-colored walls, to the vast, indifferent earth that the dwellers could not reach with swift-bone and eel-skin.

High above the corbeled crown, where a great surface oak had sent a thick, exploratory root nosing into a seam of crushed-shell mortar, the power laid a quiet suggestion against the wood. The root turned aside by the width of a hand, seeking softer loam elsewhere, leaving the dome intact.

Deep beneath the floor, in the dark limestone veins that fed the weeping brick, it steadied the water table—not raising it to drown the steps, not letting it dwindle in the dry decades to come, only holding the ancient pressure even, drop for drop, as it had been for centuries.

And around the sunken, slatted oculus up in the surface air, it built no wall and cast no spell that would trouble a swift’s wing or a midge’s flight. It only folded a quietness of notice over the mossy stones, so that the eyes of passing things—wanderers, quarrymen, builders of roads—would slide across the hollow without curiosity and walk on.

Inside the rotunda, nothing changed.

After a time, the elder unfolded from the wall with slow, liquid economy and waded down the concentric steps, broad toes feeling for the rounded slate, sliding between the swirling eels. It knelt at the central grate, drew a notched stone from its belt of water-mold, and began to clear a fresh mat of drowned midges from the bone.

The clay jars boomed in rolling, staggered waves. The standing ripples held their crisp, unbroken geometry across the black water.

The power lingered only a moment longer—not to mend, not to improve, only to witness something whole—and then withdrew into the dark behind the brick, leaving the old balance undisturbed.
