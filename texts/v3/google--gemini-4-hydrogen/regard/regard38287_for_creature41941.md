At first, the power’s impulse was to mend.

It had come upon the pocket by chance—if anything like chance existed for an awareness that moved through strata the way light moves through water. Out of the vast, slow architecture of the crust, a single root-cellar’s worth of hollow limestone had caught its attention, singing with a fierce, enclosed vibration.

Looking in, the power saw what any vast and sunlit thing sees at first glance: cramped ceilings, bone-cold damp, a roar loud enough to split a skull, and small, crooked beings crawling in the dark over their own dead.

For a breath—a span long enough for a stalactite to thicken by the width of a hair—the power considered what it could give them.

It could part the ceiling and let down a shaft of real sun, warm as summer noon. It could hush the water to a gentle brook, so they might hear one another’s voices. It could straighten their curved spines, open their scarred-over ears, and sow the wet slate with sweet green moss and fruit-bearing vines. It could lift them out of the cellar altogether and set them in a valley where the sky had no lid.

Then the power paused, and did something wiser than pity: it stopped looking, and began to listen—not to the air, which was only a wall of white noise, but to the stone.

It pressed its attention against the outer ribs of the cavern, the way a builder presses a cheekbone to a wet wall, and waited until the roar broke apart into its true voices.

It felt the steady, deep-throated thrum of a clear clay conduit carrying water down from the roof. It felt the sharp, nervous chatter of a weir choked with lime-grit, and three tiers away, the small, purposeful *tap-tap* of a horn-tipped finger clearing the pebble loose. It felt the slow, braided tension of sinew cables humming under the weight of a passing builder, and the brief, warm press of knuckle against knuckle in a two-finger alley—a whole conversation conducted in balance and weight, without a single wasted breath.

It traced the grammar of the notched parapets. It watched an old builder climb to a dry alcove under the ribs, tuck their knees into their lime-crusted cloak, and lay their jaw against the stone—not in despair, not in loneliness, but the way a musician lays an ear to the body of an instrument that will go on playing after their hands have stopped.

The power understood then what it had nearly done.

To bring them sunlight would blind their milky eyes and bake the iron-slime beds into dead dust. To hush the water would be to cut out the world’s tongue, leaving them stranded in a terrifying, unreadable silence. To widen their ceilings would be to tear down the very walls that held their voices, their maps, and their ancestors. They were not trapped in the rock. They were the rock’s own consciousness, grown small and patient enough to tend it from within.

So the power withheld the sun. It withheld the wind, the silence, the green, and the sky.

Instead, it looked upward—not into the pocket, but above it, through hundreds of feet of fractured limestone, clay seams, and the blind, shifting weight of the world’s crust. Up there, closer to the rumored surface, things were less constant. Faults crept. Frosts heaved. Far-off rivers changed their beds, and surface creatures dug, or built, or burned. Any one of those careless motions could, in a century or ten, snap the aquifer’s thread and leave the pocket dry as bone—or crack the dome and drown it in mud.

Very gently, so gently that not a single drop in the catch-basins missed its beat, the power wove a quiet permanence into the stone around the pocket. It did not freeze the world above; it only taught the deep strata to arch around this small hollow, the way a hand cups around a flame without touching it. It secured the aquifer’s slow, unvarying pulse so that neither drought nor thaw would break its rhythm, and set the limestone ribs to hold their shape against the grinding weight of ages.

Nothing inside the cavern changed. The mist still hung cool and heavy, smelling of wet chalk and cold iron. The blue-white grease-flames still marked the steepest drops.

Only, high up in the crooked, unreachable fissure, where a single thread of surface water always seeped, the power loosened one tiny, wedged pebble that had been blocking a crevice for fifty years.

Down into the highest catch-basin tumbled a dry, unbroken splinter of bat-bone, light as a reed and clean as ivory—just the right thickness for a needle, or the delicate trigger of a sluice-gate.

A builder patrolling the upper tiers felt the faint, unfamiliar *tink* of it through the soles of their feet. They paused, pressed a flat palm to the weeping wall to read the vibration, and then crept unhurriedly toward the basin in the dark, fingers already reaching.

The power watched them retrieve it, turn it over in their horn-tipped hands, and tuck it safely into their cloak. Then it drew its attention back through the stone, leaving the pocket as it had found it: damp, deafening, unbroken, and entirely its own.
