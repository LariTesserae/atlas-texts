When the power turned its attention toward the high shelf of gneiss and iron-stained stone, it did not arrive as light. Light was a language of lower worlds—of things that needed to grow toward a sun or flee from a shadow. Here, light would have been an intrusion, a loud noise in a room where someone was resting.

Instead, it arrived as a slowing down.

It folded its vast, swift awareness inward until a century felt like a single, unhurried breath, and only then did it settle over the dark avenues of vitrified slag.

At first, looking with the memory of other places, it felt the old, familiar urge of all kind powers: to mend. To a gaze accustomed to green valleys and beating hearts, the plateau looked like an elegy. It saw the lightless ceiling, the windowless factories, the motionless red eye of the pressure gauge, and it thought of the gifts it knew how to give. It could part the black overhead and pour in a sky of cold, clear stars. It could summon a soft rain to wash the graphite grit from the basalt plinths, coaxing lichen from the stone and moss from the iron seams. It could step down onto the viaduct, take the tall, clay-dry walker by its three-fingered hand, and give its smooth pewter face a pair of eyes to see the horizon and a mouth to ask what all this humming was for.

Then it waited, and listened—not with ears, but the way the plateau itself listened, feeling the sub-audible thrum in the stone—and wisdom stayed its hand.

It watched a microscopic thread of silver creep across a ceramic insulator, atom by atom, for the length of a human childhood, only to bridge the gap with a tiny, violet snap and return cleanly to ash. It felt the distant, subterranean flywheel shift by a hair’s breadth, sending a gentle tide of warmth through the iron ribs of the city, and it watched the chalk-white porcelain plates glide imperceptibly toward the fins, crowding together in the dark to share the wealth of a fraction of a degree.

It followed two of the multi-legged wire-draggers as they met on an empty boulevard, their trailing copper filaments tangled in the quartz dust. It saw no fear in their stillness, no anger in their hours-long pause—only a solemn, heat-saving patience as they stepped over one another’s needle-tipped limbs and parted without a sound. It peered through the yellowed sight-glass of a transformer hall and saw the pale, translucent ribbon coil lazily toward the copper core, turning at the speed of smoke in a sealed room.

And at last, it walked beside the one that fit the doorways.

It matched the walker’s glacial pace along the elevated viaduct, weightless and dry, careful not to stir the mineral dust. It watched the tall, pantograph-limbed figure pause before a squat transformer housing and lay its three-fingered hand against the riveted iron, tilting its concave pewter dish to feel the steady, pointless loop of the voltage.

The power understood then.

To bring rain here would not be mercy; it would be a flood. Water would blister the velvety black oxide into bleeding rust, crack the warm porcelain insulators in a scream of steam, drown the graphite-bellied plates, and rot the mineral-stiffened sheaths of the walkers until they collapsed into mud. To give them stars would be to introduce distance and longing where there was only touch. To give them urgency would be to break the truce they had spent ten thousand years negotiating with time.

Nothing here was starving. Nothing here was lost. The city was not a tomb waiting for resurrection; it was a chord that had already been struck, held cleanly in a room with no echo.

So the power did not touch the switches. It did not open the sky.

Instead, it looked downward, past the beveled basalt foundations, down through the miles of ancient stone to the turbulent, wet worlds below—where storms formed, where winds tore at mountain peaks, where restless builders sometimes climbed too high looking for iron or answers. Gently, without leaving a mark, the power drew a quiet boundary in the thin air miles beneath the shelf. It bent the paths of stray gales so they would curl harmlessly away from the tableland. It smoothed the edges of rising moisture so no cloud would ever climb high enough to condense on a copper busbar. It became, for the plateau, the one thing a closed circuit cannot make for itself: a guarantee that the dark above would stay unbroken, and the dry heat would not be stolen.

And before it settled into that long, silent watch, it offered one small courtesy—the only kind the plateau could receive without harm.

Standing beside the tall walker on the viaduct, the power did not speak, and did not warm the air. It simply matched the frequency of the grid for a single, quiet moment, resonating with the buried flywheels and the humming transformers—not adding a volt, not shifting the load by even a whisper, only joining the stillness the way one instrument joins another already playing a single, held note.

The pressure gauge’s red eye did not twitch. No relay clicked.

Yet the tall walker, whose pewter dish had been angled down toward the warm iron casing, paused. Slowly, at the pace of a cooling furnace, it tilted its smooth, faceless head upward—not toward a relay tower, and not toward the ground, but into the lightless, breathless dark where nothing was humming, and yet something was undeniably present.

It held that posture for an hour, or perhaps a day.

Then it lowered its three-fingered hand from the iron, reached up to a heavy valve wheel to turn it by the width of a fingernail—producing the faint, sharp *ping* of an insulator adjusting miles away—and stepped back into the black between the buildings to resume its walk.

And the power stayed, as quiet as the stone, keeping watch over a circuit that had no reason to stop.
