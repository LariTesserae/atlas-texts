When a power that is both benevolent and wise turns its attention to the high ridges, it arrives the only way it can without breaking what it has come to see: without light, without heat, and without sound.

To bring a sun here would be an act of violence. To bring a voice would be a flood. So it folds its own vastness down until it is no heavier than the slate-grey haze, and it looks.

At first, what it feels is the old, familiar ache of mercy—the impulse to mend. It sees the tilted districts leaning out over the abyss like sinking ships, the bridges severed in mid-air with their missing spans floating seventy feet above them, the avenues that lie about distance. It sees the tall, spare beings with their travertine skin and their recessed visors of dark glass, kneeling in the chalky dust to scrape rusted fungus into the seams behind their knees so the stone will not lock them in place.

A power that was only benevolent, and not wise, would have begun at once to heal.

It would have set a true plumb line to the chasms and gently lowered the floating arches back into their sockets. It would have poured a warm, wet breath up from the valleys—just enough rain to rinse the ash from the colonnades, just enough green to soften the razor-thin walkways, just enough air to carry a spoken name from one tower to the next. It would have reached into the travertine joints of the walkers and eased the dry friction there, sparing them the slow, ceaseless labor of keeping themselves uncalcified.

And in doing so, it would have killed every living thing in the city.

The wise part of the power watches a little longer before it moves a finger. It watches a shadow-gatherer crawl out from under a broken arch and press its broad, chalky belly flat against a fallen scrap of dark, drinking in the only shade that keeps the grey light from turning it to pavement. It listens—not with ears, but with the patience of rock—to the dead air inside a three-hundred-foot shaft, where the papery, salt-glass chandeliers hang by single filaments, turning an inch an hour as they drink the static hum of grinding seams. It watches a silica glider unfurl over a mile-wide gulf, needing nothing from the sky except its emptiness.

Then it watches one of the walkers pause at the frayed edge of a plaza, read the bunching of space through its slate visor, and step cleanly across a foreshortened mile.

It understands. This is not a ruin waiting for a builder. It is not an exile waiting for home. The breakdown is the weather; the friction is the pulse; the silence is the room they need to hear each other through the soles of their feet. To anchor the city would starve the towers. To soften the walkers would leave them unable to bear the wind. Even the slow, encroaching habit of the stone—the threat that one day a joint will stiffen for good—is what keeps their movements so deliberate, so spare, so entirely present to the bend of their own limbs.

So the first thing the power does is the hardest thing power ever learns to do: it stays its hand.

It draws back the warmth of its nearness, lest the cellar-cold shafts begin to sweat and slump into mud. It gathers up its own gravity, lest the inverted bridge-spans shudder and fall. It leaves the distances broken, the light flat, the dust dry and unmelting in the corners of the stairs.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and benevolence does not leave a greeting unspoken when it has already been noticed. Across the chasm, three of the tall beings have stopped on a forty-degree avenue, their glider-membrane cloaks rustling faintly against their shins, their dark mineral bands turned toward the place where the air feels slightly denser with attention.

So the power offers two small, native courtesies.

First, it sets an unmarked boundary around the lower skirts of the ridges—not a wall, for walls belong to builders, and not a lock, for nothing here is hoarded. Only a quiet persuasion in the thermal currents, a subtle thinning of the paths, so that the loud, wet, tool-carrying world below will rarely find the way up. Those who come here by accident will feel the air grow too thin and turn back toward water and noise; only the rare few who know how to stand still long enough for dust to settle on their shoulders will ever reach the parapets.

Second, it steps onto the sagging, razor-thin walkway between the ridges—weightless enough not to strain the dried sinew of the stone, yet real enough to touch it. It presses no voice into the air. Instead, it sets a single heel against the curbstone and sends one slow, measured tremor through the mineral crystal.

A pulse too low for ears. A phrase made entirely of weight and timing.

It says nothing about rescue, and nothing about tomorrow. It says only: *I see you. I will not mend your sky.*

Across the gulf, the three figures stand motionless for a long moment, reading the vibration through the jointed soles of their feet. Then the tallest of them shifts its weight—just a fraction, a deliberate settling of travertine against travertine—and sends a single, dry, rhythmic answer humming back along the bridge.

Not gratitude. Not worship. Simply recognition, from one immense patience to another.

Then the walker lifts its foot, steps across the folded mile of air, and vanishes into the bruised twilight of the towers, leaving the city to go on unspooling at its own unhurried pace.
