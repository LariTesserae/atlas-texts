At first, the power did nothing at all.

That is the hardest discipline of wisdom: to arrive at a strange, newly made place and withhold the hand.

It looked down through the jagged lattice of unweathered limestone and translucent selenite, past the Plate-Gliders clinging like frost to the fissures, past the long, white lines of arc-lamps, down to the pale, silent floor where a city of grown wood and copper machines hummed just below the threshold of hearing.

A lesser benevolence—one driven by pity rather than understanding—would have wept for them, and in weeping, drowned them. It would have seen the bone-dry air, the matte, ash-grained skin of the Tenders, the slow bead of clear gum hardening at the corner of a grieving eye, and thought: *They are parched. I will give them rain.*

And the rain would have swollen the chalk-cane until it burst its iron ribs. It would have rotted the springy lichen-felt into black mire, shorted the high-voltage busbars in a blinding ruin of steam and copper, dissolved the selenite ceiling, and choked the paper-winged moths. To give this valley water would be to erase the very grammar by which it lived.

So the power stayed its hand, and watched, and learned the valley’s own grammar.

It watched a Pruner climb a skeletal tower in total silence, trimming back the eager, copper-hungry tendrils with ceramic shears and sealing the cuts with hot wax. It watched a Silk-Reaper coax a long-legged loom-spider with the gentle tick of a Leyden-wand, trading stunned chalk-moths for conductive grey silk. It watched a Plain-Warden walk the wide, empty bark-plain—testing the felt with a bronze probe, sipping warm, oily sap-broth from a glass flask—keeping the firebreaks clear so that a single spark would not turn the basin into a furnace.

The power saw that there was no misery here, only vigilance. The Tenders were not surviving in spite of the dryness; they were *made* of it. Their quietness, their finger-signs, the coded *thrum* of brass mallets against bracing-cables, the copper wire braided into their hair and the bronze straps trailing from their heels—all of it was a delicate, ongoing conversation between human hands, living bark, and raw voltage.

To make their towers stop twisting toward the busbars would be to take away the Pruners’ craft. To silence the static would be to starve the Snap-Foxes and blind the Grit-Backs. A wise power does not sand down the friction that gives a world its shape.

Yet, because it was wise, it also saw what the Tenders could not see—the two fragile seams where a newly riven world might tear before it had time to grow into itself.

The first seam was above them.

High beyond the selenite plates, in the surface world the Tenders never visited, real rain fell. The basin was young; the fractured limestone of its ceiling was still settling under the weight of the earth above. One sharp subterranean shift could crack a broad selenite plate wide enough to let a surface watershed pour down—not a gentle mist, but a sudden, roaring flood that would wash away the dust-gardens and drown the mills before a single cable could be struck in warning.

The power reached into the unweathered roof, so lightly that not even the Plate-Gliders stirred in their updrafts. It did not seal the fissures or thicken the selenite into darkness; the cool, crisp daylight was part of the valley’s lungs. Instead, it coaxed the crystalline lattice at the molecular edge, lending the brittle plates a faint, glass-like ductility—just enough that when the mountain settled, the roof would flex and reseal rather than shatter. The surface water would find its way around the basin, never through it.

The second seam was below.

The power admired the Plain-Wardens and their wide, empty bark-plains. Courage and care belonged to the Tenders; it would not steal their responsibility by making the valley fireproof. A careless mill should still be able to burn, if a Pruner grew lazy or a busbar was neglected. Consequences were how the city learned.

But a basin still figuring out its own balance is only one bad tremor away from total ash—a single snapped main line throwing an arc across dry resin, leaping the firebreaks faster than any Warden could walk.

So, in the deep, raw limestone beneath the basin floor—the stone that the Grit-Backs’ ancestors had not yet reached, the mineral dust that would rise in slow plumes over the next hundred generations—the power folded a trace of quiet grace. A faint, non-aqueous salt, indistinguishable from the chalk around it.

The Grit-Backs would grind it without knowing, and vent it into the brisk, cedar-scented air. The chalk-cane and the lichen-felt would drink it with the dust. It would not make the wood wet, nor dull its hunger for electricity, nor stop a small fire from catching if a Wardens’ watch failed. Only at the terrible, roaring heat of a true conflagration would that trace salt wake—melting instantly into a thin, glassy crust that choked the flame at the edge of the bark-plain, turning a valley-ending inferno into a single, survivable scar.

Then the power paused, and considered the Tenders themselves—not their survival, which was now given time to endure, but their quiet, solitary hearts.

Should it leave them a sign? A voice in the hum of the copper? A gentle light in the ceiling when their shifts ended?

No. A visible miracle would make them look upward instead of outward at their own world. It would turn their careful, equal partnership with the cane and the spider into worship of a stranger, and make them feel small in the valley they were just beginning to learn.

Still, as the slow flywheels locked for the night and the Tenders climbed to their dry, cedar-scented beds, the power lingered for one more moment.

Somewhere out on the plain, a Warden stood alone in the bright, motionless hush, resting a hand against a tensioned steel bracing-cable to feel for the distant *thrum* of the next district. Far away, another Warden struck a weighted brass mallet against the wire.

The power did not change the message, nor add a word of its own. It only smoothed, by a hair’s breadth, the crystalline grain of the steel—so that the vibration reached the distant Warden’s boots and fingertips a little cleaner, a little warmer, carrying all the steady, unspoken companionship of the valley without losing a single note to the dark.

Then the benevolent power withdrew, leaving behind no altar, no legend, and no rain—only a bright, dry world, free to keep growing into itself.
