It did not arrive as a light in the sky, for the sky here was only a lid of soot, and light would have been a violence to eyes shaped for cherry-glow and static.

If it arrived at all, it arrived as a deeper stillness beneath the stillness—a weightless attention settling into the bedrock, feeling the *tick* of cooling glass and the dry, rhythmic *thump* of a cairn being rocked miles away.

For a long time, the power simply listened.

Benevolence, when it is young or hasty, has a single, terrible reflex: it looks at a hard place and wants to soften it. Looking down at the razor-edged scoria, the starved sulfur-vents, and the small, dust-caked figure curled tight in a roofless ring, a merely kind power would have reached out at once.

It would have parted the soot to let a sun fall upon the slag. It would have gathered clouds and poured rain over the thirsty basalt. It would have cooled the restless faults, thickened the air, and coaxed green roots to split the obsidian.

And in doing so, it would have killed every living thing in the basin.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

The power watched through the stone and understood. Rain here would not bring life; it would turn the fine mineral ash into a suffocating paste, scald the vents into steam, and drown the dry, slow-burning chemistry of the charcoal filaments. Thick air would carry the roar of every collapsing spire, shattering the creatures’ fragile peace. Cooling the faults would steal the borrowed warmth from their joints until they locked like cold iron. And to give them softness—soft skin, soft beds, an easy horizon—would be to unmake them, turning masters of the knife-edge into helpless strangers in a world they no longer knew.

They did not need to be rescued from their world. They belonged to it, as tightly as the spun-iron filaments belonged to the lip of the seam.

So the power watched closer, pressing its attention against the basalt the way the creatures pressed their flat, earless jaws, learning what hurt them and what held them up.

It saw the patience in their long, blunt-padded fingers, testing every brittle crust before trusting their weight. It saw the quiet, unsentimental care with which they stacked windbreaks—not only for their own backs, but to shelter a patch of gray spores so the next traveler might eat. It saw the solitary dignity of the rings, and the small, stubborn defiance of the leaning cairns.

It saw, too, where the margin was thinnest. It felt the terror of a vent guttering out while the nearest warm seam lay three miles of knife-edge ridge away in the dark. It felt how the deep chasms broke the vibration of the rocked cairns, swallowing the *thump* before it could reach another’s palm, leaving the lonely ones to believe the basin had finally emptied.

And so, at last, the power acted—not as a king remaking a realm, and not as a parent sweeping a child from the floor, but as a mason slipping a single, unseen wedge beneath a leaning stone.

Far below the crust, where the magma moved in blind, sluggish tides, the power laid a quiet hand upon the heat. It did not stop the faults from shifting or the new cracks from splitting open; the creatures lived by the newness of the rock. Instead, it steadied the rhythm. It tuned the deep convection so that when an old vent began to cool and stiffen, a fresh seam would part somewhere within a day’s careful crawl—never too close, never without effort, just close enough that the cold would not catch them mid-ridge.

In the upper air—thin, dry, and turbulent—it nudged the thermal funnels by a fraction of a degree, just enough that the gray spores lifted from the rattling filaments would find the lee of the stone windbreaks a little more often than the open chasm.

And into the fractured, glassy bedrock of the basin, it wove a subtle continuity. Where deep faults had once severed the stone and muffled every tremor into silence, the rock now remembered how to carry a pulse across the dark.

Nothing in the basin looked different. The sky remained a starless lid of soot. The scoria still shattered like pottery underfoot. The creatures still crept low on four limbs, scraping chalky tracks across the obsidian, grinding sulfur-heavy filaments between wide molars, and curling into roofless rings when the ash blew hard.

Yet one night—or what passed for night beneath the unending dark—a creature crouched beside a cooling seam. Its joints were beginning to stiffen. The filaments here had turned to dry ash, and the wind was rising upslope, snapping with pale-violet static.

It reached out to the leaning cairn beside it, gripped the base, and rocked it.

*Thump.*

The stone answered beneath its palm, clearer than it had any right to be, running out across the miles of jagged slag and plunging down the chasm walls without breaking.

*Thump. Thump.*

The creature paused, pressing its earless jaw flat against the warm basalt, waiting as it always waited for the silence to settle back in.

Far away, across two ridges and a fault that should have swallowed the sound, another cairn rocked in answer. And beneath them both—deeper than the magma seams, steadier than the shifting crust, asking for no name and offering no light—something vast and quiet held the rock together.

The creature drew a slow breath of thin, warm air, uncurled its stiffened fingers, and began to crawl toward the next ridge, where a faint, sullen cherry-glow was just beginning to split the dark.
