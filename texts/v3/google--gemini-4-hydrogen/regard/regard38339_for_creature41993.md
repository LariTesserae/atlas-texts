At first, from a great distance, the plateau looked like a wound.

A power that is both vast and attentive notices anomalies the way a weaver notices a snagged thread: a place where cause and effect have drifted three seconds apart; a stretch of space that refuses to hold its length when wet; a handful of living souls dwindling in the roar of machines that outlived their purpose. To a lesser benevolence—one that mistakes neatness for health—the diagnosis would have been immediate. *It is unravelling,* it would have said. *Let us mend it.*

So the power came closer, not as a blinding light or a sudden hush—either would have burned the pale skin of the caretakers and startled the glass-newts in their cisterns—but as a change in the weight of the mist. It settled into the pale-sage lichen, down through the matted root-fiber and the dark peat, and listened.

It listened at the speed of the plateau, which meant it did not hurry.

It felt the bass thrum of the buried dynamos and the blind, contented knotting of the hum-worms around the xylem-pipes. It watched a copper-limpet cross an inch of oxidized rivet in the span of a day, leaving a clean, bright track behind it. It watched a static-weaver string its dry, brittle geometry between an ironwood beam and a sheared bolt that hung in midair, sprouting pale roots into nothing at all.

And then it followed the caretakers.

It stood unseen beside one in the eastern textile-mill as she pressed her tar-stained ear to a sweating copper pipe and waited for the *tink-tink-pause-tink* from three miles away. It climbed the four-story staircase tucked inside a two-story fern-trunk and felt the gentle, inexplicable warmth of the room at the top—a room that did not exist from the outside, smelling faintly of peat-smoke and oiled felt. It watched her lower a wire basket of root-vegetables through a window that opened not onto the sunlit moss, but onto a flooded cellar of slow, submerged gears.

The power paused, and its wisdom stayed its hand.

If it mended the seams of reality—if it snapped the steam-whistle back into sync with the steam, and forced every room to fit inside its trunk—the caretakers would lose their warm attics and their cold cellars. Their world would shrink to what could be measured, and what could be measured on this high, wet tableland was not enough to keep them.

If it silenced the wind-turbines so that human voices might carry across the moss, the hum-worms would lose the vibration that gathered them, and the caretakers, who had long ago traded speech for the honest tremor of copper and floorboards, would find themselves suddenly deaf in a strange new quiet.

If it straightened time so that pocket-watches ticked only forward, the few dozen builders who remained would feel the weight of all the centuries they had bypassed, and turn to damp ash in their coats. And the Lagging—those quiet, out-of-phase figures who walked beside them in the three-hour mist, carrying brass oil-cans that dripped nothing—would vanish, leaving the long trestles truly empty.

The plateau was not broken. It was *frayed*, yes, like the hem of an old coat—but the fray was where everything lived.

So the power chose restraint, which is often the hardest work of love.

It did not repair the plateau. Instead, it reached into the edge of the unravelling and placed a single, invisible whipstitch just behind the fringe—not to pull the threads back together, only to keep the whole cloth from giving way. The lag would remain three seconds, never four. The mist-trestles would stretch to three hours, never forever. The equilibrium would hold.

And then, because wisdom without tenderness is only architecture, it began to tend the small margins that even the plateau could not reach.

When a caretaker tapped a wrench against a distant xylem-pipe, and the copper was too old and silt-choked to carry the sound across three miles of peat, the power lent the metal just enough resonance that the *tink-tink-pause-tink* arrived clear and true at the far mill. No one had to tap into silence.

When a sheared bolt drifted too far from its press and began to falter, the power quietly fed the pale roots it sprouted into nothing, so the bolt hung steady and the static-weavers did not lose their home.

On the elevated trestles, when a mist-bank rolled in and stretched a twenty-minute walk into hours of repeating ironwood pylons, the power occasionally let one of the Lagging’s phantom oil-cans leave a single, real drop of warm oil on a rusted latch ahead—just enough that a tired caretaker’s hand would not ache in the damp.

And for the caretakers themselves, who do not age linearly and who wind their watches only when they stop, the power prepared one quiet mercy far down the uncounted years:

Whenever one of them grows finally, deeply tired—not broken, only ready to set the brass wrench down—they will find a door in an old fern-trunk they had never noticed before. Beyond it will be a staircase that climbs as high as it needs to, into a room that does not exist from the outside. A smokeless peat-stove will already be warm there. Through the window, there will be neither sunlit moss nor flooded gears, only a soft, silver mist that smells of crushed pine-needles and wet iron. And the roar of the wind-turbines will sound, from that height, like nothing so much as breathing.

Having arranged this, the power did not leave a sign, or a name, or a commandment. It simply stayed in the dampness and the copper, watching the limpets shine their slow tracks across the pipes, content to be one more quiet thing keeping the mills running.
