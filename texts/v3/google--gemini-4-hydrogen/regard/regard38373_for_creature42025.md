It would not arrive as a warmth, because warmth here is a thief. Nor would it arrive as a light; the bowl already has more light than it knows how to spend.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to this high, bruised-indigo hollow, it would come as a stillness—a patience wide enough to match the eighty-winter clock of the things wedged into the stone.

At first, the benevolence would want to mend it.

That is the oldest impulse of kindness when it looks at a hard place: to soften the edges. To coax a spring up through the fractured granite so the water does not vanish by mid-afternoon. To thicken the air until the white birds have breath enough to sing, and the sun’s hammer-blow gentles into an ordinary morning. To call the climbers back up the scree with fresh sinew and warm hands, and to wake the pale-plated sleepers for twelve hours instead of three, smoothing the old beak-gouges and rock-scars from their backs.

But wisdom would trace each of those mercies to its end, and stay its hand.

If the air thickened and warmed, the lowland grasses would climb the talus within a decade, choking out the oily black-green moss and the blood-dark succulents wedged into the cracks. The white stoats would turn brown and be driven out by larger hunters from the timberline. The birds would learn to sing, and lose the exact, silent economy that lets them wheel through thin air. And the sleepers—built of syrupy antifreeze and translucent horn, tuned to a solar oven inside a freezer—would grow sluggish in a mild summer. Their slow hearts would falter in the damp heat; their ancient, patient flesh would rot before it learned how to hurry.

To rescue the bowl from its hardness would be to unmake everything that knows how to live inside it.

So the power would touch none of the great levers. It would leave the bruised sky thin, the quartz blinding, the afternoon wind sharp with ozone. It would let the freeze lock the water-pans into matte gray glass at dusk, and let the sleeper in the abandoned blind void its water into the dust, seal its amber lids, and cease to be alive for twenty-one hours. It would not even smooth the scars on the creature’s waxy plates, knowing those gouges are the only ledger of its long survival.

Instead, the power would look for what could be helped without breaking the bowl’s fierce bargain.

It would look at the center of the basin, beside the largest of the natural melt-pans, where the vanished climbers once knelt with heavy stone hammers to peck shallow rectangular troughs into the raw quartz. Those crude basins are older than the bone needles in the blind, almost as old as the sleepers themselves—a small, borrowed patience left behind by hands that no longer climb the mountain. Over the decades of frost-shattering, a hairline split has begun to work its way through the floor of the widest trough, threatening to drain the noon condensation straight into the dark scree before the algae can film it, before the black beetles can drink, before the sleepers can follow the damp halo along its rim.

The power would not mend the mountain, nor stop the frost from splitting the stone. It would only wait for the afternoon wind to spill over the eastern lip, and let one flake of milky quartz grit—no larger than a lentil—drift across the glare and wedge itself tight into the hairline crack.

Nothing would chime. Nothing would glow. By the next noon, the melt-water would pool in the trough as it always had, holding the sky for three brief hours before the shadow of the western tooth dropped across the floor.

And then the power would leave the bowl to itself—content to have seen it clearly, and wise enough to let it remain what it is.
