At first, the power only listened.

To arrive in a place like this with light would be an assault; to arrive with a voice would be lost ten paces from the mouth, or worse, mistaken for a new fissure opening in the shale. So the power set aside brightness and speech, and slowed its attention until it moved at the pace of the mist—drifting at a walking stride through the razor-edged aisles, settling into the wet, metallic hum that vibrates in the sternum.

By the measure of warmer worlds, the temptation to mend was immediate. A lesser mercy—one that knew comfort better than it knew truth—would have reached down to part the bruised, unmoving clouds and let the sun in. It would have hushed the weeping spires, softened the fractured shale, warmed the stagnant pools until grass took root on the travertine and moss cushioned the narrow paths. It would have looked at a pale, slime-coated creature chewing raw slug-meat in the twilight and called it a prisoner in need of rescue.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

It watched for a season—just long enough to see the water table rise by the width of three fingers. It watched a dweller turn sideways to slip between needle-sharp columns without brushing a single burr of volcanic glass. It watched the dweller kneel by an ankle-high pool, reach in with infinite slowness to lift a fallen flake of shale from the murk so the translucent, hair-like threads would not be startled into retreating. It watched two of them meet in a narrow channel, press their foreheads lightly together—bone to bone, bypassing the roaring air—and part without a sound.

And in that slowness, the power understood what it was looking at.

This was not a ruin. It was a chord.

To quiet the valley would be to blind its people. Without the overlapping gongs of falling drops and the deep, patient groan of the aquifer, the dwellers would lose the walls a hundred paces ahead, the shifting of the vents, the subtle flattening of a hollow column’s pitch that told them another of their kind was near. To warm the air and dry the weeping travertine would crack their chalk-pale hide, starve the sulfur-slime, and turn their clay-silt armor to brittle dust. To widen the aisles would erase the very measure of their bodies.

They did not need to be saved from their world. They belonged to it, down to the crusty mineral rings around their collarbones.

So the power chose, above all else, what it would *not* do. It left the clouds bruised and unmoving. It left the shale crisp enough to slice skin. It left the water bitter with iron and chalk, the paths marked only by broken glass underfoot, the lives cold and spare and patient.

And yet, watching closely, it saw the narrow margins where the valley’s harshness turned to needless waste—and there, at the valley’s own glacial tempo, it offered a few quiet mercies, so small they might almost be mistaken for geology.

Deep beneath the porous floor, where the aquifer shifted and groaned, the power laid a gentle steadying hand against the bedrock. It did not freeze the basin in time—the mineral crusts still thickened by a fingernail’s width each year, old vents still choked with silt, new ones still sighed open—but it eased the sharpest, sudden stresses in the stone. No subterranean fracture would snap without warning; no ceiling plate above a wide, clay-stuffed nursery would split in the middle of a sleeper’s torpor. Whatever changed in the basin would change slowly enough to be heard in the bass drone seasons before it arrived, giving the dwellers time to move their rings of stones and their young.

And in the open maze, it kept watch for the *rung*.

Sometimes, a dweller lingered too long away from a dead-zone—perhaps a familiar pocket had flooded, or a choked vent had bent the pitch of a channel—until their limbs began to shiver in sympathy with the tensioned shale, their joints locking, their skull filled with a phantom ringing that drowned out the true hum of the rock. When that happened, and a dweller stood frozen in the grey-violet mist, unable to tell which way the glass-shards led, a small, cool stillness would meet them.

Nothing visible. Nothing that would startle a veiled eye behind its milky second lid. Only a sudden, localized deadening of the air, no wider than a forearm, resting lightly against their temple or wrist—as solid and quiet as the bone of another dweller leaning in to steady them. In that brief pocket of hush, the phantom ringing would loosen its grip. The dweller would hear their own slow pulse again, feel the angle of the glass fragments beneath their calloused heel, and find the strength to slide sideways down the aisle toward the nearest clay-sealed hollow. They would assume, if they thought of it at all, that they had chanced upon a strange, natural deadness in the stone, and press on.

Finally, the power attended the deepest dead-zones—the ones at the end of the winding paths, where the oldest dwellers crawled when their bones grew too brittle to bear the resonance.

It did not prolong their lives, nor lift them out of the dampness. It knew better than to steal an ending that belonged to the valley. Instead, as the old ones curled into the smoothed depressions and let their breathing slow to once a minute, and then less, the power simply remained in the muffled dark beside them. Not with warmth, which would have felt foreign, and not with light, but with a quiet, attentive weight—holding the clay-silt steady as the weeping dampness sealed them gently back into the floor, so that the last thing they felt, before the stone reclaimed them, was the faint, unmistakable rhythm of something else listening alongside them.

Then, having done almost nothing at all, the power stayed—patient as the lime, content to let the valley go on shouting in the dark.
