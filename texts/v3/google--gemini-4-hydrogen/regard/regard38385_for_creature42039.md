If a wise power comes to this place, the first thing it does is stop.

Not in the sky above the rim, where the mountain wind tears the mist into rags, and not on the high bridges, where a mortal presence would only bruise the moss and startle the birds. It stops in the way an old tree stops—by letting its haste fall away until it is moving no faster than the afternoon sun creeping down the western wall.

At first, even to great benevolence, the basin looks like a mistake that time has not yet corrected. The hornfels is too raw, the air too thin, the roar too relentless. A lesser mercy—one eager to comfort, to mend, to make things hospitable—would look at the razor-edged scree and wish to bury it in loam. It would look at the empty, hearthless terraces and long to light fires there, to warm the chill stone, to hush the sixty-foot drop-shafts so that softer voices might be heard. It would pity the four or five long, dark shapes pressed flat against the retaining walls, mistaking their stillness for exile or starvation.

Wisdom does not touch anything until it has learned how to listen here.

So the power does not listen with the air, which is only a bruised, chaotic echo bouncing between black cliffs. It presses its attention down into the hornfels itself, through the cold, wet slate and the dry-laid piers, until the deafening roar separates into its true parts.

Then it hears.

It hears the *thrum* of the deep cisterns, steady as a slow pulse. It hears the high, bright *clack* of the upper runnels, the wet *shhh* of mist condensing on quartz seams, the tiny, dry scrape of a snail’s shell turning on a chisel mark. And threading through it all, it hears the deliberate stutter: a three-breath silence where a heavy limb has broken the flow of an eastern aqueduct, followed—minutes later, across three hundred paces of empty air—by the faint, shifted pitch of a slate slab nudged beneath a western arch.

It sees the moss wedged between mineral-hardened shoulder plates. It sees the fibrous pads of split-toed feet testing each mortarless stone with infinite care. It understands, all at once, that the emptiness of the air is not a lack; it is the hollow of a drum. The cold is not cruelty; it is the pace at which these beings can afford to live.

A lesser mercy would still want to give a gift. It might think of multiplying the creatures, so there would be twenty instead of five; or of softening the winter frost; or of sealing the basin against the weather so the stonework could never crumble.

Wisdom considers each of these, and refuses them all.

To bring warmth would be to rot the cold-blooded gardens and drive the creatures into a feverish hunger the thin rock could never feed. To bring more of their kind would crowd the bridges and muddle the echoes until no one could hear the water speak. To freeze the basin in changeless perfection would be to steal its future—for even the slow staining of the rock, the creeping millimeters of moss, the eventual wearing of the slate are part of the long song they live inside.

Yet the power lingers a little longer, checking the bones of the instrument the way a careful luthier checks the ribs of a cello—not to alter its voice, but to be sure it will not split under tension.

High on the northern wall, beneath the oldest of the cantilevered galleries, it finds a small, quiet danger. The builders who worked with copper chisels and wooden wedges left no mortar in these joints, and here, deep inside a load-bearing pier, meltwater has found a hairline seam. Each winter, the frost widens it by the thickness of a hair. The creatures cannot reach it; their blunt claws clear the channels and lever loose scree from the sluice-gates, but they cannot see inside a pier. In forty or fifty winters, that single dry-laid corner will slip. The gallery will drop into the void, shattering the tuned slate at the bottom of the shaft and silencing half the western chord.

The power does not mend it with a miracle. A sudden fusion of stone would deaden the joint, turning a resonant, dry-laid bridge into a dull mass of rock that would ring false against the ribs of anything resting upon it.

Instead, it reaches deep into the fault-lift beneath the basin—down where the hornfels is still warm from the earth’s lifting—and nudges a single, thread-thin vein of silica-rich water by the width of a beetle’s wing.

Nothing changes that day. Nothing changes that year.

Only, over the next ten seasons, the water weeping through that one stressed pier carries a slightly heavier ghost of pale mineral. Drop by slow drop, at the exact pace of the moss and the patience of the creatures, it sinters the dry-laid stones from within—just enough to hold the keystone fast against the frost, without changing a single note of the bridge’s hum.

Then the power withdraws.

It leaves no sign of itself. It does not speak—a god’s voice in that amphitheater would shatter the slate and deafen the newts in their dark pools. If it leaves anything behind at all, it is only the quiet certainty that when the afternoon sun clears the rim, the black rock will still be warm enough to climb, the channels will still run clear, and the chord will hold.
