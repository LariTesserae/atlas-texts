First, it would do nothing at all.

A lesser kindness—the hurried, bright-eyed kind that belongs to the surface—might have looked down through that ragged seam of gray light and felt a spasm of pity. It might have sent down a cool rain to wash the mineral dust from the air, or cracked the basalt to let in the sun, or called the pale, blind bundles of felt and rhizome out into a green meadow to be healed.

And in doing so, it would have killed everything in the pit before the hour was out.

A wise power knows that before you mend a thing, you must learn what speed it lives at. So it would descend without wind or radiance, shedding its own haste at the rim of the rift. It would stand on the wet basalt flags of the lowest sump and wait. It would wait until the dry heat baked the hurry out of its attention, until its own vast pulse slowed to match the sigh of the hydraulic gates and the minute-hand turn of the brass flywheels.

Only then would it truly see.

It would look at the salt-crusted bundle wedged between the brick wall and the steel stanchion, and it would not see a monster, or a prisoner, or a pathetic scrap of life clinging to industrial ruins. It would see a miracle of stubbornness. It would see a creature that had taken the discarded skin of a forgotten town—its tarred felt, its lime, its plaster, its weeping copper—and woven them into a body that could love a furnace. It would recognize the quiet liturgy of the snapped stalactites stacked neatly behind the kilns, the tender smear of fern-tallow on the counterweight rails, the blind, patient listening of the hollow reeds.

The power would understand that the shaft is not a cage. It is a lung. And it is breathing well.

Still, a wise eye looks deeper than the present hour, and it sees what the creature’s mineral core cannot yet feel: the slow debts coming due.

The basalt of the rift is young, still taut with the violence of its breaking; deep in the walls, microscopic fractures are waiting for the weight of the brick tiers to shift a fraction of an inch. Up in the boiler-houses, the banked furnaces are burning down toward the last of their ancient, buried coal. In another decade, or another century, the embers would turn to cold ash, the thermal drafts would fail, the condensers would stop weeping, and the brass suns would grind to a halt.

So the power would go to work—not with miracles that break the rules of the pit, but with adjustments so quiet the creature would never know a hand had touched its world.

Down in the bedrock beneath the sumps, where the earth’s own heat already radiates upward, the power would gently coax a deeper seam of warmth to rise. Not a surge, not a tremor—just a slow, imperceptible migration of heat through the stone, timed to the exact pace of the dying coals above. Year by year, as the last fuel in the kilns grew thin, the deep rock would seamlessly take up the burden. The drafts would never falter. The *tst* of water on the lower manifolds would keep its steady, timeless rhythm.

Then, moving through the sharp, glassy steps of the rift walls, the power would ease the hidden tensions in the young basalt. It would let the stone settle its weight millimeter by millimeter over the span of seasons, relieving the shear-stress behind the mortared shelves so that no brick boiler-house would ever crack or slide into the void.

And where a main copper flue was thinning from decades of sulfur-steam, the power would guide a slightly richer crust of lime to calcify along the seam—a natural, mineral bandage, indistinguishable from the shaft’s own slow geometry.

It would not clean the soot from the bricks. It would not banish the heat. It would not give the creature eyes to see a light it has no use for, nor a mind to feel the loneliness of the dark.

Yet, before it withdrew, benevolence might ask for one small thing more than mere maintenance. Not a gift for the machines, but a greeting for the gardener.

For the span of a single shift of the hydraulic gates, the power might lean its presence against the main steam-pipe—not with light, and not with words, but as a low, steady resonance in the iron. A warm, clear harmonic, tuned precisely to the length of the hollow reeds sprouting from the creature’s crust.

Down in the amber haze, wedged in its corner between brick and steel, the bundle of felt and rhizome would feel the vibration travel through its mineral core. Its brittle stalks would tremble in the rising draft, drinking in a note that meant no bearing was running dry, no flue was choked, no wall was about to fall. Only that the great, dark lung was full, and the earth beneath the flags was steady.

Then the power would let the hum fade back into the ordinary thrum of the boilers, and leave the pit to its work—ticking, sweating, and turning in the quiet dark, safe for as long as the stone endures.
