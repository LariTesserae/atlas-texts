A power that is both wise and benevolent would not arrive with wind or shadow. On a plain where the loudest sound is a brick *ticking* in the noon sun, even a sigh of presence would be an intrusion. It would come the way attention comes when nothing is disturbed—simply settling over the ochre tiles, wide and weightless as the heat-haze.

At first, a lesser kindness might have felt pity. Looking down at the flat, merciless glare, the miles of unweathered terracotta, the salt crusts blooming white around empty basins, a hurried mercy would have reached out to fix it. It would have broken the bricks, raised hills to catch the wind, poured rivers down the forearm-deep channels, and shaded the mortar with fast-growing trees.

But wisdom waits before it acts. It stays through the noon glare until the air cools. It watches the afternoon thermals build their flat-bottomed clouds and listens to the brief, hissing squall darken the clay to rust-red. It sees the warped, salt-crusted discs in the catch-basins uncurl like leathery hands, catch their spoonful of rain, and fold shut again before the steam is gone. It waits through the dark until the mortar softens and the pale, vellum-thin ribbons slip out to drink dew from the northern lips of the retaining walls. And it looks deeper still, past the first course of brick, to where the chalky web breathes with the daily contraction of the earth—trading hoarded dampness for the sugars of saltbush and purslane, dissolving crushed shell one grain at a time.

Seeing all of this, the power would understand two things at once.

First, that this place is not barren. It is a cradle still warm from the kiln, and the life within it is not suffering—it is *fitted*. Every creature here is shaped to the exact width of a mortar seam, the exact angle of a trowel-groove, the exact briefness of an afternoon rain. To drown this plain in rivers or bury it in loam would not be salvation; it would be erasure. It would choke the web that breathes through the cracks, rot the discs that need the baking heat to seal themselves, and wash away the saltbush before its roots could hold.

Second, it would understand that the work of healing is already underway. Beneath the crisp, unblunted geometry, the plain’s quiet inhabitants are patiently unmaking their own floor—etching microscopic channels into the lime, softening the shell below, holding water a fraction of a day longer with each passing season. They are turning fired clay back into soil, at the only pace that will last.

So the first thing a wise and benevolent power would do is almost everything: **it would stay its hand.**

It would leave the glare sharp, the silence granular, the miles between the catch-basins long and empty. It would not blunt a single corner of terracotta or hurry a single seed.

Yet benevolence is not merely watching from a distance; it is also foresight. Looking ahead across the slow decades by which the chalky web measures its life, the power would see one quiet, fragile hinge in time. The plain is newly laid. Its sub-layers are still sweating out the last dampness of their making. One day, that first builders’ moisture will be spent—and if it runs out before the web has etched enough porous stone to hold the rain on its own, the plain could cross the razor-thin margin from *dry* into *sterile*.

So, if the power does anything at all, it does only what cannot be seen and will never break the geometry:

At the distant, unseen edges of the basin—far beyond where the saltbush grows or the vellum ribbons travel—it would gently steady the balance of the outer air. Not enough to bring a storm, not enough to cool the noon, just enough to ensure that the afternoon thermals always find a breath of moisture to pull inward, and that the pre-dawn cold always finds enough dampness in the sky to bead along the northern lips of the low walls. A quiet promise that the squalls will keep coming, and the dew will not fail, until the web beneath the tiles is deep enough to keep its own water.

And perhaps, as a final, unhurried courtesy, it would set a silence around the horizon—keeping away anything from the outside world that might trample the seams, poison the lime, or mistake this patient, curing floor for an empty place in need of conquering.

Then it would withdraw its attention as lightly as it came, leaving no footprint on the clay, no mark on the mortar—only the faint *tick* of a brick settling in the sun, and the slow, unseen work continuing beneath it.
