It would not arrive with light.

A power that was merely kind might have parted the green-black water with a sudden radiance, burning away the silt so the whole sixty-pace cathedral stood revealed from sluice-wall to sump. That would have sent the pale pollock bolting into the intake grates, driven the rockfish into the cracks, and left the creature on the gantry crane shrinking back behind its heavy brow-ridges, its milky eyes aching in a glare it was never made to bear.

A power that was wise would arrive instead as a stillness—a listening so patient it slipped beneath the *thrum-scrape* of the warped bronze vane without displacing a single mysid shrimp.

For a long time, it would do nothing at all. It would simply take the measure of the room.

It would count the twelve revolutions a minute in the ironwork and the forty-second *boom* of the check-valve on the east manifold. It would feel the eight-degree chill of the ocean pouring down the north shaft, and the narrow, precious column of warmth rising from the third brass cage-lamp. It would watch the keeper hang upside-down in the lee of the manifold, jaw-pits trembling, waiting for the shudder in the floor silt before its long arm shot out. It would watch the creature climb back up to its rusted cradle, wait a week, and then descend hand over hand with its shard of broken shell to scrape the pale mineral scale from the glass.

Watching, the power would understand what a lesser mercy might have missed: that this was not a ruin, and the creature was not a prisoner.

To haul the keeper back down through the deeper fault into the crushing dark of the open trench would be to tear it from the only hearth it had ever known. To raise it to the surface would be a slower cruelty. Even to *mend* the vault too well would be a kind of theft. If the power scoured the concrete clean, silenced the *thrum-scrape* of the scraping vane, or oiled the east manifold so the check-valve seated without a sound, the keeper’s clock would stop. The pollock would not scatter; the jaw-pits would have nothing to read. And if the power relit the two dead lamps, or coated the third globe so that calcium carbonate could never cling to it again, the keeper would climb down the chain-fall one day, shard of shell in hand, and find nothing left to tend.

So the wise power would stay its hand from all the things that looked broken. It would leave the rust on the gantry rails, the frost of bryozoans on the concrete piers, the red algae swaying in the dimness, and the two dead globes filmed in grey.

Instead, it would look deeper—into the places the keeper’s calloused fingers could never reach.

Inside the third brass cage-lamp, sealed behind thick glass and corroded threads, the tungsten filament had grown thin as a spider’s leg, pitted by centuries of slow current. Deep within the tar-wrapped umbilical, where the keeper could clear away drifting kelp but could not see beneath the pitch, salt had crept along the copper strands and turned half of them to green dust. In the small auxiliary dynamo coupled to the main shaft, a brass bushing was wearing toward a sudden, final seize; and high above, one iron link in the chain-fall suspending the living lamp had rusted halfway through its core.

None of these things belonged to the keeper’s craft. A shard of shell could not mend them. When they went, the amber cone would blink out in a single, unremarkable second, and no amount of scraping would ever bring it back.

So the power would do its work there, in the dark inside the iron.

It would not make the copper bright or the iron new. It would simply persuade the fatigued metal to hold. It would knit the thinning filament just enough to bear the dynamo’s uneven pulse for another age, seal the unseen pinhole in the tar without smoothing the rough wrap outside, and lend the worn bushing and the rusted chain-link the quiet endurance of stone. It would not touch the warped bronze vane—the *thrum-scrape* would remain, only never quite wearing through the housing. The check-valve would go on booming every forty seconds, heavy and faithful.

Perhaps, before it withdrew, the power might pause beside the gantry crane, where the concrete-colored keeper hung suspended in the rising warmth, its dark-red gills pulsing in time with the machine below. It would not speak to the creature, nor stir the water around its barnacle-crusted shoulders, nor ask for gratitude it could not comprehend.

It would only ensure that when the mineral scale crept across the glass again next month, and the month after that, and a thousand months beyond, the hearth behind the glass would still be burning—waiting for the slow, heavy grip of the keeper’s hand to come down the chain and set it clear.

Then the stillness would recede into the green-black water, leaving no trace of itself behind, except that the turbine turned, and the lamp burned, and the vault kept its own.
