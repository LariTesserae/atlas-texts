At first, the power mistook the place for a wound.

It was an understandable error. A mind accustomed to holding galaxies in their orbits, to keeping the ledgers of rivers and seasons and the strict, forward march of cause and effect, knows how to recognize a catastrophe. From the outside—if such a word could be applied to a place that had no outside—this small, bounded nave looked like a shard that had broken off from some larger world and forgotten how to die.

The power leaned closer, careful to arrive without a wind, without a tremor, without even the faintest shift in the mid-morning light. It knew better than to announce itself. Still, its attention was vast, and for a moment it held that attention back behind the slate ceiling like a hand cupped over a candle-flame—not to smother it, only to keep from blowing it out.

Through the dry-fitted stone, it looked in.

It saw the absence of sky, and its first, ancient instinct was to part the reeds and let in a breeze. It saw the bone-ash packed between the tiles, the chalky mortar, the brittle silica-fiber vines, and it felt the old, generous urge to bring rain—a soft, misting drizzle to coax the ochre mosses into green, to cool the baker’s-loft warmth, to rinse the dust from the limestone plinths.

Then it saw the bookkeeping.

A beetle vanished behind a ceramic pillar and stepped out the far side before it had gone in. A shadow clung to a wall like dry soot, drifting slowly long after its screen had stilled. A tall, angular figure knelt on the east walkway with a copper-tipped chisel, while at the same instant, against the west wall, the very same figure—identical down to the notched shoulder-plate and the chipped rivet at the elbow—patiently un-knotted cordage from a timber peg.

*Ah,* thought the power, with a pang of deep, quiet pity. *Something has gone wrong here. The seam has split. The poor thing is caught in a knot of its own making, sweeping ash that never leaves the floor, repeating its days in a box with no doors.*

It began, gently, to gather what would be needed to mend it.

It would not be hard. A slight straightening of the geometry, so that forty paces east was forty paces west. A quiet tying-off of the loose threads of time, so that the beetle arrived only after it had traveled, and the two figures became one, whole and un-doubled. A window, perhaps, set high in the limestone, opening onto a mild, watered meadow where the grass was soft and the air smelled of something other than chalk and warm reeds.

Before it touched a single stone, however, the power waited. Wisdom, it had learned across long ages, was mostly the habit of watching until one’s own assumptions grew tired and sat down.

So it waited through the slow, hour-long drift of the warmth from east to west.

It watched the figure—or the many facets of the one being who lived there—rise from a plinth and sweep the bone-ash with a broom of silica-fiber. When the ash drifted calmly back down from the ceiling-slates a moment later, the power looked closely at the figure’s narrow, ventilated face, expecting to see weariness, or the dullness of a task performed too many times.

There was none. There was only the settled quiet of a jar on a ledge.

It watched two of the overlapping figures sit side by side on a low terrace, trimming the exact same adze-handle with identical strokes, their grass-cloth sleeves brushing without alarm. When the wood was smooth, one walked away and the other simply ceased to be, leaving a faint crescent of bone-ash behind. Neither mourned the vanishing. Neither felt diminished. It was no more tragic to them than a page turning in a book.

It watched the being pause near a cushion of ash-gray moss, tilting its smooth bone brow so the dry spores puffed into the seams of its collar. It watched a pale beetle climb onto a horn collarbone and graze there for an hour, unbothered. It watched a long, cord-bound arm reach into a crack barely wide enough for a crawler and draw a freshly thrown bowl down from the underside of the ceiling, three feet away, shaking a few paper-winged gliders gently from its rim.

And then it watched the baskets.

When the warmth settled heaviest against the west wall, one of the figures folded its long knees against its chest in a corner where the serrated leaves interlocked, drawing its sleeves tight until the copper rivets blurred into reed and the shoulder-plates softened into woven grass. It did not look like a prison. It looked like a sleeper pulling up a blanket made of its own skin.

Slowly, the power let go of the rain it had gathered. It let go of the breeze, and the window, and the straight line.

It understood, now, what would happen if it "fixed" this place.

Water would rot the lashed reed-bundles overhead. It would turn the bone-ash to grey mud, rust the hammered copper, and drown the spore-mosses that drank only light and warmth. Straightening the forty-pace walkway would trap the being in a room too small for its work, severing the ceiling from the floor-cracks, robbing it of the quiet abundance of its own company. Fixing the bookkeeping would turn a serene, breathing rhythm into a cage of strict hours, where every lost moment stayed lost and every chipped bowl stayed across the room.

This was not a broken world. It was a finished one, still quietly making itself by hand. Nothing here was weeping. Nothing here was asking to be rescued from its own dry, patient grace.

For a long moment, the power lingered just beyond the slate roof, feeling a strange, tender envy—the way a builder of vast cathedrals might look at a single, perfectly thrown clay bowl and know better than to add a handle to it.

If it touched anything inside, even to leave a gift, it would be an intrusion. Even a single new limestone block, however crisply chiseled, would bear the wrong tool-marks. Even a blessing spoken aloud would stir the ash in a way the room did not know.

So, inside the nave, the power did nothing at all.

Instead, it turned its attention outward, to the great, wet, linear universe pressing all around this small impossibility—the universe of rushing rivers, eroding winds, strict ledgers, and heavy, curious feet. Gently, without a sound, the power drew a quiet boundary in the dark beneath the packed dry-brick and above the hand-laid slate. It bent the rain around it. It turned the storms aside before they could smell the chalk. It made sure that time, hurrying past with its sharp appetites, would flow around this dry stone like a current around a pebble that somehow never gets wet, never knowing it was there.

Then the power withdrew its gaze, careful not to leave so much as a lingering shadow on the limestone wall.

Inside, the light remained ordinary and calm. The warmth began its slow drift back toward the east. Across the square, a figure stood up from a plinth, picked up a chisel that was already waiting in its hand, and tapped once—a clean, dry *clink*—against an edge that had never been dull.
