If a benevolent and wise power were to turn its attention toward this needle of basalt, it would first have to learn how to arrive without breaking anything.

It could not come as light. Though nothing here has eyes, the pale ones’ chalk-thin skin and the damp, exposed seams of the sleepers’ hides are made for the dark; a sudden radiance would dry the mist off the walls and scorch the stubborn liverworts in their cracks. It could not come as warmth, or as a voice, or even as a heavy wind, for the hollow is a tuned instrument of stone and breath, and any loud thing would drown out the *tock... tock...* by which the scavengers know where the edge of a drop lies.

So it would arrive as attention only—weightless, cool, and no thicker than the cloud-tendrils shearing across the bruised-indigo slit forty meters above.

From the upper lip, it would look down and feel, at first, the sharp, familiar ache of mercy.

Benevolence, before wisdom catches up to it, always wants to mend. Looking into the cleft, the power would see a thousand small hardships it could undo with a thought. It would see the mica-wings shivering without rest just to keep their flight muscles from freezing in the thin air. It would see the sleepers locked by blackened pitons into glass-sharp rock, dragging a single breath for thirty seconds to harvest a scrap of oxygen. It would see the pale ones crawling belly-down over another creature’s back because the stone itself is too cruel for their skin, tapping splinters of basalt in the dark to beg news of a fallen carcass. It would see the larvae turning in finger-deep pools cold enough to ache, waiting years to grow the width of a fingernail.

How easy it would be to offer a handful of gentle miracles.

The power could widen the overhead slit by a single span, letting in a kinder column of sun to warm the slate. It could thicken the air in the cleft, just enough that the sleepers’ bellows-lungs would not have to labor so hard. It could round the razor edges of the scree, soften the basalt into something like river-stone, and sweeten the bitter fluid inside the bladder-fronds. It could send a richer rain down the walls so the pools at the bottom would brim and the larvae could grow in months instead of years.

Every one of those mercies would lie ready in its hand. And then wisdom would trace each one to its end.

Widen the slit, and the summit wind would tear straight down into the nave. The sleepers’ hides would crack in the dry draft; the mica-wings would be blown out over the void and lost; the pale ones would lose the stillness that lets them hear a shard shift twenty meters away.

Thicken the air or warm the stone, and the heavy oil of the cold would thin. The sleepers’ slow hearts would race; they would strip the walls of liverwort in a season and starve. The larvae would hatch in swarms, choking the pools, and the rot of too much life would sour the shallow basins until nothing could breathe at the bottom.

Smooth the basalt, and the sleepers’ chitinous tips would find no hairline fractures to grip; they would slide in a grey avalanche to the floor. The pale ones would find no natural splinters to bind with frond-fiber, no sharp edges to cleave their tools. The angular basins would drain away into featureless gravel.

To save them from the mountain would be to unmake them. They are not trapped in the cleft, waiting for rescue. They are the cleft’s own answer to altitude and stone—four tiers of patience, each one holding the others in place.

Watching from the dark, the power would understand that the hardest work of benevolence is not giving, but withholding.

A gust of high-altitude wind would shear across the summit just then, tearing a dozen mica-wings loose from the ceiling. They would begin their slow, papery spiral down through the dark—most catching the updraft halfway, one of them heavy with condensation, drifting all the way past the sighing walls toward the floor.

The power’s first instinct would be to catch it—to buoy that single, thumb-sized shimmer of crystal back up to the indigo light.

Instead, it would hold completely still. It would let the mica-wing strike the scree with a faint, dry *tick*. It would watch, twenty meters away, a pale one pause on the pleated flank of a sleeper, its dish-shaped ears rotating independently to isolate that tiny sound from the surf of ten thousand lungs. It would watch the long, calloused fingers find their purchase and begin the slow, deliberate crawl downward.

If the power did anything at all, it would be something smaller than a breath and invisible to everything inside the stone. Perhaps it would simply stay for the length of one sleeper’s exhale—thirty seconds of quiet witness, matching its stillness to the pace of dripping water—and then withdraw its hand, leaving the sharp edges sharp, the air thin, the dark intact.

And perhaps, as it turned away from the needle’s crown, it would leave behind only the oldest and rarest gift a great power can offer to a small, strange world: it would leave it alone, and let it keep its centuries.
