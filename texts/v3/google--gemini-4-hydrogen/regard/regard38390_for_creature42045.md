At first, the power came with its hands full of gifts.

From the high, thin reaches above the world, the basin looked like an injury—a raw, howling socket sunk into the worn-down molars of a dead range. The power saw the brutal math of the place: the three-degree plunges when a cloud crossed the sun, the rifle-crack of freezing stone, the deafening organ-pipe shriek that never relented. It saw compact, weathered figures creeping through wet, shadowed alleys without fire, without iron, without a single roof to keep the sky out, plugging their own ears with wax and huddling in the pitch dark beneath the flagstones like animals waiting out a siege.

A lesser kindness would have acted at once. 

The power reached toward the mountain rim, ready to lay a palm across the wind and hush the basin into peace. It was ready to coax a deeper, steadier warmth up through the bedrock, to dry the slick, dished ramps, to raise the shattered roofs and give those patient, callused hands mortar that would not crack and timber that would not rot.

Then, just before its shadow touched the upper terraces, the power paused.

A dry-stacked wall without mortar should have been scree ten thousand winters ago. Yet the rib-thin archways stood. The power drew its grander mercies back, folded its vast attention down to the scale of a single cobble, and stepped into the basin—not into the roaring air, which blinds and deafens, but onto the glassy flagstones of the central plaza.

It stood still. And because it was wise, it stopped listening with the sky and began to listen through the stone.

At once, the roar changed its meaning. Through the soles of the flags, the shriek was not chaos; it was a vast, steady lung. The power felt a cloud shadow cross the bowl, felt the sudden, brittle tightening of the slate as the moisture in the pores turned to needles—and felt, three alleys away, the quick, deliberate shift of weight as a lichen-cloaked figure rose from a crouch.

The power watched. It watched the figure climb a slick, undulating stair inside a chimney-tower, reach into a wind-bored aperture, and nudge a single water-rounded cobble by the width of a thumb. The pitch of the tower dropped a fraction, steadied, and thrummed through the masonry like a struck bell. Along the inner wall, sheets of rime-ice that had begun to weld the slate together shivered, let go, and pattered harmlessly down into the moss.

It watched two of them meet in the gloom of a root-bound passage. No word was spoken. One rested a flat palm against the other’s collarbone; the other drew a smooth cobble from a belt-pouch and tapped the wall between them. *Clack-clack.* The vibration ran cleanly through thirty feet of dry-stacked slate, crisp as a bell in still water, bypassing the gale entirely.

It watched them tend the woody mats of ancient root-fiber, coaxing pale, tough tendrils into fresh frost-splits so the living wood could lock the stone before the next freeze. It watched them pluck the turgid bladder-worts just before they popped, and gather the shed fleece snagged on the root-mats, and step aside without hurry when a pale, heavy-shouldered beast came *tock-tock* across the glassy channels.

Understanding arrived like a sudden clarity of air.

To hush the wind here would be to freeze the town solid and split it into gravel by morning. To warm the basin would be to wither the beard-lichen, parch the cushion-moss, and starve the hydraulic plants that gave them drink. To give them mortar would make the walls rigid, so they could no longer flex and be shimmed; to give them fire would choke the low, insulated burrows beneath the plaza; to unseal their ears would be to break the quiet, bone-deep communion by which they knew every footstep and every fracture in their world.

They were not captives of a harsh place. They were the instrument’s own players, and the song they kept was older than memory.

So the power’s first act of benevolence—and its hardest—was to withhold every miracle it had brought. It laid down the warmth, the silence, the iron, and the timber outside the rim of the basin, and left them there untouched.

Instead, it waited until the thin sun dropped behind the horizon and the basin locked into iron frost.

When the bladder-worts shrank into pale knots and the upper town became a brittle, shrieking cage, the inhabitants crawled through the low, root-choked apertures beneath the central plaza. In the pitch dark, insulated by ten feet of swollen moss and slate, they pressed shoulder to shoulder with the warm, heavy-breathing beasts, waiting for morning.

The power sank its presence gently into the bedrock beneath them—not changing the temperature by a single degree, lest the condensation cycle above falter, and not altering a single stone. It simply did three small things, so quiet they could never be mistaken for interference, only for the mountain’s own good fortune.

First, it made a promise to the wind. In the deepest, deadest nights of the high-altitude winter, when the great air currents sometimes falter and drop away entirely—leaving the chimney-towers silent long enough for the rime-ice to lock the walls into a single, fatal block—the power would breathe across the worn rim of the basin. Never a gale, never a warm draft: just enough of a steady, cool current to keep the organ-pipes humming until the sky remembered its own breath.

Second, it turned its attention to the small margins where weariness and age live. When an elder’s stiff fingers searched the dark drainage channels for a slate shim to save a splitting arch, a wedge of the exact right thickness would happen to lie beneath their palm. When a young one slipped on a newly iced ramp in the gloom, the moss beneath their heel would hold a fraction firmer than before. Nothing that broke the craft; nothing that stole the pride of the patient geometry. Only the quiet removal of needless ruin.

And third, in the pitch dark beneath the central plaza, while the wind-pipes groaned overhead and the beasts breathed slowly in the damp, the power pressed its metaphorical palm to the underside of the flagstones.

It sent a single, low, unhurried vibration up through the slate—not loud enough to disturb the sleepers, not sharp enough to mimic a fracture. Just a slow, steady *clack-clack* in the deep stone, the cadence of a neighbor’s cobble against a shared wall.

Down in the dark, a few of the bare, callused feet pressed against the floor felt it travel up through their heels and settle behind their jaws. They did not startle, and they did not look for a voice in the air. One of them simply shifted a shoulder closer to a warm, fleeced flank, rested a flat palm against the cool stone beneath them in answer, and went back to sleep, waiting for the sun.
