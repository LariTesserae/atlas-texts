At first, the power did nothing at all, because it knew better than to arrive in a quiet house with its hands full.

It did not descend as a pillar of light, nor did it speak from the bright, unremarkable sky. Had it spoken through the air, the inhabitants would not have heard it; had it dimmed the sun to offer them shade, their lidless eyes, tuned to the glare off the shallow pans, would only have been confused. Instead, the power simply turned its attention downward, narrowing its vast awareness until it could feel the slow, heavy *thrum* of the vascular bladders beneath the bone-ivory floor.

It listened for a long time.

It felt the way the living ribs carried the dampened notes of a palm pressed against an arch half a mile away. It watched an inhabitant kneel by a stepped basin, pressing a spatulate hand over a speck of wind-blown silt until the bitter salve dissolved it, leaving the plant-skin as glossy and unblemished as an hour-old shoot. It watched two others gather at a seam where a dormant bud-scale had begun to swell, leaning their weight into the neighboring buttresses until the local pulse quieted and the shoot subsided back into sleep.

Watching this, the power felt the first, sharp tug of a lesser kind of pity—the urge to *fix*.

It saw how bare the plateau was. It saw the deafening, relentless roar of the siphon-spires, the endless brightness with nowhere to hide, the vast distances between a dozen solitary figures who never touched except to soothe a seam. Above all, it saw the slow arithmetic of their kind: they did not multiply, and yet, over spans of years, one would walk out to a quiet pan, lie down along a rib, and weld itself into the floor.

A lesser power might have reached down and altered the terms. It might have quieted the roaring water so the beings could rest, forgetting that the roar was the very medium of their speech. It might have given them seasons, or night, or the capacity to bear young, forgetting that a world without rot cannot bear crowding, and that appetite would only teach them to tear the skin of the world. It might have touched the dormant bud-scales and commanded the plateau to finally *finish*—to burst into leaf and flower and towering canopy.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

To force this place into bloom would be to break a held breath. The plateau was not stunted; it was poised. Its beauty lay precisely in that impossible threshold—the moment of just having come into being, held open by twelve pairs of patient, wet hands. And the beings themselves were not prisoners of the engine; they were its consciousness, the quiet will that kept the world from choking on its own abundance.

Still, benevolence is not indifference. Having understood what it must not touch, the power looked outward, beyond the shallow rim of the plateau, to the lower valleys and the wide, turbulent sky.

There, it saw the things that no pair of spatulate hands could dissolve: the ash-clouds of distant mountains waiting to drift upward, the killing frosts that sometimes climbed too high, the virulent blights carried on storm-winds that could overwhelm the pale-jade skin in a single night.

Without altering a drop of tepid water, without casting a shadow across the pans, the power set a gentle, invisible curvature in the high air above the rim. It did not seal the plateau off—the faint mist still rose into the open sky, and the rare, stray spores from the valleys still drifted down now and then, giving the inhabitants their quiet, purposeful work along the causeways. But the ruinous storms, the heavy ash, and the freezing gales would find the air above the basin strangely slippery, sliding around the rim and down into the valleys without ever touching the living floor.

Then the power looked inward once more, to the slow arithmetic it could not—and would not—undo.

It knew that even with the sky kept gentle, the centuries would still pass. One by one, over spans of time too long to measure on an unweathered floor, the inhabitants would slow down. One by one, they would walk out to the quietest corners of the pans and give themselves back to the ribs, until at last there were no hands left to quiet the seams.

The power did not forbid them that rest. To keep them walking forever would be a cruelty disguised as preservation. Instead, it made a silent, unhurried promise to the engine beneath the floor: that it would keep watch, and when the very last of the tenders lay down along a low rib and welded skin to skin until the boundary was forgotten—only then, when no one remained to hold the threshold, would the power gently lift its hand from the bud-scales, and let the plateau finally open, and grow wild, and finish what it had begun.

Until that distant day, the power asked for nothing and announced nothing.

Yet now and then, when one of the inhabitants grew slow and walked out to the edge of a quiet pan, far from the loudest spires, to press its wet cheek and long limbs against the pale-jade skin for the last time—it was no longer entirely alone. The power would bring its vast attention down to that single, unremarkable rib, matching its stillness to the slowing pulse, and stay quietly beside it in the warm water until the seam softened, and the boundary was forgotten, and one more smooth ridge hummed softly under the open sky.
