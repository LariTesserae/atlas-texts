If a power that was both benevolent and wise turned its attention to this shelf, it would not arrive with a sound. A voice out of the sky would scatter in the thin air before it reached the cooling towers, and a shadow across the unblinking sun would only make the tenders pause, squinting up through their inner lids, wondering if a storm had somehow climbed above the cloud-lid.

Instead, its attention would settle the way heat settles into iron—gradually, without announcement, until it was simply present in the frame of the place.

At first, looking down from the height of its own nature, the power would feel the oldest reflex of kindness: the urge to mend what looks starved. 

To eyes accustomed to greener worlds, the shelf looks like an unfinished sentence. There is no soil in the cracks, no moss to soften the knife-edged basalt, no bird to break the dry *tick* of a distant hammer. The air is so thin that the people who live here must ration every breath, drawing it in on a thirty-second tide and wringing it dry before letting it go. Their water is hot, oily, and trapped in dark sumps. Their skin is the color of unglazed clay, dusted with salt-sharp ash.

A merely benevolent power—one that pitied before it understood—might have reached down with a gentle hand and thickened the air. It might have coaxed a veil of high cloud over the glare, or sent a soft, steady rain to wash the crystalline grit from the roofs and wake whatever dormant spores lay sleeping in the lower world.

Wisdom would stay its hand.

For as the power watched, slowing its own perception to match the turning of the heat-exchanger spokes, it would trace what those mercies would actually do. 

Thicken the air, and the tenders’ deep, narrow chests—built to draw a long, slow measure from emptiness—would labor and drown in the sudden weight. Bring rain, and the uninsulated pipes would rust through in a season; the precious, sulfur-heavy sumps would overflow and dilute, starving the pale, waxy ore-corals in the dark; the silica-backed scuttlers would be washed off the ledges, and the cartilage-winged gliders would drop like wet parchment into the haze. Coax grass into the seams, and the ash would turn to mud; the iron would go quiet under the noise of wind in leaves, and the tenders would lose the clean, dry hum through the soles of their sandals that tells them how their world is breathing.

They were not suffering here. They had made a covenant with raw stone and trickle-current, and every piece of the place—from the leathery filter-feeders on the manifolds to the brass-tipped rods in the tenders’ hands—fit together with zero slack. To soften it would be to break it.

So the power would do nothing, at first. It would simply stay and watch, measuring time not by the pinned sun, but as the tenders did: by the millimeter-thick accretion of copper on the vat plates.

It would watch a tender pause at a junction box, press a clay rod to a riveted pipe, and rest their jaw against the warm metal to hear a message traveling half a mile through the stanchions. It would watch them step around the filter-feeders without disturbing them, and scrape salt-crust down into the basalt fissures where the stone-plated scuttlers waited. It would follow them into the cool, dim pockets behind the slag walls, where thin threads of condensation bled into pale clay tiles, smelling of wet earth where no earth existed. And at the end of a long cycle, it would stand unseen beside two of them on the rim of the terrace, watching a glider drift past at eye level over the flat white sea of cloud.

In that stillness, listening the way the tenders listened—not through the thin air, but through the solid frame—the power would finally notice something the tenders could not yet feel.

Miles beneath the vent-field, below the deepest borehole and the dark sumps, the shelf was too young. The crust had pushed upward in a single violent heave and stopped hard. Deep in the blind dark where the basalt met the planet’s furnace, the rock was cooling unevenly. A brittle, crooked tension was locking itself along a hidden seam—not enough to shake the surface today, or even in this generation, but enough that in a few dozen cycles the heat-exchangers would begin to starve as the vents pinched shut, or a sudden, dry shear would split the basin and drain the sumps into the haze below.

Here, at last, was a place where kindness and wisdom agreed.

The power did not repair the world from the sky. It sank its attention down through the sintered slag, past the humming busbars and the boiling manifolds, into the unweathered stone itself. And there, with the patience of a tender easing a sticking valve in the third sump, it tuned the rock.

No tremor reached the alleys. Not a drop of oily water spilled from a catch-pan; not a single brittle ore-coral snapped in the dark. Deep underground, the locked seam eased by fractions of a millimeter over the span of an afternoon, bleeding off its shear into a wide, even bed of warm stone. The throat of the central vent settled open—neither widening into a rupture nor pinching closed—promising a steady, quiet pulse of heat for a thousand cycles more.

Up on the upper terrace, a tender walking between the cooling towers, braided grounding-strap trailing against their shin, suddenly stopped.

Through the copper nails of their sandals, the continuous vibration of the city—that faint, distant train that never quite arrives—had changed. It was no louder, and no faster. Only cleaner. A slight, unnoticed dissonance in the deep bass beneath the refinery had smoothed itself out, like a heavy shaft settling true into its bearing.

The tender stood motionless for a full half-minute, drawing one slow breath of the kiln-dry air. Then they knelt, pressed the brass tip of their clay rod to a sweating pipe, and rested their palm against the iron.

Three streets away, another tender paused beside a vat, feeling the same quiet trueness rise through the soles of their feet.

Neither could have said what had happened. Nothing in the city was broken, and nothing had been added. After a long moment, the first tender tapped twice against the pipe—*tick, tick*, a small acknowledgment sent into the bolted frame of the world—and walked on into the shadow of the exchangers.

Above the rim of the shelf, the unblinking sun shone on the bone-pale roofs. And the power, having left no monument and asked for no recognition, withdrew its attention as quietly as a glider slipping out over the edge of the cloud.
