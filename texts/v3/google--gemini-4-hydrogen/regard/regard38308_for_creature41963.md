It would not come as a light. Light, here, is a thin and precious currency, measured out in grey slivers through the ceiling faults; to pour more of it into the valley would be to blind the pale-ringed eyes that have learned to read the sheen of a damp seep fifty yards off, and to scorch the translucent ferns clinging to the mist.

Nor would it come as a voice. A voice is only air, and the valley already has more sound than it can hold.

Instead, the power’s attention would arrive the way condensation arrives on the limestone: slowly, without announcement, settling into the cold stone and the roaring air until it had taken the measure of the whole rift.

For a long time, it would do nothing at all. It would simply watch.

It would watch the flat pewter haze dissolve the far walls. It would watch the hundred thousand bats and swiftlets hanging in their restless, chittering carpets, and the pale crickets twitching in the dry talus, and the slick salamanders crowding the mineral pools. And then it would notice the small, pale figures moving through the margin of the stone—how they did not fight the roar, did not stack rock upon rock, did not try to hurry a world that had no hours.

A lesser kindness—one that knew pity better than it knew wisdom—would have reached in at once to mend what looked like hardship.

It might have thought to quiet the cavern, lifting the deafening wash of wings and insect-clicks so the people could speak to one another in the open air. Yet the wise power would see that the noise is the sound of the valley’s abundance; to silence it would be to empty the ledges of meat, of oil, of the soft, musk-scented hides that keep the ceiling’s cold drip from their skin.

It might have thought to smooth the young, glass-sharp bedrock so it would no longer chew the edges of their folded leather wraps, or to bring them timber for a hearth and a warm fire against the stone-chilled air. Yet it would see that fire in a sealed rift is a slow poison, turning the grey air to a choking storm that would smother the roosts and drive the people out into a brighter, harsher world they were not made for. And it would see that the unweathered limestone, hard and unforgiving as it is, is the very thing that carries the clean, unmistakable *tock* of a cobble from one companion’s hand to another’s bare soles.

It might even have thought to lift them up through the fault-lines to the surface, where there are seasons, and rivers, and soft earth. But looking at their wide, bowed shoulders, their steady, unhurried hands, and the quiet trust with which they tucked their flint flakes and copper hooks back into the dark crevices—knowing the stone would keep them safe until they returned—the power would understand that these people were not trapped here. They were home.

So the power would withhold its hand from the floor of the valley. It would not touch the battered river cobbles resting beside the snail shells. It would not dull the flint, or soften the stone, or wake the still air with a wind.

Instead, it would turn its attention upward, into the blind, crushing weight of the earth above the ceiling, where the valley itself had no eyes and no craft.

There, along the ancient fault-lines hundreds of feet overhead, the mountain was always shifting, imperceptibly slow, pressing inward on the narrow, crooked fissures that fed the rift. Left to the indifferent geology of the surface, a careless tremor or a century of patient weight might one day pinch those slits shut—cutting off the thin grey light, stalling the slow breath of air, leaving the mineral pools to stagnate in absolute dark.

Quietly, without a sound that could compete with the swiftlets, the power would lay a steadiness into the upper rock. Not a rigid wall—stone must still be stone—but a gentle, enduring balance, so the fissures would hold their width against the grinding of the crust. And in the unseen veins of limestone feeding the damp seeps, it would ensure the slow condensation kept its rhythm, even through long droughts on the surface that the valley-dwellers would never know had happened.

That would be all.

Below, on the flat, fractured floor, one of the foragers would lift a river-rounded cobble and tap it twice against the bedrock to call a companion’s eye to a cluster of nests. The sharp pulse would run cleanly through the limestone, arriving in the soles of the other’s feet, and the two of them would move on together into the pewter haze, never knowing they had been watched, or loved, or kept.

And the wise power, satisfied that nothing had been broken in the name of helping, would withdraw—leaving the damp tools in the dark, and the endless, roaring stone closing over where they stood.
