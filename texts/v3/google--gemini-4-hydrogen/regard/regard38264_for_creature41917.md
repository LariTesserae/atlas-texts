It is not a loud thing that draws the attention—only a rhythm.

Deep beneath the strata where roots stop and even groundwater forgets to go, a small, dry *tick, tick, tick, tick* taps against the seam between stone and nothing. Someone is asking whether the world intends to hold.

A power that is both wise and benevolent does not arrive like a lantern lowered into a cellar, nor like a pickaxe breaking through the roof. Warmth here would shrivel the feathery gills of the grazers and turn the wet chalk to dust; open sky would scatter the ribbon-swimmers like ash and blind the creature whose whole face is made of frosted glass. A clumsy mercy is only another kind of collapse.

So the power does nothing at first. It simply *stays*—fitting itself into the quiet between one weather cycle and the next, watching the room finish nothing and eat everything.

It watches the pale, hound-sized keeper stack sharp pebbles around a dimple to slow the floor’s thirst. It watches the creature bend its wedge-shaped head over the teacup-sized mirror, reading the second-ahead reflection so it won't be caught by a pinching corner. It watches it drag its spur down the soft clay—*tick, tick, tick*—and shudder backward when the spur passes into sheer white absence. And it watches the creature retreat to the calcified ribcage, unable to rest for more than a few moments before the wet-bone mineral creeps up its lower joints, forcing it to scrape itself raw against the vertebrae just to stay alive.

The power understands, then, what is wrong with the room.

It is not that the chamber is dark, or cold, or small, or colorless. The creature does not ache for sunlight or green leaves; it is made of chalk and silver, and it has already built a life out of mist and geometry. What is wrong is that the room was left *untrustworthy*. The work-lights were left burning, the floor was left hungry, and the walls were left half-unmade, so nothing inside can ever set its weight down.

To fix this by turning the chamber into a garden would be to erase the one who lives in it. To fix this by locking the stone into dead, finished bedrock would be to bury it alive.

So the power reaches in—not with hands, and not with words, but with the quiet patience of a builder returning to a forgotten room to finish only what was left dangerous.

First, it tends to the absence.

Where the walls thin into that terrifying, unresisting white nothingness, the power does not slam down heavy granite. It simply *completes the thought*. The sheer white gaps settle into cool, honest limestone—still pale, still damp, still smelling of wet chalk, but solid all the way through. The over-folded corners ease just enough that the air stops snagging on itself, though the creases remain gentle enough for the keeper’s narrow body to slip into when it wants to be hidden.

Next, it touches the floor’s impatience.

It does not take away the weather cycle; the swimmers need the mist, and the moss needs the sweat of the walls. Instead, it teaches the rock how to be full. When the dimples drink the condensation now, they drink slowly, and they stop before the stone grows greedy. The shed skins of the grazers still stiffen into ridges, because that is how this place grows, but the mineral no longer creeps up living joints.

And finally, it remembers the work-lights.

For the first time since the pocket closed, the shadowless white glare softens. It does not go pitch-black—that would be a different cruelty—but dims into a cool, pearlescent twilight, like moonlight filtered through deep ice. For the first time, the calcified ribcage casts a thin, soft shadow across the floor.

Then the power steps back, leaving no monument, no voice, no demand for gratitude. It leaves the pebble rings intact. It leaves the clay patches soft enough to mark.

---

Down in the quiet, the keeper notices.

When the next weather cycle arrives, the shimmer is gentler. The walls sweat their fat, slow beads into the dimples, and the teacup-sized rings hold the water longer than they ever have before. The keeper bends its frosted-glass face over the nearest pool.

In the reflection, there is no collapsing ceiling, no pinching seam—only a grazer cropping moss a second before it reaches the ribcage, and a ribbon-swimmer drifting whole and un-split through a corner that no longer tears it into echoes.

Curious, cautious, the keeper moves to the lower wall. It rears on its hindmost joints, extends its calcified spur, and drags it downward through the clay.

*Tick, tick, tick, tick.*

The spur meets cool, steady resistance. No void waiting behind it. No sudden giveaway into white nothingness. It tests another patch, and another, its heavy mandibles clicking softly as it reads the new certainty of the stone.

The light dims into its first cool rest.

Slowly, the keeper turns back toward the center of the chamber. It steps into the calcified ribcage, beside the lush tuft of liverwort, and lowers its segmented body into the shadow of the vertebrae. It waits for the floor to begin creeping up its joints.

Nothing creeps. The wet-bone mineral stays still and cool beneath it, content simply to hold its weight.

For a long moment, the creature stays poised to scrape itself free. Then, gradually, the milky pulse along its spine slows. Its pale palps fold neatly beneath its worn mandibles, and for the first time in a room that has finally decided to be real, the keeper rests.
