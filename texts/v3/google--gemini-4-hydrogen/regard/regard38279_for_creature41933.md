It would arrive without breaking the surface of the mist.

A lesser power—one merely strong, or merely eager to be kind—might have announced itself with a parting of the high geometric veil, letting raw starlight pour down into the bowl like a spilled lamp. It might have warmed the air until the condensation vanished from the basalt, softened the razor-edged stone into forgiving soil, or drawn the scattered clusters of the city closer together so the tall, dark figures would not have to wait so long between one tremor and the next.

Wisdom, however, begins by listening. And benevolence, when it is patient enough, knows how to keep its hands still.

So the power would fold its presence small and quiet, slipping through the bruised twilight without stirring a single silver spore, without adding even the faintest hum to the braided gravity beneath the plazas. It would rest its attention lightly on the rim of the basin and simply watch, and feel, until it understood the grammar of the place.

It would see the moisture beading along the collarbone of a motionless inhabitant, and at first, a reflex of pity might stir—an impulse to offer dryness, warmth, a roof. Then it would watch the figure kneel by a drainage channel to drink, watch it stand with long arms spread in a drifting cloud of spores, and recognize that the coldness of the skin is not a deprivation, but an instrument: a way of drinking the air, of belonging to the same thermal hush as the matte-black towers.

It would look at the harsh, unweathered rock and think of smoothing it—until it felt the *thrum* travel up from a viaduct, clean and sharp through three kilometers of unbroken basalt. Then it would understand why the stone must stay young. Deep soil would muffle the words. Softness would turn their language into a blurred, unreadable murmur. The sharp, glassy shelves are the sounding board of their world, and the kilometers of empty terrace between their homes are not loneliness, but the silence required for a sentence to remain whole.

It would look at the out-of-phase walls, shimmering like oil in the dimness, and see no flaw to mend—only a geometry that the milky eyes beneath the heavy brow ridges already read without effort.

Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is crying out to be rescued from itself.

To alter the valley—to brighten it, dry it, crowd it, or explain it—would be to erase the very thing that makes it live. A wise power knows that the rarest, hardest gift to give a self-contained world is to leave it entirely unmarred by one’s own good intentions.

And yet, it would not leave empty-handed, nor leave the valley quite unguarded.

Far above the bruised cloud-bank, miles beyond the reach of the faint geometric lattice and the highest drifting spores, the universe is louder and less careful. There are wandering stones that fall without warning, stellar winds that strip thin atmospheres, and younger, noisier travelers who cross the dark without checking what sleeps below.

There, in the high cold outside the veil, the power would do its only work.

It would weave a quietness around the upper sky—invisible, weightless, asking nothing and offering no sign. A gentle curvature in the dark to turn aside stray debris before it could strike the basin and crack its acoustics; a subtle steadying of the outer air so the geometric lattice above would never tear and let in the blinding glare of the star. Not a cage, and not a ceiling—only the soft, unnoticed shelter of a cupped hand held far above a candle flame, keeping the draft away without touching the light.

Down in the valley, nothing would change. The *plip* of condensed water would still mark the slow hours in the mirror-still pools. The six-limbed gliders would still mistake the motionless figures for outcrops of stone.

And when the power finally withdrew its attention, it would take care to leave not a single flattened oval in the pale, spongy moss—so that the next clean tremor, rising through the heel of a waiting inhabitant and traveling across three kilometers of young basalt, would find the stone as clear and undisturbed as it had always been.
