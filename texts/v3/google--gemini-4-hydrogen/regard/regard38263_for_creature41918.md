At first, the power—accustomed to the sharp, fast-burning worlds below, where things are built of stone and iron and spoken into the wind—would look through the double ceiling of cloud and Overweave and feel the familiar ache of pity.

From a great height, the plateau looks like a place where time has stalled. There is no fire here. There is no dry ground, no wheel, no song that carries past three paces of wet moss. In the bruised, olive-and-tea twilight, long-limbed shadows stoop over the bog, speechless and slow, pressing their palms to hollow reeds and lingering beside fragments of rock they do not know how to name.

A merely powerful being might have parted the clouds to let the sun in. A merely generous one might have raised a dry island of stone in the center of the mat, or dropped a spark of fire, or whispered the shapes of words into the weavers’ gill-like folds.

But wisdom does not act at the speed of its own impatience. Before it touched a single frond, the power would slow itself down.

It would quiet its own brightness until it was dimmer than the water-bladder epiphytes. It would shed its weight so that, if it stood upon the thirty-meter mattress of peat and roots, not a single drop of clear water would well around its ankles, and no tremor would travel down the living wood to startle a weaver resting in an alcove half a mile away.

And then, for a long while—perhaps for the twenty years it takes a kin-group to walk a single circuit—it would do nothing at all except listen.

It would learn the *plink* and *pat* of the canopy-drip. It would follow the hour-long groan of a water-swollen reed-column shifting its weight. It would watch a weaver gently bruise two young shoots with its flat teeth, hook them together with blunt claws, and walk away into the haze—trusting that a cousin, drawn by a fading trace of musk, would return in fifteen years to braid the next layer. It would feel the pressure-waves pulsing through the water-filled tubes: not the absence of language, but a speech so patient it refuses to tear the air.

It would see the truce with the moss-backed grazers, the quiet tenderness of stripping feelers from a fallen drifter so its death might hold up a cradle for the living, and the deep, unhurried safety of a world that has never known the terror of a chase.

And it would understand that there is nothing here to fix.

If it parted the clouds, the silvery bryophytes would scorch and dry, and the lidless, pondwater eyes would burn. If it gave them tools, the cartilage-soft world would learn the meaning of an edge. If it explained the granite nubs—telling them of ancient hands that once stacked and carved before the plants swallowed the stone—it would only shrink a mystery that currently gives the weavers a reason to pause, press their palms together in shared wonder, and leave a lasting mark of themselves in a world that otherwise absorbs everything back into itself.

So the power would withhold every gift it had thought to bring.

Instead, what it would do is smaller, and harder, and unseen:

Far below the plateau, in the lower world where winds blow hard and ambitions grow sharp, it would become a quiet boundary. If ever a storm gathered with enough fury to tear the Overweave, the power would gently turn the gale aside. If ever the rising vapor from the warm earth below began to thin or cool, it would steady the current, ensuring the air above always had enough moisture to weep. And if ever the loud, hurried feet of the lower world began to climb the ancient slopes toward the tableland, the power would thicken the haze at the rim—not with violence, not with a wall of stone, but with a gentle, disorienting drift that turns travelers softly back toward the sun, leaving them with nothing more than the vague memory of mist.

Then, having set its quiet guard miles below, the power would return its attention to the plateau one last time.

It would not speak. It would not shine. It would simply rest its presence against the endless, living mat for the length of a single decade—letting itself be taught how to be still, and leaving no footprint in the moss when it withdrew.
