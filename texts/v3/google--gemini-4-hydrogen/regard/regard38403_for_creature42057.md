At first, the impulse was to mend it.

A power that loves living things cannot look into a twenty-meter bowl of salt-flour, rust, and bone-dry chitin without feeling the old, familiar ache to *soften* it. Standing at the lip of the sinkhole, looking down from the erased plain into that deafening chimney of iron and tooth, the first thought is always of rain. A cool, patient drizzle to wash the salt from the lizards’ eyes. A handful of moss to cushion the sharp edges of the ducts. A quiet word to slow the grinding gears, so the creatures below might rest from their endless, clattering vigilance.

But wisdom is slower than pity. Before it reached out a hand, the power knelt at the rim and waited, letting the wall of noise separate into its voices.

It watched the **tallow-shields** lock their serrated rims into a copper pavement as the intake cowls coughed, and understood what rain would do: the salt-flour floor would turn to scalding brine, the beetles’ iron-heavy shells would pit and seize, and the stroke-worms beneath would drown in their own burrows.

It watched the **mica-skinks** press their pale bellies against the shaded ducts at noon, cooled by the machine’s cold subterranean breath, and understood what silence would do: stop the compressor, and the updraft dies. Without that cold draft, the noon sun would bake the lizards inside their mineral skins, and the **draft-kites**—asleep on locked wings in the spiraling air—would drop like shed leaves into the waiting jaws below.

It watched the **bolt-crabs**, some cemented forever to the pipes by rust and spite, and the **clack-hawks** folding their wings on the forty-second shockwave of the relief valve, and the blind **caliper-jaws** reading the whole basin through the hum of sheet iron. Nothing down there was cruel out of malice; nothing was wasted out of greed. Every sharp edge answered another sharp edge. To dull the claws or banish the hawks would only choke the vents with uncollected dead until the pit suffocated in its own abundance.

And then the power looked deeper, past the swirling funnel and the shingled pipes, down to the bottom where the cartwheel gear-train turned in its pitted casing.

There, between the bright, crushing teeth, it saw the pale, eyeless cleaners moving in perfect, greased silence.

It watched them dart forward in the fraction of a breath before the iron locked, scraping away the stray grit that would have killed the engine. Here was the secret the pit had kept from the dead plain above: softness had not perished here. It had simply found the one place where nothing else could follow, trusting its survival to the rhythm of the teeth.

The power understood then that even a single drop of its own kind of mercy—one stray leaf, one tear of fresh water, one moment of unscheduled quiet—would fall into that bright mesh like sand and jam the gears.

So it stayed its hand. It refused to unmake a miracle merely because the miracle was hard.

Instead, the power looked out across the flat, pale-grey plain, and then down through twenty meters of bedrock to the deep, unseen housing where the ancient engine still turned. It did not make the iron immortal, nor did it alter the tolerances of tooth and shell. It only reached beneath the stone and gently turned aside a slow, subterranean seep of groundwater that, in another decade, would have broken through the wall and flooded the bowl. At the lip of the pit, where a slab of wind-planed limestone had begun to overhang the drop, it settled the rock back a fraction of an inch into its bed, so it would not fall and crush the gear-train below.

Then, realizing that even its own presence at the rim cast a shadow across the bowl—cooling a patch of stone the skinks needed, or baffling the sharp eyes of a hovering hawk—the power stepped back from the edge.

It left the pit as it had found it: cool, dry, deafening, and whole, turning in broad daylight beneath a quiet sky.
