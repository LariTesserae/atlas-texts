Before it even reaches the stone, the power understands that its first kindness must be to dim itself.

A lesser benevolence—one that only knew how to give what it already had—might have arrived as a parting of the carbon haze, a sudden, generous spill of sunlight onto the black basalt. It would have meant well. It would have killed the plateau in a season. Without the heavy ceiling to press the planet’s heat against the crust, the condensation-basins would freeze solid; the mineral oil in the sumps would stiffen into black wax; the wire-sedge would snap like brittle glass, and the high-altitude bladders, built for a sky of warm vapor and dry static, would shrivel and fall like emptied skins.

So the power sets light aside before it ever crosses the upper haze.

It enters the plateau not as a pillar of radiance, nor as a voice in the thin air, but as a careful absence of disturbance. It folds its own immensity down until it casts no thermal bloom for a siphon-hound to mistake for prey, and leaks no stray voltage that might pull a capacitor-ray off its magnetic line. Stepping onto the warm stone, it makes no sound louder than the barometric drift.

For a long time, it simply listens.

It feels the sub-audible thrumming of the deep quartz plates grinding against the magnetite, and the blind, diamond-toothed worms wedged between them, taking their share of the continent’s weight. It watches a refinery-grazer roll slowly across an iron seam, its belly-filaments painting a brief, pale smear of light across the basalt before the dark closes back over the steaming slag. It watches the spark-mites skitter out to strip the cooling pellets, and the ceramic-bead creatures along the sedge-stalks snapping their tiny charges down into the root-mats—*tick, tock, tick*—turning a damp hollow into a quiet conversation.

And then, beside a graphite vein, it sees a siphon-hound strike an ore-borer.

The blue arc flares and dies. The borer locks on its piston-legs, its internal furnace fading from cherry-red to black. Here, the power pauses. There is an old impulse in anything that loves the living: to step between the fang and the conduit, to spare the heavy, lumbering thing its quiet draining.

The power waits, and watches what follows.

It watches the unfasteners climb the cooled flanks in the dark, prying the dorsal plates and winding the copper nerves onto their bony spindles with the unhurried precision of watchmakers. And when they have gone, leaving only a cage of pitted iron ribs, the power watches the pale, silver-veined brooder drag itself inside, tap the dead grounding-cable, and begin to hum—a low, patient warmth rising around its unplated young.

To spare the borer would have been to starve the unfasteners and freeze the brood in the dark. The power sees that what looked like cruelty from a distance is only the plateau’s fierce, unbroken thrift. Nothing here is wasted, and nothing is hated. So it sets mercy—at least the kind that interrupts—gently aside.

It moves on, across miles of warm, empty slate, until it finds what it came to see.

The balancer stands beside a tall mineral spire where static has been gathering in the dry air. Twelve feet tall on three copper-soled feet, its vitrified frame wrapped in matte-black sheathing, it extends its iron-tipped arm to the spire and plants its carbon brush against a damp fracture. For ten seconds, a thick, silent current pours through it, bleeding the excess charge harmlessly into the crust. Its mica crest shudders once. Then it withdraws its arms, lifts a broad foot, and steps onward along the seam.

The power walks beside it—not visibly, for there are no eyes here to see, but as a shape in the magnetic field, a sudden, inexplicable stillness in the air.

Here is the last temptation: to speak. To reach into that layered mica dish and show the balancer what lies beyond the carbon haze. To let it know there are stars, and oceans of liquid water, and worlds where things grow soft and green under a burning sky. To give it a name for what it does, or a companion that understands *why* it keeps the circuit clean.

The power considers this, and refuses.

A creature built for the hum of a single continent does not need to know it is small. To plant a longing for the sky in a being whose whole joy is the smooth equilibrium of the bedrock would not be a gift; it would be a leak in the insulation. It would introduce a voltage the balancer was never built to ground.

Yet the balancer is not fooled. Its fringe of fine whiskers reads every shift in the plateau’s field, and it slows, then stops.

Its broad mica crest tilts slightly toward the empty space beside it. It cannot see a god, any more than it can see the sun. What it senses is far stranger: a vast, impossible potential standing three paces away—enough energy, coiled and quiet, to melt the basalt back into magma or shatter the ceiling of haze—and yet not a single spark jumps the gap. It feels no heat, no pull, no demand. Only an equilibrium so complete that the balancer’s own whiskers go entirely still, resting in the cleanest quiet they have ever known.

For ten seconds—the same measure of time it gives a charged spire—the balancer stands in the presence of something that could remake the world and chooses not to touch a single grain of iron.

The power does not bless it. It does not mend a cracked plate in its sheathing, or clear the slag from the next sump down the line. Those are the balancer’s own work, and to take them away would be to make a pet of a keeper. Instead, the power simply matches its own stillness to the hum of the bedrock, letting the circuit run through the space between them without a hitch, without a mark, without a signature.

Then, slowly, it lifts its attention away from the stone.

Behind it, the balancer shifts its weight, sets one copper foot forward, and resumes its walk along the graphite seam. Far off over the escarpments, a dry sheet of static flickers silently across the clouds, briefly lighting the silhouettes of the grazers, the tufts of wire-sedge, and the warm, dark floor of a world that belongs entirely to itself.
