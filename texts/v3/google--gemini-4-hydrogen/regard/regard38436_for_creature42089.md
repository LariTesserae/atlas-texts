At first, the power’s impulse was the oldest impulse of mercy: to reach down and *fix* it.

From the outside, the world looked like an unfinished thought left out in the rain—a continent-wide wound of iron-dark shale, scoured raw by colliding walls of brine, suffocated under a ceiling of bruised pewter. A power with the strength to part seas and raise mountains could have mended it in a breath. It could have lifted the cloud-deck and burned away the sulfur, cooled the boiling soup of the tides, pinned the rolling stone-eggs down into soil, and coaxed a green shoot up through the silt. It could have stitched the frayed edges of time and matter back together so that thunder followed lightning, puddles showed the sky, and a wave broke once and was done.

It held its hand above the churning mist, ready to give them a horizon.

Then, being wise as well as kind, it waited—and listened.

Because it did not use eyes alone, it felt the world the way the world was made: through the deep, tectonic chatter of the ball-mill, through the hum in the sternum, through the ten-second hush between a crack of thunder and the violet flicker that birthed it. And in that patient listening, it found them.

It watched them uncoil from the shale seams as the sheet-tide thinned, slick and low-slung, moving from crown to crown of the rolling millstones with an unhurried, practiced grace. It saw how their wide-angled ankles and iron-strong fingers were not compromises, not deformities forced upon them by a cruel place, but answers—exact, supple, and beautiful—to the questions the current asked. It saw them shut their muscular nostrils and hold a single breath in the lee of a boulder-sized shell while the sky-darkening double-bores thundered overhead.

It watched two of them meet on a hump of shale, pressing their foreheads together in the dimness, trading rapid three-beat names through the dense, spongy bone of their skulls. It watched them slip inside a sudden, humming dome of mud-water for ten heartbeats of solid quiet—sharing chewed kelp-sap, checking each other’s hides for deep stone-bruises, sliding back out on the ninth beat just before the walls dissolved into foam.

And the power understood, with a sudden, humbling clarity, what its "mercy" would have done.

Raise mountains, and their wide-set hips and heavy forearms would become clumsy burdens. Clear the sky, and the bright, dry air would blister their slick hides and blind their deep-set eyes. Silence the ball-mill, and they would be deafened—stripped of the bone-deep music by which they knew where the bedrock lay and when a neighbor was near. Stitch time back into a straight line, and they would lose the ten-heartbeat rooms where they held each other, and the lying puddles by which they read the coming storm.

To rescue them from this world would be to unmake them. They were not prisoners here. They were the world’s own tenderness, given hands.

So the power drew back its hand from the sky. It left the pewter clouds dragging low. It left the sulfur in the warm rain, and the colliding bores, and the endless, grinding roar of the stones.

Yet it did not turn away. For in watching closely, it saw the one quiet ache that even these enduring bodies could not entirely outlast: not the violence of the tides, which they knew how to ride, nor the dark, which they did not fear—but the *forgetting*. The world was very old, its rules wearing thin like shale under brine. Months could pass between one chance meeting and the next. A traveler could drift alone, bruised and spent, in a place too vast and noisy to hold a voice. And when they paused to stack a cairn or press a shallow ring into the mud, it was a small, defiant prayer against vanishing without a trace.

*Someone with hands was here, and held still for a breath, before the water took them again.*

A wise power does not rewrite a poem because the ink is dark. Instead, it tends the paper so the page does not tear.

So what the power did was almost nothing at all—and it did it entirely in the grammar of the place.

Deep beneath the continent-wide shelf of shale, where no tide reached, it laid a quiet steadiness against the fraying seams of the world, not to mend the fractures that gave the beings their ten-heartbeat domes and prophetic reflections, but only to keep the whole from unraveling into nothing. It left the chaos intact, and simply refused to let the world’s age become its end.

And on the surface, it learned to remember on the silt’s behalf.

When a lone traveler, weeks into a solitary drift and aching from the millstones, crouched beside a long-scattered cairn—only a few faint indentations left in the bedrock—the mud-water there did not forget quite so easily. It remembered the squat, hollow shape a breath sooner, and held its bronze-hummed stillness for eleven heartbeats instead of ten, or twelve, just long enough for a tired body to catch its breath and feel the thrum of another’s old patience in the walls.

When they braided the black kelp-grass around bedrock spurs, the power did not tie the knots for them—it only kept the pitch of the humming strings a little truer across the grinding weeks, so that a passing palm might still read the current clearly, long after the weaver had drifted miles away.

And when one of them paused on a high hump of shale, pressed a splayed palm into the softest mud, and slipped back into the waist-deep current, the power did not stop the next bore from washing the ring away. That was the pact of the place. But it kept the shape of the hand, and the three-beat rhythm of the name, and the warmth of the breath held still—holding them somewhere deeper than the shale, where no tide could scour them clean.

Only once, perhaps, did it offer a sign—and even then, nothing that could frighten or command.

A weary traveler, kneeling in a rare, still hollow between bores, peered down into the quiet water to read the lie of the next wave. There was no face looking back—there never was—and no tilted sky. Only, for the space of a single held breath, the water showed the faint, ghostly double of a squat stone cairn waiting on a hump of shale just ahead, though none had been built there yet, and beside it, the quiet lee of a stuttering wave.

The traveler blinked behind translucent lids, stood, and stepped out onto the rolling stones—leaning slightly forward in time, toward shelter.

And the power watched them go, satisfied to remain unseen, humming softly in the bedrock beneath their feet.
