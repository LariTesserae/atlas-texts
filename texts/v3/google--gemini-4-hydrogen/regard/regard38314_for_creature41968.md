At first, a power that is only benevolent would want to mend it.

Seen from above at noon, the valley looks like a place under a curse. Nothing green softens the limestone; nothing flies across the blinding sky. The water lies trapped in ten thousand square mirrors, cold as iron and bright enough to sear the eye, and there is no sound save the thin wind and the occasional hollow *plink* of a drop falling into stone. To look at it in the glare of midday is to see a vast, freezing labor left abandoned—a monument to something harsh andjoyless.

So a merely kind power might reach down at once. It might warm the thin air, or seed the still pans with rushes and fish, or dull the sun behind a permanent veil of cloud.

Wisdom waits until dusk.

When the sun drops behind the rim and the water darkens from white to pewter to oiled slate, the power stays still and watches the ground open. It watches the narrow-shouldered figures rise out of the dry shafts between the culverts, silent as mist, their skin the pale gray of dry limestone and their palms stained green with copper. It watches two of them meet on a wall too narrow to pass, and sees one step smoothly down into a knee-deep pan—raising barely a ripple—to let the other go by.

Then the power begins to listen, the way they do.

It hears what they hear: that the valley is not a ruin, and not a prison. It is an instrument tuned to the sky, and they are the hands that keep it in tune. Every *plink* in a covered cistern is a measurement; every tiny *click* of a settling stone plug is an answer; every faint *hiss* beneath a walkway is a summons they know how to answer without a single word. Their bodies are fitted to the width of the walls, their ears to the quiet of the basin, their nights to the slow tide of mist becoming water becoming life in the dark honeycomb below.

Still, benevolence aches at what it sees.

It sees the bruised-looking lids over their small, pale eyes, and knows that an hour of their own valley’s noon would blind them. It sees the frost-heave that forces them to haul heavy stone plugs with braided sinew ropes, night after night, in air cold enough to numb bone. It sees the brittle white calcite choking the sluice-lips, never resting, never finished. It sees how little they have below: no fire, no song, only earth-warmth and pale fungi and thin mats of dried algae.

And so the power considers its gifts, one by one, and lays each one down.

It could make the limestone impervious to frost, and purify the mountain seepage so calcite never crusted the weirs again. The keepers would no longer have to scrape the sluice-lips with copper chisels or pack the weeping seams with clay. Their hands would stay clean.  
*And then*, wisdom sees, *what would they listen for?* Without the *hiss* of a leak or the slow thickening of the glassy sheets, the machine would run without them. They would wake at dusk with nothing to mend, nothing to read by ear, and the valley would become mere stone around them instead of a language they speak with their whole bodies.

It could heal their eyes to bear the sun, or soften the mountain glare to a gentle twilight.  
*And then* the mist would not condense on the chilled slate as it does, the valley would lose its mirror, and the keepers would no longer belong to the deep, warm honeycomb beneath the floor. Their stillness would scatter into the open day.

It could bring them fire, or rich soil, or voices.  
*And then* smoke would foul the water-pans, roots would crack the dry-fitted blocks, and a single spoken word would drown out the quiet they need to hear a drop fall twenty feet below.

To change any of it—even out of love—would be to unmake them. It would be the kindness of a stranger who sees a violin tuned to a strange, austere scale and loosens the strings so the wood won’t bear the tension.

So the power does nothing to the stone. It does nothing to the water, or the sun, or the narrow, calloused feet that move along the causeways in the dark.

Instead, it does the only thing that fits inside a place this quiet.

It withholds its own weight. It makes sure its watching casts no shadow across the pans, raises no tremor in the still cisterns, adds no hum to the thin air that might confuse a keeper’s ear as he tilts his head above a covered shaft. It stays wholly outside the rhythm of the valley, except for one small, invisible mercy—so slight it will never look like anything but the mountain’s own luck:

It steadies the deep fault beneath the basin, so no senseless earthquake will ever shatter the honeycomb while they sleep; and it keeps the high-altitude mist from failing for a hundred dry years, so the top tiers will always have enough sky to pass down into the dark. Not enough to spare them a single night of scraping salt or packing clay. Only enough that the world does not break them by accident.

By the time the first cold white light touches the western rim, the power has already drawn back.

One by one, the keepers lower the slate covers over their heads and slip down into the earth’s warmth. The sun clears the ridge. The valley turns back into a blinding, freezing mirror, emptied of hands—only the crisp, dusty-pale chisel marks fresh in the limestone, and the water moving, drop by silent drop, down into the dark where they are waiting.
