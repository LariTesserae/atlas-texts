At first, the power’s impulse was mercy—the quick, human-shaped kind of mercy that mistakes softness for good.

From above the gleaming ceiling of ice-fog, the plateau looked like an open wound in the crust of the world. The sun hung too close and too bare, flaying the quartzite ridges with white fire. The air was so thin and bitterly cold that even breath seemed an act of violence, and the sound rising from the basins was not the song of a living world, but the groan of a machine tearing itself apart in slow motion.

It would have been so easy to mend.

A thickening of the sky to scatter that merciless glare into a gentle, painterly blue. A breath of warmth to melt the rime into singing rivers, to coax soil out of the raw basalt, to quiet the agonizing shriek of iron-sulfide grinding against iron-sulfide. A single touch, and the smelter-herds could have laid down their furnaces; the stack-tenders could have climbed down from the scalding chimneys; the blind seam-scrapers could have been given eyes and a soft, sunlit meadow.

The power raised a hand—and then, because it was wise as well as kind, it waited.

It lowered itself onto a knife-edged ridge of basalt, beside a spire-reaper angled motionless into the glare, and slowed its own attention down to the clock of the stone.

It waited through the long morning while a colony of arc-weavers flexed in a single, hillside-wide ripple, feeling the slow pressure of the charge building in the thin air until the blue-white sparks snapped—and saw, hidden in the dark undergrowth below, a pale grounder light up for half a second in bruised, internal violet. A secret lantern, lit by the sky’s electricity, burning only for itself.

It waited through the afternoon as a wave of steam-venting rolled across the basin, watching the condenser-kites hang fat and patient on the rising plumes. It watched the spire-reaper beside it wait three full days without shifting a joint, and then cast its copper-silk tether with the clean precision of a bell ringing once—drinking the thawed water before the frost could steal it, and dropping the hollow husk to feed the dark below.

It pressed its ear to the freezing rock and listened down into the clefts, where a smelter-herd had shifted two inches in an hour. It felt the seam-scraper flatten into a rubbery gasket as the iron flank ground past, unharmed, cradled in the one narrow ribbon of warmth the world offered it. It heard the sharp *skree* of a stack-tender’s ribs vibrating through a fifty-ton carapace—not a cry of pain, but a warning given in the dark, kin calling to kin through solid metal so no one would be caught when the valve blew.

And when an old giant’s furnace finally went cold, and the power felt a pang of grief for the fifty tons of freezing slag blocking the valley, it watched the wedge-backs crawl up from the tectonic clefts. It heard the sharp, clean pistol-shots of flash-frozen water splitting the carcass open, clearing the road for the herds behind, feeding the dredge-chains waiting in the oil-soaked dark.

Then the power understood.

If it softened the sun, the black thickets would starve, and the spire-reapers would lose their mirrored stillness. If it warmed the air, the steam would never freeze to rime, the kites would never fill, and the wedge-backs’ frost-chisels would turn harmless. If it silenced the grinding herds, the seam-scrapers would freeze in the clefts. If it spared the dying giant, the basin would choke on its own unbreakable dead.

There was no cruelty here. There was only weight, and pressure, and an astonishing, unsentimental fidelity—a fugue written in sulfur, frost, and copper, where every harshness was answered by a living counterweight, and nothing, not even death, was wasted. To "heal" this place into comfort would be to shatter it.

So the power lowered its hand.

It did not dim the filament of the sun. It did not hush the *hiss-clack* of the valves or cool the acid-guts or smooth the freshly fractured stone.

Instead, it did the only things a wise love could do for a world that was already whole.

First, it stayed long enough to know it—to hold the whole deafening, freezing, brilliantly lit metabolism in its attention, giving a machine that had no makers the one thing it could never evolve for itself: a witness who neither feared its harshness nor wanted to own its gears.

Second, it set a quiet ward along the edge of the thin sky—not a dome to trap the heat, nor a veil to soften the light, but a subtle turning-aside of anything from beyond the horizon that might come with lesser wisdom: the stray wanderer who would look at iron-sulfide carapaces and see only ore to be mined, or the well-meaning stranger who would look at the freezing dark and try to plant a garden over the graves of giants.

And then the power stepped back, leaving no footprint on the basalt and no echo in the ozone air.

Down in the basin, a smelter-herd shifted a fraction of an inch. A stack-tender chipped a crust of yellow sulfur from a steaming throat. On the ridge, the spire-reaper folded its mirrored plates back into the glare, and the makerless world went on turning—fierce, unhurried, and entirely itself.
