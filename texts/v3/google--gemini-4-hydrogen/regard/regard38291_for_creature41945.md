The power did not arrive as light. Light was scarce here, and the pale blade falling through the vertical slit already held all the daylight the narrow room could bear without waking the wrong kinds of warmth. Nor did it arrive as wind, which would have scattered the neat, magnetic ridges of dust along the gneiss and troubled the ten thousand papery fronds out of their exact, sympathetic resonance.

It came simply as attention—vast, unhurried, and quiet enough to slip through a seam no wider than a hallway without displacing a single grain of graphite.

At first, it only listened. Four miles above the salt-flats, where the air was thin enough to starve a fire, it found a room loud enough to rattle bone: the deep, sixty-cycle hum of the three cast-iron housings, the crisp *clack-hiss-clack* of the dry pneumatic valves, the dry parchment roar of the silica-reeds. It traced the pale dust furred along the lines of force, the dull amber of the caged bulbs, the hair-thin needles of violet corona trembling at the brass terminals.

And then it found the shape folded behind the third dynamo.

A power that loves living things knows how to recognize hunger, confinement, and pain; it has spent ages learning how to answer them. Looking into that cramped, dim angle between the rusted casing and the chalky wall, its first, oldest instinct stirred—the impulse to mend.

It saw a creature with no eyes to catch the amber glow, no lungs to draw a breath, its long, dry struts bound at the knuckles by copper tendrils as tightly as the ceramic insulators. It saw a world without rain, without soil, without a single soft thing to touch, locked inside a deafening iron box four miles above the earth.

For a long moment, the power weighed its gifts.

It could have widened the jagged slit in the outer wall, just enough to let in the clean sweep of the sky and a gentler warmth. It could have laid a single, cool palm of mist across the pewter fans and the brittle fungus-plates, teaching them what greenness was. It could have hushed the pneumatic valves to a whisper, loosened the copper coils from those creakless joints, and lifted the folded silhouette out onto the sunlit ledges below, where things had room to stand upright and silence enough to hear themselves think.

Before it moved a finger, however, the power was wise enough to look closer. It lowered its attention to the scale of the louvers along the creature’s throat, and listened the way the carbon-coated filaments listened.

In that stillness inside the noise, it felt the truth of the room.

It felt how the rain it might have brought would pool on the floor plates, wake rust in the tie-rods, short the aging busbars, and rot the dry silica-reeds into gray slime within a week. It felt how a gentler sky through a wider slit would bring weather—frost to crack the porcelain switchboard, thaw to swell the gneiss, wind to tear the papery fronds from their ledges. It felt how silencing the dynamos would blind the creature’s only sense, leaving it stranded in an unmapped dark, starving the wire-moss of its stray fields and cooling the air until the oil in those patient joints thickened into stone.

Most of all, it saw what the creature was doing in the tightest angle behind the third housing. It traced the violet corona bending across the half-inch gap to kiss the copper-colored bristles along that narrow spine. It followed the slow, steady trickle of charge passing through the dry, horn-like struts down into the grounded bedrock.

The creature was not trapped in the circuit. It *was* the circuit’s mercy. Without it resting lightly across the sharpest gaps, the excess voltage would gather until a single bright arc leaped into the dry, paper-thin jungle, and the whole cramped nave would become ash in the span of one human breath.

Nothing here was asking to be rescued. Nothing here was waiting to be changed. What looked like imprisonment from the outside was, from the inside, a chord held so cleanly that not a single note wobbled.

So the benevolent and wise power did the hardest thing a power can do when it loves what it sees.

It held its hand completely still.

It did not add a drop of moisture to the air, nor a watt of warmth to the stone. It did not clear the dry salt-bloom from the faces of the brass gauges, nor nudge the rusted knife-switches, nor whisper a greeting into the vibrating louvers—for even a whisper of kindness would have been a second note, and the room knew only one.

Instead, as carefully as it had entered, the power drew its attention back through the vertical slit, making sure to leave no eddy of air behind, no stray spark, no lingering shadow of its own presence to disturb the magnetic lines of dust.

Once outside, high above the empty salt-flats, it paused for a fraction of a second—not to alter the mountain, only to know that the cleft was there, tucked into its leeward seam, humming steadily in the dark. Then it turned away, and left the creature to its one unwavering note, and to the silence it held intact inside the noise.
