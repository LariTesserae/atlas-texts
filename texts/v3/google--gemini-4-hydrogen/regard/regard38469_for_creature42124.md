It would not arrive with wind, because nothing here knows wind. It would not part the cloud-sea or brighten the sun, because the light is already bright and ordinary, and the valley has no use for spectacle.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward the basin, it would come the only way this place understands: by slowing down until it could hear the forty-second *thump* rising through the basalt.

For a long time—measured not in hours, which do not exist here, but in pulses of the aquifer—it would simply watch. It would feel the warm, stinging brine push up through the porous stone and watch the waxy reeds draw their quiet current. It would see the beetles grazing the oxidized seams of the dwellers’ backs, and the pale amphibians sheltering in the shadow of their flanks. It would listen, through the conductive water, to the slow, patterned tingles of voltage passing between three half-submerged boulders in a mineral pan, and see the brief, dry *snap* of pale blue static jump between two blunt snouts in the wet gloom.

And it would see the oldest dweller, heavy with centuries of precipitated copper and hanging moss, settling into a deep terrace pool for the last time—pressing its translucent throat to the stone as its breath ceased and its internal current merged with the valley’s hum.

Here is where a merely kind power might reach out a hand.

Kindness without wisdom might look at the stiffening joints of the old dweller and want to scrub the copper away, to spare it the weight of its own years. It might want to widen the channels so the gravel never needed turning, or give the dwellers sharper eyes, or voices that could carry through the thick air, or a longer life free from the slow return to stone. It might see the pure copper nodules rolling into the gravel beds and think of all the things that could be built, if only someone here knew how to build.

A wise power would stay its hand.

It would recognize, almost immediately, that to unburden the dweller of its copper would be to unburden it of its belonging. To spare it the settling into the pool would be to break the circuit that keeps the terraces alive. Nothing here is suffering; nothing here is unfinished. Even grief has no purchase in a place where the dead do not leave, but simply become the floor the living rest their bellies on. To improve a balance this ancient would be a kind of vandalism.

So it would do nothing to the valley. It would not even send a pulse of its own down into the brine to say *I am here*, lest the dwellers spend the next thousand forty-second intervals waiting for a voice that does not belong to their world.

Instead, if it acted at all, it would look outward—down over the weathered rim of the basin, through the flat white ocean of clouds, toward the lower world where things do rush, and where things do break.

There, without raising a single wall or leaving a single mark on the stone, it might gently thicken the mist along the steep, forgotten approaches to the saddle. It might turn the curiosity of wandering climbers toward other peaks, and nudge the dreams of miners and mapmakers toward other seams of ore, miles away across the sea of cloud. Not a barrier—for nothing in the basin wants a cage—only a quiet, continuous distraction, laid softly over the lower world so that nothing loud or hurried ever stumbles up into the heat.

And then, before withdrawing, it would let its attention rest for one last forty-second interval on the mossy back of a dweller in the shallows—light as a dragonfly, asking nothing, changing nothing, simply glad that the engine was humming at the roof of the world.
