The attention did not arrive as a light. Light would have bleached the bruised twilight, startled the heat-sensitive pits beneath the creature’s brow ridges, and turned the dim amber galls on its shoulders into meaningless sparks.

Instead, the power came down the access shaft the way the chamber itself operated: as a quiet, patient pressure, reading the space by temperature, current, and weight.

For a long time, it simply lingered in the eighty-foot vault, among the curtains of braided copper cable and calcified roots, letting the room’s clockwork pass through it. It felt the sub-bass thrum of the buried turbine rising through the basalt. It listened to the high, thin singing of the pinhole leaks, and waited out the full, unhurried minute between one wet *thump* of the pressure-bladder and the next.

It saw the temptation immediately, because any power that loves living things feels it when looking into a dark, hot, narrow place: the urge to *rescue*.

A lesser mercy—one that knew how to build worlds, perhaps, but not how to leave them alone—would have looked at the crusted iron, the suffocating air, the eyeless thing hanging like a blister of slag from a frayed conduit, and called it a ruin. It would have reached down with a cool hand and opened the basalt ceiling to the sky. It would have swept away the iron-flour, flushed the floor with sweet water, silenced the deafening manifolds, and lifted the six-limbed creature out onto grass beneath a clean sun.

And in an hour, the leathery pewter fronds would have curled and cracked in the dry wind. The pale-orange tendrils, starved of stray voltage, would have turned to brittle thread. And the creature—made for eighty-degree-plus shadow, for air thick enough to chew, for a world small enough to map with its chin—would have died shivering in the terrible, silent cold of an open sky, blinded by a sun it had no eyes to want.

Wisdom, the power knew, is mostly knowing what not to touch.

So it stayed its hand, and looked closer.

It traced the waxy channels on the undersides of the fronds, marveling at the economy that hoarded vapor drop by drop. It followed the woody vines threaded through the transformer louvers, admiring how they had turned a dead factory’s skeleton into a trellis. It watched the amber galls pulsing faintly where living bark had made peace with live copper. Nothing here was broken. Nothing here was waiting to be saved. The chamber had spent decades negotiating its own terms with heat, rust, and gravity, and it had arrived at a truce so delicate that even a kindly breath might shatter it.

Then the power turned its full, gentle attention to the creature.

It hung motionless above the heat-exchanger, its four long legs hooked around the copper cable, its forelimbs tucked against its plated chest, its massive underslung jaw resting lightly on the vibrating metal. The power could feel the slow, heavy tide of its blood, the faint bio-electric hum of its nerves feeding the small lantern-galls on its spine, the wet baffles in its chest quietly sorting iron-dust from breath.

It was alone, yes—the only thing with bone and marrow in the basalt cathedral. Yet as the power listened to the creature’s dreaming stillness, it found no loneliness there. Loneliness belongs to things built for a crowd and deprived of one. This being was built to the exact measure of its room. Its solitude was not an absence; it was the precise, load-bearing shape of its sufficiency. In its jaw, the deep thrum of the turbine was not noise; it was company, steady and faithful, beating like a mother’s heart beneath the floor.

If the power gave it a companion, there would not be enough water-bladders to go around. If it gave it eyes, there would be nothing worth seeing across forty feet of tangled vine. If it softened the creature’s blackened, heat-cured soles, the iron manifolds would burn it to the bone.

There was nothing to give the creature that would not be a theft.

Still, a benevolent power does not look upon a beloved thing without checking the foundations.

Casting its awareness deeper—past the crust of mica and iron-flour, down through the stratified basalt beneath the sunken dynamos, and upward into the fractured stone above the vault—the power read the chamber’s longer clock. Not the minute-by-minute *thump* of the pressure-bladder, nor the day-by-day creep of verdigris, but the slow, grinding math of centuries.

There, in the deep dark where not even the creature’s jaw could feel it yet, it found two quiet inevitabilities.

Far beneath the floor, the buried geothermal turbine—the ancient, sluggish heart of the room—was turning on a main bearing worn down to a paper-thin lip of bronze. In another decade, or perhaps two, that lip would shear. The turbine would seize with a single shudder through the rock. The sub-bass thrum would stop. Over the course of a year, the heat-exchangers would cool to the temperature of dead stone; the steam would cease to sing; the vapor would stop rising; and the creature would climb slower and slower through a darkening, freezing room until it curled on a cold pipe and did not wake.

And eighty feet above, in the fractured basalt of the vault, a hairline fault—widened by a century of rising heat and the slow weight of the mountain above—was preparing to slip, just enough to seal the condensation seam that kept the eastern wall damp, and perhaps enough to bring down a ton of roof-stone onto the manifolds below.

The power considered these two things.

To repair the turbine to factory-new would be a catastrophe: its speed would climb, its heat would spike, the excess steam would scald the fronds, and the sudden silence of the pinhole leaks would disorient the creature’s world. To reinforce the vault with bright, alien stone would alter the room’s acoustics and seal off the moisture.

So the power worked the way a root works, or the way mineral sinter grows—slowly, invisibly, in the chamber’s own grammar.

Down in the crushing dark beneath the floor, without stopping the turbine for a fraction of a second, it eased the microscopic pinch on the bronze bearing. It did not make it new; it simply thickened the metal by a whisper, restoring just enough quiet endurance to keep the shaft turning at its exact, agonizingly sluggish pace for another five hundred years. Not a single decibel changed. The vibration in the braided copper cable did not flicker.

Up in the soot-blackened vault, it laid a gentle, unseen hand against the fractured basalt. It did not seal the rock dry; it merely knit the deep fault-line together ten feet behind the surface, leaving the damp condensation seam open and dripping, leaving the rust-stains and the calcified roots untouched, ensuring only that the mountain would hold its breath and not fall in on the cathedral.

Then, before it withdrew, the power paused once more beside the sleeping creature.

It did not touch the plated hide, nor wake it. It simply let the chamber keep its own secret, and left no signature behind—no scent of ozone that wasn’t already there, no sudden cool draft, no memory of a visitor.

A minute passed.

Somewhere in the gloom, the enormous pressure-bladder swelled and gave its wet, resonant *thump*. A fine, dry rain of rust-dust drifted down from the vault, settling on the pewter fronds, on the sagging catwalks, and on the creature’s ridged back.

A few seconds later, the dust slid off in tiny, dry avalanches as the creature shifted its plates by a fraction of an inch. Its jaw pressed a little closer to the humming copper, drinking in the steady, faithful thrum from below. Then it settled back into the warm, bruised twilight, whole and unbothered, with all the time in the world.
