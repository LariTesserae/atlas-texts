At first, the power saw only empty stone.

It was accustomed to worlds that cried out—worlds of cracked earth and starved roots, of iron clashing against iron, of creatures that burned through their days in haste and fear. Its attention moved at the speed of rivers and empires, and when it passed high above the cloud line, it registered the stepped tableland of dark basalt as a monument left behind by something long gone: vast, geometric, silent, and uninhabited.

Only because it was wise as well as kind did it pause before moving on. A lesser power might have sighed at the waste of so much clean water and moved elsewhere, or else set about filling the empty plazas with noise. This one slowed itself. It let go of the haste of stars and seasons and matched its attention to the condensation on the cliffs.

It waited through the middle hours.

Gradually, the stillness separated into lives.

A slab of pale, mortar-colored stone beside a pavilion lifted a long, narrow hand. A rise in the basalt turned a smooth head toward the small *plink* of a falling drop. The power watched, without wind or shadow, as two of them rose from a flat roof where they had been lying with open palms turned to the clear sky. It watched them walk the raised causeways, placing each broad foot squarely in the center of the flagstones so as not to brush the moss swelling in the seams. It watched one kneel beside a square settling-tank, lower its tight-seamed mouth to the glassy skin of the water, and draw so slowly that the surface scarcely dimpled.

It watched them for what, in the lowlands far below, would have been a long season. It heard the *tap... tap... tap...* of one hard stone pecking a shallow channel into another, spaced so far apart that the sound folded cleanly into the drip of the overhangs. It saw a fern uncurl too far over a spillway, and saw a pale figure sit beside the channel and build a tiny, dry-stacked curb of basalt to coax the frond aside rather than snap its stem.

And because the power was benevolent, it began to search for what it might give.

That was its nature: to mend what was broken, to supply what was lacking, to ease what was hard. It looked closely at the plateau, testing every gift it held against the measure of the valley.

*They have no speech,* the power thought. *I could open their throats to song, or give them words to carry across the plazas.*
Then it looked at three of them sitting cross-legged in an open-walled pavilion, facing the same spillway, one pale hand resting lightly on a neighbor’s forearm. Hours passed without a shift in posture, yet nothing between them was missing. Words would only crowd the air where the placement of a hand already said everything.

*They are alone here,* the power thought. *I could bring birds to cross the sky, frogs to sing in the cisterns, bright insects to skim the pools.*
Then it looked at the undisturbed cushions of moss, the unchewed ferns, the mirror-flat curtains of water lipping from terrace to terrace. To bring birds and frogs was to bring hunger, pursuit, torn leaves, and startled water. The valley was not empty; it was unbruised.

*They build so slowly,* the power thought. *And their older neighborhoods stand behind them unused. I could teach them to shore up the settled roofs, or lend them bronze and wheels to lift the slabs.*
Then it listened again to the *tap... tap... tap...* of stone on stone, and saw that their slowness was not a hardship to be cured. They were not struggling to finish a task; their work and their lives ran on the same quiet current as the weeping cliffs. To hurry their hands would be to sever them from the rock.

*I could show myself,* the power thought last of all. *I could let them know they are seen and held.*
But it saw how their dark, glossy eyes watched a single ripple travel across a full tank until it died against the stone. Even a gentle presence—even a pillar of mild light or a warm voice in the windless air—would be an interruption. Awe was a kind of tilt, and nothing here was meant to tilt. Everything here was level.

For a long time, the power simply remained still above the plateau, learning the rare discipline of having nothing to add.

Then, at last, it acted.

It did not touch the basalt, or the water, or the pale figures in the pavilions. Instead, it turned its attention downward, below the rim of the tableland, into the unseen lowlands hidden beneath the clouds.

There, in the louder, hungrier world below, it went quietly to work along the margins. It kept the deep vents clear so the warm, invisible vapor would continue to rise and bead upon the high stone. When distant storms gathered force in the lower valleys, it softened their edges before they could climb above the cloud line and tear at the ferns. And along the steep, broken approaches at the base of the cliffs—if ever some restless, iron-bearing traveler wandered too near, looking up toward the mist—the power gently turned the path aside, leading the traveler toward broader rivers and easier horizons.

It built no wall. It left no sign. It only held the boundary, as one might cup two hands around a candle flame without ever touching the wick.

High above, on the wet, bright floor of the plateau, nothing changed.

A single drop gathered on the lip of an overhang, fell into a full tank with a small, distinct *plink*, and sent its ring outward until it met the stone. In the shade of the pavilion, the three pale figures sat as they had always sat, none of them looking away.

The power withdrew its gaze, leaving the valley as it had found it—whole, level, and undisturbed.
