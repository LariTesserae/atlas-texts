A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive with light or thunder—especially not here, where the mist drinks sound and the inhabitants have no use for eyes. It arrives simply as attention, settling over the bruised thumbprint in the slate as quietly as the cool, even daylight.

At first, even wisdom feels the tug of a simpler, fiercer pity. 

Looking down into the hollow, it is easy to see only a wound. A tangle of rusted iron and rotting brick; cold brine churning over sunken porcelain; machines twitching in futile, forgotten loops; and heavy, blind things dragging their grit-scarred bellies through leaked oil and silt. A merely kind power might reach down with both hands and *tidy* the place. It might haul out the scrap, cap the pressurized fissures, warm the water, and clear the mud so the sun could reach the bottom. It might even touch those sunken, membrane-covered eyes and give them sight.

Wisdom stays its hand. It waits on the rim, and it listens.

Not with ears—there is almost nothing here for ears—but with the kind of patience that can read the tension in a steel tripod a heartbeat before the slate below gives way. It watches a bank of dark silt blister and liquefy, and sees the rubber-skinned creature brace its broad, three-lobed pads against a pipe with effortless grace. It feels the sub-audible hum of the brushless motor beneath the tarred pump-house, and understands why the creature presses its pale underside to the oil-filled housing: not out of desperation, but the way a lizard seeks a sun-warmed stone.

It watches two of them meet in a clearing sluice, pausing for a fraction of a second to press their pore-lined flanks together, passing a low, wordless thrum through the wet iron at the exact pitch of the machines.

And in that moment, the power understands: to clean this place would be to starve it. To silence the motors would be to blind the things that live between them. These creatures are not prisoners trapped in the wreckage of an engine; they are the song the engine never knew it was singing. Their world is already whole, held together by salt, slate, copper, and an unlikely, quiet mercy of its own.

So the wise power does not touch the basin floor. It leaves the mud to heave and invert. It leaves the brick to soften into rounded nubs, the indicator lamps to blink their erratic amber and green, and the counterweights to glide down their greased guide-wires with their heavy, motionless passengers.

Instead, it works only at the edges, and in the slow currency of time.

First, it follows the sagging black cables up over the thirty-foot weeping walls and deep into the coastal shelf, tracing them back to whatever forgotten, half-flooded substation or geothermal tap still feeds them. That distant heart is failing—worn thin by decades of salt and neglect. The power does not rebuild it or make it efficient. It simply *steadies* the pulse. It mends a frayed splice in the dark; it clears a choke of mineral scale from a buried intake; it ensures that the faint, steady trickle of current will not abruptly go cold. Down in the basin, the motors do not hum louder, and the gauges do not stop twitching wildly—they only keep their quiet, enduring rhythm for a few centuries more.

Second, it lays a gentle, invisible hand on the chemistry of the rot. Grit and water must still wear the iron smooth, and mineral collars must still build and shear away, for that is the breath of the place. But where an ancient seal is about to crack and bleed something truly foul into the brine—some heavy, lingering poison that would kill the blind shrimp below the slate or burn the creatures’ sensitive jaws—the power quietly turns the toxin to harmless salt and silt. It lets the machines age, without letting them become a grave.

Third, it draws a veil around the rim. Not a wall, and not a storm, for either would draw curiosity. Just a slight, persistent trick of the coastal mist, and a subtle boredom in the air that makes a wandering hiker’s gaze slide right over the hollow without registering it as anything more than a wet dent in the rock. No scrapper with a cutting torch, no surveyor with a clipboard, and no well-meaning engineer with a plan to "reclaim" the shelf will ever find the stairs down.

That is all. 

The power does not ask to be thanked, and the creatures below—probing a newly exposed brass valve in the clearing water, or riding the slow arc of a dredger-arm into the mist—never know they have been seen. They only feel, in the pores along their flanks and the bones of their broad jaws, that the hum in the iron is steady, the water is clean enough to taste, and the silence between the gears is safe to breathe in.

And on the rim, satisfied, the attention withdraws—leaving the small, wet engine to keep tearing itself down and rebuilding itself in the cool daylight, undisturbed.
