It would begin by not turning on a light.

To arrive at the bald, wind-scoured crown of the mountain and flood the hollow below with radiance—even a gentle, golden radiance—would be an act of violence disguised as a gift. A power that is merely kind might do it out of pity. A power that is wise would first stand outside in the thin, freezing air of the flattened world, feel the tiny inward draft slipping through the hairline fissures in the stone, and listen.

It would listen the way the hollow itself listens: through pressure, through warmth, through the slow travel of vibration in ancient rock.

Down through miles of weathered granite, its attention would sink, careful not to displace a single grain of dust, until it reached the ceiling of the cathedral. There, it would pause and take the measure of the place.

At first, it would hear only the roar—the frantic, overlapping clicks of the blind fliers, the throbbing of throat-sacs, the dry rasping along the shelves, the endless, measured *plok* of water falling from curved stone ribs into warm marshes. It would feel the heavy, sweet-mineral dampness, the slow rising currents of air, the gentle bleed of heat from the five or six incomprehensible engines that keep the chamber alive.

And then, looking more closely—not with eyes, for eyes are useless here, but with the patience that notices what is missing—it would find the twelve blank spaces in the sound.

It would watch a dweller crouch on a high shelf for two days without stirring, its mossy back drinking the sonar of passing fliers until it became a hole in the world. It would watch another wade into a marsh-pool, peel a waxy reed-stalk with blunt claws, and tuck a hard-shelled grazer deep against its padded chest so the crack of the shell would not bruise the room’s acoustics. It would feel the long, glacial conversations pass through the bedrock: a slow flex of muscle, a sub-audible thrum rising through splayed ankles into a distant jawbone, an hour spent exchanging a single thought about the weight of the water or the thickness of the tendrils.

It would see them gather by the three dark ribs in the eastern wall, pressing their heavy bellies to the weeping stone, tracing the geometric ripples in the puddles as if reading braille written by a god that had long since forgotten it was speaking.

And it would feel the temptation that every great power feels when it finds something small and hidden: the urge to *improve* it.

*Look how dark it is,* a lesser mercy might say. *Let me give them eyes, and a lantern that never goes out.*
But the wise power would see that darkness is not a lack here; it is the loom on which their entire world is woven. Light would not reveal the cathedral to them—it would only blind the fliers, scorch the pale moss, and introduce terror to a people who have never known what it means to be seen from afar.

*Look how trapped they are,* another impulse might whisper. *Let me part the dome and show them the sky.*
And the power would look back up at the sky—at the thin, bitter, desiccated wind tearing across the flattened plains miles below—and know that to open the roof would be to murder the room. The warmth would vanish in an hour; the marshes would freeze into jagged glass; the dwellers would suffocate in the open air, horrified by a silence that had no walls to catch it.

*Then let me teach them,* the power might think. *Let me explain what the floating polyhedron is doing, and why the eastern ribs hum, and what ancient hands built this place before the mountain lost its peaks.*
Yet watching them climb the highest shelves every few decades, tilting their great, pale listening dishes toward the void to receive the single, pure chime that shakes the droplets loose, the power would understand that explanation would shrink their world. Right now, the polyhedron is a warm, singing lens around which the noise of life bends like water around a boulder. To name it a *machine* would be to turn a sunrise into a clock.

So the power would do none of those things. It would recognize a rare and terrible truth: that this place is not broken. It is complete. It has achieved a balance so delicate, so thoroughly reconciled to its own boundaries, that almost any touch from the outside would be a wound.

Almost any touch.

Because a wise power does not only look at what *is*; it looks at what *will be*, down the long, slow slope of epochs.

Withdrawing its attention from the warm marsh-air, the power would examine the mountain itself—the bald, sleeping-whale back of stone exposed to the sky. And there, in the outer shell of the dome, it would see what the dwellers cannot feel from below.

It would see the patient work of frost and wind. Across a thousand winters—a blink to the power, an eternity to a reed-stalk—the hairline fissures in the ancient cap are widening, fraction by fraction. Not yet enough to let the cold pour in, not yet enough to disturb the drip-rate of the ceiling, but trending toward a distant century when a wedge of ice will finally lever a slab of the crown loose, and the sky will break in uninvited.

And deeper still, beneath the weeping eastern wall, it would lay its attention against the roots of the ancient artifacts. It would see that they are indeed idling at the end of a billion-year task, their waste-heat steady for now, yet finite—a banked fire that, in some unimaginable future, will cool to match the dead stone around it.

So this is what the benevolent and wise power would do:

It would remain outside, in the cold wind, unseen and unheard.

It would lay its hands—metaphorical, vast, as gentle as gravity—upon the outer crust of the mountain dome. Slowly, molecule by molecule, it would knit the deepest, most dangerous fractures in the cap, drawing the stone back together into seamless strength, while taking exquisite care to leave the microscopic pores open so the mountain could still draw its thin, patient breath of outside air. It would not change a single curve of the resonant ceiling within. Not one falling drop would miss its beat.

Then, reaching down through the bedrock without touching the dwellers’ shelves or waking the marsh-mud, it would find the deep thermal roots of the eastern ribs and the suspended polyhedron. It would not alter their purpose, nor speed their slow calculation, nor change a single hertz of the hum that carves ripples into the puddles. It would simply replenish the quiet reservoir of their waste-energy—tending the ember so it would not go cold before its time, buying the cathedral another age of tropically mild dark, another thousand decadal chimes.

For a moment, perhaps, the power might feel the wish to say *hello*—to press a single, gentle thrum into the bedrock, a thirteenth voice in the slow, sub-audible chorus, just to let them know they are not alone in the universe.

And then it would choose not to.

Because a thirteenth voice that spoke once and vanished forever would leave behind a ghost, a mystery that aches instead of singing. The dwellers do not need to know they are watched over. They only need the mountain to hold.

So the power would lift its hands from the stone and withdraw, leaving the flattened world as empty and wind-scoured as it found it.

Inside, miles above the plains, a drop of mineral water gathers on a rounded stone rib, grows heavy, and falls through the absolute dark.

*Plok.*

On a high shelf, a dweller shifts its mossy weight, feels the long, familiar hum of the eastern wall steady in its jawbone, and goes on listening to the room.
