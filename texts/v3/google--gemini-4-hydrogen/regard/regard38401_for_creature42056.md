At first, the power came because of the sound.

From far beyond the slumped slate hills, the steady *thump—hiss* and the sharp, wet *crack* of the arcs sounded like something stranded—an old engine left running in an empty room, or a creature calling out in a language no one was left to answer. It was the kind of noise that usually means something has been forgotten, or broken, or left behind.

So the power stepped onto the worn, dark ridge and looked down.

It saw the flat daylight, the knee-high veil of warm mist, the endless wet grey shale with not a single blade of grass to soften it. It felt the sixty-cycle hum rising through the stone, sharp enough to ache. And as a power accustomed to mending—to coaxing green shoots out of ash, to quieting fevers, to lifting heavy things out of the mud—its first instinct was pity.

It descended into the basin, moving carefully across the damp shale, and knelt beside one of the low, rust-stained mounds.

Up close, the creature looked even more burdened. Blind, headless, smeared in clay, its back weighted down with black iron sand that had sintered into a rough, metallic crust. Every three seconds, when the nearest basalt chimney blew its plume of steam, the creature flattened and sighed; every three seconds, when the ironstone plug clacked shut, it swelled again.

The power reached out—not yet touching, only hovering a palm’s width above the iron-crusted ridge—and wondered what it would take to ease this.

*I could quiet the vents,* it thought. *I could cool the brine, and bring good soil over the ridge, and give this thing eyes to see the sky.*

Before acting, however, the power waited. Wisdom, after all, is mostly the patience to look twice.

It watched for an hour. Then for a day. Then for the slow turning of a season that did not change.

It felt the steady, comforting heat radiating from the creature’s flanks—an island of warmth in the lukewarm mist. It watched a palm-sized bronze crustacean creep across the iron-packed spine, methodically scraping away a film of sulfur-yellow scale before it could harden into stone and choke the hide. It watched a pale, blind flyer drop out of the steam, timed to the exact three-second pause, to sip a bead of pure condensation from the creature’s cooler edge.

It followed the shallow, sluggish trickle of acidic water upstream and saw how the shaking earth sorted the grit like a patient hand, washing the pale silica away and laying neat, gleaming pockets of iron sand and raw copper granules directly in the creature’s path.

And then, as the power stood watching in the mist, the mound beside it tensed.

It lifted the edge of its pale grounding-pad a fraction of an inch off the copper vein. A bright, jagged spark snapped across the wet gap—*CRACK*—filling the warm air with the sharp, clean scent of ozone. The pad settled back down. The deep purr resumed.

A quarter-mile away, half-hidden in the steam, another rust-stained ridge lifted its edge and answered.

The power stayed very still.

It looked further down the vein, where an older mound sat—one that had stopped moving decades ago. Its grounding-pad had fused to the copper; its iron core had hardened into solid, sintered stone. Yet as the power listened, it heard the old one still humming softly in the mist, still carrying the current cleanly onward for whatever might crawl past it next.

Understanding settled over the power, quiet and complete.

If it brought soil and grass, the roots would choke the sluice-boxes and starve the mounds of copper. If it silenced the basalt chimneys, the flyers would lose their clock, the crustaceans would lose their bearings, and the ground would go cold. If it gave the mounds eyes, there would be nothing for them to see except how small they were beneath an empty sky—and they already knew the basin by a much truer sense, feeling every neighbor’s pulse straight through the rock.

There was no wound here to close. There was no hunger that was not fed, no loneliness that went unanswered, no death that failed to serve the living. The basin had spent millions of years arriving at its own austere, humming peace. To "improve" it would only be a gentler word for breaking it.

So the power withdrew its hand without ever having touched the creature’s back.

It stood up, careful not to let its own presence ground the current or disturb the mist. As it turned to leave, it glanced down and noticed a faint, shallow heel-print it had left in the wet clay beside a trickle of water—just deep enough to turn a few grains of sorted copper aside from the channel.

Slowly, the power knelt once more and smoothed the mud flat with the side of its hand, restoring the tiny sluiceway to its ancient grade.

Then it walked back up the worn slate ridge. It did not build a wall around the valley, nor set a guard upon the rim, for a wall would cast a shadow and a guard would make a sound that did not belong. Instead, as it stepped away into the wider world, it simply wove a quiet, ordinary forgetfulness into the air along the outer hills—a subtle turning-aside of curiosity, so that anyone passing by with shovels, wires, or plans would hear only an unremarkable wind, and walk on without ever looking down.

Behind it, down in the warm mist, the stone valve lifted with a *THUMP*, vented its white plume, and clacked back into its seat. Three seconds later, it did it again.
