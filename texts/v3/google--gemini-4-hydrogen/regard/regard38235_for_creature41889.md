At first, it only listened.

From a vantage that had no need of hills, the attention settled over the basin like a change in barometric pressure—silent, weightless, taking the measure of the shimmering haze. It heard the overlapping *tink, tink-tink* of the frogs rolling across a mile of ankle-deep water; it felt the low, wet rumble of a bull grazer’s bellow traveling through the limestone shelf before it ever reached the air. It saw the whole flat table at once: the tea-colored wallows, the salt rings, the bone-pale wading birds spaced out like markers on a surveyor’s chart.

Benevolence, when it first arrives, is almost always tempted to mend.

Looking down at the blind loaches nosing through the murk beneath the bacterial felt, the power considered giving them sight—clearing the tea-dark water, sharpening the world into edges and colors. Looking at the chimney-builders, toiling pellet by pellet to stay an inch ahead of the creeping tide, it considered holding the water steady so they might rest. It saw the tiny frogs sealing themselves in papery skin when the clay cracked dry, the lizards pressed flat against sun-bleached bark for a scrap of shade, the midges rising like smoke only to fall before dusk. *Nowhere to hide,* it thought, *and nowhere to go.*

It could have raised a ridge of blue hills along the northern rim to catch the clouds and send cool, rushing rivers down through the clay. It could have enriched the leached silt with dark loam, drawn forests up out of the chert mounds, softened the glare, quieted the metallic clamor into something gentler. It could have given the old, algae-backed grazers a longer memory, or spared the silt-worm from the loach, or eased the dry season so no frog ever had to wait in the dark under the sedge roots.

For a long moment—if time can be measured where nothing is in a hurry—the power weighed each of these gifts.

And wisdom stayed its hand.

To raise a mountain would be to bury the pans. To send a rushing river would scour away the olive felt, drown the chimneys, and wash the salt-crust spiders out of their torn-gauze crowns. Sight would be no kindness to a loach that lives in the tight seam between stone and silt; eyes would only be soft things to bruise against the lime, where barbels already taste everything worth knowing. And if the water never shrank to concentrate the fish in the wallows, the wading birds would starve; if the midges did not fall in a soft, continuous rain, the skimmer-bugs and the algae would thin and fail.

This place was not broken. It was not waiting to become a forest or a garden. It had spent ages shedding everything unnecessary—every peak, every torrent, every hurried thing—until it settled into this final, sunlit posture. Every creature on the plain was already tuned, down to the width of a toe-pad and the curve of a bill, to the slow, inch-by-inch breathing of the basin.

To improve it would be to unmake it.

So the power did almost nothing.

It did not touch the water, or cool the sun, or alter the salt in the clay. It only leaned a little closer to the warm afternoon, the way one might cup a hand around a flame without touching the wick—not to change how it burns, only to keep a sudden, careless wind from blowing it out before its time. Then it withdrew its hand entirely, leaving no sign on the mud, no shadow on the water, no hush in the reeds.

Down in the shallows, a patch of bacterial felt swelled with gas, tore loose, and floated upward. A loach darted into the brief shade underneath. On a baked-earth rim, a crustacean hooked its narrow claw over the lip and waited. And across the flat, shimmering miles, the frogs caught the rhythm again—*tink, tink-tink, tink*—loud and unafraid under the wide-open sky.
