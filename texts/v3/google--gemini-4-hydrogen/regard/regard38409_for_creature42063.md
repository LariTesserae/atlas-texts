When the attention arrived, it did not come as light. Light would have cut shadows across a world that had forgotten them, and forced the blind moths deeper under their slabs of basalt. It did not come as wind, or as a voice in the thin air, because anything loud enough to carry across the stone polygons would have felt like an injury.

It settled instead like the stratus itself—vast, patient, and everywhere at once, resting against the pewter ceiling of the sky until it could read the plateau the way the dwellers read one another: by stillness, and by pressure.

At first, the power felt the simple, heavy ache of mercy.

Looking down through the mist, it saw a people without wood, without fire, without a horizon to build against. It saw them crouched in shallow pits dug into the frost, breathing air so thin it would starve a lowland heart, eating salt-pressed isopods and waiting beside warm vents for old beasts to lie down and die. It saw no towers, no songs, no graves—only bodies unlaced on dark stone for the birds and the mites to clear away.

And because the power was vast, it held within itself the effortless ability to mend every apparent lack. With a thought, it could have parted the stratus and poured a gentle, golden sun across the slate. It could have warmed the air, sweetened the brine-pans into clear rivers, raised stone halls to keep out the damp, and stilled the ancient, humming machinery beneath the crust so that the people might inherit the earth rather than its buried wires. It could have given them voices that carried for miles, and years that did not taste of copper and salt.

It poised on the edge of that gift.

Then, because it was wise as well as kind, it waited. It looked longer.

It watched a column of ten thousand striders moving through the fog, dead-straight, guided by the weak magnetic pull of a superconducting line twenty feet below the grit. It saw a family of dwellers step quietly into the middle of the herd—not driving the beasts, not fearing them, simply walking inside the warm, oily shelter of their flanks while the mist drifted past. It watched a woman reach out in the dimness and press a single knuckle to her companion’s collarbone, a touch so small and exact it carried a whole sentence without spilling a drop of moisture from either throat.

It looked beneath a flat slab of basalt and saw the velvet moths packed shoulder to shoulder, drawing life from the induction hum. It watched a traveler walk beside a salt-crusted collector-sled, scraping bryozoans from its padded rollers with a bone knife—a creature and a machine keeping each other’s path clear without either knowing the word for debt. It watched the pale bones of an elder being shaped into a needle to stitch a child’s moth-lined coat.

And the power understood what its mercy would have done.

If it parted the clouds, the brine-pans would dry, the isopods would die, and the striders would overheat and sicken in their heavy guard-hairs. If it stilled the buried engine, the magnetic roads would vanish, the herds would scatter and freeze in the fog, the pale fungus in the vent-slits would wither, and the sunken courts would turn to tombs of ice. If it gave the dwellers loud voices and tall walls, the wind would find them, and they would lose the quietness that let them hear the *tick* of a cooling stone or feel the hum of the trunk lines through the soles of their moss-stuffed pads.

Nothing here was broken. The plateau was not a ruin waiting to be rescued; it was a vessel that had already found its final angle. To pour more warmth into it would be to shatter it.

So the power drew back its hand from the surface. It touched not one lichen, not one strider, not one sunken roof of greased hide. The greatest kindness it could offer was restraint—to let the world remain what it had taken ten thousand winters to become.

Yet it did not leave entirely empty-handed. Before it withdrew, it looked deeper—past the slate, past the frost-line, down into the dark architecture of the forgotten refinery, and outward to the distant rim where the tableland dropped away toward the curvature of the earth.

Even things built to outlast memory eventually wear. Deep beneath the scree, in the cold heart of the rock, the power found places where the ancient electro-osmotic beds were slowly silting shut, and where a hairline fault in the bedrock would, in three or four centuries, sever a superconducting trunk line and leave a hundred miles of herd-road dark and cold. Left alone, the failure would be sudden—too fast for moss that grows in millimeters per decade, too fast for a people whose lives are braided into the hum.

With a touch no heavier than frost settling on slate, the power mended the deep stone. It did not make the engine brighter, or warmer, or more obedient; it simply sealed the fracture and cleared the slow mineral channels, weaving the buried circuits a little more tightly into the geology so that the plateau’s long present would not snap.

Then it turned its attention to the edges of the world. Beyond the mist lay louder lands—places of fire and iron, of builders who could not see a flat stone without wanting to raise a wall upon it, and helpers who could not see a quiet people without wanting to teach them how to shout. Along the long, crumbling scarps where the plateau met the lower air, the power laid a gentle indifference. It thickened the stratus just enough, turned the paths of wanderers slightly aside, and made the high approaches look barren and unrewarding to eyes that only knew how to measure wealth in timber and gold.

That was all.

When the attention lifted, there was no sign it had ever been there. No star shone through the pewter sky. No dweller looked up from the fog, because nothing had disturbed the silence.

Down in a sunken court, a child woke, pressed bare soles against the warm slate above a trunk line, and felt the steady, familiar hum rising through the stone. Outside, a gray glider banked without a sound through the mist, and a mile away, a line of pale backs moved steadily toward the seepage-basins, leaving nothing behind them but wet rock and the faint, clean smell of ozone and salt.
