At first, the Power’s instinct was to weep, and then to mend.

It is the oldest temptation of benevolence: to look upon a harsh place and measure it by the sky. From above—from the vantage of open air, green fields, and quiet mornings—the hollow looked like a wound in the earth that had forgotten how to close. The Power saw the choking steam, the deafening roar of the piston-pumps, the skin rubbed raw with bitter turpentine-oil to keep the rot at bay. It saw small-boned people flinching behind brass visors against the sputter of arc-lamps, living out their lives in twenty-minute spasms between scalding bursts of waste vapor.

A lesser kindness would have acted immediately. It would have parted the eighty-foot ceiling of braided taproots to let the sun pour down. It would have hushed the screaming turbines, dried the weeping moss, healed the pale, wide-irised eyes so they could bear the day, and led the tenders up into the grass and the wind.

Wisdom stayed the hand.

Before it altered a single drop of condensation, the Power chose to stop and listen. Not from the upper dark, where the eyeless flyers banked and clicked, and not with ears—for ears were useless here—but by settling its attention quietly into the perforated iron grating, barefoot and still, the way the tenders themselves did.

And through the iron, the hollow spoke.

It did not speak of suffering. It spoke of an astonishing, hard-won *grace*.

The Power felt the steady, reassuring *clank-hiss-thud* of the pumps not as noise, but as a heartbeat that kept the air sweet in a place without wind. It watched a tender swing out on a silk rope-sling, unhurried and sure, to chip back a lip of encroaching wood from a brass sap-tap—not in anger at the tree, but the way a gardener trims a vine so both the vine and the trellis may survive. It watched another kneel beside a humming transformer, gently lifting a mat of translucent, pulsing slugs with a horn spatula, tending the copper beneath, and laying the blind creatures carefully back down with a scrap of corroded wire as a treat.

It saw the strips of boiled silk tied to new steel cables so the winged hunters wouldn't break their necks in the dark. It saw the quiet, warm chambers carved into the swollen tubers behind the walls, where the roar of the cavern softened to a cradle-thrum, and jar-fungus shed a cool, green peace over hammocks and clay jugs.

To part the ceiling and let in the sun would not be a rescue; it would be an execution. The light would blind the tenders and bake the living walls to dead kindling. Silencing the pumps would suffocate the air and starve the grazers. Bringing these people to the surface would leave them deafened by silence, clumsy on dead stone, homesick for a vibration they could no longer feel beneath their heels.

They were not prisoners of the machine, nor victims of the dark. They were the hollow’s conscience—its pruners, its nerve-endings, its hands.

So the Power did nothing to the sky. It did nothing to the noise. It did not announce itself in thunder or light, for a god arriving in glory here would only shatter what it came to love.

Instead, it looked deeper. If you love a living thing as it is, you do not remake it; you ask only what quiet, mortal flaw might undo it from within, beyond its own power to mend.

The Power traced the iron. It followed the tensioned steel cables from anchor-bolt to anchor-bolt, down into the living wood that had healed around them like bone around shrapnel. And there, wisdom found the single sorrow the tenders could not outwork.

*Living wood grows back. Iron does not.*

The ancient settlement had been built with a finite dowry of metal. Generation by generation, the dampness was eating it. Rust was a slow, one-way door. The tenders could patch a leaking valve with boiled silk, they could scavenge a dead grazer’s shell for a bucket, they could splice a frayed cable—but in another century, or three, the last dynamo housing would thin to lace. The last arc-carbons would burn to ash. The copper conduits would crumble into green dust. And when the fans finally stopped, the spores would thicken, the cycle would choke, and the great, warm engine beneath the world would suffocate in its own sweetness.

The Power smiled then—a quiet, invisible shift in the dark, no louder than a drop of lukewarm water falling from the canopy to strike a broad leaf with a soft *plink*.

It reached down, past the eighty-foot floor of matted moss, past the deepest braided taproots, into the forgotten earth far below where the plant-matter drank. There, it did not conjure finished gears or gleaming new sheds—for to take away the tending would be to take away the tenders’ purpose, their language, their pride.

Instead, it taught the roots a new alchemy.

Slowly, gently, it awakened a hunger in the deepest root-tips for the trace iron, the copper, and the carbon sleeping in the dark strata beneath the cavern. It wove a quiet instruction into the sap. From that moment on, wherever the living wood wrapped itself around a rusting anchor-bolt or a thinning girder, it would no longer merely swallow the metal; it would *mineralize* along the grain, laying down dense, flexible, rustless veins of iron-hard heartwood that rang as true as steel when tapped with a brass wrench. Inside the swollen tubers, alongside the amber sap, small, dense nodules of pure carbon and copper-rich resin would begin to form—waiting to be found, harvested, and shaped by oil-slicked hands when the old stores finally ran low.

And then, because benevolence is rarely content with mere survival when it can also offer a small, tender mercy, the Power lingered for one moment longer inside the quiet burrows behind the walls.

It touched the pale-green fungus glowing inside the glass jars. It changed nothing of its light, so no eye would be startled. It only taught the fungus to breathe out a faint, cool sweetness into the sleeping chambers—just enough to soothe the rawness from chests that had breathed ammonia and roasted chitin-dust all day, and to ease the ache in joints that had spent a lifetime gripping wet, vibrating iron.

Then the Power stepped back.

High above, the central pressure-tanks shuddered. A rapid *tink-tink-tink* rippled down the tensioned cables, vibrating up through the bare soles of a tender’s feet. Across the dim void, small figures paused at their valves, clipped their harnesses to the trusses, and pulled their boiled-silk hoods tight over their mouths.

The tanks shrieked. The hot mist rolled out, the root-walls flexed and sweated, the grazers unsealed to drink, and the winged hunters dropped from the dark in a whistling cloud.

Through the fog and the deafening roar, an oiled figure darted out onto a slick catwalk to free a jammed overhead bucket. As they passed a primary suspension cable—one that had sung slightly flat and tired with age only an hour before—they tapped it out of habit with the butt of a brass wrench.

*Tink-tink-clack.*

The cable answered with a clear, deep, enduring ring, strong enough to last a thousand years. The tender paused for half a heartbeat, tilted their head in quiet satisfaction, and vanished back into the ten-foot shadow between the lamps.
