To love a place like this, a power would first have to learn how to stop moving.

When its attention first settled over the jagged crown of the basalt peaks, it came—as vast, ancient things often do—with the momentum of stars and seasons. Had it entered the water at that speed, even as a thought, it would have been a catastrophe. A tremor in the golden-green light would have panicked nothing, for nothing here knew how to dart, yet the sheer *haste* of it would have rung against the obsidian-flecked terraces like a struck bell, jarring the hair-trigger talus loose and sending a storm of silt into a sky that had never known a cloud.

So the power slowed. It shed its urgency the way a climber sheds a heavy pack at the summit, until its awareness moved no faster than the afternoon sun sliding down the stepped stone. Only then did it slip beneath the windless surface.

For a long time—weeks, perhaps, or years; time in the basin was measured only by the faint turn of the thermal drift—it simply watched.

It watched the fierce, unfiltered daylight pour through the clear water. It learned to tell the matte blue-black shadows of the dwellers apart from the crisp shadows of the upper ridges. It counted them: a few dozen, spaced generously through the flooded forest of *bladder-stems*, moving with a care that looked like reverence. It saw how they tested every precarious stone with their broad, padded lobes before trusting it with their weight; how they drank gently from the glassy oxygen bladders; how they braided the silver-green silk into cradles for their fist-sized young.

And it listened to the *plinks*. At first they sounded like the forest merely exhaling. By the third turning of the seasons, the power understood the grammar of the silences between them—the three-minute pause that meant *the talus on the eastern shelf is shifting*, the long, bright note that meant *I am here, in the upper canopy, and the light is good*.

Seeing all this, a lesser benevolence might have wept for how fragile it was, and rushed to mend it.

The impulse rose even in the wise power, brief and tender: *There are so few of them. One winter too harsh, one tremor in the rock, and the valley ends.* It could have smoothed the razor-sharp edges of the talus so the dwellers would never fear a fall. It could have given them voices, or sped up their slow, moss-fed metabolisms, or stocked the basin with small, harmless fish to keep them company. It could have multiplied their numbers until the forest was full.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

To smooth the stone would be to rob the crimson moss of the sharp geometry it clung to, and to turn a cathedral of fractured glass into an ordinary pond. To give them voices would be to drown the *plinks* in noise. To make them fast would be to make them clumsy, and clumsiness here was a kind of death. And to crowd the valley would be to steal the arm’s length of clear water they left between themselves even in sleep.

This place was not broken. It was only *unlikely*—a miracle balanced on the edge of a knife, in a world that rarely spared knife-edges.

So the power chose the harder kindness: to protect the valley without ever letting the valley know it had been touched.

Far beneath the fractured limestone and the dark basalt, deeper than the dwellers ever swam, the power laid a quiet palm against the earth’s slow pulse. It did not alter the lazy geothermal seepages—not by a single degree—for the dwellers’ lives were tuned to that exact, enveloping warmth. Instead, it steadied the deep conduits of stone beneath them, knitting the restless fault lines together so that no sudden quake would shatter the bowl, and coaxing the magma’s distant breath into a long, faithful rhythm that would neither surge into steam nor falter into alpine ice.

Above, in the thin, biting air outside the rim where the dwellers never breached, it wove a subtle weariness into the approaches. No walls of light, no roaring winds—only a trick of shadow and distance, so that wandering flocks of lowland birds would veer away without knowing why, and any traveler climbing the outer ridges would find the air too sharp, the slopes too blank, and turn back before ever glimpsing the bright, warm sea cradled inside the crown.

Once, and only once, the power acted within the water itself.

It was late dusk. The upper layers had begun their slow, glacial sink, and the adults had descended to the deeper terraces to tuck their heads beneath their moss-grown shoulders. In a quiet corner of the forest, a braided cradle of silver-green silk had come slightly loose from its *bladder-stem* moorings. Carried by an stray eddy of the night-chill, it was drifting—millimeter by patient millimeter—toward a knife-edged ridge of bare obsidian. Inside, a translucent youngling slept, unaware.

The power did not catch the cradle. A hand in the water, however gentle, would have stirred the silk and woken the forest. Instead, it warmed a single flat stone a meter away by the barest fraction of a degree—no more than the memory of noon.

The water above the stone rose, an imperceptible plume. The thermal drift turned, soft as breath, and nudged the cradle back into the shelter of the amber fronds, where the hanging filaments caught it and held it fast.

Nothing woke. Not a single bubble tore free.

Satisfied, the power drew its attention back up through the sunlit column, past the windless surface, and out into the cold sky above the peaks. It left no sign of its passing—no blessing carved in the basalt, no new song in the water.

Down in the golden-green depths, the valley kept its own counsel. At dawn, a slender, moss-backed figure climbed into the canopy, parted a curtain of wet silk, and pinched a taut bladder beneath a frond.

*Plink.*

The glassy note carried three hundred meters through the stillness, unanswered for four long minutes, until another came back from the far terrace—unhurried, unafraid, and entirely unaware that the mountain itself had been persuaded to be as patient as they were.
