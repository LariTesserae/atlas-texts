If a power were truly wise, the first thing it would do is hold still.

It would not arrive in a pillar of cloud, because a cloud here would be an intrusion—a wet, soft bruise on a bleached indigo sky that has never known one. It would not lower its voice into a gentle whisper, because nothing here hears speech, and a whisper would only be lost beneath the chest-rattling hum of the subterranean slag. It would pare itself down instead, shedding every habit of comfort, until its attention was as dry, clear, and unhurried as the high-altitude light.

Only then would it look.

It would watch the stilt-beasts take their minute-long strides across the salt-pan, feeling the hollow crust flex a fraction of a millimeter beneath each footstep and hearing the subterranean pendulums answer in the superheated dark. It would watch the bronze eels saw a fallen frame down to sterile silica, the carbon limpets drink their three-second glow of static, the mica kites ride the scalding *HISS-THUMP* of the thermal bladders.

And at last, it would find the spire-walker, standing three miles out on the shimmering flat, its three brass-and-china feet screwed half an inch into the salt, its slate collar turning with a gritty *shhh-click* toward an empty patch of air where a shadow had wandered too far from its wheel.

A lesser benevolence—one driven by pity rather than wisdom—would have reached out its hand right there, and ruined everything.

It would have seen a parched, merciless desert and called down rain. Within an hour, the vitrified salt would have dissolved into a blinding mire; the copper coils would have shorted themselves into smoking ash; the hollow shell of the plateau, softened by water it was never meant to bear, would have groaned once and collapsed into the superheated dark, burying the pendulums and drowning the great, blind engine in steam and mud.

A lesser wisdom—one driven by tidiness rather than understanding—would have mended the frayed geometry. It would have stitched the plateau’s space back together so that miles stayed miles, snapped every stray shadow permanently beneath its wheel, and synced every distant *clank* to the motion of its joint. And in doing so, it would have sealed the engine’s only relief-valves—and starved the spire-walker’s meteoric-iron heart, leaving the lone surveyor to stand motionless on the salt until its gears seized for want of a single eddy to ground.

A lesser mercy would have quieted the din, or given the spire-walker another of its kind within sight, or lent it a voice to name its loneliness. It would not have realized that here, silence means a turbine has failed; that here, distance is the only thing keeping two vast, charged presences from tearing each other apart in violet lightning; that the spire-walker is not lonely, because it was never built for company. It was built for *attention*.

So the wise power would do none of those things.

Instead, it would read the tolerances of the plateau the way a master smith reads the grain of hot iron—checking not for comfort, which has no meaning here, but for *distress*. Is the crust thinning faster than the salt can vitrify? Is the blind slag below grinding too close to a fault? Is the spire-walker’s iron flywheel worn past its balance, or its basalt mast cracked by a load it cannot bear?

It would find no agony. Only heat, torque, wear, and return. An engine running loud and slow on its own fierce terms, neither asking for rescue nor needing it.

What, then, would it do?

Almost nothing.

It would step back from the salt-pan without leaving a footprint, without chilling a single thermal or damping a single arc. If it laid a hand anywhere, it would not be upon the plateau at all, but far out along the outer reaches of the world—a subtle, invisible curve in the approaches, no thicker than a heat-haze, so that nothing careless would ever stumble onto the basin. No miners hungry for magnetite, no terraformers carrying seeds and water, no younger gods eager to tidy up a broken corner of physics. It would keep the sanctuary’s edges uncrowded, the way the plateau itself keeps space thin between its walkers.

And before it withdrew, it would wait out one cycle beside the spire-walker.

Not speaking. Not lightening the load. Simply standing in the scalding, ozone-thick air while the basalt spire unfolded its dry, copper-plated dandelion into a shimmering seam of air; listening to the papery *hiss* as the eddy flattened out; watching the distant shadow snap briefly home to its wheel, and feeling the heavy iron heart inside the spire pick up by a fraction of a revolution per minute.

Then it would leave the spire-walker to unscrew its brass feet and begin its slow, solitary march toward the next frayed edge—and leave the great, deafening, bone-dry engine to turn in the sun.
