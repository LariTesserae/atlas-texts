At first, looking down from the high white sky, the power felt the old, familiar ache of wanting to mend.

It saw a bowl no wider than four hundred paces, brimming with noise and wet effort. It saw tall, narrow figures with moss-freckled skin hanging like pale fruit from swaying cables, never allowed to sleep with both halves of a mind at once. It saw their chambers rot back into pulp before a thought could cool, their elders wade into tea-colored soup without a stone to mark where they had stood, their voices drowned before they left their throats by the *clack* of snapping vines and the bass thrum of gravity-lenses.

A lesser kindness would have reached down at once.

It would have hushed the din so they could hear themselves think. It would have dried a strip of ground into solid, unyielding earth, built them a house that did not dissolve, given them beds that did not sway over ozone-scented air. It would have carved their names into the soapstone rim so the basin might remember them, and it would have halted the invisible clearing-pulses so the reeds could finally grow tall and rest.

And in doing so, it would have killed them—slowly, gently, out of pure pity—turning a living engine into a quiet museum, and its editors into ghosts with nothing left to tend.

Because this power was wise as well as gentle, it did not reach down. Instead, it waited, and learned how to listen in the basin’s own register.

It stopped listening to the air, where the marsh-birds shrieked and condensation drummed like rain, and laid its attention flat against a tensioned lignin cable. Through that narrow bridge, the chaos resolved into rhythm: the steady, deliberate pulse of water-columns hauling light upward; the crisp, coded taps of hardened fingernails announcing a loose knot on tier four; the warning tremor of a terrace shifting its weight.

It slipped into the sudden, green-tinted shadow behind a bank of snapped louvers and watched two figures stand inches apart, their chests washing slowly from amber to faint violet. There was no hurry in that light, and no loneliness. The noise of the workshop roared just outside the leaves, holding the world at bay, while inside the brief dark they read one another cleanly and in peace.

It followed a glowing sphere up a shimmering pillar of uncontained water, past the velvet-shelled climbers with their salt-crusted joints, and felt what the liquid ledger actually held: not desperation, not mere survival, but a fierce, unsentimental caretaking. *This tower leans south. This intake is choked with copper moss. Here is the last warmth of someone who walked into the pool three seasons ago, given freely back to the stream.*

The power understood then that nothing here was broken. The transience was not a curse; it was the only reason the four hundred paces had not choked on their own abundance. The lack of monuments was not poverty; it was the open avenue that let the wind and the birds pass through. To spare these beings their vigilance would be to take away their craft, and to give them permanence would be to lock them in a room that could no longer breathe.

So the power did nothing to the towers. It did not quiet a single cable, nor lengthen a single shadow, nor soften the end of a single life.

Yet as it prepared to withdraw, its attention drifted outward from the humming towers, across the flat green-brown marsh, to the only thing in the basin that was not part of the cycle.

The rim.

Barely knee-high now, rounded and grainless, softened into something like wet soapstone by ages of warm water and patient erosion. Beyond it, the world dropped away into a flat, featureless haze. Everything *inside* the hollow was endlessly rearranging its own bones, endlessly renewing its calcium and lignin in the sun—but the bowl itself was tired. Its rock had reached its final, flattened exhaustion, holding the whole frantic, luminous engine just barely above the edge of nothing.

If the rim wore down by another hand’s-breadth, the tea-colored soup would spill out into the haze, the root-mat would tear, and the water-columns would lose their anchor.

So the power reached beneath the basin—deep below the submerged intakes, below the ribbon-finned schools and the braided roots, where no webbed foot or double-hinged hand would ever touch—and quietly mended the patience of the stone.

It did not raise the rim into a wall. It did not make the soapstone dry, or sharp, or new. To any eye looking across the hollow, the lip remained exactly as worn and humble as before. Only, deep in the ancient bedrock, the slow surrender stopped. The bowl would hold its water. The avenues would stay open.

Then, before it left, the power did one last thing—so small it might have been a trick of the muggy air.

Into a passing water-column, it breathed no command, no revelation, no promise of rescue—only a brief, nameless pulse of warmth, tuned to the exact frequency of amber and faint violet. Perhaps an editor, cradling a glowing sphere in the green shadow of a gallery, felt a strange, quiet steadiness pass between their four palms, like the hum of a bridge that was holding well.

And then the power withdrew, leaving no footprint on the moss, no monument on the rim, and no silence in the sun—only the clear, unbroken din of a world allowed to keep finishing nothing, and keeping everything alive.
