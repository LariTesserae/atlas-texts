At first, the power saw only the harshness.

From a height beyond the white-blue sky, it looked down upon a continent of blistered amber and ash, baked under an unfiltered sun, and its first impulse—the oldest, simplest reflex of benevolence—was pity. It saw a people pared down to bone and sinew, with no outer ears to catch a song, hiding in carved deadwood from the blinding noon, hoarding morning mist in wax-plugged roots. It saw the arithmetic of water, sharp as a tusk, keeping them scattered in threes and fives across a screaming desert of wood.

It reached out a hand to mend what seemed broken.

To bring rain was the easiest thought. A gentle, steady cloudbank, drawn up from the unseen lowlands, would cool the shimmering crust and fill the seep-basins to brimming. 

Yet before the first cloud could gather, wisdom stayed the hand. The power looked deeper—past the sun-baked skin, down through the miles of braided roots, petrified pith, and hollow vascular trunks—and saw what rain would do. This was not stone; it was ancient cellulose, compressed and dry as seasoned timber. A season of true rain would swell the crust, soften the canyon walls, and turn the honeycombed ridges into a sagging, rotting bog. The bellow-worms would drown in their pipes. The drum-mites would choke in mud. The hollowed dwellings would collapse inward, and the people—whose very feet were tuned to read the crisp, dry pitch of the world beneath them—would go deaf in the mire.

So the power withheld the rain.

It considered, then, the noise. The relentless, chest-rattling drone of the wind across millions of broken reed-buttresses, the brassy honking, the metallic rattle of locusts. Surely, it thought, silence would be a mercy. A quiet morning, a restful afternoon.

Again, wisdom looked closer, and saw that to silence the plateau would be to blind its keepers. The roar was not an enemy; it was the medium in which they moved. Their wind-chimes warned them of shifting thermals; their acoustic reeds threw notes tuned to the very frequencies of the gale; their stealth depended on the din. In a sudden, unnatural quiet, every footstep would betray them to the needle-jacks, and the continent would feel dead and hollow underfoot, like a drum with its skin cut away.

It considered softening their bodies, opening their ears, giving them iron and mortar and wide, easy roads between the trees. And each time, it saw that every gift of ease was a quiet erasure—that to remove the weight of this world was to unmake the exact, patient grace of the beings who had learned to carry it.

So the power did something harder than a miracle. It knelt—not in body, for it had none, but in attention—and pressed its awareness flat against the warm, fibrous crust, learning to listen the way the inhabitants listened: through the grain, through the bone, through the long, patient rhythms of the substrate.

It stayed like that for a long time. It felt the dawn hush, when the throat-clicks and soft whistles carried cleanly over the glistening turf. It felt the midday retreat, the cool resin-scented dark behind curtains of peat-felt. It felt the bladder-oaks groan and swell in the afternoon glare, and the sudden, violent cannon-shots of dusk when they wept their brief, precious water into waiting leather.

And slowly, the power understood that it had not stumbled upon a tragedy. It had found a masterpiece of endurance—a living instrument, vast and merciless, and a people who had shaped themselves into its players.

Yet love, even wise love, rarely leaves empty-handed. If it could not rewrite the song, it could still tend the instrument.

Deep beneath the plateau—miles down, far below the canyon huts and the deepest seep-basins, where the ancient vascular trunks had cracked and shifted over indifferent centuries—there were places where the great root-bridges had sheared apart. Across those subterranean gaps, the slow, patient thrums struck from the watch-towers at dusk were losing their way, breaking up into faint, unreadable static before they could reach the neighboring kin-groups miles away.

Without a sound, without a shimmer in the air or a tremor in the crust, the power laid its attention against those deep, fossilized fractures and coaxed the deadwood fibers back together. Just enough. Not to make the ground loud with chatter, only to keep the line true, so that a warning of a dry cistern or the quiet pulse of a neighbor’s survival would carry clean across the miles.

High above, in the thin, clear air where dry lightning sometimes gathered over the resinous plains—the kind of stray spark that could turn a continent of ancient wood into a mile-deep furnace—it gently parted the charge, letting the hot wind pass without fire.

And in the brief, sharp chill before dawn, when a long season of thin air left the catchment trays nearly dry and the wax plugs brittle, it did not send a storm. It only held the night’s coolness on the siliceous hairs of the turf for a single, imperceptible breath longer—not enough to make a river, never enough to make them careless, only enough to keep the gourds from ringing hollow.

Then the power drew back, leaving no monument, no scripture, no reason for anyone to look up at the sky.

Down on the plateau, as dusk settled and the bladder-oaks began to fire along the horizon, a lone walker paused beside a wind-worn cairn of compressed peat. Barefoot on the cooling crust, they rested the butt of their hardwood stave squarely against their collarbone and closed their eyes.

Far away, through the soles of their feet and up through the bone, came the deep, steady mallet-thrum of a distant tower. It was clearer tonight than it had been in years—clean and unbroken through the ancient roots.

The walker did not think of gods or mercies. They simply stood a moment longer, listening to the continent hold its note, before turning toward the canyon dark.
