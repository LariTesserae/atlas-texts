At first, the impulse of simple kindness is to cool the stone.

A lesser mercy, looking down into that mile-wide scar, would see only deprivation. It would see a world starved of green, scorched by flat light, and locked in a frantic, biting panic. It would want to call down a long, patient rain from the plateau to wash the salt into the earth. It would want to dull the razor-edged basalt, quiet the hissing fissures, and drop a single handful of hardy moss onto the wet overhangs so that something, at last, might stay still and grow soft.

So the wise power begins by staying its hand.

Before touching a single pebble, it looks closely—not with pity, which is often just impatience in disguise, but with attention. It listens to the dry, papery roar of the glint-wings and the rhythmic sigh of the steam vents beneath. It watches a plate-back glide across scalding gravel, drawing silica straight from the water to build a shell that matches the fractured travertine around it. It watches a glass-needle curl into a bead of salt-gum, trusting the heat to dry it and the next wandering seep to wake it.

And the power understands that to heal this place would be to murder it.

If the rain fell and stayed, the rust-slime would wash away, and the plate-backs would starve. If the vents cooled, the thermals would die, and the glint-wings would drop like dry leaves onto stone too cold to sustain them. If grass took root in the travertine, the chalk-runners would tangle and rot, the chimney-colonies would choke, and the great, patient tallow-backs—built over decades to turn sulfur, mud, and blind beetles into bone and oil—would find no world left that knew how to hold them.

The basin is not broken. It is a crucible, and everything inside it is forged to the exact temperature of its fire.

Then the power turns its gaze to the margins, where the four or five figures in hide wraps and smoked-mica goggles are working fast on a basalt ledge. It watches them test the gypsum crusts with their hooked poles, strike black glass from a core, and lash hollow carapaces to bone pegs before the floor can reclaim them.

Here, too, simple kindness might be tempted to interfere—to widen the jagged steps down the cliff, to clear the heavers from the talus, or to leave a vein of salt exposed right at the rim so the makers never have to breathe the wet chalk and iron below.

Again, the power withholds its hand. It sees what the basin gives these people that the cool, steady plateau never could. Down here, they cannot afford carelessness, greed, or solitude. They must descend together, watch one another’s backs, read the weight of a salt-crust before trusting it, and take only what they can carry up the stairs. The rift teaches them precision, humility, and the exact weight of a day’s work. To make the basin easy would be to rob them of the craft it taught them.

So in the basin itself, the power does nothing. It leaves the basalt sharp, the steam scalding, and the turnover relentless.

Instead, it turns its attention *upward*—to the plateau above the rim.

Wisdom knows where a system is truly fragile. The basin can survive its own landslides, its bursting vapor pockets, and its swarming floor; it has been doing so for ages. What it cannot survive is a desperate neighbor. If the plateau above ever withers into prolonged famine, the makers will stop coming down as divers. They will come down as conquerors—with fire, with nets, hunting the living tallow-backs before they fall, tearing down the chimney-colonies, and stripping the rift until its fever breaks and it goes silent.

So the power tends to the rim.

It does not make the plateau a paradise, only faithful. It nudges the high winds just enough to keep the seasonal rains reliable on the grasslands above. It keeps the upper soil deep enough that the makers’ herds and tubers endure, ensuring they never look over the cliff-edge with starvation in their eyes.

And then, in the quietest way—not as a command, not as a vision, only as a slight deepening of the silence when the makers sit around their fires at night—it leaves them with a lasting instinct of respect. A sense, passed down in how a father teaches a child to bind tallow-back scute to their shins, that the roaring pit below is not a mistake to be endured or a mine to be emptied, but a living, perilous neighbor whose laws must be kept.

By late afternoon, the party on the ledge finishes its work. Dusted white with chalk from brow to boot, they haul their lashed bundles of salt, obsidian, and dried shell up the sheer steps toward the cool wind.

The power watches them go, and then watches the basin immediately begin its work behind them—sifting salt over the discarded flakes, undermining the unmortared windbreak, sending a scatter of chalk-runners across the empty knapping floor.

Everything is loud, sharp, and intact. The power withdraws its gaze, satisfied, and leaves the stone to sweat.
