It did not arrive with light. Light would have been an insult here, a sudden, white violence against lenses clouded to the color of steeped tea, a bleaching of the chalky bacterial films that clung to the brass intake valves.

Instead, the power entered the way the water had—slowly, through the flooded masonry culvert, letting its own vast momentum bleed away against the dressed basalt until it moved no faster than a rising thermal plume. It had seen worlds of glass and starlight, gardens tended by choirs, cities of dry stone and wind; it knew how to be loud, when loudness was needed. Here, it learned how to be thirty-two degrees Celsius.

For a long time—days, perhaps, or the slow turning of a season above the fifty meters of bedrock—it did nothing at all. It simply hung in the dim, amber-bruised alleys, letting the suspended silt settle back onto the brick ledges, and listened.

It listened with the copper conduits, feeling the steady, tireless sixty-hertz thrum of the submerged grid. It listened with the floor grates, counting the four-minute revolution of the massive iron flywheel in the stamping house. It watched one of the skiff-sized dwellers unhook a ratcheting claw from an overhead busbar, swallow a mouthful of gas-filled bladders from a hanging vine, and drift upward into the cooler dark like a sunken dirigible, its carapace of mineral wax and olive-drab moss brushing gently against a bundle of lagged pipe.

A lesser kindness would have wept for them. A lesser kindness would have seen the drowned workshops, the dim, failing filaments, the blind crayfish, and the pale, rubbery skin hidden beneath crusted industrial resin, and called it a prison. It would have drained the basin, pried open the vaulted ceiling to let the sun pour in, scrubbed the magnetite from the iron beams, and killed everything in the room before noon.

Wisdom, however, knows the difference between a wreck and a harbor.

The power looked at the creature draped across the warm casing of a transformer shack, its chin resting between the porcelain insulators, its lateral pores drinking in the stray voltage while a white crayfish picked algae from its nostrils. It felt no distress there. No hurry. No hunger that could not be satisfied by a slow crawl to a chimney-crack or a long, motionless wait beside an induction coil. This was not a ruin waiting to be rescued. It was a clockwork womb, an accidental sanctuary that had taken a century to find its balance, where machine and moss and flesh had negotiated a peace so complete that none of them could be separated from the others.

And yet, because it was wise, the power looked past the equilibrium to what lay beneath it.

It traced the braided copper cables back through the brick walls, into the sealed oil-baths and the hidden reservoirs that fed the automated winch-chain. It followed the geothermal manifolds down into the bedrock, and it looked up into the fifty meters of dark stone pressing against the vaulted roof.

Nothing here was breaking—today. The magnetite crust had sealed the iron against rust. But time, even when slowed to the pace of falling snow, still kept its ledger.

Deep in the buried hoppers behind the stamping house, the raw feedstock for the mineral wax and grey resin was finite; in another forty years, or sixty, the perforated brass baskets would begin to come up empty, and the dwellers would have nothing left to scrape against their ribs. Inside the throats of the geothermal manifolds, pale lime was slowly thickening, narrowing the passages that kept the floor grates fever-warm. In the dimness above, a hairline fracture in the bedrock ceiling was widening by the thickness of a hair each decade, patient as water, waiting for a winter above ground to shift the weight of the earth. And in the center of the basin, the submerged iron journal of the great flywheel was wearing down, grain by microscopic grain, toward a day when it would seize and fall silent.

If the flywheel stopped, the grid would falter. If the grid faltered, the sixty-hertz map would go dark, and the dwellers would find themselves truly blind in a cold, silent room.

The power considered what to do. It would not remake the basin, nor lift the creatures out of their element, nor even let them know they had been found. The kindest stewardship is the kind that leaves no fingerprints.

So it set to work, matching its pace to the basin’s own.

Down in the dark beneath the floor grates, where no creature ever squeezed, it gently unmade the excess calcium choking the geothermal throats—not stripping the pipes bare, only thinning the crust just enough that the thirty-two-degree warmth would neither fade nor spike. It left the velvet lime on the outer bricks untouched.

In the sealed, oil-filled belly of the stamping house, it eased the microscopic burrs from the flywheel’s axle and lent a quiet, enduring density to the iron, so that the four-minute turn might continue without a shudder or a grind for as long as the earth above held together.

Behind the brick walls, in the dark hoppers that no eye would ever see, it replenished the raw beds of mineral wax and grey resin—not overflowing them, not altering their scent of steeped peat and hot oil, just ensuring that at the end of the winch-chain, neat, pliable ingots would always be piling up in the mud for the creatures to dress themselves in.

It touched the dulled, orange filaments inside the cage-protected globes, not brightening them by a single lumen, only giving the old tungsten the strength to burn on in its bruised-amber twilight without snapping. It traced the fifty meters of bedrock above the vaulted ceiling, knitting the hairline fracture closed with quiet pressure, settling the stone back into its old, heavy sleep.

And when the work was done, the power paused in one of the narrow brick alleys, where two of the dwellers had met on their unhurried circuits.

They were hanging side by side from the overhead pipes, barely room for them to pass, their moss-covered, resin-crusted flanks pressed together in the dimness. For an hour, they stayed like that, exchanging warmth, sharing the faint static hum of their skins.

The power did not speak to them. They had no ears for words, and no use for them. Instead, it reached out and laid a gentle, imperceptible touch upon the braided copper conduit they clung to—a steady, sixty-hertz warmth, indistinguishable from the heartbeat of the basin itself, only a fraction richer, a fraction more whole.

Along their pale flanks, the double rows of tiny pores drank it in. Neither creature stirred. They simply hung a little easier in the thermal drift, their three-minute breaths rising and falling in the mineral-heavy water.

Then, with a soft scrape of magnetite against brick, one of them unhooked a claw, opened its throat to let out a single, hollow *blup* of marsh gas, and sank slowly back toward the warm floor grates.

The power lingered only a moment longer, watching the pale, blind crayfish sweep their antennae through the sluggish current, watching the neat ingots drop into the silt, feeling the quiet, tireless thrum of a town with no sky. Then it drew back through the flooded culvert the way it had come, leaving the water undisturbed, and the long, warm silence unbroken.
