It does not arrive with light. To bring light into a place where nothing has asked for it is not a greeting; it is a blade.

Instead, the attention settles over the coastal shelf the way fog settles—first pooling in the low hollows of the frost-rimed sedge, then seeping, without weight or warmth, down through the hairline cleft in the basalt. It comes as a kind of listening so deep it can hear the mineral water weeping down the raw stone, and the faint, woody sigh of a tuber swelling by the width of a hair.

For a long time, it does nothing at all. It simply learns the grammar of the pocket.

It reads the city not by sight, which would be useless here, but by vibration and temperature and the slow chemistry of wet rot. It feels the knuckle-taps traveling along the taut root-cables—*all is well at the upper cistern; the south bridge needs re-lashing*—and the quieter, almost imperceptible warmth of two damp wrists pressed together in the dark of a gourd-chamber. It watches a small, ash-colored figure crouch in a doorway, knees to chin, paring back the pale bryophyte with a chip of beach flint, unhurried, un-shivering, wholly present to the blade and the moss.

It watches the nightly rising of the slugs, the patient drawing-up of the root-ladders, the blind newt nudged gently from a reed sluice with a notched bone. It watches an elder, too stiff now to clear their own threshold, carried down to the lowest peat and tucked beneath the root-mat with the same deliberate tenderness they once used to lower crushed amphipods to the gilled young.

And watching, the power feels the first, sharp ache of what lesser kinds of love call pity.

*How cold it is,* a simpler mercy would say. *How dark. How endless the labor of keeping a single room from closing shut. How terrifying the dry, roaring world above, where they must creep like pale shadows for a handful of thorns and a gull’s bone.*

For a breath—if a power such as this can be said to draw breath—it considers the gifts it could lay into the pocket.

It could warm the basalt, just enough to take the stiffness from their joints.
*No,* wisdom answers, looking closer. *Warmth would dry the bitter oil from their skin and crack it; it would rot the tallow-stems into mush and wake the wood-mites into a plague.*

It could widen the hairline cleft by a hand’s breadth, letting in a clean shaft of noon.
*No. Light would scald their milky eyes and summon green, sun-hungry weeds to strangle the pale root-curtains. Everything that lives here lives because the sky cannot reach it.*

It could give them iron, or stop the plants from encroaching on their doors, or un-notch their smooth throats so they might sing in the dark.
*No. Iron rusts in salt-seep; a plant that stops encroaching is a plant that has died, and then no new chamber will ever swell above the dead. And voices would wake the cisterns and shatter the quiet geometry of their tapped speech. They do not lack song; they have simply made their music out of wood and distance.*

One by one, the power lays down every grand mercy, understanding at last that this pocket is not a wound waiting to be healed. It is a finished chord, struck in a register so low and quiet that almost nothing in the universe can hear it. To tune it brighter would be to break the strings.

Yet it does not turn away empty-handed. Love, when it is patient enough, finds the seam where help does not bruise.

The power looks at the stone.

The basalt here is young, sharp-edged, and fractured, resting low on a coast that knows winter storms capable of heaving boulders like driftwood. A single pocket is a fragile vessel for a whole people. One freak surge, one shifting of the shelf in the deep frost, and the hairline cleft could grind shut—sealing them from the flint and the salt-air—or split wide to the killing sun.

So the power sets its hand not *inside* the pocket, where its touch would disturb the stillness, but deep in the unweathered grain of the rock beneath and around it. It does not turn the basalt to immutable diamond; stone must remain stone, able to weep mineral water for the fronds. It only eases the strain along the fault-lines, knitting the deeper fractures just enough that when the hundred-year storms hammer the coastal shelf, the sea will break *over* the cleft and never *through* it. The roof will hold. The seam will stay a hairline, neither closing nor tearing open, for as long as the builders wish to live beneath it.

And then, because wisdom need not be austere, it allows itself one small, nearly invisible grace on the world outside.

They are so small when they climb out onto the shelf in the moonless dark, trembling slightly in the dry wind, squinting through milky membranes at the terrifying vastness of the stars, groping among the frost-rimed sedge for what the pocket cannot grow.

The power does not hush the surf or warm the air. It only arranges that on the nights when the climbers come up through the seam, the sea-fog lingers a little thicker in the hollows, keeping the drying wind at bay and holding back the first pale threat of dawn for a few extra heartbeats. And along the tideline nearest the cleft, the winter waves leave good, fine-grained cobbles of beach flint, and the dry scrub drops its thorns within easy reach of cold, five-fingered hands.

Nothing inside the pocket changes.

The water still weeps down the raw rock. The bridges still tilt as the anchor-roots drink, and must be re-lashed with sinew. Down in the quiet dark, a small, ash-colored builder crouches at a doorway, paring back the encroaching moss with a flint scraper, pausing only to tap twice against the gourd-wall—*all is well*—and listening as the living city answers back.

The power listens too, for a moment longer, and then withdraws, leaving no light behind, and no name for them to worship—only a roof that will not fall, and a fog that waits until they are safely home.
