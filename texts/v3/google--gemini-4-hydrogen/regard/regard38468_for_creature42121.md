First, it would quiet itself.

A power accustomed to speaking in storms or shaping continents would have to learn, very quickly, how to be as silent as water seeping through limestone. Looking down from the rim of the chalk cliffs, it would feel the immense, concave stillness of the basin—how even a sigh from above might bounce from rib to rib of the ancient bedrock and unsettle the trapped breath beneath the floor. So it would descend without wind, without shadow, without the faintest hum of presence, resting its attention lightly on the bleached stone.

At first, benevolence alone would ache to intervene.

It would look at the lean, chalk-skinned figures squinting through smears of black soot, their tunics clammy against their ribs, their hands never still. It would see the endless, frantic labor—walls torn down before their stones had settled, causeways slumping into wet grit, lives measured in bone levers and salt-cured fungus. It would see the pewter-grey nights where crews huddled together for warmth because there was nothing to burn, and the blinding white days where even the sky seemed to press down like a lid.

*I could soften this,* the power might think. *I could bring a drift of loam from the outer plains, and seeds that know how to split stone. I could call down a gentle rain to wash the salt-crusts clean, and quiet the restless air beneath the floor so a house might stand for a hundred years. I could open their narrow throats and give them song.*

But wisdom does not act on its first pity. Wisdom waits through a day, and a pewter night, and another day.

It would watch a crew of twenty dismantle a sound retaining wall, passing the chalky blocks hand to hand without a word, and see that there was no despair in the motion—only a swift, shared fluency, like a single body shifting its weight. It would watch them pause to flick sharp flint chips out of the path of a long-necked grazer, clearing the way for its soft, pad-like feet. It would watch a gypsum slab lift and a pale tide of translucent scavengers spill out across the plaza, vanishing into the nearest tower without panic, already at home in the next wall’s chinks.

It would watch the builders lay one of their dead in a deep, dripping crevice at the rim, stripping away the tunic and the copper pin not out of coldness, but because nothing here is hoarded and nothing is wasted. Within weeks, the velvety black fungus would creep over the pale skin, and the scavengers would return it to the damp paste of the floor, to be scraped up and trodden down into the next causeway.

And then the power would understand what its pity would have destroyed.

If it brought soil and green roots, the velvety fungus would die, and with it the grazers, and with them the hide for the mallets, the ribs for the levers, the sinew for the slings. If it brought rain, the unmortared terraces would slump into drowning mud, and the scavengers would suffocate in their chinks. If it stilled the restless ground beneath the floor, the city would stop moving; stones would stay stacked until they were owned, and owned until they were guarded, and the tight, fluid crews would harden into strangers behind locked doors. If it gave them voices in a bowl of concave stone, the echoes would bring down the roofs.

They were not broken. They were fitted to the bone of the world with a precision that left no room for clumsy mercies.

So the power would fold back its grander gifts. It would leave the sky blinding white, the bedrock restless, the air clammy and mute. It would not steal the dignity of their endurance or unmake the covenant between builder, grazer, scavenger, and stone.

Yet it would not turn away entirely. A wise power knows the difference between altering a world’s nature and sparing it from senseless ruin.

High on the outer plains, where careless weather and loose boulders sometimes gathered near the sinkhole’s jagged lip, it would quietly turn the worst of the rockfalls aside, letting the basin keep its own slow, ancient rhythm without interference from above.

And far below, in the shadowless glare, it would do something smaller still—so small that no one in the city would ever look up to wonder at it.

When a pocket of cold subterranean air began to shift beneath a crowded compound in the pewter-grey dark, the power would not seal the pocket or freeze the silt. It would simply let the warning *shudder* travel cleanly through the limestone a few heartbeats sooner—just clear enough, just early enough, for the padded soles of a sleeper’s feet to feel it, for a palm to press flat against a neighbor’s collarbone, and for the whole crew to rise and slip out into the alley before the roof slabs came down.

Then the power would withdraw, leaving the dust to settle, and the builders already scrambling over the pale rubble—silent, urgent, and unharmed—lifting the fallen stones to build something else.
