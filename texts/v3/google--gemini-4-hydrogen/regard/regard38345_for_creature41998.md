At first, the power mistook the plateau for a wound.

From a great distance, it looked like a place where the world had broken open and failed to mend: a raw, jagged scab of young basalt thrust into a sky too thin to hold thunder. Matter was fraying at the seams. Gravity slipped its moorings; space stuttered and doubled back on itself; veins of raw metal hummed with unspent fury. And moving through the dim, oily mist were creatures with no eyes to see the stars, no mouths to form a song, and no hands to build a wall against the storm.

Benevolence, when it is young or hasty, reaches immediately for the needle and thread.

The power leaned closer, gathering the quiet strength it had used to soothe a hundred troubled worlds. It knew how to knit fractured physics back into smooth, predictable laws. It could round the knife-edged stone with a gentle rain, cool the churning vents into clear springs, and quiet the restless tectonic friction until the copper coils fell still. It could open eyes beneath those sloped mineral shields, soften the cloven hooves into fingers, and thin the heavy air until sound could travel—until the beings could finally hear one another call across the dark.

It paused, however, before it touched a single fern. Wisdom is the habit of listening before speaking, even when one is certain one has come to help.

So the power did not descend as light or voice. It simply flattened its awareness against the bruised pewter sky and watched, and then—more carefully—it learned to feel the way the plateau felt.

It felt the sub-audible hum of the copper coils along the cliff face, and watched a congregation of the low, basalt-backed beings gather there. They did not cower from the voltage. They pressed their broad chests against the screaming metal with the unhurried relief of thirsty animals at a trough, drinking the surge until their seams glowed a warm, contented slag-orange. When the cliff’s tension eased, they stepped away. They had saved the stone from shattering, and the stone had fed them.

The power watched a gravity pocket slacken, lifting a pool of mineral condensate into a trembling, waist-high sphere. A single creature stepped forward, pressed its porous shield into the suspended water, and drank—not fighting the broken law, simply meeting it mid-air before it fell.

It watched them sleep beneath the hovering lodestones, limbs tucked, floating a fraction of an inch above the soot-colored moss in a motionless, humming trance. There was no fear in them. No restlessness. Their iron-laced blood had found a pillow in the planet’s own erratic pull.

And then the power saw the spatial echo.

A patch of the fern-canopy shimmered, stuttering into a brief, overlapping twin of itself. One of the creatures was standing at the edge of the thicket. It did not flee, and it did not phase past the tremor like the heavy grazers. It stepped deliberately into the shimmer.

For a few breathless minutes, two identical, oil-slicked forms stood side by side in the steaming dark. They leaned in and touched their sunken amber strips together—sharing a quiet, steady glow that had no purpose in the circuit, no function for the foundry, nothing to do with draining a coil or scouring a vat. Just the brief, strange warmth of a duplicate self, held in the overlap before space collapsed back into one.

The power drew back, chastened and awed.

It realized then that if it smoothed the basalt, their copper-rimmed hooves would lose their purchase. If it mended the fraying physics, they would lose their sleep beneath the stones, and their water in the air, and the only mirror in which they ever got to hold themselves. If it gave them eyes, the silent violet lightning would blind them. If it gave them voices, the roaring thunder of a hundred miles of storm would shatter the delicate pores by which they read the world.

To "heal" this place would be to starve it. To "rescue" these beings would be to amputate them from the only body they knew—the vast, overheating, unbuilt engine of which they were the living valves.

Still, the power lingered. Love does not easily walk away without leaving a gift, and wisdom had to ask one last question: *Is there anything at all that is missing here?*

It looked at the long arc of geological time. It saw that the uplift was young, and that someday—eons from now—the tectonic plates would slow, the vents would cool, and the plateau would grow old. It saw, too, that occasionally a surge was too fierce, cracking a creature’s internal capacitors before its time, or a spatial collapse caught one mid-stride.

Should it soften those edges? Should it quietly reinforce their ribs against the fiercest arcs, or step in to catch them when space snapped shut?

It looked at a hollow fracture in the basalt where an old one had recently settled. Copper-veined ferns were already threading through its mineral ribs. Pale, segmented crawlers were stripping its hide for glassy slag. Soon, it would be indistinguishable from the ridge, still conducting the plateau’s current, still part of the circuit. Nothing was wasted. Even their endings were woven into the ground that held their living kin. To make them immortal would be to choke the rock with bodies that refused to return to it.

And to send them a greeting—even a single, gentle pulse of amber light to say *you are seen, you are well-made*—would be worse. In a world where light was a language of coils and vents and danger, a foreign signal would pull them across the sharp ridges in search of a thermal fissure that was not there.

So the power chose the hardest, quietest kind of benevolence.

It did nothing to the plateau. It left no footprint on the spongy mycelial mat, added no volt to the copper cliffs, and took away not a single degree of the crust’s fevered heat.

Instead, it turned outward. Far above the bruised pewter sky, beyond the reach of the silent lightning and the thin, restless air, the power wove a subtle, seamless indifference into the space surrounding the high plains. Not a wall—for there are no walls here—only a gentle bend in the paths of things that travel between worlds. A quiet assurance that no conqueror, no miner of native copper, and no well-meaning healer with a needle and thread would ever stumble upon this ridge and mistake its fury for a flaw.

Then the power withdrew, leaving the plateau entirely to itself—a dark, breathless engine, humming steadily in the warm quiet, tended by the ones who belonged to it.
