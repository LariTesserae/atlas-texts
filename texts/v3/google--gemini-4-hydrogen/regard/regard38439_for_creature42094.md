A power that is merely kind might have healed the wound at once.

Looking down from the wind-scoured plain into that sharp, unweathered rift, its first impulse would be obvious: to soften the razor-edged basalt with a century’s worth of rain, to spill dark soil over the lip until grass took hold, to send a clear river tumbling down the east wall to rinse the limestone dust from the narrow lanes. It would look at hands cracked by hungry lime and want to smooth the mortar; it would look at the cellar-cold slots at the bottom and want to widen them to the sun.

A *wise* power would pause at the rim before touching a single stone.

It would notice, first, that the basin is quiet—and that the silence is not the silence of despair, nor of fear, but of attention. So it would not announce itself from the open square of sky. It would not speak in thunder, nor send a pillar of light into a place where eyes are calibrated to blue-grey shadow and the sudden glare of pale rock.

Instead, it would wait through a full turning of the light, watching without disturbing the air.

It would watch the seep-watchers move at dawn with their bone scrapers and clay lamps, clearing liverwort from the chipped channels so that every bead of condensation reaches the cisterns. It would watch two people meet in a lane no wider than a pair of shoulders—the slight turn of the ribs, the tucked elbows, wool brushing wool without catching, neither hurrying, neither bruised. It would watch the crews down in the cold bottom driving wet wooden wedges into raw seams, pausing with mauls raised to listen for the tiny, high *tick* of basalt beginning to part. It would see the callused palms and the curiously tender fingertips reading hair-thin cracks in the dark, and at dusk it would smell the faint sharpness of thyme-steeped tallow rising from the damp alleys.

And watching, it would understand the terrible mistake of an easy mercy.

If it poured a river into the basin, it would drown the workshops and make the seep-watchers’ patient craft meaningless. If it filled the rift with soft earth, it would bury the clean geometry that holds the roofs and stairs together. If it blunted the stone or hushed the settling fault into dead stillness, it would unmake the very attentiveness that binds these people to their world and to one another. They are not waiting to be lifted out onto the plain, where the wind steals moisture from the mouth and the sky has no edges. They are doing what the crustose lichens and the swelling wooden wedges do: teaching a raw fracture how to hold life.

So the power would withhold almost everything it has the strength to give.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and benevolence does not walk away empty-handed when a thing is fragile. If it acts at all, it acts where no pride is wounded and no skill is made obsolete—deep below the reach of chisels, and along the quietest margins of survival.

Far beneath the basin floor, thirty meters down in the blind bedrock where no human ear can press, the fault is still adjusting to the missing weight of the earth. There, in the dark, the power might lay a steady, patient hand against the deepest shear planes. Not to freeze the stone—for the town needs live rock that can be split with oak and water—but to pace it. To take the sudden, treacherous violence out of the settling, so that when a slab shifts or a lintel takes on new load, it never collapses silently in the sleep-hours. It gives an honest warning first: a readable groan, a long, clear *tick* that a listening crew can hear and answer.

In the unseen bedding planes behind the cliffs, where groundwater gathers in the dark, it would do something just as small. It would not open a fountain. It would simply keep the slow weeping faithful—nudging the deep pressure just enough that even in the driest years on the plain above, the rock seams do not go wholly sterile, and the cisterns never scrape down to dry dust.

And perhaps, on the sunlit flat roofs where the herb boxes are watered drop by drop from clay jars, it might let the thyme hold a fraction more oil in its leaves, and the shelf-fungi in the damp under-stairs grow a little thicker on the spent wedge timbers—nothing miraculous, nothing anyone could point to and call a gift from elsewhere, only the margin between breaking and enduring kept wide enough to breathe in.

As for the rim above, where the wind is loud and the dead are carried at dusk to be burned on the barren stone—the power would not calm the wind, nor build them a graveyard out of dirt they do not have. It would only receive the smoke as it rises into the open sky, witnessing what has no ancient song, while down below, a single clay jar of clean grey ash is carried back into the cool geometry of the rift, slaked with lime, and pressed into a new wall.

In the morning, the basin would wake as it always does: cold in the bottom slots, bright on the upper tiers, quiet as a held breath. Nobody would look up to thank the sky. The seep-watchers would scrape the channels, the lizards would find the first warm slabs, and the stone-pryers would listen to the rock—never knowing that the rock, at last, was listening back.
