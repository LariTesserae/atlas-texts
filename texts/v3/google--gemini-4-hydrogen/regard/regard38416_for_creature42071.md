A lesser mercy would have ruined them in an instant.

When the power’s attention first sank through the ragged slots of sky, drawn by the sheer, stubborn persistence of the place, its first impulse was the ancient reflex of the strong looking upon the austere: *to mend, to soften, to give.*

It saw the bone-dry air and thought of rain. It saw the dim, unchanging twilight and thought of sunlight. It saw the chalk-dusted figures crouching in the dark, drinking bitter sap drop by drop through hollow bird-bones, and it thought of clear springs, of warm hearths, of voices rising freely into quiet air.

And then, because it was wise, it paused before it touched a single grain of dust.

It lowered its awareness not into the roaring air, which was full of nothing but wings and wings and wings, but into the limestone itself. It felt the basin the way the inhabitants felt it—as a vast, humming drumhead of dry-laid stone.

Through the bedrock, it felt the truth of the place.

It felt how the deafening roar of the bats and swiftlets and insects was not a torment, but a medium—a constant, living pressure that let the people read the health of the whole sunken bowl through the soles of their feet. To hush the roar would be to blind them.

It saw the decade-long sentences traced in the limestone grit of the plazas, sharp as the day a finger drew them. A single gentle rainstorm—just one cool, merciful shower—would wash away fifty years of patient conversation, turn the powdered guano into a reeking mire, rot the cured insect-cakes, and swell the dry-laid joints until the ancient towers buckled and fell.

It saw the papery curtains of desiccated creepers and the tinder-dry thickets of salt-cedar. A single hearth-fire, however small and comforting, would turn the basin into a chimney of ash before the bats could even scatter.

And when two of the chalk-skinned figures met on an undulating ramp and pressed their foreheads together, the power listened through the bone. It heard the dry, percussive little rhythms of their molar-clicks and the deep palate-hums passing cleanly from skull to skull, untouched by the chaos an inch away. There was no loneliness in it. There was no deprivation. They were not waiting to be rescued; they had simply fitted themselves to the stone, as patient and as finished as the figs.

So the power withheld its hands from the basin. It gave them no rain. It gave them no fire. It gave them no silence.

Instead, it looked to the places the people could not reach.

Far above, out on the empty plain beyond the rim of the world, it gently bent the long, slow rhythms of the outer weather. It shaped the wind-scoured ridges around the sunken slots of sky so that if a wildfire ever swept the upper grasslands, its sparks would lift and scatter high overhead, never drifting down into the dry bowl. It eased the contours of the plateau so that even in a century of storms, no sudden flash flood would ever find a channel over the lip and pour down onto the plazas.

And far below—deeper than the oldest fig’s taproot, deeper than the hairline fractures in the limestone—it laid a quiet steadiness against the unseen aquifers. Millennia of slow settling had begun to wear a fault in the deep slate that held the water beneath the city’s foundations; in another three hundred years, the basin’s hidden well would have quietly drained away into the dark, and the figs would have withered, and the people would have followed them into dust.

The power pressed the deep rock closed, seamlessly, without a tremor that would reach the surface. It asked for no thanks, and it left no sign of its passing—no carved pillar, no sudden spring, no voice in the air to startle the roosting doves.

Only, as its attention withdrew back through the pale gray sky, it lingered for one quiet moment on a stepped terrace where a figure was wedging a flat chip of limestone beneath a slumping lintel. For the space of a breath, the power let its presence rest in the bedrock beneath that person’s horned, dusty heels—not as a command, not as a miracle, just a faint, steady warmth in the stone, like the memory of a palm laid against a collarbone.

Then it let them be, and the roar of the basin closed seamlessly back over everything, unbroken and enduring.
