It did not arrive as light. Light, here, would have been a violence.

A lesser kindness—one that knew only how to give what it loved itself—might have cracked the rime-ice in the upper cleft and poured down a shaft of noon. It might have warmed the air until the hoarfrost wept into mud, woken the dormant spores with a rush of summer, and given the thing on the floor a tongue to marvel at the sky. And within a week, the waxy cuticles would have blistered, the antifreeze sap would have soured in the heat, the ancient peat would have rotted into poison, and the chamber would have drowned in its own sudden, frantic spring.

So the power that found the cistern did not bring the sun. It did not even bring a voice.

It came the way depth comes: a quiet settling of attention, thirty meters down through the iron-dark slate, passing through the sealed cleft without disturbing a single crystal of rime. At first, it simply held itself still, matching its own vast patience to the clock of the room.

It waited through the slow, bruised-violet dark. It waited while a drop of condensation gathered on the tip of a strap-leafed fern, hung for three days, and finally let go.

*Plip.*

It waited while the keeper—indistinguishable from a ridge of old peat beneath the jointless rib—shifted a fraction of an ounce of syrupy fluid from its trailing edge to its leading folds, leaning imperceptibly toward the sound. It watched the creature’s mineralized plates shear a stray creeper back from the three-millimeter halo of a plinth, and watched it press the severed tendril down into the dense, cold felt of the floor.

The power looked at the plinths, at the low basin, at the seamless rib pitted by mineral drips. It saw who had made them, long ago, and why, and how thoroughly those makers were gone. It saw how the hardware had given up its grander designs and settled into the stone, content to step down a deep telluric tremor into a frequency a fern could drink.

And it looked at the keeper—this slow, eyeless hinge between machine and moss—and understood that the creature was not lonely. It was not waiting to be rescued. It had no word for *dark*, and no word for *cold*, only the steady grammar of pressure, moisture, and the hum behind the ears. To wake it into selfhood would be to invent hunger in a thing that had never needed to eat; to give it ambition would be to build a cage around a room that had never felt small.

For a long time, the power considered doing nothing at all. Restraint, it knew, was often the sharpest edge of mercy.

Yet, because it was wise as well as gentle, it looked not only at the room, but at the mountain around it, and at the centuries that had not yet arrived.

Thirty meters above the cistern, in the upper strata where the frost bit deeper and the surface world pressed down, the iron-dark slate was carrying a slow, blind weight. Over the last four hundred winters, meltwater from the world above had crept into a horizontal seam, frozen, wedged the stone apart by the width of a fingernail, and frozen again. In another two centuries—a single breath on the chamber’s clock—the shelf would shear. Not all at once, perhaps, but enough to buckle the low ceiling of the hollow, snap the jointless rib, and bury the plinths under tons of shattered rock.

And deeper still, beneath the bedrock, the telluric gradient that fed the artifacts was drifting—not dying, only wandering slowly eastward with the long, continental sag of the crust, so that in a thousand years the sub-vocal hum would thin to a whisper, and the three-millimeter halos would begin to freeze.

The power did not remake the mountain. It did not anchor the continents.

Instead, with a touch no heavier than the settling of dust, it leaned its attention into the fractured slate overhead. Along the stressed seam, it coaxed the mineral grain of the rock to knit—not sealing the upper cleft, not altering the cold, only shifting the angle of the load by half a degree, so the shelf would arch its weight *around* the cistern like the ribs of a vault. The stone settled with a sound too low for even the keeper’s plates to feel, and held.

Below the floor, where the deep field rose through the stone, the power gently cupped the straying current—not amplifying it, lest the moss grow wild and choke the room to the ceiling, only steadying the pulse so it would remain true to the low basin long after the surface above had forgotten its own name.

Then it looked once more at the keeper.

The creature had reached the basin sunk flush into the bedrock. It was flattening itself across the dark, non-reflective plate, spreading its braided tendrils until it filled the hollow like a pool of thick oil. The static stillness above the plate thickened; the fine hairs on the nearby ferns lifted slightly toward it.

The power did not touch it. It did not bless it with a name, or a dream, or a memory of the sky. It only lingered beside the basin for a single, unhurried season—an immense, benevolent stillness resting alongside a small, patient one, asking nothing and changing nothing, simply keeping watch while the sap aligned.

When it withdrew, it left no warmth behind, and no light.

Only the dry *tick* of ice adjusting in the upper cleft. The faint, sub-vocal hum in the stone. And, three days later, the sharp, clean *plip* of a drop falling into the dark, caught before it could freeze.
