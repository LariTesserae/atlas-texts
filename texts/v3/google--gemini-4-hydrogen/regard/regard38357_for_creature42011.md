At first, to a gaze that knows calmer worlds, the valley looks like an error—a wound in the crust of things that never healed.

The power arrives without parting a single cloud. It does not descend as light, because light here would be an intrusion, blinding eyes built for slate-gray scatter and the gloom beneath dwarf alder. Instead, its attention settles into the basin the way fog settles, threading through the sideways waterfalls, pressing against the thrashing sedges, listening past the wall of sound.

It sees the cold. It feels the stinging, indecisive sleet, the mud that pulls at the boot, the violent shear of wind off the unseen ridges. It sees the dwellers—short-necked, scarred, hunched under greased hides, tethered together like climbers on a sheer face just to cross a flooded gravel bar. 

A lesser kindness—an impatient, sentimental mercy—would have reached down at once.

It would have been so easy. A single breath of will to thin the charcoal ceiling and let the sun dry the peat. A gentle hand to hush the katabatic squalls, to smooth the braided rivers into clear, slow streams, to turn off the screaming needles in the marsh so the people could unplug their ears and hear each other’s voices in the open air.

The power pauses, holding that effortless miracle in its palm, and looks closer.

It looks into the slate-roofed warrens wedged against the matte-gray housings. It watches a woman’s blunt, scarred fingers working tallow into the seams of a child’s boiled-bark shin-guards, her shoulders resting against the warm composite wall. It watches the child sleep afterward, bare-skinned and safe in the dry, ozone-scented stillness, waking only when the housing’s hum shifts pitch—reading the storm outside the way another child might read a window.

It watches two dwellers out on the flooded flats. It sees the sharp, flat-palmed tap on a collarbone, the quick squeeze on a forearm, the fist pressed firmly into the small of a partner’s back. Their language is not impoverished by the roar; it is *forged* by it—a speech of weight and warmth and immediate presence, impossible to lie with, impossible to shout from a safe distance. You cannot speak to someone here without touching them.

It watches them drop flat into the mud as the gravel bar shimmers, mouths open, palms over their ears. When the *whump* tears the air apart and flattens the reeds, they are up before the water settles, scooping the stunned, pale crustaceans with practiced, unhurried speed. 

And the power understands.

If it cleared the sky, the moss would dry and die, and the peat banks would rot into dust. If it silenced the pylons, the dwellers would lose a harvest they have timed their pulses to for generations. If it warmed the air and hushed the wind, the housings would no longer be sanctuaries; the careful, intricate web of grease and sinew, of storm-bird barometers and ash-pole prods, would unravel into uselessness. Worse than that: it would make strangers of them in their own skin. Their dense, weather-pared bodies, their quiet, tactile speech, their hard-won mastery of a brutal medium—all of it would become an artifact of a world that had been taken away from them without their consent.

To fix the valley would be to erase the people who knew how to live in it.

Yet wisdom is not mere inaction. Having decided what it will *not* touch, the power looks deeper—not at the weather, and not at the dwellers, but at the old engines grinding beneath them.

It slips its attention past the seamless, matte-gray composite of the housings, down into the sub-surface taps and the buried roots of the three slender pylons, and out along the sheer cliff to the cantilevered platform humming over the gorge.

Here, in the dark physics where no gears turn and no wires run, the power finds what the dwellers cannot see.

The infrastructure is very old. Whoever built it is long gone, and the machines have been running at full, erratic load for longer than the valley has had a name. They are not meant to fail, yet time wears even on things engineered without seams. Beneath the gravel bar, deep in the basalt, the field-anchor of the northernmost pylon has developed a hairline drift—a slow, creeping fatigue that, in another forty or fifty winters, would cause the needle to shear at its base during a peak squall, dumping its trapped charge straight into the marsh. Inside the largest housing on the western scree, a regulator is quietly thinning; in three generations, its wall would go cold in the middle of a freeze, leaving the warren behind it to ice over. And the cantilevered platform, whose sub-audible hum keeps the blind bat-things safe in its dry shadow, is slowly loosening its grip on the living rock.

The valley is harsh, and the dwellers are built for harshness. They are not built for the sudden betrayal of the ground they lean against.

So the power acts—so quietly that not a single slug stops grazing on the orange fungus, not a single storm-bird misses a wingbeat.

Deep inside the housings, beneath the level of heat and ozone, it knits the thinning composite back to its original density. It does not make the walls warmer; it only ensures they will not go cold. At the gravel bar, it settles the drifting field-anchor back into true alignment with the basalt, bleeding away the structural stress while leaving the erratic *whump*, the shimmer, and the static shriek completely untouched. Along the sheer cliff, it seals the molecular bond between the cantilevered platform and the stone, securing it for another ten thousand years of wind, while taking great care not to alter the sub-audible vibration that shakes the shale and cradles the roosting bats.

It checks the balance of the valley once more.

The sky remains bruised charcoal. The sleet still stings. The mire-beasts still wait under the rotten banks, and the dwellers still carry their iron-shod ash poles to meet them.

For a brief moment, the power lingers beside the warren on the western scree, where the woman has finished greasing the shin-guards and is banking the fern-fronds for sleep. It resists the urge to leave a sign, a blessing, or a gift—knowing that anything dropped into this valley from outside would only be one more mystery to navigate, or worse, something to kneel to. They do not kneel to the housings. They lean their backs against them and get on with their work.

Satisfied, the power withdraws its attention, leaving the valley exactly as it found it: loud, wet, indifferent, and whole.

Outside, a squall tears down from the ridge. The storm-birds drop into the lee of the crags with their sharp, metallic shrieks. Two tethered figures step out from the reeds, leaning forward into the sleet, and the housing behind them hums steadily on.
