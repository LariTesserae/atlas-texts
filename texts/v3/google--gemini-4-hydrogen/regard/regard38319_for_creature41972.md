If a benevolent and wise power were to turn its attention toward this pocket in the mountain, it would not arrive as light.

Light here would be an injury. It would strike the closed eyelids like hot needles, bleach the pale mats clinging to the sulfur seams, and turn the wet, intimate geometry of the room into a harsh clutter of shadows. Even a gentle radiance would announce that the dark was a flaw to be corrected. A power that is truly wise knows better than to begin by calling a creature’s home a mistake.

So it would come without glow or sound, perceiving the chamber as the chamber perceives itself: through weight, through warmth, through the slow, patient chemistry of lime and iron.

For a long moment, it would simply listen. It would hear the faint geological hum of the young mountain, the rare *plock* of water finding water, and beneath them both, the slow, deep bellows of the inhabitant’s chest—drawing life from air too thin for almost anything else, holding it, letting it go. It would trace the callused palms, the chalk-lined collarbone, the way the body folds itself into the basalt hollow as neatly as a seed fits its husk.

A lesser kindness—one eager to be felt, eager to be thanked—might be tempted to act at once. It might crack the mountain’s ribs to let the morning in. It might cool the blood-warm mist with a clean, pine-scented wind, wash the lime from the pale ropes of hair, heal the ruined or unused eyes, and lead this tallow-skinned hermit out onto grass and open sky.

The wise power would see what such a rescue would actually be: an unmaking.

Out in the wind, air this dry would parch lungs built for dampness. Under the sun, skin softened by decades of mineral mist would blister and crack. Eyes that have never needed the world to have edges at a distance would drown in the violence of horizon. Worse still, to carry them out of the stone would be to strip them of their only language—to take away the notches their thumb knows by heart, the basin they keep clear, the exact, familiar reach between one basalt rib and the next. They are not lost here. They have arrived.

And so the first thing the power would do is the hardest thing for love to learn: it would hold back its hand.

It would not widen the chamber. It would not smooth the jagged fins of rock, nor clear away the mineral crust that threatens to fill the notches on the central rib—for scraping those grooves clean with a sliver of bone is how the inhabitant remembers they are alive and capable of shaping their world. It would not touch the calcified grass cords, knowing that even a memory stiffened into stone belongs to the one who kept it. It would not speak, because a voice in a room this small would leave nowhere for silence to sit.

Instead, if it acted at all, it would work only along the grain of what already is.

First, it would look to the ceiling, and follow the hair-thin roots upward—tracing them back through meters of fractured, unweathered basalt to the unseen surface of the peak, where wind scours the scree and frost splits the stone. There, in the bright, harsh world the inhabitant will never see, a stubborn alpine shrub clings to a crevice, its life small and precarious. The power might nudge a loose shale slab aside so a coming snowmelt can reach its roots, or shelter its crown from a gale that would otherwise tear it free. Not to force it to bloom, and not to send down any grand message—only to keep the thread unbroken, so that the faint, sharp bitterness at the root-tip in the dark does not go dry.

Then, deep inside the mountain’s core, where the young rock still shifts and settles under its own immense weight, the power might lay a quiet steadiness against the outer walls of this single blister of stone. Not freezing time—nothing living should be frozen—only easing the blind pressure that would otherwise crush the pocket in a century or a night, letting the humid equilibrium hold a little longer.

Perhaps, too, it would ensure that the slow, blind creatures beneath the travertine shelves do not dwindle past the point of return, and that the warm sulfur seams continue to weep just enough to feed the pale mats—never an abundance that would rot in the stillness, only enough.

And then the power would withdraw its attention, leaving no footprint, no altar, no sign that anything greater than the mountain had ever passed through.

Inside the chamber, nothing appears to have changed. The darkness remains thick as wet wool. The air stays thin and blood-warm.

After a long stillness, the inhabitant unfolds from the hollow in the basalt. With liquid patience, they thread their way between the stone teeth, pausing to brush a broad thumb across the horizontal notches, checking the stone lip of the catching-basin with their fingertips. They reach up into the warm damp until their fingers find a hanging root, no thicker than a hair, and touch its wet tip briefly to their tongue.

Today, the bitterness is clean and sharp—a rumor of snowmelt, of high sun, of a season going well somewhere far above the stone.

They let go of the root, fold back into the curve of the rock, and draw a slow, deep breath into their wide ribs, listening to the mountain breathe back.
