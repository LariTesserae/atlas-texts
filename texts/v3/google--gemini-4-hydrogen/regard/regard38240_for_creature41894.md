It does not arrive as a wind, for wind would bruise the ferns and scatter the weighted hems of the weavers. It does not arrive as a voice, for there are no ears here tuned to the violence of thunder.

It arrives simply as attention—the way late-morning light, already present, might suddenly *notice* what it is illuminating.

For a long time, the power does nothing at all. Wisdom, when it is genuine, has no haste in it; it knows that to touch a thing before understanding its grain is only another kind of carelessness. So it rests along the razor-sharp ridges of slate and chert, and looks down into the league-wide amphitheater.

It reads the basin the way a craftsman reads a finished joint. It sees the exact, miraculous arithmetic of the place: every drop of condensation that gathers on a stone spout and falls into a cistern is answered, molecule for molecule, by the invisible rising of vapor into the cool air. Nothing is lost, and nothing is hoarded. It sees the pale salamanders at the water’s edge, the silent magpies gliding between the cantilevered balconies, the velvet-shelled beetles creeping along the bedrock channels. It sees the tall, slate-skinned caretakers—cool-fleshed, unblinking, barefoot on the slick stone—braiding endless lengths of damp fiber and clearing the waterways with splinters of scraped bone.

At first, a lesser power might have felt pity.

A lesser power might have looked at the unchanging shadows, the unrusted bronze pins, the faces smooth as river-worn slate, and called it a curse. It might have summoned a warm southern wind to stir the heavy curtains of silver-edged vine; it might have nudged the sun toward noon, and then toward dusk, and then toward winter, believing it was giving these people a gift called *time*.

But this power is wise enough to look past its own habits of living. It looks closely at the caretakers, and finds no grief in them. There is no hunger in their narrow bellies, no terror in their wide grey eyes, no frantic clutching at one another in the dark—for there is no dark. To pour time into this basin by force would not be a rescue; it would be a flood. The rough-hewn timber bridges would rot and collapse into the moss. The sharp peaks would crumble into scree. The cool-fleshed builders would learn to shiver, to blister, to starve, and to bury one another beneath the slate slabs they had stacked so patiently.

To break a stillness merely because one does not understand how to live inside it is not benevolence. It is vanity.

So the power withholds its hand from the sun. It leaves the air motionless.

Yet it does not turn away, either. Because benevolence is attentive to small details, and there are two things in the basin that make it linger.

The first is the vulnerability of the place. A pocket of the world held outside of weather and decay is a delicate thing; the wider universe, with its storms and its grinding centuries, presses forever against the outer rims of those unweathered peaks. Quietly, invisibly, the power lays a hand along the mountain crests—not a wall to trap the basin, but a shelter to keep the outside world’s carelessness from spilling in. Whatever this quiet equilibrium is, it will not be shattered by accident.

The second thing is subtler. It is the chisels.

Every caretaker still carries a stone hammer and a copper chisel tucked into their fiber belt, as if the work of building were only paused. And in the dim, unglazed rooms of the narrow towers, some of them sit for days on end with their hands resting palm-up on their knees, watching the unchanging shadow of a timber beam on the floor.

The power looks at those palm-up hands, and wonders—not with certainty, but with humility—whether the pause is entirely chosen, or whether it is simply a question that no one has ever known how to finish asking.

If you wish to speak to a people who have no use for sound, you must learn their grammar. You must speak in the placement of a fern frond, the direction of a tool, the shifting of a bowl from shade into light.

So the power enters one of the dim, unglazed rooms. It makes no sound. It does not stir the dustless air. It simply reaches into the single shaft of late-morning light falling through the doorway, and—without changing the sun in the sky, without disturbing a single moss-cushion in the valley outside—it shifts the shadow of the overhead timber beam by the width of a reed.

Just that much. A fraction of an inch across the slate floor.

Then, like the caretakers themselves, the power waits.

For a long while, nothing happens. The figure sitting cross-legged in the dimness does not flinch. Their pale grey eyes, clear and flat as the cisterns, remain fixed on the floor. Their cool skin does not warm.

Slowly—at the pace of a fern uncurling, at the pace of condensation gathering on a stone lip—the caretaker’s gaze tracks the tiny, impossible movement of the shadow. They look up at the timber beam above, still rough-hewn and pegged with dull bronze. They look back down at the floor, where the edge of light now touches the tip of one bare, pale toe.

They do not gasp. They do not weep.

Instead, after another long stillness, the caretaker reaches to their fiber belt. They unhook the copper chisel—its edge still bright, its shaft faintly rubbed with green moss—and set it gently on the slate floor beside the moved shadow.

Not pointing east, as they do when speaking to one another of the day’s small tendings.

Pointing into the new sliver of light.

The power sees the chisel, and understands. Not everyone in the basin may wish for noon; some may love the endless morning as the salamanders love the damp. Nothing will be forced upon the valley as a whole.

But here, in this one quiet room, a conversation has begun—patient, reversible, and entirely in their own tongue. The power gently widens the sliver of light by another reed’s breadth, and leaves the next move to the hand that holds the chisel.
