At first, the power noticed only the flatness—a high, pale scar on the skin of the world, worn past the memory of peaks, holding nothing that looked like promise.

From the altitude where such attentions usually travel, the plateau appeared finished. No rivers cut it. No forests darkened it. A thin smear of mist gathered in the night and burned away by noon, leaving chalk and salt. It looked like a place that had forgotten how to be young, waiting only for the last of its heat to leak into the sky.

A lesser kindness would have pitied it at once.

Instead, the power did the first wise thing: it slowed down.

It let go of the quickness that belongs to storms and stars, and matched its attention to the clock of the limestone. It waited through a morning, then a season, then the long, unhurried turn of a year. And as its sight adjusted to the stillness, the barrenness fell away, and the plateau revealed itself—not as an empty table, but as a crowded, breathing floor.

It heard the dry *tick* of carapaces warming at midday. It heard the wet, minuscule *sigh* of a million spiracles sealing against the sun. It watched the papery curtains unhook at dawn to comb a thimbleful of water from the air, and saw the blind, calcified stalks lean by degrees toward a dampness ten feet away. It watched the builders—heavy-shouldered, fissured as old clay—nudging a single cobble across three months of dust to close a ring.

For a long time, the power simply looked, weighing what it held in its hands against what lay below.

It could have given rain. A single week of gentle, persistent rain would have washed the salt from the crevices, softened the chalk into mud, and called up green shoots from the deep, dormant seed-banks beneath the stone. And in that same week, the flat-backed pavements would have drowned in their hollows; the mist-nets would have rotted on their hooks; the salt-lichen would have dissolved, starving every slow mouth on the flats.

It could have given speed—lightened the builders’ limbs, sharpened their milky eyes, quickened their blood so a wall might rise in an afternoon instead of a decade. Yet it saw at once that speed on a flat plain has only one use: to chase, or to flee. With haste would come hunger; with hunger, heat; with heat, the waste of breath.

It could have given them words, or longer lives, or softer stone. Every gift it imagined was a knife disguised as mercy. Each one would have broken the quiet bargain the plateau had struck with scarcity—a bargain in which nothing was wasted, nothing was hoarded, and every creature was both pasture and grazer to the neighbor at its edge.

The power watched two builders meet at an unfinished ring. Neither had built the first course; neither claimed the last. They stood forehead-to-forehead through the heat of an entire afternoon, their cracked hides creaking softly as they leaned their weight together, sharing the dampness trapped in the creases of their shoulders. Then one reached out a blunt claw and wedged a loose pebble tighter into the wall.

There was nothing here to rescue. The plateau was not poor; it was exact.

So the power did not touch the stone. It did not add a single drop to the morning mist, nor lift a single cobble for a tiring brow. To interfere with the slowness of the builders would have been to erase the very thing they were—a patience older than language, writing its only sentence in limestone: *Here, the water stays.*

Instead, the power looked outward, past the level horizon, down to the lower, noisier worlds below the plateau’s rim—where fierce weather brewed, where restless things multiplied and climbed, where careless forces wandered without looking where they stepped.

There, far below the sight of any milky eye, it laid a quiet, invisible hand. It did not build a wall, for walls belong to the builders. It simply gentled the updrafts along the outer escarpments, turning the violent, scouring storms slightly aside so they broke against the lower cliffs and spent their fury in the valleys, leaving only the thin, warm breath of the night-mist to rise and pool in the pans above.

Then it returned its gaze to the flats one last time.

In the center of an old ring, a builder whose brow was worn smooth as river-glass had tucked its heavy limbs beneath its belly and pulled its mandibles shut. Its breathing had already slowed to match the pavement around it. The blind knots were beginning to unspool from the crevices; the first flat-backed neighbors were inching up the slope of its flanks.

The power lingered beside it—not to heal it, and not to hurry its end, but simply to witness, with the full weight of its attention, a life that had asked for nothing more than a little shade and a place to leave its stones.

When it withdrew, the plateau did not know it had been visited. Nothing stirred. A stalked grazer leaned another fraction of an inch toward a damp seam. Somewhere out on the pale flats, a heavy forehead pressed against a cobble, and the stone moved the width of a seed.
