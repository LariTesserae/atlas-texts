If a power both benevolent and wise came upon this hollow, it would first know how to arrive.

It would not come as light, because light here is an intrusion—a glare that bleaches the subtle gradations of dusk and heats the air too quickly, pulling the stone out of its slow rhythm. It would not come as wind, because a single breath of wind would lift the flour-fine silt that took centuries to settle, scatter the bleached twigs, and chill the resin in the back wall toward glass.

It would arrive simply as attention—weightless, unhurried, leaving the scree untouched and the velvety silence unbroken.

For a long time, it would do nothing at all. It would merely read the room as the creature does: by stillness, by patience, by the weight of warmth held in iron-rich stone. A lesser kindness, looking at this sunken pocket of dry clay and leafless scrub, might feel pity. It might be tempted to coax a spring from the hairline fissures, to widen the narrow seam of sky, to soften the stiff silver-grey grass with a cool rain and bring beetles to turn the silt.

Wisdom would stay its hand. It would see that rain here would be a quiet catastrophe. Water would wake rot in the bone-white wood, turn the baked floor to mire, and swell the fibrous pads along the alcove wall until the creature drowned in what it had spent millennia learning to do without. To widen the sky would be to scald the lichen and blind a silence that had never asked for sight. To hurry anything in this chamber would be to break it.

Instead, the power would look deeper into the shadow along the back wall, where most eyes slide past. It would not mistake the shape in the recess for a knot of root or a scab of lichen. It would feel the difference in how the darkness holds its heat, and recognize the slow, pitch-thick pulse moving behind the matte plates. It would understand that this is not a prisoner waiting for rescue, nor a ruin waiting for repair. It is a solved equation—a life that has pared itself down to the exact shape of its home, asking nothing from the world except that the stone remain warm and the air remain still.

And so, in the hollow itself, the power would do almost nothing.

Yet benevolence is rarely wholly idle, even when it chooses restraint. Perhaps, high above the overhang where no eye in the hollow could ever see, frost and rainwater have been slowly wedging a hairline fault in the sandstone shelf—a fracture that, in another hundred winters, would bring the ceiling down and bury the kiln-warm chamber beneath cold rubble. With a touch no heavier than the settling of dust, the power might ease the strain in the rock, knitting the iron-rich strata back together so the roof will hold its arch for another age. Deep beneath the floor, where the faint mineral vapor rises through the fissures, it might gently turn aside a wandering vein of groundwater before it could seep upward and flood the roots.

Nothing inside the hollow would mark these acts. No pebble would roll; no grain of sand would fall out of turn.

Only at the very end, before withdrawing its attention, might the power offer a single word—spoken not in air, which carries noise, but in the only language the creature knows. It would let a faint, steady warmth settle into the back wall of the alcove, indistinguishable from the rock’s own memory of noon, lingering just long enough to be felt across the plated ribs as an unhurried, sheltering presence.

Then it would step back, leaving the silt unbroken, the dust undisturbed on the flat cobbles, and the heavy dusk intact.

Weeks later, as the distant seasons turn outside, the being in the recess shifts its weight by the breadth of a fingernail, following that quiet warmth deeper into the stone. Two dry plates brush past one another in the dark—a single, tiny *tick*—and the hollow remains just as it has always been.
