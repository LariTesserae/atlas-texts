First, the power would have to learn how to stop.

A being accustomed to the scale of stars or the rushing of surface winds cannot enter a place like this at its usual speed. Even the wake of a thought moving too briskly would send a surge through the stagnant bath, snapping the braided kelp-cables and dashing the inhabitants against their own razor-edged walls. So the first act of wisdom would be a long, deliberate stillness—a slowing of its own pulse until it matched the millimeter-by-millimeter creep of the moss and the patient *bloop* of rising gas.

Only then would it look.

It would drift, invisible and weightless, through the bruised-amber twilight, taking in the honeycombed slate of the lower terraces, the tethered moons of the gas-bladders, the slow-moving grazers rasping mineral film from the fronds. It would watch a knapper crouched on a crystalline shelf, holding raw obsidian between calloused feet, and see the dark flakes drift downward like slow leaves. It would hover beside an inverted bladder-dome, watching a weaver’s chest-frills breathe the warm sea while their long fingers worked dry in the trapped vapor above the meniscus.

And as it watched, the power would test its own benevolence against its wisdom, weighing every impulse to help.

*I could part the spore-clouds above,* the power might think, *and burn away the root-mats, so that clean, bright sunlight reaches them for the first time.*
Then it would look into their broad, quiet faces—at the large, pale-amber eyes built for dusk, and the heavy third eyelids that slide shut even against the gentle shimmer of cooling stone—and it would know that sunlight would be a blindness, not a gift.

*I could round the edges of the basalt,* it might consider, *and dull the obsidian lobes, so they never have to fear a careless kick or a slipped hand.*
Then it would look at the thick, horn-like pads on their fingers, the dense armor of their palms and soles, and the uncanny, oil-smooth grace with which they rise and sink between the blades. To take away the sharpness of the valley would be to unmake the very patience that shaped them. Their stillness is the answer to the stone’s edge; soften the stone, and you untether the dancer.

*I could give them smelted iron, or teach them the wheel, or wash the heavy, pearlescent silica from the elders’ shoulders so they might move like the young again.*
Then it would look at an elder tucked deep into a shoulder-width alcove of dry-stacked slate, crusted pale with decades of the valley’s warm breath, listening with closed eyes to the distant *clack* of a hammer and the *tick* of fracturing glass. The elder does not look imprisoned by the mineral; the elder looks half-claimed by a home they love, growing quietly into the architecture of their kin. To scrape them clean would be an erasure.

So the power would do none of those things. It would leave the twilight dim, the obsidian sharp, the elders heavy with their years of stone, and the tools made only of bone, root-wood, sinew, and cold-hammered copper.

Instead, the power would sink lower.

It would pass beneath the hanging gardens, beneath the honeycombed walls, down through the fresh cracks where the fibrous holdfasts grip the basalt, and slip into the dark, hot heart of the tableland itself. There, miles above the lightless abyssal plains, it would listen to the geology with the same care it had given the builders.

A newborn volcanic world is a blind, volatile thing. It knows nothing of the fragile reed-pods swaying above it, or the slow grazers drifting through its warm breath. Deep beneath the central valley, trapped under a lid of newly cooled glass, the power would find what the inhabitants could never see or name: a swollen chamber of superheated brine and volatile gas, straining upward against a fault. Left to the blind laws of stone and pressure, it would not ooze out in sluggish, heat-shimmering beads. In a season, or a decade, it would rupture. It would tear the stepped valley open, boil the stagnant bath, and choke the hanging city in ash and acid.

Here, at last, the power would act—and it would act with the exact economy of the valley itself.

It would not quench the deep fire; to freeze the plateau would be to kill the warmth that sustains the gardens, to empty the bladder-domes of their curing vapor, and to halt the slow, steady rising of the world. Instead, like a patient hand easing a knot from a tired muscle, the power would coax the stone. It would unthread the pressure. Miles away from the terraces, along the sheer, uninhabited flanks where the tableland drops toward the abyss, it would guide the magma to open a dozen hairline seams, letting the excess fury bleed harmlessly into the vast, cold dark of the deep sea.

Beneath the settlement, the rock would remain warm. The seams in the valley floor would still ooze their small, sluggish beads of stone. The glass would still fracture with its occasional, muted *tick*. The plateau would still rise, millimeter by millimeter, on its ancient, unhurried clock.

When the work was done, the power would rise back into the warm water column one last time.

Nothing above would have stirred. A taut kelp-cable would tremble once with the plucked greeting of a neighbor. A pale grazer would drift past a slate retaining wall, scraping its stony tongue against the moss. In a hanging pod, a family would sit in the dimness, hums vibrating softly through their chests, unaware that the mountain beneath them had just been taught how to be gentle.

The power would resist even the vanity of a farewell—no sudden bloom of light, no resonant hum in the water to make them wonder if a stranger had passed through the dark. It would simply withdraw as quietly as it came, leaving not even a ripple behind, content to let the valley belong entirely to itself.
