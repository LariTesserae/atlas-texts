At first, a lesser kindness might have mistaken the hollow for a prison.

Looking down through the choked cleft, past the wedged boulders and the blind, tangled roots, a power accustomed to sunlight and open sky would see only what was missing: no horizon, no wind, no green thing growing, no companion to answer a call. It would see the sealed hollows where eyes ought to be, the brittle lime crusting a living spine, the creature’s long stillness in the grey-green gloom—and it would feel the sharp, urgent ache to *fix*.

To widen the crack and let the noon sun pour in. To touch the smooth oval of the head and part the skin over working eyes. To lift the flat, pewter-colored body out of the warm seep and set it somewhere wide and dry and full of songs.

But wisdom does not hurry, and true benevolence looks twice before it touches.

So the power did not enter—not as light, and not as wind, for even a gentle breath would have rippled the rimstone pools and startled the thing in the basin into hiding behind the columns. Instead, it simply slowed its attention down to the speed of the cave.

It watched for the length of a single drop gathering at the tip of a stalactite. Then it watched for a season of rain on the unseen hillside above. Then for ten years, and then for fifty.

At that pace, the hollow stopped looking like a grave and began to look like a lung.

The power saw how the creature uncurled without breaking the silence, how its ribs rose once every few minutes to taste the iron and wet lime. It heard the tiny, dry *tick* of the mineral plate fracturing off a knuckle, and watched the pale amphipods scull upward through the warm water to graze on the fallen flakes. It saw the blind salamander climb straight over a submerged shoulder, and saw the creature remain as still as calcite so the smaller life would not be disturbed.

It watched the clean tracks rasped into the rust-orange biofilm slowly fill back in over weeks—an exact, unbroken arithmetic of hunger and regrowth, with nothing left over to rot and nothing taken before it was ready.

It saw, too, the hand resting on the rim of the unbroken red clay bowl during a cold rain. It understood that the creature did not mourn the people who had left the bowls behind, nor did it long for them. It only knew that this particular stone was rough, and that the water inside it jumped gently when struck from above.

And the power looked at the waiting—that faint, ancient habit still coiled at the center of the creature’s chest, long after its object had been forgotten—and saw that it was no longer a wound. Time and lime had smoothed its edges, just as they had smoothed the chisel-marks in the passage between the columns. The waiting had become indistinguishable from peace.

To widen the cleft would be to burn the biofilms with algae and dry the skin that needed the film of water to hold to the rock. To open the eyes would only show the creature how small its world was. To bring another of its kind would break the math of the pools, turning patience into scarcity. To lift it out into the open air would be an execution.

So inside the hollow, the power did nothing.

It left the air still and warm. It left the pewter skin mottled with sulfur and rust. It left the bowls on their dryish ledge, one cracked, two brim-full, and it did not sweep away the old soot on the ceiling or pry the notched rib-bone from the floor. It did not even speak a blessing into the dark, knowing that to a creature built for silence, any voice—even the gentlest—would sound like the scraping of boots and the glare of tallow lamps.

Only far above, on the noisy, hurried hillside that the creature had never seen, did the power lay a single, weightless finger on the shape of things.

It encouraged the roots of the beech trees to knit a fraction tighter around the boulders choking the cleft, holding the earth steady against collapse. It kept the deep fractures in the bedrock open and clear, so the warm seep would not fail in a century of drought, nor flood in a century of storms. And when travelers or quarrymen or wanderers passed along the ridge above, the slope beneath their feet always seemed just steep enough, the brush just thick enough, to turn their steps gently toward another valley.

Then the power withdrew its gaze, leaving no warmth behind that did not belong to the rock, and no shadow that did not belong to the stone.

Down in the grey-green twilight, the next drop gathered at the tip of the stalactite, hung heavy for a long moment in the still air, and fell—*plip*—into the mirror of the basin, where something old and patient rested, unbroken, at the center of its world.
