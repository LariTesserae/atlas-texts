When the power came upon the seam in the basalt, it did what wisdom always does before it acts: it stopped, and it listened for forty seconds.

From ten paces out on the dry plain, there had been nothing to notice—only the glare off young stone and the distant, chalky rim of the salt pan. It was the warmth that drew it in, a faint, coppery exhalation rising into the cooling air of late afternoon. It leaned over the crooked lip of the cleft, careful not to cast too wide a shadow across the unroofed slot, and looked down.

At first, its benevolence—which is an impatient thing when left to itself—wanted to mend everything it saw.

It saw a world packed to the point of suffocation. It saw the sharp, unweathered obsidian-slate threatening to slice soft bellies; the rusted junction box weeping water; the milky chitons jostling for a hair’s breadth of copper busbar; the blind oil-skimmers scurrying anxiously along their amber threads to keep a wet terminal from shorting out. It saw the bladder-slugs in the bottom trough, waiting to be pared by the shears of the crabs, and it saw the flat, rubber-skinned amphibian wedged like a folded belt into the pinched triangle behind the transformer, its lidless eyes milky in the gloom, its ribs compressed between hot iron and cold stone.

An ordinary kindness would have reached down and widened the crack. It would have roofed the pocket against the night freeze, wiped the verdigris from the brass, replaced the rusted relay, and given the amphibian a proper den with room to turn around.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

It waited for the *click*.

At the fortieth second, the thermal relay inside the rusted box gave its small, polite sound, and the entire wall of silica-plated chitons exhaled in unison—a single, collective *shhhk* as the mosaic slumped by the width of a hair and a younger chiton at the margin wedged itself closer to the current. A bead of warm condensation swelled on a basalt fin, dropped, and struck a glass sight-gauge with a bright *tink*. Inside the tube, silver threads of gauge-worms rode the rising bubble. Down in the grease-dark alleys, two oil-skimmers hurried to mend a snapped strand of silk.

The power watched, and understood.

If it cleaned the oil from the manifold, the skimmers would starve; if the skimmers starved, the amber silk would rot away; without the silk, the wet terminals would short, the transformer would die, the chitons would lose their grip on the copper, and the amphibian would freeze in the dark. If it widened the pinched triangle behind the casing, the creature would lose the exact, comforting pressure of warm iron against its belly and damp basalt against its spine—the geometry that its soft, cartilaginous ribs had grown to fit.

There was nothing broken here. There was only a fierce, quiet negotiation, carried out at the exact tolerance of stone and shell.

So the power did not touch the inside of the cleft. It did not add a millimeter of space, nor subtract a single drop of mineral broth.

Instead, it followed the wrist-thick rubber cable backward through the drilled borehole in the north wall, tracing it through yards of young basalt and miles of empty, salt-crusted plain to where the larger, unseen grid was slowly dying. Out there, in a forgotten substation half-buried in alkali dust, a set of old breakers was corroding toward failure, and an oil reservoir was two winters away from running dry. The power did not rebuild the substation. It simply placed a quiet, steady hand on the single circuit that fed the borehole—ensuring that when the rest of the grid finally went dark, this one wire would keep its low-voltage pulse, not a watt stronger and not a watt weaker, for as long as the creatures below had use for it.

Then it returned to the lip of the cleft.

Dusk was settling over the salt pan, and the plain above was beginning to stiffen with frost. From the seam below, a thin plume of warm vapor rose. The power watched the amphibian uncurl from behind the transformer, slide like thick oil over the wet backs of the chitons without waking them, and hang its broad, flat head over the three iridescent puddles to wait for a crab to shed its shell.

As the power prepared to withdraw, its gaze caught on a loose, frost-wedged shard of basalt balanced on the upper rim, poised directly above the three glass sight-gauges. One hard freeze, or the passing foot of a wandering bird, would have sent it straight down into the heart of the pocket.

With the gentlest possible touch—no heavier than a shift in the wind—it nudged the shard half a finger’s width back from the edge, onto flat, solid stone.

Then it stepped away, leaving the cleft to the clear dark, the sixty-cycle hum, and the next forty seconds.
