At first, the power only watched, because watching was the only courtesy large enough for such a quiet place.

From above, the plateau looked like an error in the world’s geometry—a vast, tilted table of slate and mist where nothing burned, nothing sang, and nothing grew tall enough to cast a shadow. A lesser kindness, arriving in haste, would have seen only deprivation and set to work at once.

It would have been easy to bring mortar, to bind the dry-laid walls into rigid permanence so no keeper would ever again have to tap a chinking shard into a settling lintel. Easy to raise timber and kindle hearths in the lower rooms, driving out the damp chill that lived in the stone. Easy to part the cloud bank for good, warming the highland until trees took root in the alleys, soil deepened over the slate, and voices could carry across broad, sunlit plazas without breaking into flat echoes.

The power held all of these gifts in its palm, and one by one, it laid them aside.

With wisdom came the understanding that mortar does not forgive frost; it cracks where dry stone flexes, and once sealed, the hidden seams of the walls would suffocate. The wall-mammals would die in their dark corridors, the stagnant air would rot the lower rooms, and the city would become a tomb of rigid rubble. Fire, in those narrow, windless canyons, would fill the alleys with trapped smoke that no draft-vent could clear. And endless sun would drink the moss dry, starve the snails of their wet lichen, shrink the black glass of the cisterns, and turn the plateau into cracked, lifeless dust.

Even to multiply the keepers—to fill the lanes with bustling crowds and loud laughter—would be to shatter the stillness by which the birds hunted, the mammals listened, and the stone itself was heard.

The city was not broken. It was a finished pact between gravity, water, and patience.

Yet the longer the power lingered—through seasons of afternoon mist and clear, freezing nights—the more clearly it saw the single, quiet fragility beneath the keepers’ mastery. They were so few. A few dozen silhouettes scattered across miles of stepped slate, holding back the slow, patient weight of winter with nothing more than calloused heels, sharp shards, and wooden pries worn thin by centuries of dead hands. A single rockslide in an unwalked sector, a harsher frost than usual, a lean season in the cisterns, and the margin would narrow past recovery. Once the channels clogged and the water pooled, the plateau would take its stone back in a single generation.

So the power chose to act as the mist acts: without weight, and without announcing itself.

Nothing changed on the upper roofs where the keepers walked. No voice spoke in the canyons; no miracle warmed the dark wells. Instead, in the far, mist-wrapped reaches of the city—miles from any inhabited room, where the keepers could only walk once a year—the margins quietly held. When a heavy slab shifted above an untended runnel in the dead of night, the frost beneath it eased a fraction of a degree, just enough for the stone to settle back into its bed rather than pop free. When sedge-roots threatened to choke a distant settling basin before a keeper could reach it, a sudden, timely eddy of afternoon wind lifted the dead tuft clear.

In the subterranean cisterns, the balance of the black water stayed generous. The thread-worms multiplied just enough through the deepest winters; the pale amphibians grew fat and unhurried, and the old, wrist-thick ones lingered undisturbed in the dark. The keepers noticed only that the outer tiers seemed strangely sound when they made their seasonal rounds, and that the cisterns did not fail them. They did not question it; stone and water had always had their own slow moods.

And then, once, the power offered a single gift in the only language the city spoke.

On a western terrace, after a season of bitter rime, an old keeper sat alone on a sun-warmed slab as the evening cloud bank tore open. Beside him lay his wooden pry—dark, ancient, and worn so narrow at the tip by generations of his bloodline that it could scarcely lift a heavy lintel anymore.

He had not asked for anything. He was only sitting with his back against the warm slate, breathing the cold air back into the sky alongside the stone, letting the thin silver light soak into his oiled coat.

When he stood at dusk to climb back down into the shadow of the lanes, his foot brushed something resting on the sill of a nearby draft-vent, placed as deliberately as a limestone pebble.

It was a length of dense, close-grained wood, stripped of bark and seasoned hard by high wind, as if an impossible storm had carried a bough up from the world below the clouds and dropped it cleanly on the terrace. It was unshaped—no tool had touched it, no hand had smoothed it. That work belonged to the keeper.

He picked it up, tested its weight against his palm, and ran a calloused thumb along the tight grain. It was sound. It would outlast him, and the hands after his.

He tucked it beneath his stiffened cloak, stepped down into the narrow, glistening trough of the stairs, and vanished into the grey below—leaving the power to watch the last light leave the wet roofs, and the first quiet drops begin to fall into the dark.
