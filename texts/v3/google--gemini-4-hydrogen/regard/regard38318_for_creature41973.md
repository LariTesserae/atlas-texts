A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive here with light.

Light, in this bruised and heavy depth, would be a kind of violence. It would bleach the dead-starlight shimmer from the moss, confuse the slow chemistry of the groves, and turn the quiet, ink-dark hollows of the ruins into exposed, defenseless rooms. So the power comes without radiance, without warmth, without a voice to compete with the reeds. It simply slows itself down until its attention moves at the speed of cold water and settling silt.

For a long time—longer than a tide, longer than the slow inflation of a kelp-bladder—it only listens.

It feels the subterranean river pouring through the notches of the drowned ridges, the hiss of mineral grit spilling over the terrace lips, the low, endless thunder of cobbles rolling in the troughs. It hears the thirty-foot organ-reeds groaning in shifting chords as the current turns, and the muffled *thump* of a spore-pod bursting in the dark.

Then it notices the tension in the ruins.

It traces the fine, silt-crusted threads strung like a loom across a caved-in doorway. It feels the rhythmic, muffled *crunch* inside a dry-stone wall where a stone-hearted knot is milling glass-fern shards between the pebbles in its belly. It watches one heavy, eyeless tangle draw in water, lighten its ballast by a fraction of an ounce, lift for a heartbeat in the surge, and drop two inches downstream with a dull, stony *clack*.

A lesser kindness—one that was generous but not wise—might look at this place and feel pity.

It might wish to warm the water, to soften the freezing grip that keeps everything so close to stillness. It might wish to bring down sunlight from the upper world, to scatter bright schools of fish through the groves, to give the slow knots eyes to see the beauty of the slate terraces, or legs to walk them without borrowing the current. It might look at the collapsed corbelled roofs and the half-buried copper awls and feel the urge to lift the stones back into place, to restore what the vanished builders lost.

The wise power considers each of these gifts, and sets every one of them aside.

To warm the water would invite rot and hunger; it would breed the darting, toothed things that graze the reeds and tear the slow, defenseless fibers from their anchors. To clear the silt from the doorways would starve the eddies where the spores settle. To lift the fallen lintels would take away the very gaps across which the knots string their listening-threads. And to give these beings eyes, or swiftness, or the memory of another kind of life, would be to burden them with restlessness—to sever them from the exact physics that makes them whole.

They are not impoverished. They are not waiting to be rescued from the dark. Their coldness is their preservation; their heaviness is their anchor; their blindness is the stillness that lets them hear the entire valley sing through a single taut thread. Even their dying—petrifying slowly into the corners of the walls to hold back the surge for the younger ones—is a kind of grace that needs no improvement.

So the power withholds its hand from the plateau. It does not touch a single cobble, or tune a single reed, or hurry a single creeping knot across the terraces.

Instead, its work happens far above and beyond the rim, out in the vast, indifferent ocean where the plateau cannot see. There, in the upper waters where warmer, hungrier currents drift and the restless life of the sunlit world swims in great, swarming shoals, the power gently lays its weight against the sea. It does not wall the valley off—for the valley needs the cold, roaring river that feeds it—but it bends the outer currents just enough. It turns aside the warm eddies that might otherwise spill down onto the shelf. It keeps the scouring storms and the bottom-dragging nets and the curious, grazing things of the upper world from ever finding the notches in the ridges.

It builds no monument. It leaves no sign. It simply stands guard over the valley’s solitude, ensuring that the cold keeps coming cleanly through the dark, and that the rim above the abyss holds firm.

Down on the shelf, nothing changes.

A bellows-kelp swells tight as a drum and vents its pale plume into the roaring water. Half a mile away, a grove of organ-reeds answers with a deep, vibrating chord. In the quiet lee of a sunken wall, a tangle of grey-olive fiber tightens its threads against the surge, and a pale green-white shimmer ripples slowly along its veins—the valley listening to itself, unbroken, keeping its own company in the dark.
