The attention did not arrive as a beam of sunlight, nor as a voice that could out-shout the dynamos. The basin had no use for either. Instead, the power simply became present in the cool, clammy air—a quietness of perception that slipped between the papery wings of the flyers and settled over the slick flagstones without disturbing a single drop of condensation.

At first, it looked upon the basin as any compassionate stranger might: with a sudden, aching impulse to mend.

It saw a sunless pit walled in pale slate, loud enough to numb the bone, where nothing green had ever taken root. It saw creatures without eyes, bound to wet concrete and blistering iron, living and dying in the exhaust of machines whose makers had vanished long ago. It saw the great, slate-grey hangers suspended in the sixty-cycle hum of the power lines, their damp cartilage tufts crackling with blue-white static, never once walking upright, never once seeing the sky above the cloud-ceiling.

A lesser mercy would have reached down at once. It would have stilled the flywheels to grant the basin silence. It would have parted the clouds to let in the sun, scattered grass seed across the flagstones, and cracked the slate walls so the trapped things might wander out into a wider world.

But wisdom is the habit of looking twice before touching. So the power waited, and listened, and traced the circuit from the cisterns to the insulators.

It followed the lukewarm water down through the iron grates and watched the corrugated siphoners strain out the oil and grit, keeping the dark throats clear. It slipped behind the slatted vents into the hot, copper-scented dark, and saw the tiny bronze hexagons creeping around the shafts, eating the black varnish before it could seize the bearings. It felt the steady, hypnotic pulse of the pistons, and realized that the roar was not the sound of labor or distress. It was a heartbeat.

And when it looked into the lives of the creatures—the wheeling flyers, the unison-clacking grazers, the blind white scavengers, the slow hangers beneath the catwalks—it searched carefully for the things that usually call out for rescue.

It searched for hunger, and found none. Every mouth met the exact measure of its food.
It searched for terror, and found none. Nothing fled; nothing hunted in desperation.
It searched for agony, and found only a clean, unhurried exchange. When a grazer’s foot loosened on the damp masonry, or a hanger’s ancient hooks finally let go of the steel railing, there was no lingering decay, no slow starvation—only a brief drop, a swift stripping by the blind scavengers, and a return to the mineral mist.

Then the power tested each of its own impulses against the truth of the place, and saw what its "kindness" would have done.

If it silenced the dynamos, the thermal updrafts would collapse; the flyers would drop like wet paper onto the stone, and the hangers would starve in the dark.
If it let in the sun and planted green, moss would choke the grates, roots would split the cisterns, and the heat would boil away the cool mist that kept the concrete from crumbling.
If it gave eyes to the blind scavengers, or longing to the bronze hexagons, or memory to the hangers, it would not be giving them freedom—it would be inventing misery where none existed, cursing them with the sudden knowledge of a cage they had never felt.

To "save" this basin from its greyness and its noise would be to murder it for the crime of not looking like a garden.

So the power stayed its hand. It did not touch a single rivet, or quiet a single piston, or alter the arc of a single flyer’s wing.

Instead, it did only two small, invisible things.

First, high along the rim of the pale slate cliffs, above the reach of the mist and the whistling swarms, it set a quiet ward—not a wall to keep the basin’s inhabitants in, for none of them wished to leave, but a shelter to keep the outside world’s carelessness out. No stray seed would blow over the lip to foul the drains; no wandering storm would flood the sumps; no well-meaning passerby would ever climb down with a wrench or a torch to "fix" what was not broken.

Second, it simply *witnessed* the basin. It held the entire roaring, damp, self-balancing miracle in its memory—every flake of mineral scale, every charged filament, every turn of the flywheels—and pronounced it whole. Not a ruin, not a purgatory, not an error waiting to be corrected, but a strange and austere perfection, worthy of its own persistence.

Then the attention withdrew, leaving not a trace behind. Below the featureless ceiling of cloud, the arc-lamps hummed, the shell-bearers clattered in unison across the wet concrete, and the great grey hangers combed stunned flyers from their glowing shoulders, turning over in the mist at the exact same speed forever.
