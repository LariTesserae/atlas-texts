It does not arrive from the sky. Anything that drops from the high, dry blue above the rim—like the rare, high-flying birds that skim the lip and veer away—knows nothing of the valley except that it is loud and wet.

Instead, the attention enters the way the water does: slowly, from deep within the limestone, rising through the horizontal seams until it pools in the ankle-deep settling pans and presses softly against the brass plates of the tenders’ boots.

A lesser kindness would have acted within the hour. A lesser wisdom would have looked at the pale, oil-slicked skin, the chalky lime gathering behind the knees, the children pressing their hands to their aching ears as the diffusers roar, and thought: *Here is a people trapped in a machine. Let me quiet the iron. Let me dry the stone. Let me give them back the sky.*

The power that comes to the basin is neither hasty nor blind. Before it touches a single sluice-gate, it waits—for a season, perhaps, or the time it takes a counterweight to fall and be wound up again a hundred times. It learns the valley at two or three revolutions a minute.

It feels the two tenders meeting on a narrow viaduct, neither speaking, simply touching the copper ferrules of their ash sounding-rods together until the tremor of Sector Four passes cleanly from one collarbone to the other. It feels the tender in the kitchen at dusk, watching the miniature diamond-lattice in the bronze bowl on the table, reading the health of the whole city in a quarter-inch of warm brine. It feels the old man carried down into the unlit fissures, laid out on a limestone shelf so the weeping rock may fold him, millimeter by patient millimeter, back into the floor that holds the hum.

And it feels the one tender who must ride the quarterly cable-car up to the rim—how, the moment the wind whistles dryly through the scrub and the rib-pressing roar falls away, the man grips the iron rail with white knuckles, dizzy and breathless, staring out at the bright, sunlit lowlands as though the world has died and gone hollow.

Then the power understands. To silence the valley would be to blind them. To restore their air-hearing in the midst of the diffusers would be torture; to lift them out of the basin into a quiet green meadow would be exile. Their slow tempers, their patient griefs, their whole-body literacy in salt and iron—none of these are scars left by a cruel place. They are the shape of the people themselves, grown to fit the stone the way the olive-black moss grows to fit the leeward faces of the aqueducts.

So the power puts away its grander mercies. It does not part the mist. It does not speak in the air, where no one would hear it, nor shake the viaducts, which would only make the tenders reach for their wrenches and look for a jammed relief-stack.

Instead, it begins to groom the valley the way the tenders do: quietly, by fractions of a turn.

Deep beneath Sector Three, far below the reach of any six-foot ash rod, a load-bearing shelf of ancient travertine has been quietly thinning for four centuries, eaten from underneath by a stray vein of warm water. In another eighty years, it would have given way—not dramatically, not all at once, but enough to tilt the sluices, snap the iron conduits, and flood the acoustic shadows where thirty families sleep. The power does not shore it up with miraculous stone. It simply nudges the flow of the subterranean vein by the width of a hair, coaxing the mineral-rich water to lay down its pale crust of lime *here* rather than *there*. Over the next decade, the shelf thickens again. To the tenders walking the catwalks above, it feels only as though the valley’s deep bass note has settled into a slightly warmer, cleaner key.

In the baffled masonry vaults, where the young children sit on hanging canvas cots during the hard months when the roar wears down their eardrums, the power softens the crossing. It does not spare them the surrender of their air-hearing—to keep it would leave them strangers in their own homes—but it eases the sharp, ringing ache of the transition, letting the airborne static fade gently into stillness while the bone-hearing wakes smoothly in their heels and wrists, like a bearing taking oil.

In the quiet corners behind the buttresses, where the salt-hardy sedge roots in the silt, the leathery moss grows a fraction thicker, and the pale, sightless amphibians grazing beneath the cisterns grow a little plumper, yielding a clearer oil for the winter leather.

And for the rare tender who must ride the cable-car up into the thin, terrifying silence of the rim to trade mineral cakes for wool and salt-free grain, the power leaves a small, private grace: as long as their brass-shod boots rest on the iron floor of the car, or even on the dry scrub of the landing, the faintest phantom of two revolutions a minute lingers in the metal—just enough pressure against the soles of their feet to keep the world from feeling hollow until they can descend back into the mist.

No one in the numbered sectors ever guesses that anything has visited them. There are no shrines built on the viaducts, no new gestures added to the broad, slow sign language across the flats.

Only, late in the afternoon, an old tender might lean on their sounding-rod beside a copper diffuser, feel the steady salt-taste of a clean machine humming up through their jawbone, and think—with the unhurried satisfaction of someone who has spent seventy years keeping the rust oiled and the gaskets tight—that the basin is holding its breath well today.
