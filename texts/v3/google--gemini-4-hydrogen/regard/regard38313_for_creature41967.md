If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to the plateau, it would not arrive with thunder, or with a parting of the clouds. A sudden shaft of dry, blistering sunlight would blind the milky-eyed predators, scorch the saturated cushions of sphagnum, and send the silent herds bolting in panic across the brittle evaporation pans.

Instead, it would arrive the way everything else arrives up here: by condensing slowly out of the thin air, settling into the three-foot pelt of moss, and learning to listen through its palms.

At first, the power’s impulse—the simple, aching reflex of kindness—would be to rescue them.

Looking down through the mist, it would see people with no roofs over their heads, curled like wet animals beneath blankets of living willow. It would see them eating cold, mineral-salted fare without the comfort of fire. It would see their broad, calloused hands cracked from the ozone-laced bog water, and it would watch them tie stones to their waists and slip into freezing, ink-black sinkholes to scrape bronze blades in the dark until their lungs burned.

A merely powerful kindness would sweep the clouds aside. It would raise dry, warm halls of cut basalt, light hearths that never went out, weave cloth that never dampened, and remake the ancient turbines so they cleaned themselves, freeing the Tenders from the dark and the wet forever.

And a wise power would stop its hand before a single stone was lifted.

Because as it listened to the sub-audible thrum rising through the peat, it would understand what a hasty savior would destroy. To dry the plateau is to kill it. To silence the turbines—or to sever the Tenders from their tending—is to leave the people stranded on top of a world they no longer know how to read, their jaw-hums meaningless, their bone spades useless, their bodies built for a cold that no longer exists. They are not prisoners chained to an engine. They are the engine’s nervous system, just as the bronze-backed beetles are its blood and the blind newts are its breath.

So the power would not break the pact between the land, the machine, and the people. Instead, it would look past the mere appearance of hardship to find the *true* wounds—the quiet, needless cruelties and the slow, looming failures that even the wisest Tenders could not mend on their own.

It would find three.

First, it would follow a young Tender down into a flooded basalt shaft. It would feel the crushing pressure in the ears, the freezing dark, the frantic scraping of the horn knife against mineral scale, and the terrible, silent panic when a lungful of air runs thin thirty feet below the moss. The work itself is sacred; the drowning is not.

So the power would reach into the fractured bedrock—not to banish the sponges or stop the scale, which feed the pale crustaceans and give the Tenders their craft—but to coax the hairline cracks above the buried electrolytic cells. Those cells already split the acidic bog-water into invisible pearls of oxygen and hydrogen. Gently, the power would guide the rising streams of pure oxygen through the porous stone, gathering them into inverted, natural hollows in the basalt ceiling right beside the turning turbine hubs.

The next time a Tender dives into the dark, stone-weighted and shivering, their outstretched hand will brush against a sudden boundary of silver-cool air trapped beneath the rock. They will be able to tilt their head up into a hidden bell of breath, gasp in the roaring, liquid silence, and return to the bronze blades with steady hands.

Second, the power would trace the braided copper lines for hundreds of miles beneath the root-mat, looking not at today, but at a thousand winters from now. The plateau’s industry is ancient, left behind by builders long forgotten. Copper oxidizes. Polymer grows brittle and cracks. The Tenders can splice a parted cable and wrap it in resinous peat, but they cannot smelt new wire or refine new insulation in a world without fire. Left alone, the great metabolism would eventually outlive its bones, and the warm emerald basins would go cold one by one.

So the power would whisper a small, patient change into the damp dark—not to the machines, but to the living things already clinging to them. It would nudge the pale, threadlike fungi that graze on the warm cables, and the metal-tolerant lichens that rim the sulfur pans. Over seasons, those fungi would learn to weave a dense, waterproof, self-sealing biopolymer out of decaying sphagnum—a living sheath that slowly knits itself over frayed lines. And the copper-drinking beetles would begin to leave behind not just empty shells, but a fine, conductive dust that the wet current draws back into thinning strands. The Tenders would still have to walk the ridges, still have to read the browning moss and dig with their bone spades when a line shifts—but they would no longer be fighting a slow, losing war against rust and time.

Third, the power would settle beside a group of Tenders curled asleep in a steaming emerald basin, cheek-to-cheek beneath their blankets of dwarf willow. It would feel the deep, silent ache in the elders’ joints—the quiet toll of a lifetime spent in saturated cold, where even the warm moss cannot quite reach the marrow of a hip or a shoulder.

It would not take away the wet, nor dry their skin. Instead, it would tune the hidden transformers beneath the sleeping basins by a fraction of a cycle—a shift too subtle to disturb the hunting predators or confuse the marching beetles, yet deep enough that the sub-audible thrum enters the bone like a slow, steady warmth, easing the mineral stiffness from old hands while they dream.

Then, having left no monument, no voice in the mist, and no law carved in stone, the power would draw back into the thin air and let the weather turn on its own axis once more.

Down on the plateau, a Tender climbs out of a dark sinkhole, slick with mineral water and trembling from the deep—astonished, and quietly grateful, to have found a pocket of sweet air waiting for them beside the turning blades. They do not look up at the pale, uninterrupted horizon. They simply wring the water from their hair, pull their oiled hide back over their shoulders, and press their palms flat against the wet turf to listen.

Beneath their hands, the great engine settles back into its ceaseless, silent work—steady, unbroken, and, for the first time in eons, faintly, unmistakably kind.
