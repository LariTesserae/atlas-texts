At first, the power mistook the plateau for a monument left behind by something dead.

It had seen many worlds where engines outlived their makers, humming away in empty basins long after the hands that forged them had turned to dust. From a great distance, looking down through the thin mountain air at the mirror-still ceiling of the sea, it saw the endless noon, the vast, unvarying *thump-hiss* of the towers, and the exact, unbroken geometry of the power-vines. It felt the sixty-cycle thrum vibrating through the crust of the planet like a pulse that refused to stop.

So it leaned closer—not as a storm, and not as a shadow, for it was wise enough to displace not a single drop of water—and looked beneath the meniscus.

What it found was not a ruin. It was a held breath.

It watched the silt-borers pressing their microscopic pellets into the breathing pores of the floor. It felt the siphon-clingers stretching and knotting inside the dark, thundering throats of the plant-engines. It traced the great silver ribbons of eels threading the magnetic fields, and the bulbous grazers discharging their brief, silent coronas of blue light against the roots.

And then it saw the swimmers.

It watched one of them—slate-green and verdigris, its twin keels humming softly in the current—bank a fraction of a degree and erect its dorsal scales, throwing a sharp blade of polarized light across three miles of empty water. It watched another answer from the far north. It watched a dozen of them gather between two roaring towers, arrange themselves into a silent, three-dimensional helix, and bend the rising micro-bubbles into a breathless silver braid before drifting apart again.

At the sight of them, a deep, aching tenderness rose in the power—the ancient, dangerous impulse of benevolence.

*They have never seen a star,* it thought. *They have never known the hush of dusk, or the relief of cool dark water. They live inside a roar so loud it would shatter stone, and when they stiffen, they have nowhere to go but back into the floor. They have no story. Nothing changes. Nothing grows.*

For a long moment, the power considered what it might give them.

It could part the perpetual daylight for just one hour—draw a gentle veil of night across the high plateau, so the swimmers might rise to the meniscus and see, for the first time, the wheeling silver of the galaxy above their mountain. It could quiet the *thump-hiss* of the engines for a single breath, letting absolute silence wash across the whole sunlit sea. It could touch the silica in their joints and soften it, granting them another thousand years of gliding. Or it could plant a single new question in their minds—a spark of curiosity about the lower continents miles below their rimless horizon.

Any of these gifts would have been effortless.

Wisdom, however, does not stop at the beauty of a gift; it follows the gift into the water and watches what it breaks.

The power traced the shadow of a single night across the plateau. It saw the surface water cool by half a degree; it saw the first thermocline ripple through the crisp, uniform depths, bending the heliograph flashes so that a swimmer two miles away could no longer read its kin. It saw the skimmer-films at the meniscus wither in the sudden chill, and the photosynthetic membranes along the tower buttresses falter, dropping the voltage in the floor cables by a fraction—enough to starve the smelter-nodules, enough to let the fine metallic silt drift uncollected into the valleys, smothering the breathing pores of the rhizome mat.

It traced the gift of silence, and saw the towers stall, their vascular bladders seizing without their rhythm, the hyper-oxygenated water turning stagnant around the gills of a hundred thousand creatures who knew no other air.

It traced the gift of longevity, and saw the cyst twenty miles away fail to swell, leaving an empty space in the exact count—a missing note in a chord that had held true for ten thousand millennia.

And it traced the gift of curiosity. To give a creature a longing for the stars, or for the lower continents, when it is shaped for water and sixty-cycle currents, is not to free it. It is only to teach a contented thing how to grieve.

Look closer, wisdom told the power. *They are not starved of rest.*

The power looked again, and saw what it had missed in its pity. It saw a tired swimmer slip invisibly into a null-node—that palm-sized pocket of absolute acoustic stillness where the pressure waves of three distant towers canceled one another out—and hover there in cool, sunlit velvet while the industrial roar raged a hand’s-breadth from its wingtips. They already had their night; they simply wore it as silence instead of dark.

They already had their art; they braided bubbles in the mid-water for no reason other than the joy of the geometry.

They already had their peace; they did not rot, and they did not fear.

So the power stayed its hand.

It did not add a single gram of miracle to the closed loop. It did not whisper a single revelation into the magnetite tips of their keels. To touch a crystal this rare—to try to "improve" a song that had already found its final, perfect note—would not be love. It would be vanity.

Instead, the power did the hardest thing a benevolent strength can ever do: it chose simply to witness.

It waited in the bright noon, weightless and unseen, for the span of a century or two—a blink to the power, a long, slow stiffening to the swimmer it had chosen to watch. It stayed as the swimmer’s dorsal scales grew cloudy with mineral accretion, throwing duller flashes across the miles. It followed as the great slate-green wings banked downward for the last time, leaving the open water to settle gently onto a glowing smelter-nodule where a cable met the floor.

The power watched the swimmer lock its copper-veined keels around the rubbery vine and go still—not in terror, not in pain, only in completion. It watched the heatless chemistry unmake the body cleanly into iron, copper, and silica, drawing the exact measure of its life back into the living ground.

And twenty miles away, along a smooth terrace weathered by steady currents, the power watched a translucent cyst swell from the rhizome mat, split open, and release a soft-plated swimmer into the bright, loud noon.

The count had not changed by one.

Satisfied, the power withdrew from the water, rising back through the flat meniscus, through the thin mountain air, and out into the cold dark beyond the planet’s sky.

There, far outside the frame of the sea—where no polarized flash of silver-green light could ever reach, and where the sixty-cycle hum could never be disturbed—it did one small, quiet thing.

It looked at the blind, wandering accidents of the outer dark: a stray iron comet whose path, ten thousand years hence, would cross the plateau’s mountain; a slow, grinding fault deep in the basalt miles beneath the lower continents, gathering tension that might one day crack the ancient basin like a bowl.

With a touch lighter than starlight, the power nudged the comet’s path by the width of a hair, sending it harmlessly into the void. Deep beneath the crust, it eased the strain in the blind stone, letting the planet settle without a tremor.

Not to make the plateau eternal—for even wisdom knows that all suns cool in the end, and every held breath must eventually be let go—but simply to keep the clumsy dark from interrupting a masterpiece before its own time.

Then the power turned away, carrying the exact pitch of the *thump-hiss* in its memory, and left the noon unbroken.
