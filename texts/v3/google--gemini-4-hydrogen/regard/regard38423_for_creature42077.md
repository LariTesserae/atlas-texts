It did not arrive with light.

Light here would have been a violence. A sudden parting of the living roof would have burned the pale moss from the arches, driven the bats into frantic, colliding clouds, and dried the slow catch-basins before the frogs could find another dark hollow. Nor did it arrive with a voice, for the air was already full to bursting, and a voice—even a gentle one—would have been only another roar piled onto the din.

Instead, a benevolent and wise power arrived the way water arrives on this plateau: by cooling, gathering, and seeping in without breaking a single seam.

It settled first in the thin, cold mist above the figs, where the warm breath of the chimneys rose to meet the mountain sky. From there, it lowered its attention down a narrow ventilation shaft, past the hanging curtains of moss and the sleeping swiftlets, until it stood in the gray-green gloom of the galleries.

For a long time, it simply observed.

At first, benevolence alone stirred within it—the quick, protective ache that any loving maker feels when looking at something small and vulnerable in the dark. It saw creatures crouched in near-blindness, earless and heavy-limbed, chewing rot and spit in a roaring dimness. It saw the ceiling sagging under centuries of root-weight, the walls worn thin by condensation, the endless, grinding patience required just to keep the roof from falling in.

*I could open the sky for them,* the power thought. *I could quiet the bats and the crickets so they might hear one another speak. I could give them stone that does not soften, or fire to dry their chambers, or eyes sharp enough to see the beauty of what they have built.*

Wisdom, however, stayed its hand.

Wisdom knew that to judge a house, you must first touch its walls. So the power withdrew its gaze from the dimness and pressed its attention flat against a damp clay buttress, the way a tender presses its jaw.

Instantly, the deafening wall of wings and frog-song vanished.

In its place rose a single, vast, resonant chord.

The power felt the whole city at once—not as a labyrinth of mud and gloom, but as a living instrument tuned to a fraction of a millimeter. It felt the slow, steady pulse of the fermentation chambers warming the lower vaults like a hearth. It felt the tension of a fig root three galleries away, gripping an arch just tightly enough to hold it up without splitting it. It felt the soft, rhythmic thud of a tender’s palm striking a hollow pillar two terraces down—not a cry of loneliness, but a quiet check-in: *Here. Still here. The wall is sound.*

And it understood that these beings were not trapped in the dark. They *were* the dark’s architecture. Their oil-slicked coats were the soil for the ferns; their spit was the mortar; their bone-listening was a language far older and more exact than any word that could be spoken into air. To give them light would be to blind them. To give them tools would be to sever their hands from the clay.

Still, the hardest test waited at the outer edge of the plateau.

The power followed a very old tender as it climbed slowly toward the sagging arches where the mountain clouds pressed hardest. The moss on its shoulders hung in thick, pale beards; its horn chisels were worn nearly to the bone. When it reached a hollow pillar that hummed a fraction too high—a pillar on the verge of buckling—the old one wedged itself deep into the gap and curled its heavy limbs inward.

Its kin followed, carrying mouthfuls of wet silt and chewed pulp. Silently, patiently, they began to plaster warm clay over the elder’s back, over its mossy shoulders, over its blunt, earless head, while its chest was still rising and falling.

Here, mercy nearly broke. Every instinct of a kind power cried out to reach down, to gently peel away the wet clay, to lift the elder out into the cool air, to mend its stiffened joints and give it another hundred years of walking the galleries.

Wisdom said: *Listen first.*

So the power pressed its attention into the freshly plastered wall, right against the elder’s ribs.

There was no fear there. No struggle, no grief, no sense of abandonment. The elder’s heart was slow and deeply satisfied, like a tool set down at the end of a long, good day. Beneath the warm blanket of clay, it could feel its kin’s hands smoothing the outside of its tomb, and beneath that, it could feel the immense weight of the sagging arch settling safely onto its own shoulders. It was not being buried. It was becoming bone. In a season, its warmth would feed one last bloom of fungus; in a century, a young tender would press its palm to this exact spot and feel the elder still holding up the sky.

To pull it out would be to steal its life’s final, most cherished work.

So the power withdrew its hand from the pillar, leaving the wet clay undisturbed.

Inside the city, it did nothing at all. Not a single drip was hurried. Not a single cricket was hushed. Not a single word was whispered into the earless dark. Restraint, it knew, was the purest form of respect it could offer.

Yet before it lifted its attention back into the high mountain air, the power looked *beneath* the city—far down, past the deepest compost beds, past the crusted, trampled floors, down through centuries of buried wasp-comb and packed earth, to the raw, dead bedrock of the mountain itself.

There, in the blind stone where no root reached and no tender’s bones could ever hear, a slow fault was straining. In another two hundred years—a blink in the life of a mountain, an eternity in the life of a frog—the rock would have slipped, and the whole humming, breathing equilibrium above would have sheared apart in a single night.

The power reached down into that lightless, lifeless seam. Gently, without a tremor that could reach the galleries above, it knitted the fractured granite back together, coaxing the stone to hold fast. It did not make the mountain softer, or warmer, or easier to bear. It only made sure the floor would stay where it was put.

Then it rose back up the chimney, out into the thin, cold clouds, and left the hidden city to its own long, patient peace.

Three galleries down, a young tender stood motionless with its jaw pressed against an ancient buttress, listening to the deep hum of the world. For half a heartbeat, it seemed as though the note beneath its feet had grown extraordinarily clear—steady as an elder’s breath, deep as the mountain’s heart.

A heavy drop of condensation fell from a fern frond, striking the tender’s upturned palm. It blinked its small, recessed eyes, scooped up a handful of dark silt from the basin, and went back to mending the wall.
