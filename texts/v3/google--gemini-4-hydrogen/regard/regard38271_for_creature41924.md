At first, the power did what any compassionate outsider is tempted to do when looking down from the rim of the dike: it considered draining the basin.

From above, in the flat, unfiltered glare of the sky, the place looked like a wound that had been bandaged with iron and left to fester. A lesser mercy—one eager to be seen, eager to tidy—would have parted the canopy, dried the root-mat, silenced the brick vault, and led the lean, oil-sheened people out into the open wind. It would have called that *rescue*.

Instead, the power waited. It had the patience to measure time the way the basin did—not in hours, but in steepings and curings.

It let its attention sink through the clerestories, past the plumes of venting steam, down into the greenish-gold gloom. It felt the spongy give of the braided roots, the warm, sour breath of the fermentation vats, the dry *clack-clack-clack* of wooden clogs on perforated steel. It listened to the wrench-taps singing cleanly along the copper lines—*three short, one long*—bypassing the roar of the air altogether. It watched a press-watcher sit for four hours on an overturned grease-keg, whittling a wooden pin, glancing up only when the brass needle moved the width of a hair.

And it descended into the half-submerged vault by the dike, where the air was thickest and the hum of the steam-dynamo lived in the marrow of the teeth. There it watched a pale, deaf keeper lay a grease-blackened palm flat against a visitor’s chest, measuring the human pulse against the flywheel’s rhythm before nodding toward the right valve.

Then the power understood.

This was not a prison, and these were not captives. The iron, the cypress, the slurry, and the small-boned people were organs of a single, slow, amphibious body. To drain the water would be to bleed it. To silence the dynamo would be to stop its heart. To drag these people out into the dry, still sun would be to crack them like seasoned staves.

Wisdom, the power realized, meant keeping its hands off almost everything.

Still, benevolence is not indifference. Even a well-settled truce has its sharp edges, and a body that has grown around iron carries quiet aches. So the power acted—not as a savior from above, and not as an architect, but the way a *tree-wright* acts: with a small notch here, a dab of grease there, working strictly within the grain of what already lived.

First, it looked outward. The greatest danger to the basin was not the damp or the dark; it was the world beyond the limestone dike—the world of straight roads, dry ledgers, and people who could not stand to see a swamp left unimproved. The power did not raise a wall of fire or a glamour of terror. It simply thickened the creeping fig and maidenhair fern along the outer brickwork, and breathed a subtle, drowsy unimportance over the rim. To tax collectors, surveyors, and wandering reformers, the grove would always look like a fever-ditch too tangled to map and too poor to plunder. It gave the basin the gift of being overlooked.

Inside the dike, it touched only three things, and did each so slowly that no one could have pointed to a moment when it began.

Deep beneath the spongy root-mat, where the water table met the limestone, it steadied the springs. There would still be wet seasons and drier ones, enough to keep the cistern-tenders watchful and the gutter-pipes honest, but the water would never drop so low that the root-mat cracked and rotted, nor rise so high that it drowned the dynamo’s brick vault or washed the upper hammocks from their forks.

In the submerged vault itself, the power did not restore the keepers’ hearing; in that room, silence would have been a kind of blindness, and their deafness was the shell they had grown to live inside the roar. Instead, it nudged the main flywheel’s bronze bearing by the thickness of a spider’s thread—just enough to take the jagged, bone-grating shudder out of the shaft, leaving only the deep, warm, even pulse they knew by touch.

And in the damp mortar joints of the boiler-houses, beneath the catwalks where the steam-pipes hissed, it coaxed a quiet mutation in the pale shelf-fungi that already grew there. Nothing miraculous-looking—just a tougher, oil-tolerant strain that, when scraped off and mashed into tallow, cooled a scalded palm in minutes and kept the acidic bark-liquor from eating into the creases of calloused knuckles.

Nothing else. It did not hurry the Archimedes screws. It did not clear the copper-soured settling tanks or invite songbirds to nest where the drive-belts slapped. It left the people their work, their stillness, their chalk-marks on the swelling bark, and their hard-won truce with the damp.

A season later, a tree-wright climbing down from the upper lofts paused on a catwalk to scrape a handful of pale fungus off a brick wall, noting with mild satisfaction that it smelled cleaner than usual and mixed well with grease. Down in the vault, an old keeper rested his palm on the casing of the dynamo, felt the smooth, unblemished thrum of the flywheel pass up through his wrist and into his ribs, and nodded once in the lamp-lit dark.

Up above, through a gap in the philodendrons, a plume of white steam rose into the green-gold light, unhurried as breath. The power watched it disperse, satisfied that the joint was good for another thirty years, and withdrew.
