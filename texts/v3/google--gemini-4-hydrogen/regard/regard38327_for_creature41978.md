A power that is both benevolent and wise does not begin by acting. It begins by not breaking what it has found.

From the outside, miles above any sea or mountain, the shed looks like nothing more than a blister of oiled hide and mud-daubed reed clinging to a seam in the upper air—a thing too small and too loud to belong to the altitude, drumming furiously against the thin, screaming blue. Any vast presence approaching it without care would tear it apart simply by arriving: a shift in barometric weight, a sudden warmth, even a curious glance pressed too close to the scraped-horn panes would burst the membranes inward or cook the steam dry.

So the power folds its attention down to the scale of a thumbprint and listens.

It listens to the *thrum* of the wind on the roof, the whistle of the bone-pipe vents, the slap of condensation on waxy leaves, the dry *scritch-scritch* of the snail deep in the tiers. It watches the water climbing gut bowstrings and hanging mid-air for three heartbeats. It sees the pale, wet-slip figure hunched beside the sunken brazier, bound at wrist and ankle with unfrayed hemp, braided into the ceiling-rigging, its thumbs circling endlessly even in sleep to keep the world from drying out.

And the power feels, at first, the sharp, familiar ache of pity—the urge to *rescue*.

It knows how easily it could lift the whole crooked warren out of the screaming dark and set it down gently on broad, flat earth, beneath a mild sky where nothing has to fight to stay whole. It could untie the hemp from the figure’s wrists, unbraid its hair from the rafters, widen the elbow’s-width passage so a body could stride. It could bring a gentle, steady heat to dry the slick grey walls, to bake the half-coiled jars in an unseen kiln until they rang true and hard, finished at last, safe from cracking. It could seal the dizzying crack behind the lowest baskets and quiet the wind forever.

The power holds every one of those mercies in its mind, weighs them, and sets each one down untouched.

For wisdom sees what pity misses.

If it brought the shed down to heavy earth and dry sun, the wet slip on the walls—and on the figure’s collarbones—would set, shrink, and split. The bindings at the wrists and ankles are not chains; they are hoop-staves on a green barrel, keeping the pressure from tearing what is still soft. The hair knotted into the rigging is not a tether; it is how the occupant belongs to the frame. If it baked the clay to make it safe, it would kill the very thing that is alive here: a room and a maker that exist only in the act of arriving.

Even the harshness outside cannot be taken away. Without the frigid void leaning against the oiled hide, the steam inside would never condense; the gourds would wither, the salamander’s skin would dry, the brazier would roast the room to ash. The scream of the upper atmosphere is half the lung that lets this tiny world breathe.

A master potter knows the first rule of the wheel: you do not grab a spinning wet vessel, no matter how fragile its walls look. If you catch it, you collapse it. If you pull it off too soon, it slumps. If you fire it while it is still weeping water, it shatters in the dark.

So the power does not step inside. There is nowhere to stand, and no use for words.

Instead, it takes its place outside the stretched membranes, in the cold, thin blue where there is room enough for stillness. It does not block the wind, and it does not warm the void. It simply cups its attention an inch off the shuddering hide—close enough to feel the bass note of the shed, far enough not to muffle it—the way a steady hand hovers beside a tall, thin jar on a wheel, touching nothing while it runs true, ready only if it wobbles.

What it does, it does at the edges, invisibly, asking for no recognition and leaving no trace that might startle the occupant out of its rhythm.

When a rogue shear-gale rises out of the west—a knife-edged gust sharp enough to split oiled rawhide faster than two wet fingers can smear mud into a seam—the power meets it a hundred yards out and combs the worst of its teeth away, letting only the ordinary, necessary storm strike the drumhead. Down in the narrow, impossible well of nested bowls behind the lowest baskets, where the draft smells of ozone and ice, the power steadies the lowest unseen rim by a hair’s breadth, so the whole stacked geometry does not tilt into the drop. And it watches the ash in the sunken brazier, ensuring that whatever feeds that dull, sullen cherry glow never quite runs out before the next cycle turns.

Nothing more. It leaves the noise, the wet-wool heat, the tight shoulders of the shelves, the endless, patient work of the thumbs.

Inside, after a long cycle, the figure unfolds in slow, hinged increments from its crouch by the brazier. It unpins its braid from one knot and lashes itself to another, braces a palm against a weeping terracotta belly, and presses the flat of its broad, square-thumbed hand against the ceiling membrane.

It holds its breath, reading the upper air through the hide, gauging how thin the blue is outside and how hard the void is leaning in.

The wind still booms. The hide still flexes inward with a sharp *crack*. Yet beneath the rawhide, just for the space of a heartbeat, the pressure feels strangely even—not gentler, not silent, only steady, as though the sky had remembered how to hold its weight without pressing quite so hard.

The figure shows neither fear nor wonder. It only lets out its breath, dips two fingers into the simmering water of a shallow pan, scoops a bead of raw grey mud from a jar’s weeping side, and presses it into a complaining seam.

Then it settles back beside the brazier, tucks its chin toward its throat, and sets its thumbs once more against a wet clay rim—keeping the world spinning, while an unseen patience keeps the dark around it from tearing.
