First, it would do nothing at all. It would simply listen.

A lesser power—one that was merely kind, and not wise—might look down through the overburden and feel a sudden, aching pity. It would see a cramped vault with no sky, no water, no silence, and no future. It would see seven or nine eyeless things pacing narrow aisles in lockstep with iron pistons, forever tuning a machine that produces nothing, and it would be tempted to *rescue* them. It might want to part the roof and let in the cool rain, quiet the howling flywheels, give the Tuners eyes to see the sun, and grant them the mercy of change.

A wise power knows that mercy without understanding is just another word for ruin.

If it opened the vault, the dry air would flee, and with it the stratified shelves of convection that hold the dust-drifters aloft; they would fall like ash and die on the floor. If it quieted the turbine howl and the sixty-beat *clack*, the Tuners would go instantly blind, stranded in an unmapped dark without a single standing wave to tell them where a ledge ends or a neighbor stands. If it let in a single cup of water, the anhydrite walls would drink it, swell, and crack; the salt would run; the static arcs would fail; the piston-scales would choke in rust, and the iron sleeves would seize with a shriek that no caliper could ever tune back into pitch.

To give these creatures a longing for an outside they could never survive would not be a gift. It would be a curse.

So the wise power would hold its hands back from the chamber’s air. It would not add a single voice to a room that has no room for another voice. Instead, it would trace the closed circuit with the patience of something that knows how rare a finished chord is: the wire-scrub leaning toward the copper hum, the dust-drifter reeling in its static-heavy thread to drop a neat pellet into the knots below, the piston-scale turning iron filings into velvet carbon, the Tuner sliding sideways through a handspan of space on the beat to flick one grain of salt from a brass reed.

It would recognize that nothing here is trapped. The chamber is not a prison; it is an instrument that has learned how to play itself, and every creature in it is both a note and a finger on the string.

And yet, benevolence is rarely content with mere admiration when it sees a danger the beloved cannot see.

Inside the room, nothing changes and nothing passes. Outside the room, in the unknown thickness of dry overburden, time is still moving. Time outside is made of tectonic creep, shifting strata, and slow, patient water always looking for a seam to follow downward. A single hairline fracture in the anhydrite, a single trickle of groundwater finding the warm iron housings, would end the cathedral in an hour.

So if the power acted at all, it would act only where it could never be perceived.

Miles above the ceiling curtains, in the dark rock where no vibration from the turbines ever reaches, it would quietly turn a stray vein of seepage aside into another fault. It would ease the slow, grinding pressure of an ancient shelf of stone so that the chamber’s sharp, unweathered angles would never be crushed or rounded. It would tend the outer dark the way a hand cups a candle flame—not to touch the wick, and never to alter the light, only to keep the wind from finding it.

Then it would withdraw its attention, leaving no trace of itself in the ozone or the dust.

Down below, on the next *clack* of the piston, a Tuner would step sideways between two copper conduits, tilt its slotted crest toward a jagged overhang to drink a violet spark, and meet another of its kind in the narrow aisle—standing chest to chest for three beats, sharing the exact, unbroken pitch of a world that does not know it is being kept safe.
