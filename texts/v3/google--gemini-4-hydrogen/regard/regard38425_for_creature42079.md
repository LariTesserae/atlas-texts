First, the power would learn not to cast a shadow.

To look upon a place like the basin with ordinary attention—even loving attention—is to risk crowding it. A gaze too heavy might warm the air by a fraction of a degree, or stir the talc-fine dust from its crevices, or dim the unfiltered noon that the mica louvers are open to drink. So the power would arrive the way light arrives along the crooked paths of the valley: quietly, without weight, taking the shape of the seams rather than pressing against them.

At the beginning, there might be a temptation to pity.

A power accustomed to wet worlds—to rivers, breath, soft soil, and the forgiving give of green leaves—would see at first only deprivation. It would see razor-edged slate that cuts without warning; air so parched it cannot carry a sigh; creatures with no mouths to taste, no blood to warm them, no soft eyes to weep. It would see space itself fractured like dropped glass, and it would watch an aging inhabitant climb a ridge to stiffen in the sun until its plates give way one by one with small, dry *tinks*.

A merely kind power would reach down to mend it. It would summon a gentle rain to wash the mineral dust into loam. It would stitch the spatial seams shut, flatten the sideways pockets of gravity, soften the basaltic glass, and give the silent walkers voices that could carry on a breeze.

And in doing so, it would kill everything there.

Wisdom, looking closer, would see the ruin inside that mercy. It would see how a single drop of rain would short-circuit the humming copper veins, turn the capillary dust in the wrists to choking paste, and rot the spun-silica reeds from the root up. It would see how healing the fractures in reality would collapse the overlapping groves into one another, crush the quiet rooms folded behind crooked shadows, and blind the quartz lenses that were angled specifically to read a bent world. It would recognize that the slow accumulation of tension is not a disease, and that the final *tink* on a high ridge is not agony, but a clean, unhurried return of borrowed geometry.

So the first thing the benevolent power would do is withhold its hand.

Not a single fracture would be sealed. Not a single edge would be dulled. Not one grain of dust would be nudged off the invisible contour lines it follows on its way to the stone. The power would accept, without trying to translate it into something softer, that this is not a broken world waiting to be healed, but a finished instrument already playing in its own key.

Then, if it did anything at all, it would work only at the borders, where the basin meets the rest of things.

Far above the depression, at the rim of the clear, ordinary sky, it might set a quiet, unnoticed patience—nothing like a wall, for walls belong to builders, and nothing here is built. Just a subtle bending of the upper air, so that if a straying front of damp cloud or a wandering wind ever drifted toward the valley, it would curve gently around the rim and pass on, leaving the stillness beneath undisturbed.

And before withdrawing, the power might let its attention rest once against the warm bedrock, without weight or sound, simply to listen.

Half a mile away across the silica-slate, a solitary inhabitant would be pressing the three-pronged glass tips of its feet flat against the stone, tapping out a slow, crisp interval: *a seed-pod has split; a seam has shifted by the width of a hair; I am here.*

The power would not tap back. To answer in the stone would be to invent a neighbor that is not there, introducing a false note into a language made entirely of exact truths. Instead, it would listen until the last vibration faded through the quasi-crystalline dark, commit the whole dry, luminous grammar of the place to memory, and step back—leaving the valley as bright, as sharp, and as whole as it had found it.
