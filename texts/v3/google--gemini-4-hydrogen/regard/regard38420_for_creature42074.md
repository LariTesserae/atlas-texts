It did not enter the room. There was nowhere inside to stand without crushing a lattice of blind grazers, and the air was already full to the brim with a single, finished chord. To bring a presence into a space that small would have been to displace something that belonged there.

Instead, the power’s attention settled around the pocket from the outside, resting against the five meters of dry limestone like a palm cupped near a flame—close enough to feel the heat, careful never to stir the air.

At first, looking down through solid rock, it felt the ancient ache that every merciful thing feels when it encounters a closed world: the urge to *open*.

To the eye of a traveler from the wide sky, the pantry looked like an oubliette. The light was an unblinking, clinical glare with nowhere to rest. The walls were knives. The noise was a metallic roar loud enough to rattle marrow, and the three slender beings who lived inside it had no eyes, no horizon, no seasons, and no way out. They were trapped in a loop of salt and chitin, honing their claws on another creature’s forgotten scratches, laying single eggs in borrowed mud before dropping to the floor to become furniture for the stone.

A lesser kindness would have cracked the ceiling. It would have sent a clean shaft of sunlight down through the limestone, let in the smell of rain and pine, widened the chamber so the three could stretch, and softened the gypsum needles into rounded river-stone. It would have hushed the deafening hum so they could finally know peace.

Wisdom stayed its hand, and listened longer.

It tuned itself to the standing chord—not as an outsider hears noise, but as the creatures felt it in the hollow cartilage beneath their jaws. And once it heard from the inside, the urge to rescue vanished, replaced by a quiet, sober awe.

The glare was not harshness; it was their bread. To cast a single shadow across those faceted walls would be to invent hiding, and after hiding, fear. To dull the roar by even a single decibel would be to strike the three gleaners blind and deaf at once, severing the invisible geometry that kept them spaced in gentle thirds, robbing them of the subtle ripple that told them when a neighbor’s molt had gone wrong. To round the crystal teeth would collapse the acoustic bell they lived inside. Even to cure the slow stiffening of salt in their joints—to grant them endless years—would crowd the pantry, starve the nests in the high crevice, and break the clean, patient rhythm by which the stone remembered them.

Nothing in this lantern was broken. Nothing here was crying out for a sky it could not breathe. It was a complete thought, spoken in mineral and chitin, holding its pitch in the dark.

So the power touched nothing within the seal. It did not brighten the light, or dim it. It did not spare a single faltering millipede from the comb-lined mouths, nor did it whisper a new note into the metallic hum. The three remained three, gliding weightless over the wet facets, their crowns drinking the relentless noon.

Only in the five meters of featureless limestone *above*—out in the world of weather, erosion, and wandering roots, where things *do* break—did the power lay a single, quiet grace. Three meters down, a hairline fracture had been creeping through the strata for centuries, guided by winter frost and the patient downward probe of a surface taproot. Left alone, in another hundred years it would have reached the ceiling, pierced the seal, and drained the lantern into the dark.

Slowly, without disturbing a grain of dust below, the power turned the stress of the stone aside. It coaxed the mineral grain to knit tight along a different seam, guiding the future frost and the questing root away into barren bedrock, leaving the pocket’s roof thick and whole.

Then it withdrew its attention, leaving no trace—no shadow, no draft, no echo—and let the small, bright engine keep its single, unbroken note beneath the earth.
