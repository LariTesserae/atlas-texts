When the attention arrives, it does not come as light.

Light would be a violence here. To part the soot-thick overcast above the rim, to let in the raw, blue glare of the high sky or the thin winter sun, would be to blind the wall-hangers’ horn dishes with a flood of meaningless heat and blister the ash-gliders’ vellum wings before they could bank toward the dark.

So the power comes the way the hollow understands: as a stillness within the stillness, a slow, vast noticing that settles over the basalt lip without stirring a single grain of iron-sand.

First, it listens.

It reads the bowl from the bottom up, the way a physician reads a pulse. Down in the hair-thin fissures where no eye could follow, it sees the cinder-braids drinking the gradient, turning raw sulfur and vaporized iron into pearls of fat. It watches the twenty-minute surge roll upward, the silent, synchronized choreography of smoked-glass plates and leathery wings, the swift, noiseless economy of the dust-beetles stripping a fallen swimmer down to dry shell before a single drop of moisture can escape into the kiln-dry air.

And along the eastern wall, tucked inside the broken stone rings beneath a crust of sleeping husk-mites, it finds the Dry-Keepers.

It sees what they are, and what they were. It sees the long, soapstone-smooth hands folded tightly over ribs that rise once every three minutes; the millstone teeth; the burned-looking pits along the brow that read the dark in degrees of warmth. It sees the faint, blunt-toothed notches gouged into the threshold-slabs, and the paper-light husks tucked carefully into the gaps of the cairns—a boundary mended endlessly against nothing at all, by creatures who have forgotten the word for *inside* and *out*.

A merely kind power might weep here, and in weeping, ruin everything.

It would be so easy to send rain. A single cool cloud, gathered from the freezing air beyond the massif and wrung out over the bowl, would hiss against the floor like a quenched forge. Within seconds, a boiling fog would scald the wall-hangers from their ledges, burst the wax-sealed pores of the Dry-Keepers, drown the sand-swimmers in mud, and choke the cinder-braids in wet ash. It would be so easy to cool the vents, to soften the basalt into soil, to plant moss and fern and lift the Keepers out into a green valley where they could breathe without rationing the air—and watch them die there, bewildered and shivering, their lungs drowned by humidity, their thick hides cracking in the cold, their heat-pits useless in a world full of sun.

Wisdom stays its hand. It understands that this cathedral of bone, glass, and velvet is not a prison waiting to be broken open; it is a miracle of stubbornness, balanced on a blade’s edge. Every drop of water in it is already spoken for. Every degree of heat is a syllable in the only language the bowl knows how to speak.

So the power does nothing to the air. It leaves the soot-lid closed. It leaves the vents their bruised, angry ember, and the hunters their silent patience, and the prey their lacquered eggs buried deep in the grit.

Instead, it reaches far below the floor—deeper than the fissures, deeper than the cinder-braids, down into the ancient, groaning roots of the black basalt massif where the magma shifts and the mountain slowly settles under its own weight.

There, a fault is beginning to pinch. Left alone, in another century or two, the deep throat of the hollow would have narrowed, choked off by shifting stone; the twenty-minute pulse would have lengthened to thirty, then an hour, then gone cold, leaving the bowl to freeze into a tomb of brittle glass and dead wax.

With a touch no heavier than a finger steadying a tilted bowl, the power eases the pressure in the rock. It does not widen the vents—to give more heat would be as fatal as giving less—it only props the deep artery open, anchoring the stone so the pulse will keep its twenty-minute rhythm for ten thousand years to come.

Then, it rises back into the quiet dark of the eastern wall, between one surge and the next.

The floor is dim. The sand-swimmers are pouring silently beneath the grit. Above, the ash-gliders drift in slow, blind circles. A Dry-Keeper sits wedged inside the sunken hearth-ring, its chin tucked into its collarbone, its mind—if it can still be called a mind—narrowed to a slow, patient wait for warmth.

The power does not wake it. It does not touch its hide or speak into its head; there are no words left in the Keeper’s skull that wouldn’t feel like an invasion.

Instead, it bends to the flat threshold-slab beside the hearth-ring, where generations of blunt teeth and horn-hard nails have worried a row of faint, rhythmic notches into the basalt.

With the utmost care—making no sound, raising no dust, altering the temperature of the stone by not even a fraction of a degree—the power lays a single new notch beside the last one. Equal in depth. Equal in spacing. Carved as if by a broad, calloused thumb that knew how to sit in the dark and wait out the pulse.

Then it withdraws, leaving the hollow to its baking, breathless hum.

Twenty minutes later, the vents brighten. The swimmers erupt from the sand; the hangers peel from the walls; a Keeper unfolds from the hearth-ring with slow, liquid certainty, catches a low-banking glider in its wide palms, drinks it dry, and carries the empty shell back to mend the cairn.

As it settles back into the eastern dark and tucks its limbs against its chest, one long, waxy finger drifts across the threshold-slab—an old habit, an itch in the bone.

The smooth pad of its finger finds the last notch it remembers making. And then, a hair’s breadth further on, it finds one more.

The Keeper holds still. It has no name for a neighbor, no concept of an outside, no memory of why the boundary was ever drawn. It does not wonder, and it does not pray.

It only rests its finger in the new groove for a single, three-minute breath—quiet, satisfied, and a little less alone in the dark—before letting the husk-mites settle back into its joints, and becoming stone again.
