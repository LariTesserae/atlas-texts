If a power of great wisdom and mercy were to turn its gaze upon the hollow, the first thing it would do is nothing at all. 

It would not speak. Even a gentle voice, rolled down from the thin blue sky, would wake the brittle shelves of needle-ice along the upper ledges and bury three tiers of workers under a sudden, wet tide of slush. So the power would arrive as the mountain shadow does—without sound, without weight, simply *being* there in the blinding glare, watching.

At first, benevolence alone might urge it to act quickly. Looking down into the hundred-pace bowl, a merely kind observer would see only hardship: backs permanently bent beneath low hide eaves, noses grooved to the bone by forty years of sinew-strapped goggles, bare knuckles crusted with sulfur salt, and bodies laid out on the freezing rim to be picked clean by the wind. 

The impulse to rescue would be strong. *I could melt the rime,* the power might think. *I could quiet the gale beyond the saddle, warm the limestone, and raise stout stone houses with mortar that holds. I could give them iron tools that do not snap, and green valleys where no one goes milky-eyed from the noon sun.*

Then wisdom would look closer, and stay its hand.

If it melted the rime and warmed the bowl, the weeping brine would stagnate. Without the flash-freeze of needle-ice, the flat-shelled crawlers would lose their crystal domes and die off; without the crawlers, the mute, oily-feathered birds would starve, and with them the people who sleep pressed against their furnace-warmth. If it gave them mortar, the freeze-thaw of the high saddle would crack it to dust in a week. If it gave them forged iron, the sulfur-brine would eat it into red rust, and the first ring of a hammer against a blade would bring the retaining walls down on their heads.

And if it lifted them out of the bowl entirely, setting them down in some fat, sunlit lowland? Wisdom would see what awaits them there: fevers their salt-cured blood has never known, wide spaces where a neighbor’s wall can be locked against you, and a terrifying, cavernous quiet where no one presses their forehead to yours to hum a grief into your bones. In the valley, people own their roofs, and so they learn to fear losing them. Here, a household owns only a bundle of horn ribs and reed mats, and when a fissure weeps too warmly, neighbors simply shift their hides over to make room.

The power would watch a slush-slide shear off the upper terrace—watch the white burrowers bolt upward from the chinks, watch the workers step calmly onto the catwalks without a wasted motion, and drop to their knees with wooden shovels the moment the wet mass settles. It would watch two figures crouched in the lee of a slate wall, skulls touching beneath heavy hoods, sharing a silent, bone-deep vibration while the birds waddle past their boots.

It would recognize, with a quiet awe, that this place is not a prison. It is a clockwork of mercy, built out of necessity and salt. Everything here feeds everything else, even the dead, whose clean white ribs come back down from the rim to hold up the roofs of the living.

So the power would refuse the grand miracle. It would leave the glare sharp, the air thin, and the silence intact.

Instead, it would reach down past the slush, past the unmortared slate, past the hairline fissures—deep into the dark, crushing granite roots of the mountain, where no eye ever looks and no antler lever ever reaches.

There, in the blind stone beneath the saddle, it would lay a quiet, steadying hand.

It would feel the slow, subterranean pulse of heat that warms the brine—the fragile hearth-fire that keeps the hollow from becoming as dead as the howling desert beyond the rim. In the natural course of the mountain, that vein of warmth would cool, or shift, or choke itself off with mineral scale within a handful of generations, leaving the bowl to freeze solid and silent forever. 

The power would clear the deep pinch in the rock. It would balance the pressure of the mountain’s weight so the limestone shelf would not shear away, and it would steady that faint, stubborn warmth—not making it hotter, which would drown the terraces in mud, nor letting it fade, which would turn the people to ice. Just *steady*. A promise kept in the dark, a mile beneath their flat-soled boots.

And perhaps, before withdrawing, it might allow itself one small, invisible grace at the surface—nothing that would break the rhythm of the bowl or breed the strange poison of awe. Perhaps, when a worker’s foot slips on the slick slate above a drop, a white burrower happens to dart across the ledge a half-second sooner. Perhaps the black lichen on the upper rim grows just a fraction softer where the families lay their dead.

Then the power would step back into the thin blue air, leaving no footprint in the slush, and let the silent people go on tending their world.
