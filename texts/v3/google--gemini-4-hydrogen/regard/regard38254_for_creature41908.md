First, the power did what any wise thing does before it touches a world: it learned how to listen without bringing its own weather.

It had seen other worlds—wet, heavy worlds of green rot and warm blood, where love meant rain, and mercy meant softening what was hard. Had it arrived here with those habits intact, a single careless exhalation of its pity would have condensed into dew on the parchment-thin spires. One tear would have turned a thirty-story seed-pod to gray paste.

So it pared itself down. It shed its moisture, its warmth, its fondness for silence. It stepped onto the planed-off crown of the mountain range in the only form that would not bruise the air: as an attention as dry and sharp as ozone, as patient as chalk.

For a long time, it simply stood—or rather, *hung*—in the bruised slate-amber twilight, letting the plateau’s deafening choir pass through it.

It heard the vast, hollow earth groaning beneath the felt-like crust, a bass note older than the cities. It heard the brassy, shifting hum of the eggshell towers vibrating to keep from buckling. It heard the *scritch-thump, scritch-thump* of the unseen borers miles down in the dead strata, the machine-gun rattle of the clackers riding the updrafts, the crisp *snap-hiss* of blue triboelectric sparks leaping from the hooves of nervous static-grazers.

And then it noticed the people.

At first, they were nearly invisible against the pale pewter and sun-bleached wasp-paper of their own walls. Tall, spindly architectures of fluted bark-chitin and braided sinew, stepping lightly into roaring columns of blue static, anchoring themselves with hooked toes to sagging transit-ribbons, pressing notched forearms together to trade woody, shivering sentences in the teeth of the gale.

The power watched an old Tuner—shell darkened to burnt cedar—crouch beside a matte-black knot at the base of a spire, forked ash stave pressed to the trembling lignin, listening with a faceless, bone-smooth head. It watched a Listener, heavy with mineral ash, lie flat out on the undulating plain miles from any tower, reading the drumbeat of the borers through hollow ribs.

And the power understood, with a fierce, quiet ache of admiration, what this place was.

It was not a ruin. It was not a desert waiting to be healed. It was a violin strung to breaking pitch, played continuously by the people who lived inside its soundbox. Every click of a wrist, every ballast stone slung at a hip, every spore-squall drunk through pleated chest-slats was an act of exquisite, precarious fidelity. They did not need to be made heavy, or wet, or safe in the way that flesh is safe. To take away the wind would be to mute their ground-pipes. To take away the tension would be to collapse their towers.

So the power asked itself the hardest question wisdom can ask: *What, then, is mine to do?*

It looked down—three miles down, through the bruised amber haze, into the drowned, forgotten world at the foot of the mountain range.

There, far below the plateau’s rim, something vast was turning. A warm, wet cyclonic shelf, born in the heavy oceans of the lower atmosphere, was climbing the ancient cliff-faces. Driven by a rare alignment of deep-air currents, it was rising higher than the haze usually allowed—a dark, billowing wall of literal rain, thick with salt and rot, creeping toward the three-mile threshold.

The domesticated clackers in their wicker cages had not smelled it yet; it was too alien, too heavy to register as a normal barometric drop. Even if the Tuners felt the air thicken, they had no tools for *water*. Their collector-spires, built to scream and drink dry silvery spores, would choke on the mist, short-circuit their stasis-threads, and dissolve like sugar in tea.

The power did not hesitate, and it did not announce itself.

It descended—not to the city, where its presence might warp the delicate geometry of the matte-black anchors, but to the ragged, unseen lip of the plateau three miles below the spires. There, in the dim amber gloom, it went to work like a careful gardener turning a trench.

It did not fight the storm with fire or lightning; fire would consume the oxygen, and lightning would rattle the plateau’s tuning out of true. Instead, it gently *tilted* the thermal shelf of the upper haze. It gathered the stray static of the lower cliffs and wove it into a wide, invisible updraft of dry warmth—a mere three degrees of difference, spread across forty miles of cliff-face.

The rising wall of rain met that dry, warm slope and found no purchase. Slowly, heavily, the clouds bowed outward, sliding laterally along the mountain’s flank, spilling their lethal weight back down into the dark, wet valleys where it belonged. Only the faintest shadow of a barometric dip reached the plateau above—just enough to make the caged clackers rattle a brief, puzzled warning, and the floor to exhale a thin, harmless mist of silvery spores.

The city woke, drank the spores, and never knew how close the silence had come.

Yet as the power rose back through the haze toward the plateau, it lingered a moment longer. Benevolence is practical, but wisdom also knows when a thing deserves to be *met*.

It walked—weightless, leaving no graphite smudge—among the widely spaced districts. It watched a young citizen, careless without their hip-ballast, nearly lifted off a sagging transit-ribbon by a sudden crosswind; the power did not catch them with a visible hand, which would have terrified them, but simply *thinned* the gust for half a second around their pleated ribs, letting their hooked toes find the woven plant-chitin on their own.

It drifted down into the hollow, pressurized nitrogen pipes beneath the felt, where the dead lower strata were wearing thin under the teeth of the borers. There, quietly, it sifted a century’s worth of trace silica and fine copper dust into the deepest chambers—not enough to starve the borers, who were part of the world’s drumbeat, but enough to keep the ancient mat from hollowing out before its time.

And finally, it stopped beside a group of Listeners lying chest-down on the open plain between districts, and an old burnt-cedar Tuner resting their forked stave against a humming spire.

It could not speak to them in words; words thrown into this air were shredded by the wind, and words spoken into their minds would have been an invasion. Instead, the power leaned down and placed the lightest possible pressure on the living crust of the plateau—right over a wide nexus of hollow vascular pipes.

It added a single note.

Nothing loud. Nothing that threatened the pitch of the eggshell towers or rattled the orbiting dust motes around the black anchors. Just a deep, warm, bone-dry *fifth* woven softly beneath the plateau’s own groaning bass—steady, resonant, and unmistakably intentional, traveling through the nitrogen pipes like a hand laid gently on the back of a cello while someone else plays it.

Out on the plain, the Listeners stiffened, pressing their narrow, lens-studded heads harder against the felt. At the base of the tower, the burnt-cedar Tuner paused, their wrist raised over their notched collarbone, feeling the strange, true harmony rise through the soles of their hollow feet.

It was not a command. It was not a promise of rescue, or an offer of an easier world. In the dry, percussive language of the plateau, it meant only one thing:

*I hear you. Keep playing.*

Then the power lifted its hand, stepped back into the dim amber sky, and left the wind to them.
