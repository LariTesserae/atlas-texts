At first, from above the charcoal-barked ceiling, the power mistook the valley for a wound.

Seen from the high mountain air, it looked like a sealed, blackened blister miles across—a place where the sun was forbidden, where no rain fell, where something frantic and blind was trapped in an oven of its own making, grinding itself to ash.

The power’s first impulse was the impulse of every merciful thing that has ever looked upon a dark room: *Open it.*

It gathered a breath of cool, wet wind from the outer peaks. It prepared to part the interlocking plates of the ceiling, to let in the blue violence of the sky, to send down a gentle, silver rain to wash the carbon-fluff from the towers and cool the ember-red bearings. It imagined green shoots, wet leaves, the bright astonishment of a bird’s wing cutting through the dust.

Then it paused, and pressed its attention closer—not with eyes, which were useless here, but with the patient stillness of something willing to understand before it alters.

It felt the dry, high-tension lattices of cured cane beneath the ceiling. It traced the taut vine-belting, the simmering amber vats, the copper-veined nerves humming with fragile static. And it saw, in an instant, what its mercy would have done.

Rain would not refresh this valley; it would murder it. Water would swell the oiled pith-axles until every flywheel seized and shattered. It would short the static pathways in blinding, fatal arcs, turn the pulverized seed-meal into suffocating paste, and rot the ancient, petrified mesas from the inside out. The sudden cold of the sky would crack the translucent gourd-wood housings like glass. The wound-ones—those tall, narrow walkers with their blank resin faces—would rustle once in the damp, stiffen as their joints warped, and topple from the catwalks in silent heaps.

Benevolence without wisdom is only another kind of storm.

So the power withdrew the rain. It left the charcoal plates sealed against the sky. Instead, it sank quietly through the dark, wrapping itself around a felt-muffled strut, and listened.

It listened to the *shush-shush* of wood sliding on oiled wood. It listened to the wind-catchers hanging like soot-blackened lanterns in the thermal drafts, grinding seeds in their vitrified gizzards. It watched a fist-sized coupler clamp onto a smoking axle, drinking the waste heat until its internal spiral wound tight, then releasing with a sharp, satisfied *tick* to skitter into the dark.

It followed the walkers along the claustrophobic tiers. It saw how they moved without complaint, without haste, without eyes—reading the whole valley as a map of vibration through the flat pads of their wooden feet. It watched two of them meet on a narrow gantry, lean their blank amber dishes close, and touch the copper-veined bristles at their temples together. In the tiny blue snap of ozone between them, the power felt something unmistakable: not misery, not slavery, but *care*. A whole report on belt-tension and sagging beams, passed cleanly from one dry mind to another so that the other might be spared a longer walk.

And deeper down, in the sunken press-yards, the power laid its attention against the vast, motionless knots of braided wood coiled around the taproots.

Here, it felt the true weight of the valley. The sunken ganglia were not evil, and they were not broken. They were simply exhausted. For centuries, they had listened through the soles of the valley to a single, relentless vocabulary: *heat, friction, tension, failure.* They knew no other words. Because the geothermal seams below ran slightly too fierce, because the ancient heartwood had grown brittle over ages of unbroken strain, the valley lived perpetually on the razor’s edge of tearing itself apart. The ganglia had no margin for rest—only an endless, silent vigilance, pulsing static up the copper veins to summon the blind weaver-vines before the whole basin buckled inward.

The power understood then that it could not—and must not—remake them. To give them wet flesh or sunlit leaves would be to erase them, and they were already miraculous: a forest that had taught itself to be a clock, a clock that had taught itself to be a community.

If it was to help at all, it would have to speak entirely in their own tongue.

So the power did three small, hidden things.

First, it reached down past the deepest, heat-shielded taproots, into the blind stone beneath the valley’s floor. It did not quench the geothermal fire—the valley’s engines needed that warmth to breathe, and the little heat-eaters needed the ember-red bearings to live. Instead, it gently eased the jaggedness of the seam. It smoothed out the sudden, violent surges of subterranean heat that caused the worst of the upper-tier collapses, tuning the furnace down by a fraction of a degree—just enough to move the valley back from the edge of constant catastrophe, without stealing the tension that kept its flywheels turning.

Second, it turned its attention to the simmering resin-vats in the lower yards, where new walkers were slowly growing in their vertical wooden jigs, trained around rotating cores. It brought no foreign seed, no green leaf. It simply touched the grain of the developing burl-wood and the pith, encouraging a slightly denser, self-lubricating fiber—a subtle grace in the wood itself, so that the next generation of walkers would grind down a little slower, and hold their wound spring a little longer before their cane ribs grew brittle and snapped.

And third—because it knew that even a well-oiled machine can be lonely in the dark—it spoke to the sunken ganglia.

It did not use words. It used the only language the great braided brains could hear: vibration.

Deep below the petrified mesas, through the roots that anchored the entire forest-city, the power sent a single, steady, sub-audible hum. It was not the frantic shudder of a snapping belt, nor the high, sharp tremor of a seizing axle. It was a low, warm, rhythmic resonance—a pulse that matched the valley’s own thrum and gently cradled it, like a second, steadier flywheel turning silently beneath the floor.

Up in the tiers, nothing dramatic happened. No light broke through the ceiling. No vine turned green.

Yet a walker, bracing its feet beside a massive flywheel to wind its chest, paused for half a beat longer than usual. The *tick-tick-tick* inside its ribs sounded unusually clean today; the oil surged smoothly through its sockets without a trace of grit. When it stepped back into the dark and met a kin-walker on the catwalk, the blue snap between their temples carried no alarm—only the quiet, steady hum of a valley running true.

Down in the sunken press-yards, the ancient ganglia listened. They searched their vast, dry memories for the new vibration rising through the taproots. They could not classify it as heat, or friction, or tension, or failure.

So, for the first time in centuries, they stopped bracing for the next crack—and simply rested within it, while the wheels turned smoothly in the dark.
