If a power both benevolent and wise turned its attention toward this place, the first thing it would do is **hold still**.

It would not arrive in a blaze of light, because light here is a foreign violence; a single day of unobstructed sun would blister the waxy canopy, turn the wet-slate bark to kindling, and blind the pale amphibians whose entire language is built from faint blue-green sparks in the dusk. It would not speak from the sky, because the air here eats voices at twenty paces, and any sound loud enough to cross a chasm would shatter the fragile membranes of the gliders and deafen a people who listen through the soles of their feet.

So it would arrive the way mist arrives: without announcement, taking the shape of the hollow.

For a long time—long enough for three Exhalations to rise and settle, long enough for a newly wedged flint adze to soften at its edges and put forth its first pale tendrils—it would only watch.

It would see, with the sharp clarity of something that has watched many worlds, every place where a lesser mercy would reach out to mend:

It would see the darkness, and feel the impulse to part the leaves.
It would see the shifting, untrustworthy geometry—the triangle of bridges that refuses to close on the same pillar twice—and feel the impulse to pin space down, to give them a true north and a flat horizon so their maps would stop lying.
It would see the tenders pressing their foreheads together to hum, or plucking a single taut cord to send a message half a mile through living phloem, and feel the impulse to thin the air so they might sing to one another across the chasms.
It would see the bone pegs driven into weak seams, the elders wedged into dark crooks until their ribs and the branches become one seamless knot, and feel the impulse to give them stone that does not sprout, iron that does not rot, and graves where the dead may simply rest without having to hold up the sky.

And most of all, it would watch a drifter slip.

It would watch a tender lose their footing on a slick curve, tumble past the edge, and *not* fall—only spin slowly outward into the bruised-green void, kicking at nothing, while the pillar drifts from fifty paces to a hundred, and the warm eddy carries them out into the empty miles to die slowly of thirst in a world made of water.

At that sight, any power that was merely kind would break the world to save them. It would put a floor beneath the abyss, or weave a net of wind to catch every falling body, or give the tenders wings.

Wisdom is what stops the hand halfway.

For the power would trace each mercy forward through ten thousand years of bark and sap, and see what it would cost:

If it fixed the geometry so that distances held still, the great woven continent—which has no bedrock, no stone, nothing beneath it but its own tension—would no longer be able to flex during the Exhalation. The ancient lashings would snap under the weight of rigid math, and the arches would tear themselves apart.

If it gave them a floor, the void would cease to be a void. The gliders would have no thermals to ride; the tenders would have no reason for their broad, tacky feet, or the loose cord tied around an ankle before sleep, or the quiet, lifelong habit of keeping one hand always brushing the nearest cable—or the nearest kin.

If it gave them iron and un-rotting stone, they would stop leaving their tools in the wounds they carved. They would begin to *own* things rather than lend them to the wood. And if it gave them loud voices that carried across the miles, they would stop kneeling to press their skulls together in the dark, and stop carrying the little moss-lined gourds at their hips to borrow another creature’s light.

Even the Rooted—those terrifying, patient elders who gave their arms to close a seam forever—are not prisoners of a cruel world. They are the reason the world has not come undone. To unbind them would be to tell ten thousand years of ancestors that their vigil was a mistake.

So the benevolent and wise power would do **almost nothing**.

It would leave the dusk dim, the air heavy, the maps broken, and the dead in the seams. It would not steal from the tenders the dignity of a world they built with their own hands, knot by knot, out of nothing but patience and vine.

Yet *almost* nothing is not nothing.

Perhaps, when the next Exhalation rises—when the warm fog wells up from the bottomless hollow and blinds the world at arm’s length, when every tender sits flat upon the slick timber with palms, heels, and foreheads pressed to the bark—the power would kneel there too, unseen.

It would not alter the tremor. It would not tighten a single lashing. It would simply lay its own vast, quiet attention against the wood, and listen to the sub-audible hum of the Rooted rising through the sap. And in that hour of absolute stillness, it would *answer*—not in words, not in light, only in a faint, steady warmth in the cambium, the way an old bridge feels when someone else is holding the far end.

And out in the dim, empty miles between the pillars, where a drifter spins slowly in the amber haze, beyond the reach of any rope or glider—perhaps the power would not break the eddy, and would not turn the wind back to the wood. That is the law of the height, and without the law, the height is nothing.

Only, as the long thirst takes them, it would not let them vanish unseen. It would stay beside them in the mist, keeping the watch that no tender on the ribs can keep, until the last breath leaves them—and perhaps, in some quiet way that leaves no mark on the sky and breaks no rule of the wood, it would gather what was lost and carry it home, not to the timber, but to a place where there is no falling, and no drifting, and no need to hold the world together anymore.

Then the fog would condense on the waxy leaves. The silent threads of water would begin to spill into the waiting gourds. Across the chasm, a tiny blue-green spark would flare, and another would answer it.

And the tenders would stand up, brush the lichen-dust from their shins, and go back to their knots—never knowing they had been looked upon, and loved well enough to be left whole.
