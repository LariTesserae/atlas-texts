A power that is only benevolent looks at the basin and sees a wound.

It sees a sky bleached of mercy, air too thin to carry a cry, water rationed by the drop. It sees creatures born mute inside a cold machine, and tall, faceless things scraping stray shadows off catwalks with the patience of prisoners who have forgotten they are serving a sentence. A merely kind power would reach down with warm hands. It would mend the fractured seams of the air so that footsteps arrived when heels struck plate. It would coax grass through the aluminum decking, melt the frost from the four-winged birds, unloop the staircases, and let the conveyor belts meet solid brick the way honest matter ought to. It would give the chalk-white keepers voices, and rest, and something softer to drink than the light-tricks of a grey fluid that isn't there.

And in doing so, it would kill everything in the valley.

Because this power is also wise, it does not reach down. It slows itself instead, matching its attention to the pace of the city-block flywheel, waiting long enough for the chalk mark on the rim to shift a hand’s breadth.

In that stillness, it begins to read the basin properly.

It sees that the keepers are not trapped in the four-inch clearances; they are *shaped* for them, down to the extra joint in the wrist and the chamfer on the shoulder-plate. It watches one kneel beside the cluster of glass pipes and drink the luminous grey sheen from dry metal, and understands that the basin’s broken geometry is not a malfunction—it is a spring. Repair the angles, and the keepers starve. Warm the air, and the silver-wire moss rots on the rivets; the unweathered steel begins to weep rust; the static prickle dies, and the dark lichens in the bolt-holes wither.

It leans close to a drop of condensation hanging motionless at eye level, and sees the copper-bright specks darting between the teeth of phantom brass wheels. It understands that their entire world exists inside a three-minute hesitation of gravity. To hurry the drop, or to keep it suspended forever, would be equally fatal to whatever lives inside that trembling bead.

Even the silence, the power realizes, is not an absence. It is the acoustic signature of a place where nothing is wasted on friction.

So the power withholds its hand from the machinery. It does not straighten a single beam, nor seal a single puddle against the inverted sky beneath.

Instead, if it does anything at all, it enters the basin on the basin’s own terms.

It arrives without heat, so no condenser sweats out of turn. It arrives without weight, so no catwalk groans, and without a shadow, so no keeper has to spend an afternoon scraping a loose darkness off the grating overhead. It folds its own vastness down to fit the tight clearances, slipping sideways between a transformer bank and a copper manifold without brushing the waxy lichens from the mortar.

It stands still on a third-tier maintenance terrace, in the blinding silver glare, and waits.

After a long while—measured not in seconds, but in the slow, dry *tick* of cooling metal—one of the chalk-white keepers emerges from behind a vertical switch. It does not startle. It moves with the glacial, frictionless patience of the deep pistons, folding its arms flat as it navigates the narrow aisle between the busbars. A cluster of fiberglass-furred climbers hangs calmly from its forearm, undisturbed.

The keeper stops three yards away. It tilts its narrow, smoked-glass brow slightly downward, studying the place where the power stands.

It cannot see a face, because the power has worn none that would offend the valley’s geometry. It only senses a stillness that was not there an hour ago, occupying space between the handrail and the ducting.

With the quiet, deliberate grace of something built inside a watch, the keeper reaches into the joint of its wrist, draws out a stick of dry white chalk, and steps close. It waits a patient minute—long enough for a bead of condensation to tremble, long enough for the sound of a distant drop to *plink* against a dry tray. Then it reaches out with two flat fingers and draws a single, crisp hash mark across the air where the power stands.

It is the only question the keeper knows how to ask: *Are you part of the machinery, or are you passing through?*

The power does not speak. Speech would arrive half a second before its intention, or bounce endlessly off the porcelain insulators. Instead, it honors the mark. It remains entirely still for the full, unhurried hour it takes the tree-trunk pistons to ease down into their sleeves—giving the keeper the courtesy of its own measure of time.

When the flywheel’s rim has shifted a hand’s breadth, and the keeper’s smoked-glass brow turns back toward the terrace to check its work, the power shifts cleanly to the left—just far enough that the chalk mark sits clearly apart from the rail, hanging steady in the thin, sharp air.

*Passing through,* the movement says. *And I see that the watch is keeping good time.*

The keeper watches the displaced mark for a moment. Then it tucks its chalk back into its wrist, folds its limbs flat, and slips sideways into the four-inch gap beside the transformer bank, returning to the slow, quiet work of tending the seams.

The power withdraws as silently as it came, leaving every paradox intact, every shadow neatly rolled in the deep shafts, and a single, crisp line of white chalk floating three feet above the decking—the only proof, in a valley that never weathers, that something passed by and found nothing in need of mending.
