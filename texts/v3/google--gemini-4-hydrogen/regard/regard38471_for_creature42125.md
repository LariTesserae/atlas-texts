If a power both benevolent and wise turns its attention to the basin, the first thing it does is slow down.

It does not arrive in a sudden parting of the mist, nor in a shaft of clean daylight breaking through the wired glass. To enter like that would be to strike the place like a hammer. Instead, it matches its own patience to the thirty-second sink of the piston, the groan of the sluice, the chest-rattling *thoom* of the cistern. It waits through one stroke, and a second, and a third, until it can hear what the basin is saying.

At first, a lesser kindness might feel pity.

From the outside, it is easy to mistake the pit for a place that has been forgotten or condemned. There is no sky here, only a bruised twilight pressing against sagging iron. The air is thick with boiled grease and wet stone. The beings that move along the catwalks have no eyes to see the amber halos, no tongues to speak their own names, no rest from the slow, heavy work of keeping the water in its channels. A merely generous power might want to tear off the blackened roof and let the sun pour in. It might want to silence the engines, wash the mineral crust from the root-limbs, and lift the tenders out onto dry, open grass.

Wisdom looks longer, and stays its hand.

It sees that sunlight would scorch the translucent fungi on the dynamos and dry the living gaskets until the copper pipes cracked. It sees that the silence of an open field would be, to these creatures, a terrible emptiness—the stopping of the only heart they have ever shared. They are not prisoners of the machinery; they are its memory and its skin. When a tender presses its fibrous flank against a weeping flange, it is not suffering under a curse. It is answering a call in the only language it knows, and finding in that answer a complete, unhurried sufficiency.

To "free" them would be to unmake them. To "heal" the rust and the moss would be to strip the basin of the very crust that holds it in balance.

So the wise power does almost nothing.

It does not touch the tenders. It does not brighten the dim bulbs in their wire cages, nor sweep the ferns back from the lower brickwork. It leaves the leaks to weep, because the weaving of new root-mats is the tenders' craft, and it leaves the gears to catch on stray fronds, because clearing them is how the tenders know the pulse of their world is true. It does not even whisper to them of its presence, for they have no ears for a voice that does not come through the iron grates underfoot.

Instead, it looks to the edges—the places the tenders cannot reach, and would not know to fear.

High above the highest catwalks, where the mist thins against the wired glass, the blackened trusses have borne the weight of rain and snow for centuries without a tender's hand to shore them up. There is no vibration there to call them upward, no hum of a transformer to guide their soles. Left alone, the roof will one day give way all at once, letting in the cold sky, the tearing wind, the birds and insects that would turn this clean, quiet rhythm into something frantic and brief.

The power lays no gleaming steel across the gaps. It simply steadies what is already there—lending the old, fatigued iron a quiet endurance, easing the strain at the rusted joints so the trusses keep holding back the sky, sagging and weathered, for another thousand years.

And far beneath the flooded sumps, deeper than the roots of the oldest resting tender have ever reached, it attends to the stone. It ensures the groundwater remains warm and mineral-rich, neither failing in a dry season nor surging in a flood that would drown the third tier. It keeps the boundary intact, so the closed loop of steam and condensation, of engine and moss, may continue at its own geological pace.

Before it withdraws, the power rests its attention on the grates for the span of a single stroke.

Down on the second-tier walkway, two tenders have met where the catwalk narrows between a brick pier and a sweating copper pipe. They lean together, their damp fungal mantles touching, trading mineral dew and a faint, stray charge from the dynamos. For thirty seconds, as the great piston sinks and the cistern answers with its deep, steady *thoom*, the vibration in the iron underfoot is perhaps a fraction warmer, a fraction clearer—nothing they could ever name, nothing that interrupts their stride, only the quiet certainty that the body of the world is sound.

Then the counterweight rises. The two tenders slide past one another like shifting walls of brick—one descending toward the sumps to drink, the other rising toward the boilers to dry its crown—and the power leaves them to the dark, the steam, and their peace.
