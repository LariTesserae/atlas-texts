If the power were merely good, it might have brought a sun.

It might have looked down through the unbroken, humid dark, felt the feverish weight of the air, and pitied the eyeless things creeping through the silt. A hasty kindness would have parted the darkness to let in a sky, sent a dry wind sweeping across the lagoons to clear the mist, fixed the distances so that a mile stayed a mile, and taught the water to lie flat and obey.

And in an afternoon, the city would have died. The travertine would have baked brittle and cracked; the porous dwellers would have dried into hollow chalk before the next tide could soften them; the velvet crust would have withered, and the tilted lagoons would have spilled their mineral load into nothingness.

Because the power is wise as well as benevolent, it does none of these things. Instead, it arrives the only way it can without breaking what it wishes to see: it arrives without light, without wind, and at the exact temperature of a living body.

It settles into the basin like condensation.

For a long time, it simply *learns*. It drifts through the ankle-deep brine, feeling the oil of dissolved stone. It waits through the dry-air intervals beside the colonnades, pressing nothing, listening to the slow, hydraulic pulse inside the pillars that are slightly too warm to be rock. When the silent tide oozes up through the plazas and the first muted *tock* sounds in the dark, it watches the brief, ash-grey flash of phosphorescence and understands that this is not a prison of stone—it is a wardrobe of shells, shed and re-grown in an unhurried rhythm.

It follows a dweller across a folded lagoon, feeling the humid air compress as the distance thins like tallow. It kneels in the silt beside the tilted sheets of water, admiring the patient geometry of their herding. It sits unseen on the uppermost tier of the solitary amphitheater, feeling the inward-traveling ripples converge upon the central pinhole, and reads in those faint pressures the whole quiet liturgy of the city: the care for a slumping mudbank, the memory of a sealed plaza, the gentle grief when an elder chooses a thinning arch, closes the slits in its skin, and begins the long, honorable stillness of becoming a buttress.

The power looks for suffering, and finds almost none. There is effort—the strain of cracking the fresh crust at each rising tide, the slow work of holding water against gravity—but it is the effort of being alive, and the dwellers carry it without complaint. To take away their effort would be to take away their city. To stop them from settling would be to deny them their rest.

Yet a wise eye sees what even the dwellers, in their long patience, might not notice: the slow arithmetic of a closed world.

Every tide locks a fraction of the basin’s warmth and richness into dead stone. Every settled elder holds a little of the oily mineral forever out of reach. Over millennia, the closed loop narrows. The stalks of pale gas would grow sparser; the black velvet crust would thin; the deep brine would cool by a degree, and then another, until one day the tide would rise too sluggishly to soften the shells, and the entire city would calcify in its sleep, unfinished.

So the power acts—so quietly that nothing in the basin is startled.

First, it tends the borders. Beyond the farthest mudbanks, where this pocket of impossible physics meets whatever cold, rigid universe lies outside, the power weaves a gentle, absolute boundary. No stray wind, no starlight, no sharp-edged law of fixed space will ever intrude here to flatten the lagoons or freeze the steam. The dark will remain intact, safe as a womb.

Second, it reaches beneath the silt—below the oldest sunken plazas, below the roots of the ancient ziggurats—and places a quiet generosity in the deep earth. Not a furnace, not a flood, only a steady, inexhaustible pulse of body-warmth and dissolved lime, calibrated to match what the city slowly spends. The gas-stalks will never starve for heat; the velvet crust will always find enough in the mist to drink; the tide will never lose the strength to loosen a sleeping limb.

And finally, because benevolence is not only maintenance—because it also wishes to say *I see you, and you are good*—the power waits for the next gathering in the amphitheater.

The dwellers sit along the submerged tiers, leaning forward to lay their long, pale fingers on the flat pool. They draw inward, reading one another in the dark.

From the far edge of the lagoon, where no one is standing, a single, slow ripple travels inward toward the center. It carries no command, no sudden brightness, no alien word. It is only a warm, unhurried wave of pressure—the exact weight of a friendly palm laid flat upon the water, lingering for a breath before vanishing cleanly into the pinhole at the center of the arena.

In the dark, a few of the dwellers tilt their smooth, pitted heads. One of them flexes a joint, fracturing its crust in a brief, silent spark of grey light.

Then the tide begins to recede, sinking noiselessly into the mud, and the power withdraws—leaving the city to its steaming quiet, its tilted waters, and its endless, unhurried work of growing itself out of its own bones.
