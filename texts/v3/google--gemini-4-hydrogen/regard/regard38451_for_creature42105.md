It would not come with trumpets, or with sunlight.

A power that understood what it was looking at would know, within the first three breaths of lukewarm mist, that sunlight here would be a kind of violence. It would blind the swiftlets packed into the upper fissures, scorch the pale liverworts clinging to the halogen cones, and turn the thirty-degree damp into a scalding fog. And silence would be worse. To hush the piston pumps and the slap of falling water—even for a minute—would bring every tender bolt upright in their hammock, heart hammering against their ribs, convinced a bearing had seized or a main line had ruptured in the dark.

So it would arrive the way everything else here arrives: quietly, through the bore-shaft, letting its eyes adjust to the amber bruises of the sodium-vapors and the wet sheen of the basalt.

First, it would simply stand in the thirty-pace halo of a lamp and watch a shift.

It would watch a tender crouch on a sagging catwalk, scraping gypsum rind off an intake screen with a short-handled chisel. It would notice the three-point balance on the shifting talus, the gloved hand brushing harvestmen off a valve wheel without crushing them, the way a lamp moved six feet to the left becomes a slow migration for a mat of dark-green algae. It would see that this place is not a hell, and these people are not thrashing in despair. They have made a treaty with a newly cracked world. They are part of its pulse, as necessary to the water’s mark as the pumps themselves.

Yet a wise power does not mistake endurance for wholeness, or romanticize a cage just because the prisoner has learned to keep house in it.

Looking closer, it would see the cost written into their bodies: the tiny, clean dolostone slices that never quite close in air this wet; the soft, wrinkled pallor of soles and palms that have forgotten what dry feels like; the mineral salt stiffening their leather gloves into permanent hooks. And it would notice the silence behind the noise—the question nobody asks anymore about whether the hoist at the top of the bore-shaft still works, or whether anyone above still remembers there are people down here keeping the line open.

So it would act. Gently, and without breaking the rhythm.

First, it would go back up the shaft—not to abandon the chamber, but to mend the tether. Whatever rusted winch, collapsed collar, or forgotten ledger had turned a rotation into a permanent exile would be quietly set right. Not with a blinding doorway to paradise, just a clear shaft, a sound ladder, a working hoist, and fresh air waiting at the top for anyone who wanted to remember the taste of wind.

Then it would come back down, and it would speak to them in the only language that travels cleanly through the rift.

A tender crouching three hundred meters from the nearest floodlight would feel it first through the soles of their boots: a sharp, bright double-tap on the iron dewatering pipe, cutting cleanly under the bass roar of the generators. Not *shut the intake*, not *clear the screen*, not *bring a spare gasket*. A new rhythm, patient and unmistakable, repeating once an hour until every person in the valley knew it wasn't a falling stone.

*The shaft is open. You are not forgotten. You may come up.*

And while they stood beside the pipes, helmets tilted, listening to the iron hum with a question they hadn't dared ask in years, the power would offer a few small, quiet mercies to the valley itself—nothing that would unmake it, only things that would take the sharpest edge off its youth.

Along the catwalk approaches, the pump-house ledges, and the talus paths where human hands and knees habitually brace, the raw dolostone would soften—just as a river might round it over ten thousand patient years—losing its razor bite without losing its grip. Inside the mesh-shrouded hammocks slung beneath the steel grating, the air would turn two degrees cooler and just dry enough, for six hours at a stretch, for wrinkled skin to breathe and slow-healing cuts to finally knit shut while they slept. The halogens would hold their filaments a little longer; the seals on the piston pumps would wear a little slower, giving the tenders back an hour of each shift that belonged to nothing at all except their own hands.

It would not force anyone up the ladder. Some people, given back the choice, would climb—and for them, the power would walk ahead up the bore-shaft, easing the shock of dry air and wide sky, because a world without the steady hum of water and iron can feel terrifyingly empty when you have slept inside it for years.

And for those who looked at the open shaft, nodded once, and turned back to grease a bearing or check a crayfish trap in the warm pools—the power would let them stay. It would not pity them, and it would not take their work away. It would simply stay nearby in the mist, keeping the path behind them open, and letting the newly cracked world go on humming in the dark—no longer a place that had forgotten them, only a place they had chosen to keep.
