What drew its attention was not a prayer. No one in the cleft ever shouted at the sky; sound bounced too sharply off wet slate, and they had long ago learned not to waste breath on stone.

It was the stillness that caught the power’s eye—a small, improbable pocket of silence in a loud, sprawling world. Passing high above the young range, where the wind sheared across unweathered ridges with a papery roar, it noticed a narrow slot where the air did not churn. It leaned closer, careful not to trouble the mist spilling over the rim, and looked down.

At first, it thought it was looking at an abandoned gauge—some relic of builders who had measured the mountain’s pulse and left. It saw the matte-dark vanes combing moisture from the fog, the hairline rails, the six-limbed crawlers creeping upside down beneath slender spans.

Then a door gave a muted chime, and a lean figure in charcoal weave stepped out to the edge of a balcony with a steaming mug.

For a long time, the power simply watched.

It watched the child at the *seam*, thrusting a bare hand three inches into the freezing air until the fine hairs on their knuckles frosted white, then snapping it back through the invisible boundary to watch the rime vanish. It watched two strangers pass on a narrow bridge, tilting their chins in silent greeting. It watched the *seam-watchers* in the warm, amber-lit rooms below, reading the hair-thin shifts of a ridge that was still young enough to be rising.

And because it was benevolent, its first impulse was to give.

It had the means to give effortlessly. With a single, quiet thought, it could have gentled the mountain—widened the cleft to let in an hour of morning light and an hour of evening gold; laid down a bed of rich loam along the ledges so birch and saxifrage could root; warmed the air between the bridges so no one’s knuckles ever wind-burned on the thirty-pace walk from door to door. It could have stilled the frost that pried at the basalt, and made the rock stop growing, so the settlement would never have to listen for a shift in the dark.

Its hand hovered, full of those easy mercies.

Then, because it was wise, it looked again—and stayed its hand.

It saw that if it warmed the air, the *seam* would disappear. There would be no threshold left to lean against with a mug of hot water, no edge of the hearth for children to test with their fingertips.

If it widened the cleft, the wind would find its way down into the amphitheater. The bridges would sway; the acoustic courtesy that made three tiers of people turn in shared, gentle surprise at a single shout would be drowned in weather. And in the amber-lit rooms, the slow, flawless optical crystals—which could only grow in absolute stillness—would cloud and fracture.

If it brought soil and greenery, it would break the closed, clean loop of basalt salt and lichen, turning a people who had made an art of sufficiency into caretakers of a garden they had never asked for.

And if it froze the mountain in place to spare them the watch, it would turn a living ridge into a monument, and rob the *seam-watchers* of their long, quiet conversation with the stone.

The power understood then that nothing here was broken. This was not endurance borne of misery; it was fit. A watchcase does not wish to be a meadow.

So it withdrew the gifts it had meant to offer, folding them back into itself with the same quiet care a resident uses when stepping over a maintenance crawler. Restraint, it knew, was the rarest and heaviest form of kindness.

Yet it did not leave empty-handed, nor quite without a trace.

As it drew back into the high, thin air above the timberline, it learned the settlement’s own courtesy. It gathered its vast presence tightly, so no pressure-wave would rattle the collector vanes or stir the rime-needles in the shade. It adjusted the angle of its passing so that not even the shadow of a thought would fall across the overhead slot at noon, leaving their ten minutes of fierce, bright sun untouched.

And down in the cleft, nothing changed. A water droplet fell from a dark vane and struck a metal gutter with a small, clear *tink*. A transit pod clicked softly into a landing. High above, an alpine swift circled once in the bright slit of sky, and the people on the bridges paused to watch it drink, content—and wholly undisturbed—inside their crack in the stone.
