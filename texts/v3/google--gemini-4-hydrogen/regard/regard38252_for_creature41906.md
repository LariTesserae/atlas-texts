If the power had been merely kind, it might have broken the ceiling.

It would have looked down through the drifting mist, seen a sealed concrete gut hovering just above freezing, seen creatures with no eyes pressing their wet bellies against steel silos just to borrow enough warmth to move, and felt an ache to rescue them. A merely kind power would have parted the blackened trusses to let in the sun. It would have softened the poured-basalt tiles into loam, husked the salt-crusts from those heavy shoulders, silenced the turbines, and taught the cold air to hold the scent of grass.

And in doing so, it would have killed everything in the valley.

The power was wise enough to wait before it touched anything. It lowered its attention into the dark not as a lantern, which blinds, but as a weight—settling down through the wet mist until it rested on the grating, feeling the *thrum* of the subterranean intake fans rise up through the iron.

It listened for a long time.

It felt the eastern sluice-gates groan upward and watched the dwellers lock their dark, horned feet into the floor grates, turning their blunt heads into the foaming brine. It saw them neither cowering nor enduring, only *fitting*—the way a key fits a warded lock, the way water fits a channel. When the tide drained and the exhaust fans pulled the mist clear, it watched the nimble, pale inner digits reach inside the motor housings to strip away the dead bacterial scale, leaving the vents bright so the motors could run hot and true. It heard the *tink-tink... tink* of a calcified knuckle against a catwalk, bouncing cleanly off the crisp, unweathered concrete, and the double tap of an answer half a mile away in the bruised-violet dark.

The power understood then that this was not a ruin, and its inhabitants were not prisoners. The basin was young, bare, loud, and cold, built with no thought of anything living inside it—and yet life had read the blueprint better than the builders had. It had turned a machine’s waste heat into a hearth, its leaks into armor, its flushing cycles into seasons.

To bring the sun here would be to bleach the glowing lichen to ash and boil the blind amphipods in their pipes. To silence the *thrum* would be to take away the dwellers’ eyes.

So the power stayed its hand from the ceiling, from the lamps, from the cold.

Instead, it looked at the machine the way the dwellers looked at the motor housings.

The dwellers could reach the intake screens and the condensation trays, the margins and the shallow sumps. They could keep a fan from rattling off-beat. But they could not reach the deep turbine shafts buried beneath the basalt floor, nor the unseen world above that had poured this basin and might, in some indifferent season to come, forget it—or turn the switches off.

The power reached into the places the dwellers could not go.

Far beneath the settling cisterns, where a main bearing had begun to pit and grind—a microscopic flaw that would have seized the eastern intake within a decade—the power eased the metal back to true, leaving no mark, only a return to silence within the hum. Along the upper headers, where a hairline fracture in the concrete was widening toward a collapse that would have buried two of the heat-exchangers, it drew the stone together grain by grain, keeping the seam crisp and wet as if it had been poured yesterday.

It touched the electrical weather, too—not to tame it, for the dwellers read the ozone with their barbels, and the leathery-winged things needed the warm pockets behind the baffles—but to steady a few aging relays whose porcelain had begun to hairline, so they would snap clean and sharp rather than arc wildly across a flooded grate.

And it did one thing more, small enough to escape the notice of anything that did not live by heat and salt.

Out in the wide, empty western flats, halfway between a mercury-vapor stanchion and the far retaining wall where no heat-exchanger stood and the floor stayed barren, a single overhead pipe-joint began to weep. Not a burst, and not enough to rattle the grates—just a slow, warm, mineral-heavy drip, rich with lime and trace iron, pattering steadily onto a broad basalt plinth just above the waterline of the twenty-minute flood. It was nothing the machine would miss, and nothing the pumps would register. Within a season, a thin, pale crust would begin to form on the stone, and the blue-white lichen would creep toward the warmth—a new station in the dark, waiting for whatever young dweller wandered far enough from the silos to claim a territory of its own.

Then the power withdrew its weight from the grating, leaving the basin as it had found it: cold, loud, dripping, and fiercely itself.

Below, the pressure in the upper headers shifted. A faint chatter ran down the vertical concrete chutes. Across the four-mile dark, heavy gray shapes peeled themselves off the warm steel of the silos and waded out toward the grates, turning their blunt, pitted faces to the east.

The two-tone klaxons groaned. The gates rose. The cold gray river roared across the tiles, washed over the salted backs, and drained away into the unseen cisterns.

When the mist cleared, a dweller hauled itself up onto a yellow safety bollard, shook the brine from its gill-rakers, and listened to the floor. The *thrum* was steady. The silos were humming.

It lifted one heavy, calcified knuckle and struck the iron twice.

*Tink-tink.*

Across half a mile of wet shadow, the basin echoed cleanly back, and somewhere in the dark, another answered.
