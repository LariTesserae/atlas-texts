The power came without footsteps, without wind, without even a shadow to dim the shafts of high-altitude sun.

For a long time, it simply knelt at the edge of the basin—or did whatever the equivalent of kneeling is for something vast enough to hold a mountain range in its palm. It looked down through the mirror-clear meltwater, past the jagged shelves of raw granite and pyrite, into the deafening, freezing room below.

Benevolence, at first, felt a familiar ache.

It saw a world of relentless, unpadded edges. It watched an arc-ribbon stiffen in the prickling voltage, take a blue-white bolt straight down its silver spine, and tumble backward in a fizz of hydrogen bubbles, stunned and faintly smoking. It watched a gasket-walker, its tail grown heavy with scraped quartz and calcium, finally lose its grip on a groaning rotor-hub and drift silently down into the waiting, toothed valves of a presser. It watched the blind salvager lumber across the talus on its three heavy legs, listening with its bone-white dish for the wet, fizzing sputter of a dying neighbor, ready to pry it open and haul its bones to the grinder.

*How cold,* benevolence thought. *How loud. How hard.*

A lesser kindness would have reached down at once. It would have warmed the water by a few merciful degrees, just enough to banish the frost-needles waiting for an exposed conduit-worm. It would have cushioned the sharp slate with soft moss, quieted the tooth-rattling *thrumm* that shook the granite walls, or gifted the salvager a pair of eyes so it might see the jewel-bright clarity of the sunbeams instead of feeling its way through a storm of noise.

Wisdom, however, stayed its hand. Wisdom looked longer.

It traced the circuit from top to bottom, and saw that nothing here was broken.

If it warmed the water, the delicate ion-gradients inside the capacitor-barrelers would falter; stray spores blown up from the valleys below would finally find purchase in the tepid bath, clouding the sharp shafts of light with green haze, choking the rotor-hubs faster than the gasket-walkers could scrape them clean. The lagging-slugs would have no reason to cling to the yellow cables, and the engine would rot from the inside out.

If it quieted the *boom* of the pressers and the *whir-groan* of the turbines, it would not be granting peace—it would be blinding the salvager. In a room this small and this dense, vibration *was* sight. Through those hundreds of trembling copper filaments, the tripod knew the health of every shelf, the tension of every silica tether, the exact second a barreller’s magnetite ribs began to crack. To silence the basin would be to plunge its caretaker into the dark.

And the gasket-walker’s fall, the arc-ribbon’s shock, the presser’s crushing jaw—none of it was cruelty. It was the grammar of the place. The garnet teeth returned to the slurry; the slurry fed the shells; the shock fed the ribbon; the dead battery built the next. To soften the stone would be to starve the gears.

The power understood, then, that this unbuilt engine did not need to be rescued from itself. It was already whole—a fierce, patient masterpiece of cold and copper, asking for nothing from the air-breathing world above except to be left alone.

So the power did not touch the water. It did not stir a single ripple that might confuse the salvager’s acoustic map, nor linger over the surface where it might steal a minute of sunlight from the barrelers’ slow, four-minute charge.

Instead, it looked deeper—past the talus, past the shadowed bottom, down into the blind, unfeeling bedrock of the young mountain itself.

Young ranges are restless. They shift and forget what they hold. Ten feet beneath the floor of the cathedral nave, where the granite was still raw from the last uplift, a hairline seam was beginning to part—a slow, silent fissure opening toward the dry dark of the peak’s interior. Already, a faint thread of meltwater was weeping downward through the stone, unnoticed by the creatures above. Left alone, the pool would drop an inch a month, then two, until the upper ledges stood exposed in the thin, freezing wind, their copper crowns dry, their arcs gone silent.

The power reached—not into the basin, not near the living circuit—but deep into the cold stone below. With a touch indistinguishable from the mountain’s own patient gravity, it coaxed the fractured granite back together, knitting the seam shut until the rock was solid and watertight once more.

Not a grain of pyrite scree shifted. The salvager, stepping carefully over a bundle of yellow conduit-worms, registered no tremor on its bone-white dish.

Having done all that needed doing, the power lingered at the rim for one full cycle—four minutes for a barreller to reach its threshold, thirty seconds for a rotor’s heavy turn, one patient minute for a presser’s jaws to draw apart and slam shut. It listened to the *crack*, the *whir-groan*, the *boom*, admiring the sheer, stubborn elegance of it all.

Then it withdrew, leaving the engine to its endless, freezing shift in the bright mountain sun.
