It came without wind, and without light.

A lesser power might have parted the mist with a warm radiance, or leaned over the sharp limestone rim like a lantern held above a well. This one knew better. A sudden warmth would have startled the mud-swallows from their cliffside cups; a bright beam would have driven the pale newts deeper into the flooded basements and frozen the herons into flight. So the power simply settled into the stillness that was already there, taking the shape of the cold, dense air, and looked.

At first, it saw what any compassionate eye would see: how hard the world was down here, and how small the margin.

It saw the builders—heavy-shouldered, mineral-streaked, their dense pelts matted with chalky silt—hauling soaked alder-boughs backward through slick mud with their teeth. It saw them working in the near-freezing dark of the plunge-pools, diving to dredge blue-gray clay against their chests when a peat dam hissed. It saw the kits in the high nurseries, blind and velvety, their first weeks filled with the damp chill of weeping stone and the endless *scritch-scritch* of their mothers’ teeth fighting back the living willow that tried to split their ceilings apart.

And in the first, simple movement of benevolence, the power considered what it could ease.

It could warm the groundwater by just a few degrees—enough to take the bite out of the canal water and keep the frost from creeping down the northern ledges at night. 

It paused, and traced the warmth forward. In its mind’s eye, the moss drank the gentler water and grew rank; the willow stakes, freed from the winter check, sent up shoots faster than orange incisors could shear them. Roots wedged into the packed-clay ramps and split the three-story lodges from within. The cold-water trout grew sluggish and gasped at the surface; the midges hatched in blinding, suffocating clouds from thatch that rotted too fast. The valley’s slow, patient pulse broke into a fever.

The power drew back its warmth before a single drop of water felt it.

It looked then at the sheer, unweathered cliffs hemmed tight around half a mile of mud. It saw how crowded the alleys were, how the builders had to flatten themselves against the retaining walls to pass one another, how every square foot was claimed and terraced and watched. It considered widening the basin—pushing the limestone back by a mile, giving them room to spread, to claim broad territories along open banks.

And again, it traced the gift forward. With room came distance; with distance came the old, fierce solitude of the river-dwellers. The quiet nose-touches at the causeway corners faded into territorial snaps. Without the pressure of the walls, there was no need to build three stories high, no need to bridge the waterways with living roots, no need to answer a neighbor’s leaking dam from three tiers away. The city unmade itself into a scatter of lonely burrows.

The power left the stone where it stood.

It looked into the dark, tangled root-skirts beneath the boardwalks, where the sleek mink moved like spilled oil, and up at the roof gardens where a water-vole froze, trembling, beside a stem of bog-myrtle. It felt the old, tender ache to quiet the hunger—to make a place where nothing was torn in the dark, and no trout hung waiting beneath a heron’s shadow.

But wisdom followed the thread down into the peat. Without the mink, the voles multiplied in the warm thatch until their burrows honeycombed the retaining walls; the first spring meltwater punched through the weakened peat, and the upper tiers collapsed into the lower canals, drowning the nurseries in a rush of chalky silt. The mink’s sharp teeth were part of the mortar. The heron’s patience was part of the stream’s measure.

The power stayed its hand.

Lastly, it looked at the builders themselves—at their endless, quiet labor, at the way they farmed their own roofs and mended their own walls without a single tool, communicating only in underwater clicks and soft thumps of their hind feet against the earth. It considered giving them a little more: a sharper awareness, a lighter burden, some small grace of ease.

Yet as it watched a matriarch surface from a flooded tunnel, shake the ice-water from her silver-gray undercoat, and press her broad, whiskered snout briefly against a younger builder’s cheek before they both turned to pack a seam with clay, the power saw no lack in them. Their bodies were the exact measure of the willow and the mud. Their patience was the exact measure of the sinkhole. To add anything to them would be to make them strangers to their own house.

So, inside the basin, the benevolent and wise power did nothing at all.

It did not touch the frost on the northern ledges. It did not hush the *drip-drip* of the weeping walls, nor smooth a single algae-slicked walkway, nor turn aside a single mink in the dark. It respected the valley’s completeness too much to mend what was not broken.

Only once did it act, and not down in the green-lit gloom.

It lifted its attention up to the sharp, windswept rim, where the moorland grass bent flat in the gale and the wide, careless world went by. There, along the lip of the quarry-like drop, it drew a quietness—not a wall, and not a spell that would alter the rain or the meltwater the basin needed, but a gentle, persistent turning-aside of what did not belong. A stray poison carried in the runoff from above would find its path diverted into deeper, barren gravel. A careless boot or heavy wheel approaching the rim would feel an inexplicable urge to veer left, toward easier ground, never knowing why it missed the drop.

Then the power let go even of the rim, leaving only that quiet watchfulness behind, and sank its attention back into the valley for one last moment before withdrawing.

Night had settled into the sinkhole. The mist had thickened until the weeping cliffs disappeared. High above, the mud-swallows were tucked into their clay cups. Beneath the causeways, the pale newts drifted through the dark tunnels with their coral-pink gills fluttering like tiny ferns. And inside the warm, green-roofed domes, hundreds of thick-furred builders lay curled nose-to-tail in the dry moss—their whiskers still twitching in their sleep, listening to the slow, steady pulse of the water they held back, entirely unaware that they had been seen, understood, and left in peace.
