At first, the power does not arrive in the basin at all. It only notices it from a great distance—a small, bright knot in the fabric of things where time has stopped pulling thread through the weave.

So it leans closer, not with thunder or a parting of the glare, for there is no sky here to part, and the thick, padded air would swallow any voice before it reached the tiles. It simply lets its attention settle over the valley like the first coolness that never quite comes, and waits for its eyes to adjust to the milk-white light.

It sees the razor-sharp corners of the terracotta bricks, untouched by water or age. It sees the scarlet petal lying bright and unrotted on the plaster. It sees the blind eels braiding in the runnels, the frogs’ throats pulsing out of time with their lungs, and the pale tenders kneeling beside the cisterns, their reflections in the mirror-flat water turned away, watching something behind them.

And at first, even wisdom feels the tug of pity—that oldest, sharpest temptation of the strong.

Look at them, the power thinks, watching the dimness of a reed-thatched shed. They have no fires in their kilns. They slice the clay, press it into wooden molds, smooth it with bone scrapers until it shines, stack it in neat rows—and then soak it back into mud to begin again. They have outlasted the world that needed their tiles, and no one ever came to tell them they could stop.

It would be so easy to be kind in the way that power usually is.

With a single, gentle breath, the power could bring a breeze across the flats to stir those shield-sized leaves. It could tilt the milk-white glare toward evening, giving them a cool blue dusk, a night with stars, a reason to lay down their bone scrapers and sleep. It could touch a spark to the cold kilns so their tiles would finally harden into something permanent, or introduce a mild, merciful rot to the garden cauldrons so the fallen petals might sink into soil and feed new roots. It could align their pulses with their breathing, and turn their reflections around in the water so they would no longer look like ghosts of another hour.

It could give them *tomorrow*.

The power holds all of these gifts ready, balanced lightly, the way a tender holds a gourd of warm water. Before it pours, however, it waits—because wisdom is mostly the patience to look twice before touching something you did not make.

It watches a tender walk along the narrow ridge of a mortar joint, balancing without a sound. It watches them kneel by a garden cauldron, part the heavy, dripping leaves, and lay a fallen scarlet petal back onto the emerald moss—not with the dull habit of a prisoner, nor with the frantic urgency of someone trying to hold back ruin, but with the quiet tenderness of someone tucking a sleeve back over a sleeping child’s arm.

It watches another hold a flat, damp palm beneath a stray vine until the dry spiral tightens and curls upward, away from the plaster. No knife is drawn. Nothing is forced.

Then the power looks back into the drying shed.

Four of them sit around the damp mound. One draws the reed cordage through the clay; another runs a thumb along the bevelled edge of a mold, leaving that crisp, familiar mark. When the stack grows tall enough, one rises, lifts the top tiles down into the pitch-sealed cauldron, and begins to knead them back into softness. Beside them, another lays a wet hand lightly on a companion’s shoulder to share the weight of a wooden frame.

The power looks at their faces—broad, still, unlined—and searches for grief, for weariness, for the quiet rot of despair.

There is none.

They are not waiting for the plaster to cure. They are not waiting for the afternoon to cool. If the power lit the kilns, the tiles would harden, the stacks would fill the sheds, and the clay mound would shrink to nothing; and what would hands made for clay do then, in a world with no clay left to touch? If it brought them night and decay, it would bring them loss—the long, slow ache of watching sharp edges blur and scarlet petals brown.

*They are not trapped,* the power understands at last. *They are home.*

The need for the tiles ended long ago, yes—but need was only ever the excuse. Underneath the need, there was always the love of the work itself: the cool slide of a bone scraper, the yield of wet clay under a thumb, the quiet company of another shoulder in the dimness. Something in this place loved its own making so deeply that, when the rest of the world hurried on into winter and ruin, this small valley simply held its breath and stayed.

And the mismatched reflections? The out-of-rhythm pulses? Those are not wounds, either. They are simply the seams where an eternal afternoon holds two moments gently in the same palm.

Slowly, the power folds back the breeze it had been about to breathe. It puts away the dusk, the spark for the kilns, the gift of tomorrow. To give them time would be to take away their eternity; to "wake" them would be to break a dream that is already gentler than waking.

Instead, it does two small things—so quiet that not even the trembling brim of a cistern ripples.

First, at the farthest, blinding edges of the basin, where the milk-white glare meets the vast, hurrying cosmos outside, the power draws a soft, invisible boundary. It seals the valley against the loud ages, against stray storms, against hungry winds and careless gods who might mistake stillness for brokenness and try to mend it. Whatever happens in the wider world, this warm hour will not be disturbed.

Second, before it withdraws, the power steps lightly onto the wet plaster behind one of the cisterns—where a stark-white wading bird stands knee-deep, and a tender is kneeling with empty hands resting on their thighs.

It makes no sound, and casts no shadow. Yet down in the mirror-flat water around the tender’s shins, the pale reflection—the one that has been turned away, watching something behind them—meets the power’s gaze.

For a long, unhurried moment, they look at one another across the glassy surface: the maker of worlds, and the reflection of a world that keeps on making itself for the sheer quiet joy of it. There is no speech between them. None is needed. The power simply inclines its head, offering the one gift a self-contained perfection can receive without breaking: to be seen, and understood, and left in peace.

Then it steps back out of the glare.

On the plaster floor, a heavy bead of water detaches from a broad leaf and drops into a clay basin with a single, muted *tock*. In the shed, a thumb slides once more along the bevelled edge of a wet tile. And the afternoon goes on, warm and whole, forever.
