The power did not arrive with thunder, because thunder would have shaken the chimneys. It did not arrive in a pillar of shade, because shadow would have cooled the rim unevenly and bent the draft.

It came simply as an attention, settling at the edge of the marl shelf twenty paces out, where there was nothing to see.

From there, it listened. It felt the flat, unvarying shear of the air sliding across the plain, and the sudden, dense wall of sound rising from the sunken pocket—a chord so thick and steady it seemed less like a noise than a piece of architecture. It watched the high, white sky pour its glare into the hollow, and it waited until its own gaze, sharper than any human eye, could separate bone-grey from chalk-yellow, and clay wall from living membrane.

For a long while, it simply took the measure of the place.

It saw the ghosts of thumb-presses in the dried paste, left by hands that had shaped these vessels and vanished long before the grit wore the rims smooth. It saw the brittle lichens clinging to the hairline seams, taking decades to claim a fingernail’s width of chalk. And deep inside the throats of the amphorae and the hexagonal cells of the honeycomb, it saw the pale, chambered beings—blind, earless, dry as wasps’ nests, trembling in their narrow sleeves.

A younger kindness might have wept for them.

The power felt, for a moment, the familiar pull of its own mercy—the urge to soften what was harsh, to widen what was cramped, to bring comfort to things that had never known it. It knew how easily it could summon a low cloud to break the blinding glare, or send a single, gentle rain to rinse the salt from the air. It could hush the deafening wind for an hour, just to grant them rest. It could loosen the fused clay so they might stretch their brittle flanks, or give them eyes to see the sky.

Then, with the patience that made it wise, it traced each mercy to its end.

A single drop of rain would turn the unglazed paste to mud, collapsing the chimneys and drowning the hollow lattices in wet silt. Shade would invite condensation, and condensation would rot the papery cartilage that had never been meant for water. To loosen the clay would be to shatter the creatures inside it, for they had grown over decades to fit those exact curves and could not stand alone.

And silence—silence would be the cruelest gift of all. Without the violent, unending tremor of the chord, the dust would settle in their chambers. Their dry sieves would choke. They would suffocate in the very air they lived on.

Every hardship here was load-bearing. The glare, the cold, the grit, the deafening roar, the unyielding walls—remove a single one, and the whole small world would come down.

To test its understanding, the power leaned—not with a body, for it had no wish to be heavy, but with a small, gathered fold of its presence, held just above the lip like an open palm.

Instantly, the draft stuttered. A cluster of chimneys beneath it dropped a fraction of a tone, the tight whistle thinning into a dry, breathy rasp.

Down in the white glare, the clay answered.

The power watched, motionless, as the pleated collars tightened by the width of a hair. It watched the papery membranes in the honeycomb blocks shiver and draw taut, dozens of blind, silent lives straining together in the roar, fighting to pull the chord back into its true pitch. There was no panic in them, no complaint—only an ancient, exquisite competence, a fierce fidelity to the note that kept them breathing.

The power withdrew its fold of presence at once.

The draft smoothed out. The collars relaxed into their grooves. The note locked flat again, dense as stone, and the glare folded the creatures back into the vessels.

For a time, the power remained at the rim, considering what, if anything, was left for it to do.

It could not improve the courtyard. It could not bless it with growth, because growth here took decades and any faster pace would crack the seams. It could not guard it with a wall, because a wall would break the wind. Even a sign, or a ring of stones, would draw curious feet—and there was nowhere to step down there without crushing a rim.

In the end, the power did only two things, both so small they left no mark upon the marl.

First, it looked outward across the flat, sun-bleached shelf—at the featureless miles of stone and dust that surrounded the pocket—and it gently inclined the paths of wandering things away. Not by force, and not with walls, but by making the twenty-pace approach seem just a fraction drier, a fraction more tiresome, a fraction less worth the walk to anything with heavy boots or careless hooves. It ensured that the place would keep the obscurity it had already earned.

Second, it stepped back from the lip, careful not to trouble the air, and left the courtyard its solitude.

The high, white sky stayed bright. The cool wind kept its steady shear. And down in the blinding hollow, unbroken and unpitied, the chalk-grey lungs held their single, load-bearing note.
