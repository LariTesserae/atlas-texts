At first, the power’s attention rests on the plateau the way the sun does—from high above, taking in the whole blinding sweep at once.

From that height, the basin looks like a wound that has been bandaged in wet green wool and then left out in the weather. The glare is pitiless. The wind comes over the rim with nothing to break it, and the sound rising from the raw fault-scarps and the concrete works is a blunt, unceasing collision. To a mind accustomed to gentler worlds, the first impulse of kindness is almost reflexive: to soften the light, to hush the screaming intake gates, to lay a hand over the cooling towers and let the turbines coast to a stop so the plateau might finally rest.

A lesser benevolence would have done it in an instant. It would have peeled back the steel penstocks like dead bark, crumbled the poured concrete into gravel, and given the basin back its silence.

Wisdom, however, does not act from the rim of a valley. It sinks.

The power lowers its awareness past the steaming crust of the sphagnum, down through the cold, tea-colored water and the dense weave of clubmoss, until the wind vanishes and the roar of the relief valves is pared down to a steady hum in the bone. There, two feet beneath the glare, it waits. It listens not with ears, but with the patience of the peat itself.

It feels the basin as the flat-backs feel it.

Through the waterlogged mat, the waterfalls are no longer a deafening crash; they are the steady pulse of the ridge’s perimeter, marking where the world ends. The galvanized pylons are thin, harmless threads of static stitched across the bog. And the turbine houses—those ugly, gray ships squatting in the moss—are warm. In the freezing dark beneath the mist, their foundations bleed a gentle, unwavering heat into the cold groundwater, and their sixty-cycle purr travels outward through the peat like the heartbeat of a great, sleeping animal.

The power watches dozens of the broad, stone-backed creatures gather in the dark beneath a transformer pad, pressed edge-to-edge in the wet moss, drinking in the warmth. It watches them haul themselves onto the mineral-slick concrete of the spillways to graze the emerald algae fed by the hissing valves. It looks into the pitch-black throats of the intake gates, where blind, hard-shelled things cling happily to the trembling seams, thriving in a violence of water that would shred anything else.

Nothing here is grieving. Nothing is waiting to be rescued. The creatures have no word for *machine*, and no memory of a time before the hum; they have simply folded the steel and concrete into their geology, the same way the liverwort folds itself over the sharp basalt. To silence the turbines in the name of nature would be to put out the only fires in a freezing dark.

So the power stays its hand.

It does not quiet the wind. It does not dim the high-altitude glare, or round off the brutal edges of the fault-scarps, or scrub the moss from the concrete walls. It recognizes that this strange, loud, unhurried truce between the bog and the works is already whole.

Yet wisdom also looks forward, and in the long view, it sees the one fragility of this borrowed peace: stone and moss age at one speed, and steel ages at another. Left entirely to themselves, bearings eventually seize. Riveted seams rust until they burst in a single, catastrophic night. And far below the plateau, in places where people still keep ledgers, someone might someday remember these automated works and come up the ridge with trucks and torches to dismantle them.

So the power does almost nothing—only two small, quiet things, so subtle they leave no mark on the surface.

First, it touches the metal and the concrete, not to make them immortal, but to teach them the patience of the basalt. The steel will still weather, and the turbines will still wear down, as all made things must; but they will do so imperceptibly, losing a fraction of a turn each decade, giving the moss and the flat-backs a thousand slow seasons to follow the heat as it fades.

Second, it lays a gentle, unremarkable forgetfulness over the maps and records far below the ridge—nothing dramatic, only the quiet certainty in distant minds that this high, wet basin is already accounted for, already running as it should, and needs no one to climb up and check on it.

Then the power withdraws.

Nothing in the basin has changed. The intake gates still scream; the cooling towers still wear their halos of steam; the wind still booms off the unweathered cliffs. Beside a shallow pool, a single cushion of copper bog-moss rises half an inch above the water. Two dark eyes, sheathed in clear membranes against the glare, open to reflect the washed-out sky—unblinking, unhurried, and entirely at home.
