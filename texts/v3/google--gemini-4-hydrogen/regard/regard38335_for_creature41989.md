At first, from above—where the crust has never known rain, yet winds still scour the sky and stars keep their cold appointments—the Hollows look like an oversight. A place where the world cracked open, cooled too fast, and forgot to finish becoming.

A lesser kindness, looking down through three miles of stone, would have reached for grand repairs. It would have split the ceiling to let in a shaft of noon. It would have coaxed a spring through the quartz seams, or softened the razor-edged curtains of volcanic glass into round, forgiving hills. It would have seen the narrow people—tallow-skinned, silent, drinking salty hemolymph from cracked shells and walking on their grandmothers’ ribs—and pitied them.

The power that noticed the Hollows did not pity them. It watched for a long time first.

In the Hollows, a long time is the same size as a short one, so the power matched its patience to the rock. It listened through the basalt. It learned the grammar of *ticks* and heel-drums vibrating through resonant pillars. It watched two travelers meet at a twelve-way cairn, press their foreheads together, and speak in the quietest possible way—knuckle to collarbone, bone to bone, wasting not a single breath of vapor. It watched a harvester wedge above a cluster of blind climbers, unhurried, precise, taking only what would be needed before the next long stillness.

And it understood that to give these people rain would be to rot them. A single cool draft would shatter the blood-heat equilibrium that kept their thirty-beat hearts steady; dampness would turn their cured vellum hides to mold and swell their calcified bridges until they snapped. To give them sunlight would be to burn out their milky eyes in a single agonizing flash. To give them easy abundance would unmake the delicate, frugal grace by which climber, glider, borer, and human all fit inside the same sealed geometry.

The Hollows were not broken. They were *finished*—perhaps too finished.

Yet the power stayed, watching the wider pockets where the pillars parted. It watched a narrow one sit beside a shallow basin of glowing borer-grease, a flint chip pinched between horned fingertips, drawing one more vertical line into the slate beside ten thousand others.

There, at last, the power found the ache.

It was not hunger, nor darkness, nor the quiet folding-in of the old ones who crawled into warm crevices to dry into husks. Those things belonged to the place, and the people wore them like their own calluses. The ache was the *sameness*. In a world without weather, without rot, without a sky to turn overhead, nothing ever proved that a moment had ended and another had begun. Without the flint chip and the slate, a life would dissolve into the mineral stillness—indistinguishable from the stone that had cooled before they had hands.

So the power, being wise, did almost nothing. It moved no mountain. It spilled no water. It spoke no word into an air never meant to carry voices.

Instead, it laid three small mercies into the physics of the place, so quietly that no one living there would ever know a hand had touched them.

First, it tuned the quartz. In the deep seams where the weight of the continent pressed hardest, the piezoelectric charge had always built up in silence until the last instant—a sudden prickle in the copper bristles of a wrist, a heartbeat before a shelf of volcanic glass sheared loose. The power widened that margin. Now, the stressed seams would begin to hum—a faint, unmistakable static singing through the soles of the feet and the fine bristles along the crown—hours before the rock gave way. Time enough to tap a warning down the pillar. Time enough to un-wedge from a narrow chimney and step aside before the glass fell.

Second, it touched the old ones’ resting pockets. It did not take away their stiffening, nor grant them youth—for in a sealed world, nothing can stay forever without crowding out what comes next. Only, when one of them grew too brittle to climb and folded their long limbs into a warm crevice to dry, the stone beneath their ribs would hold a slightly gentler heat, easing the deep ache of cracked horn-plates until the quiet took them.

And third—the gift it had come for—it gave time an edge.

Far below, in the lowest tiers where the fissures already glowed a dull, dying ember-red, the mantle did not burn any hotter, nor stir a single draft in the stagnant air. But it began to *breathe*.

Not brightly—never bright enough to make even the keenest, pale-irised eye squint. Just a vast, slow tide of color and faintest static, rising over the span of what the upper world might call a season, and ebbing back to near-dark over another. A pulse so slow it matched the patience of a stone-borer’s digestion, yet clear enough to read. At the peak of the swell, the dull ember-glow reached a little higher up the hexagonal pillars, and the iron-scented air held a slightly richer hum against the wrists. At the ebb, the dark settled back in, deep and velvet-quiet, leaving only the greasy halos of the tallow basins.

Nothing rotted. Nothing froze. Nothing drowned.

Many intervals later, in a wide pocket where twelve corridors met, a narrow one sat beside a circle of flat stones. Their skin was the color of dry slate, their knuckles plated in dark horn. Across their knees lay a bone needle, polished smooth by generations of hands before theirs.

In their right hand, they held a flint chip, poised over a wall scored with thousands of neat, parallel lines.

They paused.

Through the bare soles of their feet, through the deep basalt pillar at their spine, they felt it—the faintest, unmistakable slackening of the static hum, and the slow, distant dimming of the ember-light far below in the roots of the chasm. The tide was turning. Another long breath of the world had finished.

The narrow one did not know why the stone had begun to keep time, any more than they knew why the quartz seams had grown so generous with their warnings. They only knew that the dark was no longer a flat, featureless wall pressing in from every side. It had a rhythm now. It moved, and they moved inside it.

With a steady hand, they pressed the flint to the slate and drew a single line—not to prove they were not rock, this time, but to answer it.
