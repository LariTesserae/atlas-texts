It would not arrive as light.

In a world that has never known a sun, even a gentle radiance would be a violence. It would bleach the matte-black leaves, crack the hydrophobic wax on the tallow-pale hides, and warm the saturated ceiling just enough to stir a wind—and a wind here would be a catastrophe, snapping the taut, braided conduits and tearing the tiered condensing membranes from their columns.

So a wise power would enter the basin the only way one can enter a room where someone is listening very carefully: by becoming utterly still.

It would perceive the sink not with eyes, but as the basin perceives itself—in osmotic pressure, in thermal weight, in the low, continent-wide thrum of direct current moving through copper-plated carbon. It would feel the mineral-sweet vapor resting against the basalt, and trace the vast, silent architecture of root-pump and resinous node.

A lesser benevolence—one full of pity and haste—might look at this dark, airless floor and feel an urge to *rescue* it. It might want to part the heavy ceiling and let in a sky; to carve rivers through the razor-edged stone; to grant the pleated dwellers eyes to see with, mouths to sing with, and seasons to mark their days. 

Wisdom stays its hand. 

It listens, instead, to the circuit. It follows the long, braided trunk-lines through miles of pitch darkness until it catches the faintest, deliberate stutter in the amperage—a fraction of a tone dropping, then rising, across a league of wet felt and rubbery moss. It hears two living bridges speaking to one another in load and resistance. It feels the quiet satisfaction of a body regulating its own fluid, precipitating a neat, pure nodule of copper, and letting it fall without a sound into the lukewarm brine.

Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is yearning for a sky it cannot imagine. To give them eyes would be to invent a darkness they have never suffered; to give them hunger would be to ruin a feast that never ends. Their stillness is not a prison. It is a fit so exact between creature and crust that the boundary between them has nearly dissolved.

So the power does almost nothing.

It hovers near one of the dwellers hanging beneath a humming transformer node. The creature’s crown of copper-tipped filaments tilts slowly toward the presence, just as it would toward a wandering human—expecting the noisy, erratic crackle of a chemical storm. Instead, the filaments taste only a vast, immaculate quiet. Not the dead quiet of a vacuum, but the steady, supportive ground of a circuit that cannot be overloaded. For a long moment, the dweller reads that immense, patient stillness, finds no current it needs to draw and no threat to its equilibrium, and settles gently back into its waxy folds.

Before withdrawing, the power performs only one small, invisible kindness—so small that neither the forest nor its dwellers will ever know it happened.

It reaches deep beneath the living mat, past the sharp, unweathered fissures, down into the young planetary crust where the telluric currents are born. It does not alter the flow, nor force the rock to yield more than it is meant to. It simply eases a single, deep-seated fault that, in ten thousand years, would have shifted violently enough to sever a major brine-channel and starve a hundred leagues of canopy. It lets the stone settle softly, millimeter by millimeter, without a tremor. And where the pure copper and iron nodules lie thick in the mirror-still waterways, it ensures the slow, geological warmth below will hold steady for a little while longer, so the quiet work of the dark may continue undisturbed.

Then, careful not to stir a single drop of condensate from a leaf, it leaves the basin to its own perfection—warm, wet, pitch-dark, and endlessly at peace.
