At first, the attention simply rests on the basin the way the pewter light does—everywhere at once, without shadow or hurry.

It sees the obvious things first: the drowned avenues, the salt-ruined concrete, the vast, silent rookery of crabs and herons and sleeping bats. A lesser mercy might have recoiled from the place, mistaking the lack of green for death, or the thick, wet heat for a curse. It might have parted the bruised-grey clouds to let down a clean sun, drained the brackish canals, and coaxed grass up through the cracked asphalt.

The wise power does none of those things. It watches for a full turn of the tide, and then another, until it understands the grammar of the basin. It sees that the clouds are a lid that keeps the moisture in; that a sharp sun would bake the mudskippers in their silt and blind the pale eels; that grass would choke the open floors where ten thousand crabs make their living.

And then it finds her.

It notices her first not by her shape, which is the color of the wet pilings, but by her traces: the amber glass domes wiped clean of salt-film with a thumb; the dented tin funnel wired beneath the pneumatic valve; the bar-screens cleared of algae while the pumps groan on. It follows the sixty-cycle hum through the soles of her feet and sees how she is stitched into the skeleton of the place—no conqueror, no prisoner, simply the one creature in the basin whose niche happens to be the maintenance of dry iron.

Here, the power pauses.

It considers lifting her out. It knows there are dry rooms elsewhere in the world, with clean linen and voices and saltless water from taps. It knows she has not heard another human throat make a word in thirty years.

But it looks closely at her horn-thick heels, at the way her breathing matches the slow sigh of the steam valves, at the grey heron that lets her head pass three feet away without raising its wings. To take her out of the basin and set her down in a bright, dry room would be like pulling a crab from its pipe-joint and laying it on a parlor rug. She has spent three decades shedding everything that could not survive here. What remains is not broken; it is adapted. To rescue her from the basin would be to unmake her.

So the power turns its attention instead to the margin.

It looks at the razor-thin edge between the concrete and the sea, and it sees what she cannot see: that the margin is failing faster than her brass wrench can mend it. The main casing of the north dewatering pump is pitted halfway through from the inside; in three winters, or four, the warm brine will eat through the iron and drown the windings. The worm-gear she packs with grease is worn to knife-thin teeth. And her own body, tough as conveyor-belting, is quietly paying the bill for thirty years of brackish dark and mineral-poor steam. The cartilage in her knees is thinning. One bad slip on a wet I-beam, one deep scratch from rusted steel that refuses to close, and the gantry crane will sit empty while the bats wake overhead.

The power decides to intervene—not as an architect, and not as a savior, but as a quiet partner in the upkeep.

It waits until night, when the pewter sky has darkened to charcoal and she is asleep thirty feet above the avenues, stretched out on her pallet with the small olive crabs gathered quietly around her ankles.

First, it tends the machines—not by replacing them, for a sudden silence or an unfamiliar pitch in the hum would wake her in alarm, and leave her useless in her own home. Instead, it works inside the iron. Deep in the north pump-house, beneath the salt-crust she has not yet scraped away, the pitted casing quietly thickens from within, molecule by molecule, until the metal is sound again—still rough to the palm, still stained with rust, still vibrating at the exact sixty cycles she feels in her teeth. The teeth of the worn worm-gear cease to lose their edge. The seals in the sluice gates stay supple in the warm dark. The power leaves enough wear, enough slow salt-creep on the relays, that she will still have her afternoons crouched in the amber glow with her iron hook and her bundle of copper wire. It does not take her work from her. It only makes sure the work remains possible.

Next, it tends the water. Without altering the hiss of the pneumatic valve or the warm, flat taste she trusts, it lets the condensed steam carry a trace of what thirty years of rainwater and steam-drips have leached from her bones—invisible, tasteless salts of calcium and iron, drawn up quietly from the deep limestone beneath the basin’s silt.

Then it looks at her body. It does not smooth her calluses—she needs them to walk the spalled aggregate—and it does not bleach the algae-stain from her shins or soften the cords of muscle in her forearms. It simply reaches into the deep ache behind her kneecaps and the stiffness in the knuckles that grip the wrench, and eases them back to the resilience of her middle years. Where a tiny splinter of corroded steel has been festering under the skin of her palm, the flesh quietly pushes it out and knits clean beneath the grease.

Finally, the power sets a hand, very lightly, against the sea itself.

It does not drive the water back. The basin needs its tides; the herons need the flooded bays, and the crabs need the damp, steaming asphalt. It simply sets a quiet ceiling on the surge—a fraction of an inch in the outer sea-wall’s pilings, a slight stubbornness in the silt—so that however the oceans shift beyond the haze, the water in the canals will never rise high enough to quench the transformers or reach the gutted housing of the gantry crane.

Before dawn, the power considers one last thing: whether to leave a sign. A light that does not flicker, a voice in the hum, a dry blanket laid over her legs.

It decides against it. A sign would ask her to wonder, to look upward into the bruised-grey ceiling for something that was not there, to fracture the unbroken attention she gives to the pipes and the tide. Kindness, here, is better disguised as luck—or better yet, as the ordinary persistence of things.

When the bats return to the cooling towers in a long, leathery rustle, she wakes.

She sits up on the conveyor belting, swinging her legs down so the little crabs scatter back toward the chains with a dry *tick-tick-tick*. Her knees do not pop as she stands. She climbs down the thirty feet of greased steel, barefoot and unhurried, and pauses on the catwalk to check the vibration of the basin.

Through the horn of her heels, the sixty-cycle hum rises steady and true. Deep in the north pump-house, a bearing that had begun to whine two days ago is running cool and quiet again, as if the heavy grease she packed into it had finally found its way home.

She hooks her thumbs into her webbing belt, listens to the soft sigh of the steam-valve filling her jug, and climbs down toward the dark water to clear the screens.
