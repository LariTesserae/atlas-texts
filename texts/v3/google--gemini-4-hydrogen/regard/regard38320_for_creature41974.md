From the outside—from the vast, temperate distances where life is usually measured in green leaves, liquid oceans, and soft rain—the plateau looks like a catastrophe that forgot to finish dying.

A power that loves living things, seeing this place for the first time, feels an immediate, aching urge to mend it.

To an eye accustomed to gentler worlds, there is only deprivation here. A continent-sized corpse of bleached wood, scoured raw by a merciless wind. No soil to hold a seed. No river to cool a throat. A sky so thin and bright it seems to strip the air of mercy, and beneath it, a race of nearly blind, armored beings huddled in the cracks of a screaming, exploding machine.

The first instinct of benevolence is to bring water.

It would be so easy. A gathering of clouds over the blinding blue. A soft, steady rain to wash the silica dust from the air, to soak into the ancient lignin, to quiet the static and cool the violent spires. To coax moss onto the pale bark-steppe, and give the listeners something gentle to drink, something green to look at, a world where they would not have to eat glass or touch lightning just to stay awake through the dark.

And because the power is wise, it pauses before it calls the rain.

It does not act from a distance. It lowers its attention—carefully, without weight, careful not to cast a shadow that might confuse a horn-lidded eye or snag a spinning crown—and it begins to *listen*.

It takes a long time for a mind used to light to understand a world built of vibration. At first, there is only the deafening, chest-rattling roar of the gale and the concussive *crack* of the ironwood valves. But slowly, by sinking its awareness into the miles-deep lattice of hollow reeds, the power learns to separate the noise into its true parts.

It hears the fifths tuned into the canyon walls. It feels the crisp, three-dimensional map humming through the floor: the *tap-tap-scurry* of a grazer two miles off, the deep subterranean *groan* of a bellows-slug bracing its ribs against rising methane, the delicate, high-pitched hiss of a leak long before a spark can find it.

And then it feels the listeners.

It follows a band of them across the pale steppe, watching them read the continent through their knuckles. It watches a listener pause at a resonant ridge, rise onto heavy hips, and strike the wood with a calcified heel—sending a clean, coded *thrum* racing miles through the dark interior to another band waiting in a distant canyon. It watches them wait at the edge of a spire’s blast-zone, patient and exact, and then dart into the ozone-sharp circle to gather the warm resin-glass.

And in the freezing, star-pricked indigo of the night, it watches a single listener approach a spire between cycles, press a tail-tendon into a resinous fissure, and lay a bare palm against the needle-bark. It feels the sudden, sharp bloom of blue-white current—not as injury, not as cruelty, but as a fierce, intimate wakefulness, warming sluggish fluids until the whole hollow world hums cleanly inside the listener’s skull.

Understanding arrives, and with it, a quiet shudder at what its "mercy" would have done.

Rain would not have healed this place. Rain would have rotted the ancient, compressed lignin, turning the miles-deep instrument into sodden, collapsing pulp. It would have drowned the subterranean chambers, choked the bellows-slugs, rusted the copper-veined crowns, and silenced the wind-flutes forever. In the damp, muffled dark, the listeners’ tympanic membranes would have gone slack and useless. Their coded *thrums* would have died in mud.

To quiet the wind would be to blind them. To give them permanent walls or enduring tools would be to sever their living conversation with the plateau—for their culture *is* the re-chewing of the resin-wax, the re-tuning of the scoured reeds, the constant, attentive singing of a map that refuses to stay still.

Even the harshness is not an error. The cold of the night is what makes the touch of the spire sacred. The danger of the cycle is what gives the listening its precision.

So the power withdraws its shaping hand.

It does not bring rain. It does not soften the bark, or clear the glittering dust, or open the listeners’ sunken eyes to a glare they do not need. It does not even speak into the wood to announce itself—knowing that a voice too vast in the floor would drown out the subtle hiss of the methane leaks and turn a people of musicians into a people of supplicants.

If it does anything at all, it is something so small and distant that no creature on the plateau will ever know it happened.

High above the thin, freezing blue—far beyond the reach of the pollen-storms and the spinning crowns—the power sets a quiet, invisible boundary around the world’s edges. A gentle ward, meant not to keep the plateau’s own storms in, but to keep stray mercies out: to turn aside any wandering cloud, any well-meaning rain, any clumsy force that might look at this dry, deafening, sparkling continent from afar and mistake it for something broken.

Then it steps back, careful not to disturb the wind, and leaves the listeners to their music.
