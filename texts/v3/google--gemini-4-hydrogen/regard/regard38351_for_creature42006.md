At first, seen from a great distance, the plateau looks like a wound—a high, waterless scar where the earth forgot to finish softening itself.

A lesser power, moved by quick pity, would have acted at once. It would have gathered clouds over the distant ridges and sent down a gentle, persistent rain to wash the sharp basalt smooth, to coax green shoots out of the graphite pans, to give the silent thickets birdsong and dampness and decay.

And in a single afternoon, it would have destroyed everything.

But this power is wise, and so its first act is not to give, but to look.

It brings its attention down through the thin, warm air without stirring a grain of feldspar. It watches the bright sunlight strike the quartzite terraces and feels, beneath the stone, the microscopic flex and hum of the great circuit waking. It traces the current through the copper ribbons into the pale taproots of the groves, up into the heavy, oil-swollen leaves, down into the tiny shield-backed creatures bleeding static into the dry air, and deeper into the crevices where the living fuses coil around the roots, ready to absorb a sudden spike.

Then it notices the listeners.

It sees them folded along the terrace rims, indistinguishable from the fractured rock, their quartz crests tilted to the thermal weight of the sun. Without touching the copper—without adding a single stray volt to the load—it perceives the slow, geometric conversations flowing beneath the stone: the quiet accounting of heat and current, of an alloy rod snapping out in the basin, of the afternoon peak cresting and easing toward dusk.

The power watches through the bitter cold of night, when the listeners lock their oiled joints and wait in the dark, listening through their quartz feet to the dry *tick* of cooling metal. It watches across centuries, until one of the listeners—its quartz clouded, its carbon struts brittle—walks with weightless precision to the far edge of a basin and folds itself neatly over the hairline fissure, letting the slow mineral creep draw it back down to be remelted.

There is no grief in the act. There is no waste, no fear, no unfinished debt.

For a long moment, the power weighs every gift it knows how to give.

It could spare the old ones the fissure—but then they would linger on the bare rock, clouded and stiff, cluttering the clean geometry they belong to, severed from the crust that made them.

It could widen the groves or enrich the thin air—but that would break the exact balance of voltage and load, scorching the canopies and scattering the neat, wind-sorted rows of iron, silica, and carbon out on the flats.

It could speak to them, sending a greeting down the copper veins—but a voice from beyond the plateau would arrive only as a violent, meaningless surge, forcing the tiny fuses in the moss to swell and burn themselves out grounding it into the stone.

The power understands, then, what is required of it.

Inside the borders of the plateau, it does nothing at all. It does not shift a single wafer of fallen carbon. It does not round a single razor-sharp edge of basalt. It leaves the sun to flex the quartz, the dust to sort itself in silence, and the listeners to hold their quiet charge through the dark.

Only at the outer edges of the sky—far beyond the thirty-mile ridges—does it place a quiet, invisible hand.

If a stray, moisture-heavy storm wanders too high toward the passes, the power gently turns the wind aside. If a fragment of ice falls out of the deep dark above, it nudges it harmlessly away before it can strike the basins and melt into ruin. If anything loud, wet, or careless approaches the terraces, its path is subtly bent until it passes by, never knowing what lay just beyond the ridge.

Then the power withdraws its attention, leaving no footprint in the alkali, no echo in the copper, no trace of having been there.

The greatest kindness it can offer this place is that the plateau will never know it was watched, never know it was spared, and will continue—clean, dry, and unbroken—until its own stars grow cold.
