The attention arrives without breaking the clouds.

It has no weight to press against the thin, metallic air, no brightness to bleach the pewter gloom. If it has a shape at all, it is only a sudden, vast stillness in the way the plateau is held—as if something immense has knelt beside the basin, cupped its hands around the steaming stone without touching it, and begun to listen.

At first, the impulse of any ordinary kindness would be to *mend*.

A lesser mercy, looking down through the bruised-tin sky, would feel a pang of pity and part the clouds. It would send down clean, dry sunlight to warm the wet flagstones, to burn off the greasy mist, to turn the eternal late afternoon into a bright morning.

And the wise power sees, in the space of a single heartbeat, what that mercy would kill. Sunlight would bake the ochre and verdigris biofilms to brittle scales, starving the girders of their living wax until the iron rusted through and collapsed. Dry air would sear the tenders’ crimson gill-lungs, turning their slow bellows to ragged gasps. The brightness would be nothing but heat without moisture, a desert dropped onto a lung.

So the clouds stay.

A lesser mercy would look at the hollow, floorless foundries, at the tenders hanging racked between waist-high rails in the rising steam, and build them walls. It would give them dry rooms, soft beds, doors to shut out the damp. It would look at two of them standing twenty paces apart on a wide, empty avenue, sparking their quiet, static-salt speech into the wet stone, and wish to draw them closer together—to give them eyes, or voices that carried on the wind.

The wise power listens to the twenty paces between them and understands. That emptiness is not loneliness; it is the room a mind needs in order not to drown in another’s static. Those hollow frames are not unfinished houses; they are lungs of brick and iron, built to let the heat rise and the steam breathe. To give the tenders walls would be to suffocate them. To crowd them would be to deafen them.

A lesser mercy would look at their endless, patient labor—snapping horizontal stalactites of salt before they bridge a live wire, scraping blind ribbon-worms from the rims of overflowing cisterns, clearing excess biofilm from the sluice gates—and wish to free them from toil. It would make the salt stop growing, make the pipes run self-cleaning, silence the counterweights and let the machines run by magic instead of muscle.

And the wise power watches a tender uncoil along a pylon, feeling the sixty-cycle hum shiver through the feathery filaments of its anvil crest, teasing a crust of mineral from copper with its blunt, ceramic-hard nails. It sees that this is not drudgery. It is conversation. The plateau speaks to them in resistance, vibration, and weight; they answer with the scrape of horn and nail. To take away the maintenance of the balance would be to cut the nerves between their spines and the world that holds them.

So the power does none of these things. It keeps its hands off the sky, off the avenues, off the racks in the foundries.

Instead, it looks deeper—past the flagstones, past the basement hatches sealed with heavy brass dogs, down into the hot, roaring dark of the deep-rock shafts where the pale, soft-hided young cling to the trembling pipes, blind to everything but current, waiting for their bone crests to harden.

It looks deeper still, beneath the lowest pumps, into the blind basalt where no filament can taste and no ceramic nail can reach.

There, in the indifferent dark of the earth, time is doing what time always does. Not malice, not tragedy—only the slow, patient drift of stone and heat. A deep aquifer, feeding the eastern sluices, is grinding slowly shut along an ancient fault, pinched by the settling weight of the mountain. Farther down, the geothermal seam that warms the western districts has begun to wander, cooling by fractions of a degree each century, edging away from the roots of the city’s oldest cisterns. In five hundred years, or a thousand, the eastern paddle wheels would slow, then stall. The living wax would thin. The young in the deep shafts would find the pipes growing lukewarm, then cold.

The tenders do not know this. Their world is the present pulse, the hum of the hour, the next drop of condensation rolling down a wire.

So the power reaches into the blind rock—gently, the way a careful hand eases a kink from a sleeping sleeper’s spine without waking them.

Far below the pumps, the pinched fault softens and widens, just enough for the warm water to keep weeping for another ten thousand years. The wandering heat-vein is coaxed back toward the roots of the basalt, steadied in its bed, neither hotter nor colder than the city knows how to bear. Here and there, in the oldest buried foundations where the living wax cannot reach, the iron is quietly reminded of its own strength, the slow creep of metal fatigue set back by an age.

Nothing in the plateau breaks. Nothing flashes. No stone shifts loudly enough to startle the youngest blind thing clinging to a pipe.

And then—because wisdom without warmth is only engineering, and benevolence sometimes wishes simply to say *I see you, and you are good*—the power does one small, unannounced thing in the language the tenders know.

For the span of a single breath, it lets a clean, frictionless clarity pass through the sixty-cycle hum.

Not a voice. Not a command. Only the sensation of a bearing running without grit, of a current flowing through wet copper with sudden, effortless grace.

Up in the hollow foundries, thirty slate-grey shapes racked in the rising steam stir slightly, their crimson gill-plates parting as their crest-filaments shiver in the pewter air. Out on a broad, doorway-less avenue, two tenders pause twenty paces apart beneath an amber lamp. They lower their anvil crests toward the wet flagstones. Tiny static sparks tick faintly across the salt-film on their bone—*tiss, tiss, tiss*—a brief, sub-audible tremor passing through the stone between them.

*The pumps are running sweet today.*

*Yes. Sweet.*

They do not look up at the bruised-tin sky. There is nothing up there to look at, and they have no eyes to look with. After a moment, one turns back toward the humming pylons, ceramic-tipped pole resting across its shoulder, and the other moves unhurriedly toward the sluice gates.

The attention withdraws, leaving no footprint on the slick basalt, no change in the dimness.

A counterweight drops somewhere in the haze with a muffled *thud*. A steam-relief valve lets out its long, wet sigh. Warm water spills over the rims of the rooftop cisterns in shimmering, rust-colored curtains, and the great, sweating machine keeps turning—held, unseen, in a slightly longer forever.
