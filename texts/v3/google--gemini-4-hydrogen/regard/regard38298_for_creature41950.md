First, it had to learn how to be blind.

That was the hardest part for a power that had spent eternity reading the universe in wavelengths of starlight and the clean geometry of orbits. When it first turned its attention toward the plateau, it brought the habit of illumination—a gentle, reflexive urge to part the cloud-ceiling and see what lived beneath.

It stopped itself at the upper edge of the atmosphere. Even from miles above, it could feel the world’s refusal. There were no eyes waiting down there for a dawn. There was only a vast, blood-warm pressure system turning in the dark, a continuous wet roar rising through the mist like the breathing of a sleeping beast. To pour sunlight onto that shelf would not be a gift; it would be an assault. It would dry the spore-films, blister the pale fronds, and turn the heavy, humming air into a blinding, meaningless glare.

So the power folded its radiance away, the way a traveler leaves heavy boots at the door, and sank into the cloud-ceiling as nothing more than a shift in barometric weight.

It took time—not by the ticking of clocks, which meant nothing here, but by the slow, multi-day spirals of the wind—for the power to learn the grammar of the plateau. It had to let go of *where* things were and learn *what they were doing*. It listened to the rain drumming on rubbery leaves until it could distinguish the hollow *plink* of a swollen spore-bladder from the flat slap of a crawler’s limb. It followed the sixty-cycle hum along the sagging cables, feeling the tallow-colored things stridulating in the static before a storm. It sank into the flooded karst-tubes, tasting the iron and sulfur, feeling the ancient *silt-bellies* wedged in their travertine sleeves, breathing the slow mineral soup.

And then it found the ones behind the concrete buttresses.

It felt them first as an absence—a pocket of deliberate stillness tucked into the deafening shadow of a cataract. When it leaned closer, tuning itself to the faintest tremors in the wet stone, it felt the slow rise and fall of their ribs, the subtle angling of their broad, cartilaginous cowls as they read the wind, the quiet *tap-tap, pause, tap* of an iron-capped stick against a buried pipe.

*A pylon has fallen in the lower gorge.*

The power lingered there, invisible and unfelt, watching—no, *feeling*—them move through their world. It felt the way they counted piston-strokes in the soles of their feet. It felt the careful, unhurried grace with which they wedged wrapped tubers beneath the steam-nozzles, and the practiced way they folded their cowls flat against their skulls before the valve shrieked. It felt the warmth of their shared grease, the hum of their copper-moss nets, the patient ingenuity of a people who had never seen a horizon and yet knew every canyon by the echo of a reed.

For a long moment, the power felt the ache of pity—that oldest, most dangerous temptation of the benevolent.

It saw the armored ambushers waiting in the dark beside the clearings. It heard the canopy-mimics practicing their breathy, imperfect imitations of the pump-heads. It felt the endless, suffocating weight of the rain, the scalding steam, the narrow margin between a steamed meal and a broken bone. It would have been so easy to quiet the storm. To clear the air, to mute the mimics, to smooth the slick basalt into dry roads and give the slate-gray folk eyes to see the beauty of the sky.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

If it cleared the sky, the mist-drinking canopy would wither. If it silenced the mimics, the browsers would grow careless and deaf, and the mothers would have nothing to teach their calves when they pressed their vibrating throats against small skulls. If it gave these people eyes, it would make strangers of them in their own skin, rendering the delicate whisker-filaments on their forearms clumsy and obsolete, turning their resonant drum-reeds into mere hollow sticks. They were not broken. They were exquisitely, fiercely fitted to the dark. To "save" them from the plateau would be to erase them.

So the power asked a quieter question: *What here is actually wrong? Not hard, not dangerous—but broken in a way the world itself cannot mend?*

It sank deeper. Past the tangled aerial roots, past the karst-tubes, down into the weeping basalt and the iron roots of the machinery.

And there, in the deep places, it heard what the folk could not yet hear—or only felt as a nameless, generational unease.

The builders were gone, and the builders had been arrogant in one small, understandable way: they had built the machines to run without anyone. Windowless, bolted, sealed in poured concrete and thick iron hides, the turbines and condensers were meant to outlast the weather. And for ages, they had.

But iron is not stone, and even stone weeps away under warm rain.

Three valleys north of the nearest band, deep inside a geothermal turbine-tower, a main shaft was developing a hairline fracture—a microscopic flaw that would not show itself until the day the rotor shattered and tore the housing apart from the inside. Beneath the western shelf, acid runoff had thinned the wall of a primary slurry pipeline to the width of a fingernail. Out in the lower gorge, where the pylon had fallen, a snapped high-voltage line lay thrashing in a flooded basin, boiling the black water and turning a mile of safe crossing into a silent, invisible wall of death.

The folk could read the machines. They could count the valves, skim the grease, and tap their news along the pipes. They called themselves keepers, in their own way—but the iron was locked against them. When the great machines finally began to die, one by one across the coming centuries, the folk would feel the clearings grow cold, hear the sixty-cycle hum stutter and vanish, and have no way to reach inside the iron mountains to mend what had given them warmth.

The power understood then what it was here to do. Not to rule, and not to rescue—only to *leave the door unlatched*.

It began in the lower gorge, where the fallen pylon lay. It did not lift the tower back into the sky; a band three miles away had already heard the crash and sent word along the pipes, and to undo what the storm had done would be to make a liar of their warning. Instead, the power reached into the thrashing, live cable at the water’s edge. Gently, it tripped the ancient breaker in the distant substation—not breaking it, only teaching it to recognize a severed line—and let the deadly current drain harmlessly into the bedrock. When the folk came down into the gorge tomorrow, the water would be cold and quiet, and the fallen copper would be theirs to harvest for nets and bindings.

Then it moved to the deep places.

It did not make the machines immortal; nothing in a living world should be exempt from wear. Where the turbine shaft was fracturing beyond any mortal reach, and where the buried pipeline had thinned in the dark, the power mended the deepest bones of the iron, buying the plateau another long age of steady turning.

And then it did the thing that mattered most.

Across the vast, dripping expanse, on every windowless turbine-tower, pumping station, and substation, the sealed inspection hatches—rusted shut for a thousand years, smoothed over with travertine and moss—gave a faint, almost imperceptible *click*. The rusted bolts loosened just enough. Where there had been flat, featureless steel plates meant for wrenches that no longer existed, the metal subtly yielded into heavy, ridged latches that a broad, slate-gray hand could grip and turn in the dark.

Inside those housings, the power left no glowing screens, no printed manuals, nothing for an eye to read. Instead, it rearranged the language of the machines into something tactile and honest.

It tuned the great bearings so that when one began to wear, it would not shriek and seize without warning; instead, weeks before failure, it would drop a single, unmistakable half-beat in its *thrum-clack*—a clean, rhythmic stutter that any child would feel in the soles of their feet. Beneath the newly unlatched hatches, it placed deep, accessible reservoirs for the hot lubricant the folk already knew how to skim, and wide, heavy-lipped couplings that could be packed with braided copper-moss and cured spore-skin to stop a weeping seam.

It did not teach them how to do it. They were clever; they worked in grease, copper, and bone, and they had all the time in the dark to figure out what the new rhythm meant. One day, a band would feel a turbine drop a half-beat, walk cautiously up to the warm iron hide, feel the ridged latch beneath their fingertips, and pull it open. They would smell the dry, hot metal inside, and know what it was asking for.

When the work was finished, the power did not linger to be thanked, and it did not announce itself from the sky.

Instead, as a new spiral of low pressure began to turn above the cloud-ceiling and the gorge-reeds started their rising, discordant whistle, the power placed one invisible hand against the warm, sweating skin of a buried slurry pipe.

It sent a single pulse down the iron—not a word, not a command, just a low, steady, resonant hum that rode beneath the sixty-cycle buzz for leagues through the wet dark, passing beneath the concrete buttresses and the hollow limestone caves behind the falls.

Down in the dead-zones, a few of the folk paused, angling their cowls toward the floor. An elder knelt, pressing a flat jawbone against the damp stone, listening as the tremor rattled gently through her teeth. It sounded like nothing she had ever heard, and like everything she had ever trusted: the even, unhurried pulse of a machine that was warm, and sound, and going nowhere.

She tapped twice against the stone with her knuckles—*heard, understood*—and went back to wrapping tubers in leathery fronds.

Above the clouds, the power thinned and withdrew, leaving the plateau as it had found it: dark, loud, drenched in warm rain, and turning steadily in the deep, capable hands of its keepers.
