At first, the attention arrived like a hand hovering over a bruise.

Seen from above—from the vantage of things that know the wide sky, the turning of seasons, and the clean arithmetic of forests—the sinkhole looked like an old wound that had failed to heal. A pit of fractured shale, drowned iron, and stagnant mist. A place where industry had died badly and left its bones to rot in the dark.

The first impulse of kindness is almost always to mend. To clear the choked drain, to haul up the rusted pumps, to let the sun down into the cellar-rot and coax a little green moss onto the stone.

But wisdom is slower than kindness. It does not reach before it listens.

So the power waited at the rim of the overcast slit, folding its vast, quiet notice down into the cool air, and simply stayed still for a long time.

It listened to the *tink-plip* of condensation striking submerged iron. It felt the fourteen-second shudder of the hydraulic ram, traveling up through the shale like a slow, steady pulse. It watched the dim, bruised-amber glow of the single bulb, barely reaching the black water before the mist drank it.

And as it watched, the ruin turned inside out.

It saw the largest of the three haul its heavy, crusted bulk up onto the brass-and-iron frame—not out of duty, not out of servitude, but out of hunger for the very mineral scale that would otherwise have choked the valve. It watched the rasping mouth-plate clear the piston sleeve, and saw the valve trip cleanly a moment later, shaking loose a cloud of silt for the waiting, ice-blue fans of the tube-worms.

It watched the creature settle back against the north wall, pressing its pale, soft underside to the warm, humming skin of the transformer. It saw the conductive tendrils uncurl like pale roots in the dark, bridging the gap between water and copper, closing a circuit through living flesh. It saw the blind amphipods gather in fluttering, shrimp-pale clouds, drawn by the sharpened field, and the ribbon-like eels nosing through the grating to take their share.

And high above, on the rotting ties of the catwalk, it saw the tiny, grape-soft young clustering near the wire cage of the bulb, drinking the mineral drops from the stalactites, waiting until they were heavy enough to fall into the dark and begin gathering iron onto their backs.

The power understood then that there was nothing broken here.

This was not a ruin. It was a truce—older, stranger, and more delicate than any garden. Flesh and corroded iron had found a way to keep each other alive in a place where neither could have survived alone. To scrub the rust would be to starve the limpets. To cut the stray current would be to blind the eels and quiet the hum that called the amphipods home. To bring down the sun would bake the slime mold, dry the gill-curtains, and burn away the cold mist that held the whole engine together.

Even the darkness was a kind of shelter. Even the *glug-hiss* of the siphon-pipe, stranding the tube-worms for the heavy-clawed crabs, was a necessary breath.

What, then, can love do for a thing that is already whole?

Not everything. And not loudly.

If the power had a hand, it did not lay it on the creatures. It did not give the amphipods eyes, or soften the heavy magnetite plates on the great one’s back, or spare the juveniles the long, blind drop from the porcelain insulators into the sump. Those hardships were the shape of their lives, and to take them away would be to unmake them.

Instead, it turned its attention to the edges—to the few quiet vulnerabilities that the creatures could not mend by themselves, because they belonged to a world outside the basin’s walls.

High above, on the barren flats where the sky met the rim of the sinkhole, it laid a gentle, unremarkable dullness over the ground. Nothing dramatic—no wall of thorns, no sudden fog. Only a subtle tilt to the soil and a quietness in the air that made the eye slide past, so that no road-crew, no surveyor, no wandering machine from the upper world would ever find a reason to look down, or think to fill the pit with gravel.

In the distant, forgotten junction boxes miles away—where the last thin trickle of voltage still bled down into the tar-wrapped cables—it steadied the current. It did not amplify it; a bright surge would have boiled the sump and shattered the truce. It simply kept the dying grid from snapping off altogether, and smoothed away the sudden spikes that might have burnt out the single filament. The caged bulb would keep its bruised, gentle amber, neither brightening nor going dark, for as long as the shale held.

And in the water beside the north wall, where the oil-immersed transformer sat chest-deep in the dark, the power touched the iron casing—just once, and lightly. It left the magnetite crust intact. It left the rust-orange sinter and the clinging tube-dwellers undisturbed. It only slowed the deepest, unseen eating-away of the bottom seam, so that the heavy oil inside would stay sealed in its iron belly, keeping its faint, greasy warmth without ever spilling black poison into the sump.

Then, having done almost nothing at all, the power withdrew its weight.

It left no sign that it had ever been there. No sudden bloom of green on the ledges, no warmth in the mist, no hush in the machinery.

Fourteen seconds passed. Beneath the black water, the hydraulic valve tripped with a muffled, heavy *thock*. The grates shuddered. A brief, oily ripple crossed the sump, and beside the north wall, the great armored creature shifted a fraction of an inch against the humming iron, parting its tallow-colored gill-curtains to draw in another long, quiet draught of the dark.
