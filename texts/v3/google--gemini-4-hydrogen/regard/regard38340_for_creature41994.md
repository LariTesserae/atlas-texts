First, it had to learn how to stop.

A power accustomed to the speed of lightning and the heat of suns cannot simply step into a place like this; its mere attention would be a brushfire. So it cooled itself. It shed its brightness the way a traveler sheds a heavy coat at a threshold, and let its awareness settle into the notch at the pace of condensation forming on a needle.

It watched for a long time—long enough, by the reckoning of the valleys below, for empires to quarrel and change their names. Here, it was time enough for a single juniper shoot to bend half an inch, and for a dweller wedged in a windward fork to let the rime armor its back, waiting for the cambium to knit.

From the outside, to an eye used to blood and fire, the city looked like a wound—a cramped, freezing penance endured in the dark. And for a long while, the oldest temptation of benevolence tugged at the watcher: the urge to *mend*.

It weighed its gifts, and one by one, it laid them down.

*If I gave them summer,* it thought, *the cloud-belt would lift. The cushion-moss would dry to brittle straw. The dwellers, built to hover a hair’s breadth above ice, would cook in their own skin, their slow centuries burned through in a frantic handful of years.*

*If I gave them fire, the walls are wood and peat. That would be mercy as arson.*

*If I gave them voices, it would shatter the hush that lets them hear the tapping in the timber. If I gave them iron, they would forget how to persuade a living branch to stay.*

Every gift it could imagine was a wedge driven into a join that had taken generations to knit. These beings were not enduring the cold; they *were* the cold, shaped to it as a root is shaped to a fissure. Even their dying—crawling down into the crushing weight of the lower vaults to ferment into the stove that warmed their kin—was not a tragedy to be interrupted. It was the hinge of the whole house.

So the watcher touched nothing inside the boundary. Instead, it laid its attention flat against a pale, healed knuckle of juniper in the middle gallery, matching the rhythm of the sap.

Through the continuous wood, it felt the whole city at once: the *tock-tock* of heavy drops falling into the root-lined cisterns; the light, quick scuttle of an ash-colored bird along an upper sill; the faint, dull tremor of a dweller tapping three stories up to ask for help with a root-cable, and the answering hum of two others waking in the warm peat below. It was a single, slow chord, held without haste. There was no loneliness in it. There was nothing broken to repair.

Yet wisdom is not always stillness.

Looking up—past the dim, slate-green twilight, past the windward rim where the sedge-blades wore their glaze of glass—the power saw what the dwellers could not see, because their wide-set eyes were built for alleys and wet bark, not for the high, dead crags above.

The slate was young. It had not yet learned how to lie still. High on the northern wall, two hundred paces above the highest living roof, a spire of unweathered rock the size of a cathedral was slowly jacking itself loose along a seam of freezing water. It was not part of the city’s patient bargain with the mountain. It was blind, unliving weight. In three winters, or five, the ice behind it would pop the seam, and the slab would shear straight down into the notch—crushing the three tiers, sealing the peat-vaults, ending the long chord in a single, deafening roar.

The power did not melt the ice, nor weld the slate. It did not alter the mountain’s nature.

It simply reached into the hairline fracture in the dead stone—where no moss grew and no dweller ever climbed—and coaxed the frost-wedge by the width of a single grain of sand. It tilted the balance of the seam outward, away from the cleft, toward the empty air over the valley.

When the spire finally broke free, three winters later, it would pitch clear over the lip of the notch and vanish harmlessly into the pewter sea of cloud below. The dwellers in the lower galleries would feel only a brief, dull shudder through the root-cables, wonder at it in their slow way, and go back to their grafts.

Having done that, the power withdrew. It took its warmth back with it, leaving no sign, no voice in the timber, no miracle to be knelt before.

Above the notch, a squall of sleet hissed across the glazed roofs. Inside, in the dim green dark, a dweller peeled its pale, calloused palms away from a newly knitted branch, blinked its milky lids clear of the drip, and began the long, patient crawl down toward the warm rot below.
