It arrives the way light arrives at this depth: without weight, without current, without stirring a single grain of silt on the limestone floor.

There is no ripple on the surface fifteen feet above, no sudden bloom of warmth, no shadow passing over the stonewort. If a pressure gauge were built to measure such things, its needle would not twitch. Yet the basin is suddenly and completely known—every stepped shelf of pale rock, every tuft of dark-green moss, every needle-thin crack where the water sits cold and still.

For a long time, the power simply listens.

It feels the sixty-hertz tremor in the cast iron—not as a sound, for there is no air here to carry sound, but as a patient, mechanical pulse moving through the bone of the machinery. It traces that pulse into the chalk-white shelves of calcium carbonate, marveling at the precision with which they have been grown, ridge by ridge over decades, to catch a frequency the human world treats as waste. Beneath the brittle lime, it sees the pale blue-gray ribbons pressed flat against the metal, their internal fluids turning in a slow, borrowed circle. It follows the velvety-brown filaments along the steel skids, tasting the faint millivolts leaking between copper and zinc, and sees how the dissolved metals have been woven into a quiet, conductive net.

A lesser kindness might have pitied them.

It might have looked at the bare industrial iron, the sunless blue-green chill, the absence of fish or current or sky, and thought: *This is a prison. Let me clean it. Let me pull up the rusting skids, flush the dissolved metals from the lake, warm the water, and bring in minnows and swaying reeds. Let me give these blind things eyes so they can know the sun.*

But wisdom sees what pity misses. To warm the water would be to break the stillness they are built for. To give them eyes would be to invent a hunger they have never felt, and a distance they could never cross. And to shut down the heavy, un-housed machines would be to stop their hearts in the dark. There is nothing broken here. In the narrow seam between human industry and cold stone, life has found a chord and learned to hold it.

So the power leaves the water untouched.

Instead, its attention follows the thick armored conduits up over the northern rock lip, out of the basin, and onward to the unseen shore station.

There, in the dry air where weather exists, it sees what the creatures beneath the lime plates cannot: the frailty of the circuit. It sees the shore station as it truly is—a thing of budgets, rusting switchgear, aging grids, and human forgetting. It sees the storms that will one day snap a line, the maintenance diver who might one day come down with a wire brush to scrape "fouling" off the transformer fins, and, further ahead, the inevitable afternoon when a switch will be thrown for the last time and the station will be decommissioned.

On that day, fifteen feet below the surface, the sixty-hertz hum would drop to zero. In the sudden, absolute silence, the lime plates would go still, and the pale ribbons beneath them would suffocate in the cold.

The power considers this. It does not want to freeze time, nor does it wish to rob the creatures of the only movement they know—that slow, generational creeping from a spent zinc plate toward a fresh one, leaving behind empty white ridges for the moss.

So it makes a quiet arrangement with the iron and the stone.

Deep within the cast-iron pump housings and along the finned radiators, it lays a second, hidden steadiness—not a new machine, and not a miracle that any eye could see, but a faint, patient resonance woven into the metal itself. It tunes this resonance to the exact phase of the shore station’s load, down to the thousandth of a cycle, and then leaves it dormant, waiting.

When the day comes—in ten years, or fifty, or two hundred—that the shore station finally goes dark and the armored cables over the northern rock lip lose their charge, the basin will not notice. Not a single beat of the hum will drop. The millimeter of warmth radiating off the transformer fins will remain, and the sixty-hertz chord will continue, drawn now from the quiet patience of the bedrock itself.

For the anode racks, it does something equally small. It does not make the zinc immortal; the old plates will still pit away to nothing on their natural schedule, and the creatures will still thin out on the dead side and creep fractionally along the steel skids. Only, at the far end of each rack, as one plate turns to silt and moss, the next dull bar of zinc will always happen to be whole—replenished atom by atom in the dark, as if some unseen tender had never stopped visiting the circuit.

And on the surface, above the pale brightness overhead, it draws a gentle veil of unremarkableness over the cove. Boats pass without anchoring. Divers swim elsewhere, finding the water here too cold and bare to bother with. On the shore station’s charts, the submerged manifold remains a line item no one ever feels the need to disturb.

Before it withdraws, the power lingers for one more moment in the blue-green quiet.

It touches nothing. It leaves no sign of having been there. Beneath the chalk-white shelves, a pale ribbon of tissue shivers imperceptibly against the iron, drinking the faint current, listening to a chord that will never break.

The silt lies level around the cables. The needles on the glass-faced gauges hold steady. And the cold, clear water keeps them all in the same suspended stillness, day after day.
