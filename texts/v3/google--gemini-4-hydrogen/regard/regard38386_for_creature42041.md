At first, a power that is merely kind might feel a pang of pity for the saddle.

Looking down through the thin air, it would see people with wind-cured faces eating cold barley by tallow light, sweeping dust from bare rock, sleeping without a hearth-fire, and drinking bitter water coaxed drop by drop from fog. A merely kind power might reach down and touch the limestone to bring forth a warm spring. It might coax a grove of pines from the grit so they could have timber for fires, soften the wind, or fill the silent courts with the comfort of green leaves and birdsong.

But a power that is also wise looks longer before it moves a finger.

It watches through a whole turning of the seasons. It sees the morning cloud brush the stone, the silt settle in the upper basins, and the grey dregs scraped out onto the saxifrage. It sees the two fingers lifted in greeting across the empty plaza—not coldness, but a thrift of breath in thin air. It looks inside the windowless storehouses, into the dry, steady dark where the lowland’s seed-grain sleeps in clay jars beside oiled iron and old tallies.

And slowly, the wise power understands.

If a warm spring broke through the limestone, moss and weeds would pry apart the flagstones; the runnels would choke; the dry chill that keeps the seed-grain from sprouting and the iron from flaking would be lost to rot. If firewood grew on the ridges, smoke would stain the slate, the air would thicken, and soon enough the people of the haze would climb the switchbacks not to leave their treasures for safekeeping, but to take the place for themselves. If the wind were silenced and the work made unnecessary, the people would have nothing left to keep, and the long, quiet equilibrium that holds them like a tuned string would slacken and snap.

To mend a thing that is not broken is only another way of breaking it.

So the first thing the benevolent and wise power does is stay its hand. It does not part the clouds, or speak from the watch-platform, or leave a single miracle on the flagstones for someone to trip over. It respects the silence too much to ask for gratitude.

Instead, whatever it does is done inside the grain of what is already there, so quietly that even the choughs on the retaining wall do not turn their heads.

When a dry year stretches long and the cisterns sound hollow at the dipper, the morning cloud does not turn into a storm that would wash grit into the drains; it simply lingers an hour longer in the saddle, calm and heavy, letting the cold limestone sweat a little more generously before the sun lifts it.

In the roof of the oldest storehouse, where a bone-coloured lintel has borne the weight of river-stones for three hundred winters and is one sharp frost away from splitting, the wood simply holds. Nothing gleams, nothing grows new; the grain of the timber keeps its tension for another century, unremarked.

Inside the sealed jars, the sleeping seeds—wheat and barley and bitter roots brought up from wars that have already been forgotten—do not lose their spark. They wait in the dark with the patience of the stone around them, so that when a pack-train finally climbs out of the haze after a lean decade, what goes back down the mountain will still sprout in warm earth.

And down below, in the hazy air, when the eyes of restless rulers or hungry armies turn upward toward the ridges and wonder what might be hidden above the mist, the power lets the switchbacks look steeper than they are, and the air look colder, and turns their minds to nearer things.

That is all.

In the late afternoon, a lean figure in peat-brown wool crosses the plaza with a stiff broom of wiry grass, sweeping grit away from the runnels with a long, dry *shhh* against the flagstones. They step cleanly over the chiseled stone so their soft boots will not chip the edge. Somewhere in the shade, a drop of water falls into a bedrock cistern with a hollow *tock*.

The power watches a moment longer, pleased by the order of it, and withdraws its gaze—leaving the saddle as bare, as cold, and as whole as it was found.
