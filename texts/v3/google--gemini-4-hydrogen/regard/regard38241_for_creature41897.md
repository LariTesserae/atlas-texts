At first, the power only noticed the heat.

From above—where clouds gathered, where rivers cut through green valleys, where life was a soft, wet, sunlit negotiation—the pocket beneath the basalt looked like a wound in the crust. A dry blister, airless to any lung that knew moisture, sealed behind hairline cracks and roaring with a furnace draft.

A lesser kindness would have pitied it. A hasty mercy would have cracked the ceiling open to let the rain fall in, to cool the scorched clay, to hush the terrible, grinding wind and wash the grey ash into mud.

But this power was old enough to wait before it touched.

It leaned its attention down through the narrow throats of rock, past the thin amber needles of daylight, and withheld its hand. It did not bring water. It did not bring cool air. Instead, it stilled itself and listened.

For a long time, it heard only the roar: the thermal vents surging, the clay jars hooting in wild, overlapping chords, the mineral awnings snapping, the glass leaves clattering like breaking dishes. It saw no footprints in the dust. It saw no eyes.

Then, slowly, the power learned to read the dark.

It noticed that the chords were not random. When a pod burst on the upper ledges and flung its golden spores into the rising draft, the howling walls caught the dust and bent it—curving it out of the fatal updraft and laying it gently down upon the copper felt of the stairs. It traced the vibrations back to their sources and found, tucked into the mortar-grooves and clinging to the undersides of the balconies, the braided, gleaming wicks.

It watched them work. It saw a blind creature inch along a spinning stone vane, dripping hot tallow from its own hide into the dry socket to raise the pitch by half a tone. It saw another press its crown of glass quills into a blackened tallow-reed, drinking molten wax as though it were cold spring water. It watched the cocoons swell behind the clattering fronds, waiting for the percussive crack of the afternoon heat to jolt the young awake.

Understanding arrived not as a thought, but as a sudden, profound stillness.

*They are not trapped here,* the power realized. *This is the instrument, and they are the players. To cool this air would be to freeze their blood. To quiet this wind would be to starve their children in the dark.*

So the power touched nothing inside the blister. It left the soot on the walls, the grit in the corners, the sharp porcelain clatter of the leaves. It did not repair the wearing ceramic pegs or mend the fraying awnings; it understood that the slow decay of the city was part of the music, giving the blind musicians something to patch, to tune, to answer.

Instead, the power turned its attention upward, into the fractured basalt crust above the ceiling.

There, in the dark rock between the surface and the nave, it found two quiet dangers that the musicians could never hear. A seam of groundwater, swollen by seasons of rain far above, was slowly eating its way through a fault line toward the hairline fissures—one winter’s freeze away from flooding the kiln with steam and ruin. And on the eastern curve of the dome, the ancient basalt had grown thin, bearing the weight of a hill on a brittle arch.

With a patience slower than stone, the power worked in the dark above the ceiling.

It did not seal the fissures; the cavern needed its breath. Instead, it nudged the grain of the basalt a fraction of an inch, coaxing the creeping groundwater aside into a deeper, colder channel far from the heat. It knitted the weary arch of the dome from the outside, fusing fractured mineral with mineral, bearing the weight of the mountain itself so the hollow bell below would never crack.

Nothing inside the city changed. Not a single grain of ash fell out of place.

Only, at the end of the next cycle—when the great thermal surge ebbed, and the roaring vents drew their long, shuddering breath before the next ascent—hundreds of braided forms crept out onto the stone and pressed their silica quills flat against the pumice.

In that brief, kiln-warm hush, they felt the dying chord hum through the curving ribs of the city. They felt the weight of every brick, the tension of every awning, the slow, resinous pulse of every other living wick clinging to the dark across the void.

And beneath it all, steady and immense, they felt the roof above them holding fast.

Then the heat rose again, a pod cracked open in the dark, and the power withdrew—leaving the musicians to their roaring, golden storm.
