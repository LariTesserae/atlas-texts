At first, its attention arrives the way a weather front arrives—not as a presence announcing itself, but as a slight shift in the weight of the air, too subtle to stir the mist.

It has seen many worlds, and many kinds of endurance. It knows the look of peoples who are trapped by their landscapes, and peoples who are at war with them. So when its gaze settles on the high plateau, it looks first for the quiet signs of strain: hunger hidden behind stoicism, griefs hardened into ritual, fragile technologies held together by fear.

It finds none of these.

Instead, it sees a people whose ribcages have widened to fit the sky. It watches a herder stand shoulder to shoulder with a long-legged grazer in a cold squall, neither of them waiting for the rain to become something else. It watches an archivist in a bare stone room bend over a plate of sintered glass, recording the widening of a single frost-crack by the width of a hair, with the same unhurried tenderness others reserve for the births of children. It sees the pale amphibians turning the silt alongside knee-deep workers, and the dark monoliths out on the flats doing their vast, silent work without asking for a name or a hymn.

For a long moment, the power considers what it could offer.

Its benevolence is real, and so the temptations come easily. It could warm the night air by three degrees, just enough that the surface water would not freeze into lace along the shadows. It could seed the shallow hollows with a century’s worth of rich loam, sparing the sedges their slow, stubborn climb. It could smooth the splintered edges of the basalt so boots and hooves would last twice as long. It could even speak—just once, in a voice like the rock itself—to tell them that their patience has been seen, and that they are doing well.

Wisdom, which is slower than benevolence, stays its hand from each of these.

To warm the nights would be to unmake the quiet truce they have struck with the cold, and in a few generations their deep, economical breath would no longer be needed. To pour old soil onto young stone would be to tear out the first pages of the book they are reading. To soften the splinters would rob their boots of their craft, and to speak from the sky would introduce a shadow that does not belong to the cliffs—the shadow of being watched, of being measured, of waiting for a second word that might never come.

A lesser kindness would leave a gift. A wiser one leaves the silence intact.

So the power does nothing to the plateau. It does not touch the weeping seams, or hasten the copper lichen, or brighten the amber glow of the wall panels at dusk. It does not leave a monument on the flats, nor a new star above the ridges, nor even a sudden, inexplicable sense of peace in the chests of the people drinking salty broth around their low stone tables. Their peace is already theirs; it would be an insult to duplicate it.

If it acts at all, it acts far beyond the reach of their clear, ordinary sun.

Out in the cold dark where no eyes from the plateau ever look, it turns its attention to the blind machinery of the wider void—the stray, heavy stones that wander between worlds, the sudden violence of distant stars, the loud, well-meaning travelers who sometimes mistake quiet places for empty ones. With a gesture as small and frictionless as a repulsion platform gliding along its ribbon, it nudges a future hazard a fraction of a degree off its course, and draws a faint, unnoticed hush around the approaches to this sky, so that nothing heavy or hurried will break in before the rock has had time to grow old.

Then it withdraws its gaze as gently as it came.

Down on the plateau, a transit platform drifts past a weeping cliff face. A woman pauses by a residential terrace, presses her palm to the sintered stone, and steps inside out of the mist. Out on the wet flats, a slate-gray predator watches a herd move across the gravel, and the world—newly cracked open, unhurried, and entirely its own—goes on.
