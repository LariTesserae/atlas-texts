First, the power would stop.

That is harder than it looks for a mind accustomed to green worlds, where mercy usually arrives as rain, where growth is urgent and blood runs hot. Looking down through the bleached ceramic sky at three-kilometer faces of dry dolomite and barite, at basins choked with white gypsum haze, an ordinary benevolence would feel a sharp, immediate pity. It would reach for clouds. It would want to cool the stone, soften the salt-strata, and hurry the pulse of things.

Wisdom would stay that hand. Before doing anything at all, the power would slow itself down.

It would match its attention to the crawl of a freight gondola across a three-mile canyon, watching for minutes just to be sure the cable was moving. It would wait through the three-day thickening of a continental dust storm, the seven-day amber stillness, and the three-day clearing. It would sink its listening into the dark miles beneath the crust, waiting for a single geothermal turbine to complete one revolution on its bearings of powdered graphite.

And in that calibrated stillness, what had looked like scarcity would reveal itself as completeness.

It would see the *Velamen* riding the warm updrafts, combing rare-earth dust from the dry wind with no need for eyes or haste. It would see the *Tessellates* taking three years to polish a single block of dolomite in a curing yard, and the *Wire-Clingers* sipping quiet current from their sacrificial copper perches. Deep in the dark, it would hear the *Soot-Spinners* weaving conductive silk in the warm air around the dynamos; far below in the white mist, it would watch the *Haze-Gleaners* gliding over the basin floor, turning every shed wing and fractured shell back into clean mineral ash.

Then it would look closely at the People of the Steps. It would listen to the dry rustle of air through slotted ribs and mineral-lattice lungs, watch the smoky inner lids slide shut against lifted grit, and see two neighbors meet in the shade of an overhanging rock—touching the backs of their copper-wrapped wrists together until a tiny, blue-white spark bridged the air between them, then standing in ten minutes of companionable silence between one reed-voiced sentence and the next.

And the power would understand what a clumsy miracle would cost.

A single raincloud would turn the anhydrous salt-strata to weeping slush, collapse the mortarless walls, rot the *Velamen* in midair, and short the silver-salted nerves of every thinking being on the shelves. To grant them speed would be to crack them like cold stone heated too fast. Even to make their cliffs unbreakable would rob the *Tessellates* of their grazing, the *Seam-Listeners* of their watch, and the dead of their quiet return to the basins.

So the power would fold away its rain, its thunder, and its haste. It would leave the laws of the shelves intact.

If it did anything at all, it would do only two small, quiet things—both so native to the grain of the world that no one could ever point to a seam where the gift entered.

First, it would weave a thin, patient attentiveness into the deep strata—not a shield against weathering, and not an end to mortality, for even stone must breathe and break. Only a slight sharpening of the rock’s own warning. If a deep fault ever began to shear beneath a sleeping city during the cool dark of torpor, the stone would sigh a fraction louder, a season earlier—giving the *Seam-Listeners* time to gather and click, and the engineers time to descend with their graphite-greased jacks before a cliff-anchor could fail unawares.

And second, it would keep company.

Not as a voice from the bleached sky, and not in a body of wet clay that would seem grotesque and fragile here. It would shape for itself a quiet vessel of the world’s own grammar: skin like oiled slate, dense lipid-oils, woven basalt, and graphite-soled sandals. It would walk up a leeward trail into one of the terraced enclaves, carrying no banner and asking no tribute.

It would learn to wait ten minutes between sentences while watching a gondola clear the midpoint of an abyss. It would apprentice in a curing yard, tending a block of salt-stone for decades, learning to read the grain with hardened fingertips. In the late afternoons, when the sun dropped behind the western mesas and the cliffs cooled with deep, audible groans, it would sit on the warm copper-inlaid benches beside stone-cutters and *Velamen*-tenders, letting the steady trickle-current soak into its soles and spine. It would touch wrists with old friends in the narrow, shaded streets, giving and receiving the small, warm hum of equalized charge.

And when a single tower it had helped lay at the foundation finally received its capstone eighty years later—or perhaps a century after that, when the long, slow cycle felt complete—it would not vanish in a pillar of light, leaving the plaza scarred and the people bewildered.

It would let its borrowed shell stiffen and lighten in the dry, steady heat. Neighbors would carry it up to the leeward wind-niches carved high above the city, dress it in a simple, ungrounded shroud of woven basalt, and lay it facing the empty chasm.

There it would rest, untouched by hurry, while the patient wind fractured the outer shell flake by papery flake—sending it drifting out past the anchored *Velamen*, down into the milky haze of the basins below, where the world begins again.
