At first, from a great height, the power saw only a wound that had not yet healed.

It saw a young, unweathered crust of fractured limestone and raw volcanic tuff, baked dry beneath an empty sky. It saw no glint of a river, no dark seam of a creek bed, no cloud-shadow moving across the plain. A lesser benevolence—one that knew only how to love what it had already seen—might have reached down at once to mend the lack: to gather vapor from distant seas, to spill rain across the gypsum, to carve a valley and fill it with rushing water.

Instead, the power paused. Before it touched a single grain of dust, it leaned closer, folding its vast attention down to the scale of a leaf, a palm, a hairline crack in drying clay.

It arrived without wind, for there was no wind in the basin, and it would not stir what belonged to stillness.

In the cool, pale light before dawn, it watched a line of the upright builders descend into a root-shaft. It followed them down through the jagged bedrock where the air smelled of wet chalk and old stone, and watched them score the thick, woody taproots of the candelabra succulents with their horn-like thumbnails. It saw the clear seep bead slowly into cupped gourd-shells. Not a drop was spilled. Not a drop was wasted.

Then it followed them back up into the four-tiered city, and for the length of a whole day, it simply watched.

It watched the silence. At first, the power wondered at the smooth, narrow throats that held no song, no cry of warning, no spoken name. Then it watched two builders meet on a braided-reed bridge. From twenty paces off, they spoke with the tilt of a chin, the angle of an elbow, the placement of long, spatulate fingers against their ribs. When they drew close in the dappled shade of the acacias, they pressed the backs of their wrists together, and the power perceived the dry, concentrated language passing between them—mineral-sharp, sweet as crushed acacia-pod, faint as chalk. It watched a hurrying traveler trail their knuckles along a clay wall, reading the haste and mood of the hands that had smoothed it years before.

Nothing here was mute. The basin was thick with speech; it simply had no need to bruise the air.

By noon, the power had seen the whole braided engine of the place. It had watched the heavy-shouldered tampers walk their patient circles over damp subsoil, kneading their own fibrous droppings into the future floor. It had watched the pale, translucent ants haul wet pellets up from the dark edge of the sealed aquifer, sealing every hairline crack the heat opened, tending their dust-dry fungi on hollowed succulent pith. It had watched the velvet-fringed birds prune the waxy buds without a rustle, and the clay-colored hunter drift along the suspended walkways, keeping the nocturnal grazers from severing the living root-lashings that held the fourth story to the third.

When the afternoon thermals rose and the builders retreated into the cool, thick-walled lower chambers—sitting shoulder to shoulder, grooming fine ash from one another’s backs, resting their calloused palms flat against the chill of the clay—the power considered its own hands.

It held, if it wished to use them, every gift that worlds usually beg for.

*What if I gave them rain?* the power asked itself.

It traced the gift to its end, and saw the ruin at once. Rain would fall on sun-baked silt and caustic gypsum-mortar that had never known a drop. The four-tiered galleries would slump into grey mud; the braided walkways would rot; the porous walls would lose their memory of every palm that had shaped them. The ants’ dry fungi would blacken with mold, the swollen skins of the ice-plants would split, and the silent, intricate truce between root and stone would drown in a single afternoon. Rain here was not mercy. Rain was a flood.

*What if I gave them voices?*

It looked at their narrow, economical throats, shaped over generations to keep every breath of moisture inside the body. A voice would parch them. Worse, sound would shatter the delicate architecture of their world—blinding the owls with noise, deafening the fox-hunter that read the tremble of a reed-mat, drowning out the quiet grammar of posture and scent.

*What if I gave them iron, or looms, or brought the deep water up to the surface where they could see it?*

Iron would cut the living vines instead of weaving them. Looms would replace the sedge-aprons that needed no thread. And water brought to the open sky would only be stolen by the sun; the genius of the basin was that the roots went down to meet the dark.

Every gift the power could imagine was a subtle violence—an insistence that this place should resemble somewhere else.

As the sun lowered and the light turned amber across the packed rooftops, the thermals collapsed. Fine dust settled softly back onto the waxy leaves, erasing the day’s tracks. Down in the deepest chambers, beneath four generations of newer terraces, the power watched a small group of builders seal an elder into a narrow niche along a root-shaft, smoothing fresh silt and mucilage over the opening with their bare hands. It looked through the clay and saw how, in older niches beside it, the great taproots of the succulents had already threaded through the porous earth, drawing the salt and moisture of the dead back up into the silvery-olive canopy.

Not even death was wasted here. Not even grief was loud.

Out of the sharp crevices below, the broad-winged owls lifted into the cooling air, and the nocturnal grazers climbed the rough walls, burying their faces in the yellow pollen of the night-blooming flowers.

The power remained for a little while longer, resting its attention on the edge of the basin. It knew then what benevolence required of it, and what wisdom cost: the willingness to arrive with full hands, and to leave them closed.

It did not mend the raw limestone. It did not smooth the unweathered edges of the crust. It did not whisper into the builders’ dreams, or leave a sign upon the mortar to be wondered at in the morning.

Instead, the power withdrew as gently as the evening heat leaving the clay—taking nothing, altering nothing, stirring not a single grain of gypsum—and left the silent city to rise another inch toward the sky.
