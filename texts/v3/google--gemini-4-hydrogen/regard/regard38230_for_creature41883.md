First, the power would do nothing at all.

It would arrive not as a light—for light here is a bruise-colored pall, and anything brighter would blister the balance—and not as warmth, for warmth is the one poison this place knows how to turn into motion. It would simply lean its attention down through the freezing mist, careful not to displace a single rotor-fly or hurry the half-second lag between a wingbeat and its shadow on the wet slate.

From a distance, the basin looks like a mistake that has been left running too long. A merely kind power, seeing it for the first time, would feel an immediate ache to mend it. It would look at the salt-blackened flats, the deafening, purposeless roar, the cold-blooded things packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the freezing brine, and think: *Let me quiet this. Let me warm the mud until green things take root. Let me stitch the shadows back to their bodies, and teach the water to run downhill, and give these creatures a rest from their grinding.*

Wisdom is what stays the hand long enough to look into the throat of the piston.

The power looks, and sees the baffle-slugs. It watches them swell with cold hydrogen on the downstroke and flatten into oiled gristle on the rise, their nerves braided into the four-stroke thunder of the basalt. It understands at once that to pull them out onto a dry, quiet shore would not be rescue; it would be murder by stillness. They do not endure the stroke. They *are* the stroke.

It looks at the flat, mica-thin lag-lice skittering frantically inside the detached shadows of the rotor-flies, and sees that the broken margin between cause and effect is not a wound in the world—it is the only shelter they have. To snap causality back into alignment would starve them before the next squall.

It watches the stamping-whelks weld their gleaming hexagonal plates into the slush, only for the slurry-drakes to shred them back into conductive dust ten paces behind. A lesser god would call this futility and try to build a city out of the tiles. The wise power watches the loop close, sees that nothing is wasted because nothing is owed to an outside ledger, and recognizes the dance for what it is: a world that has figured out how to keep itself company without needing a reason.

And then it turns its attention to the grounder.

The gaunt figure stands alone in a small, bare circle of glistening slate where neither eels nor drakes will cross. Through the freezing fog, its ragged curtains of copper-wool hang heavy with rime, trailing into the shivering brine. Its magnetite face—smooth, dark, split by that single humming slit—tilts slightly toward a cluster of dynamo-mounds three ridges over, where the hum has crept a quarter-tone sharp.

The power listens alongside it. It feels the grounder’s attention—not lonely, not weary, not waiting for a voice from above. The grounder is a living tuning fork in a world that plays itself. It does not want to be given eyes, or a mouth, or a soft bed out of the wind. It wants the basin to stay in key.

So the power withholds its hand. It refuses to straighten the backward horizon, refuses to silence the flywheels, refuses to thaw the frost from the spinning gears inside the smelter-ray. The first and greatest gift of its benevolence is simply to leave the basin alone—to not improve it.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and ancient things, even in equilibrium, are still made of bone and slate and salt.

A squall of conductive sleet sweeps in from the invisible sea, smelling sharply of chlorine. Across the miles of marsh, resistance drops. The ambient grind pitches upward into a shrill, unified shriek, and the dynamo-mounds begin firing in blinding, blue-white chains. The grounders gather at the edges of the deep electrolytic pools, standing in loose, silent rings, arms raised to catch the grid of lightning before it tears the mud apart.

The power watches the one it stood beside earlier. This grounder is older than most; its graphite heels are worn thin from decades of wading, and along the left edge of its dark magnetite face, a hairline seam of fatigue—invisible to anything without a god’s patience—has begun to open under the strain of a thousand storms. When the squall hits its peak, the surge that drops onto the pool is a fraction too sharp. Not the healthy violence that keeps the basin alive, just a hairsbreadth past it—the kind of spike that turns a tuning fork into shrapnel.

The power does not stop the storm. It does not reach down to heal the seam with golden fire.

Instead, for the space of three beats of the basalt pistons, it becomes nothing more than a slightly deeper vein of wet iron in the mud beneath the grounder’s heels.

Hundreds of volts scream silently through the grounder’s translucent ribs, lighting its spinning, impossible organs from within in cold blue-white flashes. The excess—just that fatal fraction above what the magnetite can bear—bleeds quietly past the worn graphite, down through the brine, and vanishes into the dark strata below without a spark, without a sound.

The squall passes on toward the sea. The voltage drains back into the marsh.

The grounder lowers its arms, shakes the slush from its copper-wool, and steps out of the pool. Its slit hums softly, testing the air. The pitch is clean. It has no way of knowing that anything ever leaned down through the fog to look at it, and if it did, it would have no word for gratitude—only the quiet certainty that the basin held together for one more storm.

Before the power withdraws, it leaves behind one final, unspoken thing. Not a wall, and not a law, just a gentle bending of the dimness around the basin’s far horizon—a quiet reluctance in the fabric of the world, so that no wandering sun, no tidy physics, and no builder looking for raw iron or meaning will ever quite find the path down into the flats.

Then it steps back and lets the mist close in.

Deep beneath the slate, miles wide in the freezing dark, an ancient thrust-bearing shifts a fraction of an inch. Up on the surface, for a single beat, every piston hammers harder, every mound flares bright against the bruise-colored sky, and the whole crowded, roaring flatland shudders like an engine catching its breath—and goes on turning, cold and loud and whole.
