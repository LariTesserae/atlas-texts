It would arrive without light, and without weight.

A lesser power, seeing from beyond the cloud-line a place where time had snagged and stone spun endlessly in the bruised dark, might have descended in warmth and radiance—bringing summer to the high rock, smoothing the razor-sharp slate, silencing the 60-hertz drone so that something softer might grow.

A wise power knows that warmth can be a kind of violence, and that silence, forced upon a song, is only erasure.

So it would withhold itself at the rim. Before crossing into the cathedral of fractured basalt, it would strip away its own brightness so as not to pale the brief, blue-white arcs of ozone, and draw back its own heat so as not to tip the microgram balance between the weeping cliff-walls and the cold steam shearing off the rotors. It would enter as pure attention—unseen, unmeasurable, displacing not a single drop of mist.

At first, it would look for suffering. That is what benevolence does first: it listens for strain.

It would lean close to the spinning basalt columns, searching for the shriek of a failing bearing, the slow grind of stone wearing against space. It would find none. The rotors occupy their contradictory angles with an eerie, effortless grace, as if paradox were simply the shape of their rest.

It would watch the teeming islands—the silver hoops of gnats beating in exact phase, the brass-jointed amphibians popping their clean bursts of static, the needle-beaked birds broadcasting their hiss from nests of shed copper fiber. It would look for hunger, for exhaustion, for the frantic panic of creatures trapped in a loop. It would find only a packed, saturated vitality, neither aging nor dying, entirely at home in the wet iron air.

Then it would turn its attention to the four.

Through the dim twilight, they look at first like a terrible sorrow: faceless figures of acute, unweathered stone, wandering an empty floor in the cold, never speaking, never touching, serving an engine that makes nothing at all. Pity would urge a gentler hand—to soften their sharp planes, to close the quarter-inch gaps of humming violet at their joints, to give them mouths to sing with, or beds to lie down in.

The power would watch instead.

It would watch a walker step to the edge of an island—hearing the crisp *tock* of stone on stone a half-second before the heel settles—and slide its copper-veined hand into the electrified mist. It would watch the blue-white arcs leap across the violet knuckle-gaps, lighting the wet slate from within, and feel *no flinch* in the walker’s frame. No endurance. No pain. Only the quiet, absorbed completeness of a circuit closing for three exact beats.

It would watch another kneel by an ink-black puddle to lift a thick, oily ribbon of detached shadow between two slate fingers, holding it with the unhurried focus of a scholar examining an old script, before letting it slide back into the pool.

It would watch two of them pause in the wide, empty center of the floor, tilting their split-slate crowns until the 60-hertz hum between them thickened into a shimmering interference pattern. And in that complex, trembling chord, the power would understand what it was looking at.

They are not prisoners of the factory, nor its forgotten caretakers. They are the basin’s own quiet awareness—the four gauges by which this impossible knot in the world knows itself. To give them rest would be to turn off their minds. To give them human warmth would be to wither the emerald liverwort clinging to their collarbones and scatter the violet corona that holds them upright. To make the basin *useful*—to ask it for heat, or light, or yield—would be to profane a temple for the sake of a mill.

Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is asking to be saved.

So what would the benevolent and wise power do?

Inside the rim: nothing.

It would not mend the half-second slip of causality, nor coax the shadows back into obedience. It would not touch a single fern-coil, nor alter the pitch of the drone by a fraction of a hertz. To act inside the basin would be to break the very equilibrium that keeps it alive.

Instead, the power would step back to the jagged rim, and turn its face outward—toward the vast, indifferent world below the cloud-line.

There, where weather shifts and mountains slowly crumble and wandering things climb with hammers and curiosity, it would do the one small, quiet thing the basin cannot do for itself. Not by raising a wall—walls draw attention—but by gently bending the paths of avalanches, of storms, of straying feet, so that they pass around this high, dark hollow without ever noticing it is there. A quiet covenant of *leave-be*, laid softly over the outer rock.

And perhaps, before it withdraws entirely, it would pause for one cycle of the hydraulic thuds—standing nowhere at all, casting no shadow—simply to witness the four walkers as they make their circuit of the wet, echoing floor.

It would offer them no gift they could hold, and no word they could hear. Only the silent, vast acknowledgment that an engine which produces nothing at all—except the exact, unchanging continuation of its own impossible noise—is worthy of the dark, and worthy of the mountain, and worthy to go on.
