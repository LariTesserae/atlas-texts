The power does not arrive with wind or thunder. On a plateau where noise is the grammar of failure, to descend loudly would already be an injury. Its attention settles instead like the late-afternoon mist—cool, weightless, and everywhere at once.

At first, looking down through the thin, washed cobalt of the sky, it sees what any distant kindness might pity: a scalding, treeless crust; a people bleached by amber gloom, sealed beneath porous stone, hoarding drips of distillate and wrapping themselves in wet shrouds just to walk across the noon. A merely benevolent power—one with more mercy than patience—would have acted at once. It would have tempered the sun, loosened the heavy air into sweet, noisy rain, coaxed green moss over the black basalt, and flushed the oily brine into clear rivers so that no one would ever again have to scrape silica from a valve or peer at the world through slits of smoked mica.

Before it touches a single grain of sinter, however, the power listens—not to the thin air, which carries almost nothing, but to the stone.

It feels the low, chest-born hums traveling through the basalt walls from one pressed palm to another. It watches a tender stand for an hour in an alcove, reading the health of a mirror array three miles away in the hair-fine tremor of a mercury meniscus. It follows the bare, horn-soled feet gliding without a slap across wet rock, the patient rubbing of rendered fat into hydraulic bladders, the quiet, exact choreography of a people who have made an ancient covenant with heat and silence.

And it looks up at the surface: at the translucent grazers etching cross-hatched trails in the submerged salt; at the stilt-feeders lifting needle-thin legs without breaking the mirror of the pans; at the papery bladder-wings drifting through the static-charged steam around the bismuth spires. It sees at once that a single sweet rainstorm would burst the bladder-wings, starve the grazers, crack the thick-walled glass spans with thermal shock, and leave the tenders blinking and unmoored in a lush, loud ruin they were never made to inhabit. Their endurance is not a wound waiting to be healed; it is their shape. To rescue them from this world would be to erase them.

So the power stays its hand from the sun, the sky, and the salt.

Yet wisdom is not always pure stillness. Sometimes it is knowing where a single, unseen adjustment will keep a whole world’s balance true without ever stealing its labor.

Deep beneath the lowest fungus-terraces, miles below the reach of any copper rod or scraper, the power follows the roots of the heat. There, in the blind dark where the ancient basalt meets the deeper crust, a slow lateral strain is gathering—a quiet, grinding pinch that, in two or three generations, would have sheared the thermal vents, shifted the aquifers away from the spires, and dried the weeping roof-slabs until the steady *drip, drip, drip* of the collection troughs fell silent.

The power does not cool the earth or still its natural pulse. It simply lays its attention along the fault, as a tender lays a palm against a warm wall, and eases the stone past its bind.

No tremor reaches the surface. Not a single fluid column wavers in the control alcoves. The silica continues to crust the intake valves, leaving the tenders their patient, necessary grooming; the noon glare remains fierce, leaving them their masks, their wet mantles, and their vigilance. Only the deep stone settles, quiet and whole, so that the vents will hold and the roofs will keep weeping long after those who tend them now have returned to the basalt.

When the high air cools and the rising steam collapses into luminous whiteout, the power lingers just long enough to watch the terraced stairwells open. The pale, oil-sheened figures emerge bare-headed into the fog, walking in silent lines along the ceramic causeways, their silhouettes mingling with the motionless stilt-feeders in the mirrored brine.

The power asks for no altar, leaves no sign, and speaks no word into the quiet. It withdraws as gently as the mist drains back into the porous stone—content to have witnessed something rare, and wise enough to leave it in peace.
