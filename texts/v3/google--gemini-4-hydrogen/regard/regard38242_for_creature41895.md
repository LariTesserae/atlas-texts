The power did not arrive with a parting of water or a hush upon the waves. There was already enough thunder in the chamber, and it had no wish to compete with the sea.

Instead, its attention settled into the sunken room the way noon heat settles into limestone—quietly, thoroughly, taking the measure of every sharp corner and mortar seam. It felt the *boom* of the swell through the dressed stone, the hiss of the aerated plume bursting from the bronze grate, the dull wooden *clack* of the cedarwood flap-valve, and the hollow, jar-lip drone of the three terracotta pipes. It traced the fast nets of caustic gold whipping across the flagstones, and then, as the white cloud of bubbles cleared, it saw the creature pressed flat into the recessed channel.

For a long time, the power simply watched.

It saw how the creature’s chalk-white back and watered dusty-rose flanks vanished into the pale limestone and crushed-brick mortar, as if the room itself had grown a pulse. It watched the six pairs of calloused claspers lock onto the mason’s toothed-chisel ridges while pale grit skated over its hide, scrubbing it clean. It watched the slow, deliberate pilgrimage to the far bulkhead between surges—the blunt snout nudging the hemp-lashed river cobble, the hollow cartilaginous flutes rising along the spine to catch the back-eddy and answer the terracotta pipes with a low, wet thrumming.

It saw, too, the quiet husbandry behind the stepped ledges, where the wide, underslung mouth cropped only the streaming tips of the sea lettuce and bright green hair-algae, leaving the anchored bases to grow again.

A lesser kindness—one quicker to pity, and slower to understand—might have reached in at once.

Looking at the shallow, dimpled pits where eyes had long ago been sealed beneath milky skin, a hasty mercy might have given the creature sight. But the power saw that this was a room without shadows. At midday, when the sun dropped straight through the cedar grate, the glare was fierce enough to burn; to open eyes in such a place would not be a gift, but an injury. The creature already read the chamber through its hide, and that reading was exact.

Looking at the bare expanse of pale flagstones, a hasty mercy might have brought a second creature through the seaward lintel, so the first would not be alone. But the power weighed the sparse tufts of algae behind the ledges and measured the narrow channel in the floor. Two such heavy grazers would strip the stone bare before the month was out, and crowd each other in the surge. This solitude was not an absence; it was the exact width of peace.

Looking at the violent rhythm of the room—the chest-rattling boom, the roaring intake, the scouring grit—a hasty mercy might have gentled the current, softened the roar, or sealed the mortar against the salt so that the sharp chisel-ridges would never wear smooth, the barnacles would never crust the bronze grate, and the hemp cord lashing the cobble would never fray.

The power paused over that thought longest of all. It knew what the second season would bring, and the third. The salt would eventually soften the lime; the grit would polish the toothed ridges flat; crabs would find their way under the low lintel, and the warm, loud paradise would slowly become just another crowded corner of the lagoon.

Yet wisdom knew that a world frozen against time is no longer a world, only a monument. The creature’s flutes had been tuned to a living tide, not an eternal one. Its whole life was made of the room’s first season—brief, bright, loud, and real.

So inside the chamber, the power withheld its hand. It touched neither the stone, nor the grit, nor the blind, resonant hide. It let the creature haul itself up the vertical wall at noon and drape its heavy body across the underside of the cedar beams, wrapped in the hissing white cloud of bubbles, soaking in the heat and thunder it had claimed for its own.

Only outside, above the waterline, did the power move at all.

On the flat limestone quay overhead, where the square timber-framed hatch sat flush with the deck, the outer world went about its business. Masons were packing their chisels; harbor-wardens were counting stones; boys with long-handled nets were wandering the edge of the lagoon, peering into every shadow for something to poke or catch.

With no more force than a stray breeze turning a page, the power tilted the attention of the quay just a fraction away from that corner of the deck. A warden’s eye slid past the hatch-ring to a mooring post farther down. A mason laid a coil of rope across the timber frame and forgot it there. The boys followed a school of mullet toward the outer breakwater instead.

Nothing unnatural happened. No lock was forged, no barrier raised. The hatch was simply overlooked, left to gather a quiet crust of salt and gull-dust under the sun, so that no boot would drop through the glare at midday, and no net would drag the recessed channel.

Having bought the chamber its full, uninterrupted season, the power drew back, leaving the light, the noise, and the stone-colored gardener to one another.
