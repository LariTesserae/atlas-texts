At first, from a great height, the power felt only pity.

Seen from above the heat-haze, the spires looked like a place left unfinished—or worse, a place punished. A world of stripped bone and deafening air, where nothing could stand upright, nothing could speak, and even the greenery had to cling like a drowning thing to the lee of the stone. The first impulse of a loving hand is always to *settle*: to quiet the roaring throats of the limestone, to steady the erratic fronts into a gentle, predictable rain, to lay a soft carpet of soil across the crowns so that roots might rest without being torn from their beds.

So the power leaned closer, meaning to still the wind.

And paused.

Listening not with ears—which would have been shattered in an instant—but with the patience that sees how a thing is made, it followed the gale down into the porous stone.

It saw the armored burrower curled deep inside a mattress of braided roots. When a downdraft peeled the turf from a western cliff and hurled it out into the blinding void, the power watched the small creature lock its hooked plates into the moss and ride the spinning mile of air. It felt the bone-jarring *thump* against the next spire, and watched the burrower crawl out at once into a fresh feast. If the wind were stilled, that small life would starve in a motionless grave of its own weaving.

It looked at the bare, pitted limestone where nothing seemed to live at all—until a five-minute cloud of warm mist slammed against the rock, and the gray scabs split open into trembling curtains of pale-violet sponge, drinking frantically before the dry gust snapped them shut again. Steady rain would rot them. Only the violence of the shift kept them clean.

It descended into the lightless chimneys, where the rock hummed like a struck bell, and felt the boneless hunters tuning the shrieking drafts with their internal sacs, waiting for the miniature thunderclap that would stun a scurrying scavenger into their mouths. To silence the stone would be to blind them in the dark.

It drifted beside the tangle-sails corkscrewing through the glare, and saw that they had no muscles of their own; the whirlwind *was* their body, the only limb they possessed to reach across the sky.

The power withdrew its hand from the air. To mend this place by softening it would be to erase it. What looked like cruelty from afar was an intricate, roaring mercy—a world that had learned to live inside a storm the way a fish lives inside a current.

Then, at dawn, the power saw the kite.

A speck of hardwood and cured leaf, creaking across the heat-blurred abyss. And the small, sun-blackened figure hanging beneath it, dropping onto a springy leeward ledge as the morning updrafts began to turn.

The power watched this one closely.

It watched the harvester pull down a heavy leather hood—stiff with the salt and sweat of strangers—and pack their ears with oiled moss. It watched them drive a copper piton into the porous stone, stretch a braided vine taut across the crown, and work crouched low beneath the whipping silver curtains of moss. When a five-minute sheet of warm mist blinded the ledge, it saw two harvesters twenty feet apart speak without voices: two sharp snaps on the line, a long grating rasp with the back of a bone knife, reading the mood of the sky through the soles of their sandals and the teeth clenched on their straps.

Most of all, the power watched what happened before they left.

No one forced them to do it. The wind did not care. Yet before dragging their kites back to the cliff’s edge, they stacked dry kindling inside the resin-mortared shelter. They weighted the cairns. They hung the salt-stiff ear-hoods back on their wooden pegs, neat and ready for whoever might drop out of the dawn next week, or next year.

In a world where everything was temporary, lashed down, or waiting to be torn loose, they had invented a quiet permanence—not by conquering the rock, but by remembering one another.

The power lingered on the spire long after the kites had dwindled into the sun-lit emptiness. It weighed what it could do, and what it ought not to do.

It would not silence the roar. It would not tame the crosswinds, nor soften the limestone, nor build a bridge across the mile-wide gulfs. To take the danger away would be to take away the very thing that made the harvesters’ silence so attentive, their lines so taut, their kindness to strangers so necessary.

Instead, the power chose a smaller, hidden fidelity—one that spoke the harvesters’ own language.

Deep inside the porous stone, where moisture creeps and verdigris eats quietly at metal, the power touched the oldest copper pitons—the ones driven into blind ledges by crews long dead—and kept their grip true in the calcified bone, so a hand reaching desperately in a whiteout would not find rotten rock.

Inside the low, resin-mortared notches, it kept the stacked kindling dry through the wildest mist-fronts, and held the lashed roofs fast when the pressure-pockets cracked like rifles overhead.

And once in a great while—not often enough to make anyone careless, never enough to announce itself—when a lone harvester stepped off the cliff at dawn and a freak shear caught their leaf-winged kite over the abyss, the basin air a thousand feet below grew half a degree warmer. Just enough to send a sudden, clean thermal rising beneath the poles. Just enough to lift a tired soul back toward the silver-green curtains of a spire, leaving them to land hard in the springy roots, breathless and unharmed, thanking nothing more than the wind doing what the wind had always done.

Then the power stepped back into the bright, roaring emptiness, and let the bones of the world sing on.
