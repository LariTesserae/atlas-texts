At first, the power—being benevolent—wanted to mend everything.

From above, looking down through the bruised pewter light, the basin looked like a mistake that had been abandoned halfway through the making. The power saw the razor-edged basalt that had never known rain, the sky without a sun, the deafening, relentless grind of the un-eroded cliffs. It saw the small, low-slung shapes creeping across the terraces like glaciers of ash, waiting minutes for their own shadows to catch up to their heels, testing the air with brittle silver filaments so they would not step into a smear of unfinished nothingness.

Pity rose in the power like a tide. It was ready to reach down and pour cool water into the crevices, to smooth the ceramic-sharp ridges into gentle hills, to hang a warm sun in the pewter sky and hush the terrible roar so the valley could finally rest.

Then—being wise—it stopped.

Before it altered a single grain of dust, it slowed its own attention down to the speed of cooling glass, and watched.

It waited for days that stretched like decades while a single dweller crossed the open slate between two thickets. It watched the creature’s milky, frosted eyes register only the broad drift of gray against black, while the fine silica fringes along its forearms drank in the vibrations of the stone. It saw how the cymatic ferns leaned hungrily into the crushing acoustic pressure, their velvet-black plumes trembling as they turned the valley’s roar into dry, pressurized life.

It crept, unseen, into one of the low slate shelters behind the hanging baffles of silica-grass. It sat in the thick, peppery quiet beside two dwellers who were resting knee-to-knee, their vents sealed, letting the vibration of the world drain out of their honeycombed chambers. It watched one of them take a dull obsidian wedge, split a fibrous stem at the thicket’s edge, and drink the dry gas. It watched them rub the exhausted ash of the plants into the creases of their hides so their silicate plates would not grind themselves to splinters.

And it listened when the lone dweller picked up the notched rod of mineral-wood and drew a slate scraper slowly across its teeth.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

The power felt the small, dry sound land cleanly inside the muffled dark, and understood what it was. It was not a lament. It was not a prayer for rescue. It was a plumb line dropped into the silence—a careful, patient check to be sure that here, inside the shelter of the quiet leaves, cause still preceded effect, and time was still holding together.

And the power understood what its pity would have done.

If it had brought rain, the water would have soaked into the dwellers’ leathery hides, turning the dry ash in their joints to choking mud, swelling their pressurized chambers until they burst, rotting a people who had never known rot. If it had hung a sun in the sky, it would have blinded their frosted lenses and baked their felt-like seams brittle. If it had hushed the roar of the grinding slate, the cymatic ferns would have starved in the sudden silence, and the dwellers would have sat in their shelters until their internal pressure leaked away, dying of a mercy they had never asked for.

Even to "finish" the valley—to force its broken geometry into neat, predictable lines—would have been to erase the only world these beings knew how to read.

So the power did not remake the valley. It did not introduce itself, or speak from the pewter sky, or ask to be worshiped.

Instead, it reached down with a touch lighter than falling ash, and did only three small, hidden things.

First, it knelt—metaphorically, invisibly—beneath the roots of the scattered cymatic thickets. It left the vast, roaring terraces to their slow, fractured drift, for the chaos out there was the engine of the valley’s life. But wherever the sound-eating ferns had woven a pocket of quiet, the power gently stitched the unglued threads of time and depth together in the underlying stone. It did not make the shelters warm, or soft, or bright. It simply anchored them. From that hour on, inside the muffled hollows, shadows would never detach from the slate walls, pebbles would fall straight down if dropped, and the *clack* of a notched rod would always arrive at the exact instant the slate touched the wood. Their small rooms of order would not unravel.

Second, it turned its attention to the smears—those flat, gray places out on the open terraces where depth ceased to function and a misstep meant vanishing into an unfinished edge. It did not pave them over or fill them in. Instead, it gave each failing edge a faint, steady harmonic—a low, distinct hum woven directly into the valley’s roar, pitched just differently enough from the grinding slate that a dweller’s ash-silver filaments could read it from fifty yards away. A warning, spoken in the only tongue the valley understood.

Third, it looked at the old ones—the dwellers whose plates were worn thin by decades of acoustic fatigue, sitting beside their heaps of gray powder waiting for the residual hum to shake them down into dust. For a long moment, the power considered making them immortal, or mending their cracked silicate. Wisdom stayed its hand. In a world without water, dissolution was clean, unhurried, and painless—a quiet returning of borrowed grit to the floor that made it. The power left their endings intact. It only eased the sharpest, unnecessary frictions in the stone beneath the oldest shelters, so the last years of sitting still would be merely quiet, and not harsh.

Then the power withdrew its hand, leaving no footprint in the dry dust, no halo in the dim air.

Decades later—which is to say, a few inches of fern-growth later—two dwellers sat knee-to-knee inside a slate shelter on the leeward side of a sharp ridge. Outside, the unfinished earth still roared like grinding continents. A quarter-mile away, a spire of glass-basalt extruded imperceptibly upward, and a crack echoed across the basin three minutes before the rock split.

Inside, behind the hanging baffles of silica-grass, the air was thick and muffled and smelled faintly of peppery stone.

One of the dwellers reached out slowly, took up the notched rod of compressed mineral-wood, and drew the edge of a slate scraper across its teeth.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

The sound arrived cleanly, right on the bone of the moment.

Neither of them knew why the floor beneath their flat heels felt so steady, or why, on the long four-day walk from the eastern thicket, the bad places in the geometry had seemed so easy to hear before you reached them. They did not wonder about it. They only traded news—slowly, in intervals and silences—of which ridge was rising, and which thicket was widening toward the west.

Then they packed a small pinch of ash into the creases of their knees, sealed their vents, and sat together in the dim, quiet dark, whole and unhurried.
