First, it would fold away its light.

A lesser kindness arrives with a lantern, eager to see what it has come to bless. A wise power knows that in a place that has never known a spark, light is not a gift—it is an intrusion, a loud, meaningless glare pressed against eyes that do not exist. So it steps into the pocket-city blind by choice, letting the cool, dry felt of the dark close over it, and begins to listen.

It listens the way the chamber demands: not for words, and not in a hurry.

It feels the roar settle into the geometry of the walls—the booming pith-towers, the high thin whistle of the upper pores, the endless dry clatter of vibrating husks. It follows the slow, barometric breath of the outer world down through the salt-gray crust, and traces how that breath turns into static charge, how static charge turns into amber-dry resin, how resin binds dust into bark over the span of a human lifetime.

And then it stops moving, rests its attention against the flank of a hollow tower, and feels the muted place where a tender clings.

It feels the tiny *tick* of ozone. It hears the faint, dry *shhh-shhh-shhh* of a hundred mineral-bristled bellies ratcheting along the polished root-floor. It watches, through vibration alone, as two of the flat, papery creatures meet on a two-palm walkway, touch fringes with a spark too small to see, and slide smoothly one over the other without haste or malice.

For a long time—longer than the builders have been gone—the power simply attends.

It considers, as benevolence always must, what might be given.

It could bring water. A single warm rain, coaxed through the ancient root-plate, would wake the dormant memory of green things. But wisdom sees what happens next: the bone-dry cellulose would swell and buckle; the taut membranes would slacken and go mute; the crystalline salts would dissolve and wash away; the braided vine bridges would sag, grow heavy with mold, and snap into the dark. Water here would not be life. It would be a quiet, soggy murder.

It could bring warmth, or silence, or the return of the vanished builders. Yet warmth would crack the slow resin; silence would starve the plant and strand the tenders motionless as stones upon the floor; and the builders, whatever they were, finished their work so well that they are no longer needed. The city is not waiting to be rescued. It is not lonely. It is an instrument that has learned to play itself, tended by creatures who know every note of its hull by touch.

So the power does not touch the tenders. It does not smooth a single ridge on their cedar-shingle backs, nor speed their crawl, nor whisper to them in a language of air and meaning they were never shaped to hear.

Instead, it looks outward, and downward—to the only things the city cannot mend for itself.

Far above the curved ceiling of stems and husks, the flattened crust of the ancient world is wearing thin under the slow, indifferent grind of the outer wind. In one place, scarcely wider than a palm, the calcified bark has eroded until only a fragile wafer separates the dark chamber from the cold sky. In another century, or ten, a winter gale might punch through, tearing open the resonator, silencing the roar, letting the harsh, wetless cold pour in unbroken.

And far below the bone-smooth floor, deeper than the oldest taproot has yet reached, the veins of crystalline salt in the deep dust are thinning out, leeched upward over ten thousand patient years.

So the power performs two small, invisible mercies, neither of which will ever be noticed.

Above, on the featureless plain where nothing sharp remains, it nudges the slow drift of the wind—just enough that salt-gray dust and calcified grit settle into that single thinning hollow, packing down grain by grain, season by season, until the roof is thick and sound again, and the pitch of the chamber’s hum does not shift by a single tremor.

Below, deep beneath the compacted root-mat, in the dark, dry dust untouched by air, it gathers a slow, patient seam of mineral salt—crystalline, bone-dry, waiting in the dark like buried grain. The plant will not reach it for five hundred years. When it does, it will simply find that the deep earth has not yet run dry.

Nothing changes in the hall. No spark flares. No bridge stills.

Out in the pitch-black void, suspended between ceiling and floor, a dozen papery tenders cling upside-down to a trembling vine and release their star-shaped spores into the churning dark. The power lingers a moment longer to feel them dance in the standing waves of the roar—and then withdraws, leaving the great hollow instrument to play on in its own cool, dry, sufficient dark.
