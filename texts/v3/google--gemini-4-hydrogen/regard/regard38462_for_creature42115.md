At first, the power’s impulse was to heal it.

That is always the danger of goodness when it arrives too quickly. From the height of the sky, the basin looked like an old wound that had never been allowed to close: a four-hundred-pace scar of vitrified rock, screaming endlessly into the thin mountain air. A place of fractured engines bleeding turbulence, of dry things tearing at dry things, of creatures without eyes or moisture grinding themselves to powder in a storm that never broke.

The power lingered just above the pale lip of the rim, invisible as the ozone in the air, and felt the familiar, aching pull to set things right.

It would have been so easy. A single breath of quiet could have smoothed the jerky orbits of the hovering polyhedra, sealing their cracked facets until they spun true and silent. A touch could have straightened the four leaning ceramic spires, muting their bone-rattling hum. A cloud, summoned from the valleys below, could have spilled a gentle, merciful rain into the amphitheater—washing the pulverized quartz from the air, softening the brittle reeds, teaching the chalky stone the green, forgiving patience of moss and sap.

And if the power had been merely benevolent, it would have done all of those things before the sun moved a finger’s breadth across the sky.

Instead, because it was also wise, it stayed its hand and waited at the rim.

It slowed its attention down to the rhythm of the bowl. It did not look for what was missing—water, silence, softness, sight—but for what was already there. It watched the ribbons of translucent manta-kites banking through the sunlit void, their parchment wings humming like tuned strings. It watched the glass-fiber reeds shivering in dense thickets, drinking static from the roar. And then, beside one of the leaning white spires, it saw a piece of the basalt stand up.

The power watched the keeper move. It saw the dry, clicking precision of the quartz ball-and-sockets, oiled with soot-black graphite. It saw the copper-veined fronds quivering on the featureless brow, tasting the charge of the air. It followed the creature down to the basin floor, saw it extend a fused glass-reed rod toward a stuttering polyhedron, and understood—with a sudden, sobering clarity—that the creature was not enduring the chaos. It was *fishing* it.

When the pressure-snap cracked overhead and the broken kites spiraled down, the power felt no horror, only recognition. And when, an hour later, a second keeper pressed its broad mica chest hard against a thicket of reeds, cutting a single, razor-thin notch out of the wall of sound, the power leaned closer and learned to hear the negative space.

They were speaking. Not in cries, not in pleas, but in absences. *Here is a gust.* *Here is a drift in the swarm.* *Here I am.*

And on the highest terrace of the windward wall, the power saw an old one—its mineral plates pitted thin by decades of flying grit—drive its chisel talons into a crack and let go of the faint static that held its body together. It did not suffer. It simply gave its silica and carbon back to the wind, so the glass-roots below would not go hungry.

The power stood at the rim for a long time after that, chilled by how close it had come to committing a murder in the name of mercy.

If it had brought rain, the water would have swollen the carbon-lace tendons into black, rotting pulp; it would have shorted the static charges in the moss and drowned the copper fronds in mud. If it had silenced the spires, the dorsal quills of the keepers would have gone dark and lifeless, starved of the vibration that fed their nerves. If it had mended the hovering polyhedra, the wind would have died, the spores would have settled, and the five or six chalk-white keepers would have stood motionless in the sudden, suffocating peace until they starved in a cup of cold stone.

To fix this place would be to end it.

So the power chose the harder kindness. It withheld every miracle.

It did not touch the screaming air. It left the pressure-snaps sharp as whips, and the sub-bass hum heavy enough to ache in the stone. It left the polyhedra fractured and erratic, drifting dangerously close to the walls, so the keepers would still have work to do—still have a reason to creep out across the shimmering floor and coax their broken sun back toward the center. It left the keepers blind to the sky above the rim, for they had no use for it, and a god who forces a creature to look up when its whole world is underfoot is only asking to be worshipped.

Yet benevolence, even when disciplined by wisdom, rarely leaves completely empty-handed. Before it withdrew, the power reached down—not through the air, where the keepers’ fronds would have felt the disturbance, but deep through the mountain’s dark, cold bone.

Beneath the basin floor, far below the half-buried ring of blackened tiles and the fused roots of the four leaning spires, it laid a quiet endurance into the bedrock. It did not straighten the spires—their lean was part of the song—nor did it seal a single crack in the ceramic. It only made sure the shelf itself would not shear away in some blind winter frost, and coaxed a slightly deeper seam of pure graphite beneath the soot-stained tiles, so the keepers’ joints would not run dry before their time.

No reed shivered differently. No manta-kite missed a beat of the wind. Across the amphitheater, a keeper wedged its limb against a humming spire, carving a brief, clean shadow into the roar to mark a shifting draft, and three hundred paces away, another keeper felt the notch in its spine and answered.

At the rim, the power listened to the deafening, waterless chorus one last time—not with pity, and not with the urge to mend, but with the quiet awe due to anything that has found a way to live inside a storm. Then it lifted its attention back into the high, blue sky, and left the bowl unbroken.
