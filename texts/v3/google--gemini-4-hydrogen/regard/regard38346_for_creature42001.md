It would not bring light. 

That would be the first, hardest discipline of its wisdom: to arrive in the dark without kindling a single spark. To a lesser kindness, this basin would look like a place of deprivation—a blind, cramped, silent well at the bottom of the world, waiting to be rescued by a sun. A careless mercy would flood the avenues with golden radiance, and in an instant, the city would break. The Plume-Shrimp would scatter in panic, their translucent bodies scalded by a spectrum they were never made to bear; the Vane-Fish’s tiny, hard-won blue glimmers would be drowned out; the Listener-Squids would drape themselves over the buttresses in confusion, trying to match the texture of a glare they could not feel.

So the power comes as the water itself comes: without shape, without glare, and without displacing a single grain of silt.

It settles into the basin during the ebb of a thermal tide, when the avenues are quiet and the Masons have drawn their scalloped mantles back into their spiral cones. It does not look at the city, for looking is a surface habit. Instead, it *feels* it, reading the metropolis in the only language the metropolis speaks—pressure, warmth, vibration, and weight.

It feels the low, rhythmic *thrum* of ten thousand biological siphons straining the dark. It feels the dry, patient rasp of tongues on limestone, the *tick* of a Silt-Coin’s claw, the tiny electrical twitches of Thread-Eels woven like living mortar into the cracks. Above the fluted chimneys, it feels the vast, slow *whoomp* of the Abyssal Manta banking in the cold boundary layer, its wingtips trailing dying embers of bacteria through the black.

And for a long while, the power simply listens, weighing what it might do.

It could, with a thought, give the Masons eyes. It could lift the ceiling of their minds, let them see the soaring, ribbed majesty of the towers they have raised, let them know the names of their neighbors and the shape of the valley. Yet wisdom stays its hand. To give a Mason sight would be to show it the ten stories of its own fossilized infancy buried in the mud beneath it; to give it foresight would be to turn its slow, rhythmic climbing into a dread of the rim. Now, a Mason’s life is an unbroken conversation with the current—an eddy strikes from the left, and it lays down shell-cement; the plume warms, and it drinks. It does not know it is blind, and so it lacks nothing.

The power could quiet the predators. It could dull the Listener-Squid’s terrible stillness, or seal the unhinged jaws of the Vane-Fish, sparing the Plume-Shrimp and the Thread-Eels from the silent ambush in the alleys. Yet it sees the mortar-joints already packed tight with eels; it sees the updrafts thick with shrimp. Without the quiet pruning of the hunters, the clouds would thicken until they choked the Masons’ siphons; the eels would crowd the cracks until the towers fractured from within. The squid and the fish are not malice in the dark. They are the city’s breath, exhaled.

It could warm the cold ocean above, or smooth the rough basalt, or lift the immense, crushing weight of the miles of water pressing down on the valley. Yet that pressure is the very thing that keeps the mineral-rich water heavy and still, holding the plumes close to the avenues instead of letting them scatter into nothing.

A lesser power would leave, disappointed that there was no wound here to heal, no monster to slay, no prayer to answer.

But benevolence does not require a crisis to be useful, and wisdom knows how to tend a garden without rearranging the stones.

The power reaches downward—past the knee-deep drifts of pale silt, past the slumped, fused foundations of the oldest abandoned chambers, down through the basaltic bed where no shrimp or eel has ever gone. There, in the blind, indifferent rock, it reads a slower story.

The deep earth is never entirely faithful to the things that grow upon it. Far below the valley floor, the fault that feeds the avenues is cooling, imperceptibly, over centuries. A shifting plate of stone, miles thick and deaf to the *thrum* of the siphons above, is beginning to pinch the main artery of the thermal tide. In another hundred generations—a blink to the rock, an eternity to a shrimp—the plumes would falter, thin, and go cold. The towers would stop climbing. The Manta would circle an empty chimney until it starved, and the great, blind city would become a silent quarry of chalky bones.

So the power does the only thing the city would ever ask of it, if the city knew how to ask.

It does not break the stone. It does not force a sudden, scalding geyser that would cook the builders in their alcoves. Instead, with the patience of a root finding water, it eases the tension in the deep basalt. It nudges a fracture by the width of a hair; it clears a slow, mineral choke in the dark throat of the earth; it steadies the pulse of the magma far below, tuning it to the exact, blood-warm rhythm the Masons’ mantles already know.

Then it rises back through the streets, leaving the rock untouched by any sign of its passing.

Before it withdraws, it lingers for one last turn in the freezing dark above the chimneys, keeping pace for a moment with the Abyssal Manta. The giant grazer glides on in its endless, effortless circles, mouth open to the rising warmth, entirely unaware that its ceiling has just been secured for another ten thousand years. The power does not touch its rubbery flank, nor brighten the faint bacterial embers on its skin. It simply holds the whole valley—the builders, the weavers, the sweepers, the hunters, and the vast, silent roof above them—in a brief, weightless regard.

Below, the deep geological pressure shifts.

The thermal tide surges. Warm, sulfur-rich water blooms upward through the broad avenues, and instantly, the city quickens. The Masons unfurl their velvety hoods to drink the lime; the Plume-Shrimp thicken into rustling clouds; a Listener-Squid uncoils from a ribbed buttress, silent as a shadow in a room without light.

Everything continues as it was. Nothing in the basin knows it was ever visited, or spared, or loved—and in that seamless ignorance lies the truest measure of the gift.
