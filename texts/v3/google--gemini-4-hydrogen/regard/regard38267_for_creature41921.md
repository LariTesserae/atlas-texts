To enter a place like this without breaking it, even a great power must first learn to shrink.

Not in strength, but in speed. A power accustomed to the turning of stars or the broad, loud mercy of rain on dry plains would ruin the chamber simply by arriving in its usual shape. To look in with too much brightness would bleach the dark leaves and blind the wide, amber eyes. To speak—even a word meant as a blessing—would scatter the velvet-winged bats into panic and send the heavy constrictors sliding off their leaf-plates in alarm.

So the first thing the power does is slow down. It folds its attention until it moves at the pace of condensation gathering on a travertine ridge. It listens—not for cries or prayers, because there are none—until it can hear the *tock* of a heavy drop hitting stone, and the tidal widening of ribs along the lower ledges.

Only then does it truly see what is here.

It watches an older one, its back crusted with pale lime, sitting inside a waist-high ring of stacked stone. In the olive twilight, the creature turns a single iridescent beetle-wing between thumb and forefinger, tilting it by fractions of an inch to catch the bruised noon light leaking down from the fissures. Its milky third lid slides sideways as a drop of water falls near its brow, then clears again. It is entirely absorbed. Across the chamber, two others sit with their temples pressed together, sharing nothing louder than the slow syncopation of their pulses.

A lesser kindness—one born of pity rather than wisdom—would immediately reach in to fix what looks, from the outside, like deprivation.

A lesser kindness would widen the fissures overhead to pour in the sun, not understanding that the sun is a blade that would shrivel the spongy liverwort, boil the tepid pools, and blister the wet-hide skin of the ones who live below. It would give them voices to sing with, forgetting that in a crowded room of snakes and centipedes, silence is both armor and truce. It would scrape the lime from their joints, unaware that the chalk they lick from their palms is the only thing keeping their bones dense in the dark. It would sweep away the blind ants and the hunting centipedes, and in doing so, unravel the very floor the moss grows on.

Wisdom looks at the calcified root-baskets, the small heaps of white pebbles, the patience of a creature willing to spend half a day watching a green-violet sheen flare and die in the gloom, and recognizes that this is not a prison. It is a finished thing. It has its own grammar, older and more coherent than most kingdoms under the open sky.

So the power leaves the interior of the chamber almost entirely untouched.

Instead, its work begins where the beings never go.

Deep beneath the scalloped basins, where the limestone rests against the older, hotter bones of the earth, the geothermal vein is shifting—slowly, over centuries, as rock veins do. Left alone, it would eventually pinch shut, leaving the chamber to cool into damp, sterile stone, or else crack wide and scald the roots to mush. The power lays a steady, unseen pressure against the deep stone, tuning the warmth so it remains what it has always been: a slow, even breath rising through the floor.

Far above, out on the sun-baked scree where the narrow fissures open to the sky, the power does its quietest work. It turns aside the wandering foot of the hiker, the surveyor’s drill, the stray goat whose hooves might dislodge a slab of scree and plug the chimneys of light. When drought dries the scrub above, it nudges the deep roots of a surface cedar sideways so they do not snake down through the cracks to drink the chamber’s humidity dry. When winter storms bring sudden, violent rain to the hills, it channels the runoff away from the fissures, so the sunken pocket is never drowned in cold mud. It knits the fractured vault overhead—not making it new, only holding the ancient limestone just firmly enough that the ceiling does not fall on the sleepers below.

Inside the chamber, nothing appears to have changed. The snakes still glide over the leaf-plates. The blind ants still march their chemical highways.

Yet, if you were to stand on the damp peat for a very long time, you might notice two small mercies—things so subtle they look indistinguishable from luck.

High up in the throat of the widest fissure, where no bat hangs and no root reaches, a single flake of dark scree shifts aside—just enough that, for ten minutes at noon, the bruised shaft of daylight strikes a wet ridge of clear calcite on the upper wall. It does not brighten the room. It does not warm the moss. It only splits that one thin thread of sky into a faint, trembling smear of red and violet against the wet stone, lingering long enough for the wide amber eyes below to catch it, and wonder, and turn back to their weaving.

And down in the lowest, warmest basin, when the oldest among them finally grows too heavy to climb the flowstone shelves and settles into the cloudy water to stop moving, the power sits beside it in the dark. It does not stop the ending, nor wake the sleepers to grieve. It only eases the ache of the mineral-stiffened joints, and holds the warmth of the water steady, like a broad, flat hand pressed gently against a shoulder, until the breath slows to match the *tock* of the stone, and stops.
