First, the power had to learn how to arrive without blinding them.

It was an old, vast intelligence, accustomed to worlds where mercy looked like a parted cloud or a lamp lit in a doorway. When its attention first turned toward the basin, it came as a kind of clarity—a quiet, bright pressure above the ash-ceiling. And immediately, it felt the valley flinch. The tin-wings shrieked against the edges of the upper canopy; the drum-bellies flattened themselves deeper into the wet fault-cracks; down in the dark, a knot of ash-skinned hunters froze with their heels pressed tight against a basalt shelf, waiting for whatever new heat had entered the air.

Light here was a blow. Silence was a predator holding its breath.

So the power folded its brightness away. It did not part the soot-dark mist, nor still the geothermal vents. Instead, it sank downward as a low, patient weight, tuning itself to the only grammar the basin understood: warmth, grit, and the restless pulse of rock. It listened through the slate. It felt the thrumming of the throat-sacs, the fifty-times-a-second hail of mandibles, the *tock* of a root splitting a stone, the soft, oiled slide of human skin moving along a ledge.

For a long time, it only watched—if watching is the word for knowing a place by the vibration of its bones.

At first, pity tugged at it.

It saw the pale welts crisscrossing their shins where the young basalt had bitten deep. It saw the way they slept in shallow, two-hour bouts, slung like fruit in the high crotches of black-barked trunks, one watcher always upright with a bare heel pressed to the wood. It saw the blind mites burrowing whenever the bitter tuber-oil wore thin, the frantic scramble when a hinge-jaw’s heat-pits caught a hunter’s scent, the endless impermanence of a life where even a trail could not survive until tomorrow.

*I could fix this,* the power thought, and for a moment, the shape of a different valley hung above the basin like a phantom.

It could cool the deep vents and wash the ash from the sky, letting in a gentle, pale morning. It could send rain to round the razor-edges of the slate into smooth river-stones. It could quiet the deafening surf of the canopy, drive the hinge-jaws into deeper caverns, and slow the frantic vines so a wall might stand for fifty years instead of five days. It could give them dry roofs, and fire that did not have to be hidden, and sleep that lasted until dawn.

Then a giant vine-cluster reached its hour.

The heavy, musky wave of sulfur and sweet rot rolled through the basin, and the power felt the people answer it. They did not run; they flowed. Knuckles tapped against calves in the dark—*step up, fresh slate*—and notched seed-pods answered the drum-bellies in a dry *skritch-tick-tick* that wove straight into the valley’s roar. They converged through the sweating murk with an effortless, terrifying grace, cracking mineral nuts against their shin-guards, drinking warm sap from the cut stems, laughing without voices in a ten-minute feast where nothing was mourned and nothing was held back.

And the power understood, with a sudden, humbling clarity, what its pity would have done.

To give them the sun would burn their wide, pale-ringed eyes and dry the dampness that fed their world. To quiet the valley would blind them, for they saw by the shape of the noise. To smooth the stone would steal their knives, and to build them walls would trap them in one place while the living earth shifted beneath their feet. They were not broken creatures waiting to be rescued from a mistake. They had learned the basin’s song note for note, and they were dancing in the middle of it.

A lesser kindness would have remade them into something softer, and called it love.

Wisdom stayed its hand.

Yet the power did not leave entirely empty-handed. A wise friend does not tear down your house because the roof is steep; they only oil the hinge that sticks, or leave a sharper whetstone by the door. If it offered anything at all, it would have to be small enough to fit inside the valley’s own logic—something the people could find with their own hands, test with their own teeth, and claim as their own.

So the power reached down, not into the sky, but into the margins.

In the deep, cold hollows where the people dug mineral clay to mask their body-heat from the hinge-jaws, it coaxed the sediment to settle a fraction finer. It did not take away the predators, nor the danger of the hunt; it only made the clay cling a little more stubbornly to oiled skin, holding out against the slow sweat-drip for an extra hour when a hunter was far from a cache.

In the root-knots of the tuber-plants, it thickened the bitter sap just enough that the oil pressed from it sealed small basalt cuts faster, keeping the damp-rot and the blind mites at bay without changing the plant’s fierce, week-long life.

And high in the black-barked trunks, where the young branches forked above the rockslides and below the tin-wings, it gently widened the grain of a few new-growing crotches—just enough to cradle a watcher’s back without pinching, and to carry the drum-belly’s warning tremor a half-beat clearer through the wood.

Nothing in the sky changed. The roar did not lessen by a single click. When the next rockslide hissed down the scree, the people still had to move in six heartbeats, and when a hinge-jaw cornered them, they still had to dare the blinding flare of a resin-torch.

The people never knew a vast power had leaned over their dark basin. They only noticed, over the seasons that followed, that the clay in the low hollows seemed to hold better than it used to, and that a certain ridge of trees grew remarkably good forks for slinging hammocks. They tapped their rattles, flaked their obsidian, and kept moving.

As for the power, it lingered for one more bloom-hour, crouched invisibly in the warm, vibrating surf of the dark—listening, without light, to a world that needed no saving.
