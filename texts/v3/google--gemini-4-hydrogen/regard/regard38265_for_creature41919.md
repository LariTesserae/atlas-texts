At first, the power’s impulse was to mend.

It is an old habit among the wise, when they look down upon a world scarred by industry, to reach for the broom and the rainstorm. Seeing the faint, iridescent film of hydrocarbons resting on the black pools, the power could have lifted it away like grease from a broth. Seeing the pale mineral crusts stained with sulfur in the creases of those angular shoulders, it could have sent a warm, clean rain to wash the slate-grey hides. It could have reached down through five thousand meters of newly fractured granite and hushed the deep-earth pumps, giving the basin back the ancient, unbroken silence that high places are supposed to keep.

But wisdom does not act before it has finished looking. So the power waited, holding its breath in the pewter mist, and watched.

It watched a tall, sharp-jointed figure kneel at a shallow bog without snapping a single rim of ice. It watched the wide lower lip draw the oiled sheen inward before taking the cold meltwater beneath, and heard the soft, wet *clack* of the throat—no louder than a pebble shifting in the frost. It watched the narrow slits behind the jawbone open to the coal-scented fog, drinking oxygen and soot together without a cough.

It pressed its own vast attention down against the bare granite outcrops, right beside their flat chins and long forearms, and felt what they were feeling: the steady, sub-audible thrumming rising from the unseen lowlands. To the power, it had sounded like an intrusion, the distant pulse of an indifferent machine. To the ones stretched flat upon the stone, it was simply the heartbeat of the only world they had ever known—the warmth in the floor, the rhythm that kept the cold from turning absolute.

And when a cargo plane droned invisible above the cloud deck, and three of them uncurled from the scree to hum back at the sky in their low, wet register, the power understood.

They were not broken. They were not waiting to be rescued from a ruined Eden. They were what the earth had grown to meet the world as it actually was—mineral-skinned, smoke-tolerant, sharp as unweathered basalt, and wholly at peace with the strange marriage of rock and furnace. To scrub the air clean would be to starve their lungs of the only weather they knew; to silence the turbines would be to stop the music they gathered to hear. Even the frogs in the soot-rimmed bogs, the swifts wheeling through the midge-clouds rising off their warm backs, the foxes trotting past them as if they were weather or stone—all of it was already a whole, delicate thing.

So the power withheld its hand from the valley. It did not round a single knife-edged stone, nor clear the pewter ceiling, nor dim the bruised, sodium-amber glow bleeding up from the southern horizon.

Instead, its work became small, quiet, and mostly invisible.

First, it tended the margin. Far below, in the unseen valleys where the smelters churned and the pumps drove their steady rhythm into the dark, a benevolent attention settled over the vents and the smokestacks. It did not shut them down—not all at once, not in a way that would leave the high basin suddenly cold and silent—but it nudged the balance just enough that the poisons drifting upward would stay a *tang* and a *sheen*, never thickening into a blight that would curdle the salamander eggs or burn the wet membranes behind those narrow jaws.

Second, it became a keeper of the threshold. Whenever the restlessness of the world below turned its eyes upward—a surveyor scanning satellite contours for a new pass, a mining company eyeing the raw, unweathered ridges for whatever veins of ore had been thrust up from the dark—the power gently turned the world’s attention elsewhere. A ridge would seem too steep, a cloud deck too persistent, a core sample slightly less promising than the valley two ranges over. No dramatic storms, no walls of fire; only a quiet, persistent unlikelihood that kept the basin unnamed, un-cairned, and free of wire.

And finally, because benevolence is not merely careful, it offered a single gesture of kinship—in the only language that would not frighten or confuse them.

It did not speak from the clouds. It did not send a light to rival the amber haze. Instead, during a long, windless lull when the *drip-drip* of the weeping rock was the only sound in the air, the power let a fraction of its own presence pass through the deep bedrock—not interrupting the distant thrum of the turbines, but weaving into it, adding a low, steady, unmistakable *warmth* to the stone’s vibration. A note just beneath hearing, patient as geology.

Out across the talus, a dozen sharp-angled shapes lay pressed to the granite. Along the southern ridge, where several sat with their knees drawn up to their chins watching the amber glow, one or two tilted their narrow heads a fraction of an inch.

Their rigid chests expanded—barely a fraction of an inch—and began to hum. Not the high, brief note they gave the passing planes, but something slower, deeper, matching the quiet steadiness in the rock beneath them.

The power listened until the next gust swept sideways across the basin, rattling the ice-fringed sedges and driving the mist back over the scree. Then it withdrew its weight, leaving only the cold wet stone, the endless *drip-drip* into the black pools, and a valley that did not know it was being kept—only that, for a long while yet, it would have room to be itself.
