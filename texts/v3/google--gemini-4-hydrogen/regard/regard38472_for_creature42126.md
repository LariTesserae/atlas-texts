It was the bell that drew its attention first.

Three stories of bedrock and a welded steel bulkhead are enough to stop any light, any weather, any memory of the dead city above. Even a power that walks the surface of the world might pass over that forgotten patch of ground a thousand times and feel only cold stone.

But the bronze warning bell on the fourth tier had been ringing into the dark for decades—*dong—dong—dong*—a slow, patient pulse tripped by a float-switch that no hand would ever reset. To a mind vast enough to hear the groan of tectonic plates and the settling of ruins, that muffled, underwater chime rang out like a heartbeat in a sealed chest.

So the power leaned down, slipped through the bedrock as easily as heat slips through iron, and entered the Sump.

It did not come with light. Light was a surface vanity, and here the amber lamps already struggled enough against the silt. Instead, the power came as an attention—vast, quiet, and weightless—filling the churning cylinder alongside the warm, oily water. It felt the tectonic *thrum* of the lower impellers, the metallic hailstorm of cavitation, the sudden, violent slap of the pressure-relief valve. It tasted the scorched iron and sulfur.

And then the sluice-gate shifted. The current stalled for three breathless seconds.

In that suspended hush, the power felt the ironwork move. It watched—without eyes, through the vibration of the water itself—as the heavy-shouldered, six-limbed things kicked off from the snapped I-beams and glided blind into the open core, spreading their forelimbs for the passing loaches before the storm tore them back to the opposite tier with a dull, metallic *clank*.

It heard the *tap—tap* racing through the bolted skeleton of the pipework. It felt the young ones crowding beneath the third-tier junction box, drinking in the copper-tasting hum of the failing wire. It felt an elder wedged behind a pump-house in a leeward eddy, its travertine plates fused solid, slowly, peacefully turning into a wall of the town.

For a long moment, the power simply listened.

Pity—the quick, impatient kind—would have acted at once. A lesser mercy would have looked at the Sump and seen a prison: dark, deafening, scalding, blind. It would have torn open the welded bulkhead to let in the sky. It would have drained the oily water, silenced the roaring grates, given the creatures eyes to see the sun, and chiseled the heavy stone from the elders’ backs so they could walk again.

And in doing so, it would have killed every last one of them.

Wisdom stayed its hand. Wisdom tasted the water through their gill-fronds and knew that the heat was their breath, that the sulfur was their pasture, that the roaring storm in the bottle was the only sky they were ever meant to fly in. It understood that their blindness was not a lack, any more than a river lacks wings; their whole world was written in pressure, in temperature, in the bright copper tang of electricity and the *tap—tap* of iron against claw. To give them eyes would only show them the rust. To open the bulkhead would only pour cold air into their lungs until they drowned in it.

So the power did not save them from the Sump.

Instead, it looked deeper, past the violent equilibrium, into the bones of the chamber itself—and saw what the creatures could not.

The Sump was running out of time.

The surface city that had built it was dust, and its machines were living on borrowed decades. Down behind the lower grates, the bronze bearings of the massive impellers had worn down to paper-thin rings; one more season of grit, and a shaft would snap, seizing the blades in a shriek of torn metal. On the fourth tier, the gear teeth of the quarter-hour sluice-gate were ground nearly smooth. And on the third tier, the corroded busbar inside the junction box—the hearth where the young ones gathered—was eaten almost clean through by the salt and the heat. Soon, the blue-white arc would flash one final time and go cold forever.

When the machines died, the water would stop. The heat would pool in stagnant layers, choking the bacteria mats. The loaches would suffocate. The *tap—tap* would still ring through the iron, calling out into dead, motionless dark, until there was nothing left to answer.

The power could have replaced the old iron with something eternal—seamless, silent, gleaming metal that would never corrode or rattle.

It chose not to. If the grates stopped thrumming, the creatures would lose their bearings. If the pipes were made smooth and new, the *tap—tap* would echo wrong, like a stranger’s voice in an empty house. They knew every dent, every worn flange, every tremor of this old, scarred town.

So the power worked like a careful hand in the dark, mending only what was broken, and leaving every scar intact.

Down behind the lower grates, unseen beneath the swirling silt, the worn bronze bearings quietly thickened—not bright and new, just dense and whole again, keeping the exact pitch of their *thrum* so the creatures’ ribs would not notice the difference. The gear teeth of the hidden sluice-gate grew back their missing iron, coated in the same old grease, ready to turn every quarter-hour for another ten thousand years.

At the third-tier junction box, the power cupped the dying busbar. It did not replace it with a sterile lamp, nor did it stop the wire from singing. It simply steadied the copper from within, weaving its strength back together so the hearth would never go cold, while trimming the sharpest, fiercest spike from the arc—leaving the bright, intoxicating warmth and the gentle snap that sent the young ones scuttling in play, without the burn that could kill them.

It climbed to the fourth tier, where the muffled bronze warning bell still tolled its slow *dong—dong—dong* for nobody. The power did not silence it. It knew the bell was no longer a warning; it was a landmark, a steady pulse in the ironwork, a place to rest a heavy chin and feel the world turn. It only strengthened the rusted pin of the float-switch so the bell would keep ringing as long as there were snouts to press against it.

And then it visited the leeward eddy behind the pump-house, where the old one had locked its claws into the grating for the last time.

The power did not chisel away the travertine. It did not wake the elder from its slow, mineral sleep. That belonged to the Sump—the long, quiet return to the walls that sheltered the young. All the power did was rest an invisible hand against the rigid, crusted back, easing the last tight ache from joints that had gripped the iron for thirty years, and letting the warm current carry one faint, wordless certainty down through the stone:

*You are held. The town will stand.*

Then, as quietly as it had come, the power withdrew through the bedrock.

If a diver had somehow cut through the welded bulkhead an hour later and dropped into the bathwater-warm dark, they would have sworn nothing had touched the Sump in a century. The amber lamps still choked on suspended rust. The pressure-relief valve still slammed shut with a *BOOM* that bowed the catwalks. The white crabs still clung to their leaking flanges, and the blind, armored hounds of the deep still waited on the snapped ends of the I-beams.

The sluice-gate turned.

For three breathless seconds, the storm in the bottle went slack. The creatures kicked off cleanly into the warm, murky void, gliding weightless across the heart of their world before the current caught them and hurled them home.

On the third tier, a claw struck a flange.

*Tap—tap.*

And the bolted skeleton of the Sump—sound, unbroken, and tireless—carried the word all the way down to the grates.
