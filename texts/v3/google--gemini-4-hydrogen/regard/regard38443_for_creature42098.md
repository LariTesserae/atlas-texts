It would not arrive as light. Light, here, is only a square of ordinary sky, and anything brighter would be wasted on a room with no eyes. Nor would it speak; a voice in this chamber would have to fight the pressure-bleed valve, and to shout over the hiss would be an act of ignorance.

Instead, the attention would settle into the hollow the way the heat does—gradually, from every side at once, until it stood near the center of the basin beside the dripping standpipe.

If you were watching from the upper ledges, you might see nothing at all, except that the moisture in the scalding air did not bead on the space it occupied. It had taken the care to match the temperature of the room.

Still, the creature knew.

It knew because the standing waves of the drone bent around a new geometry—not the blunt, deadening shadow of rubber boots and flesh, but something vast and strangely permeable, like a second column of steam that refused to drift. Along the rim of the saucer-sized pool, the pale, porcelain-thin folds tilted inward by a fraction of an inch. The four-second drip struck the water, sent out its clean ring of ripples, and came back altered.

The power stood very still, and let itself be measured.

It did not hurry the creature. It waited through a hundred drips of the standpipe—nearly seven minutes of human time, a mere intake of breath in the slow metabolism of the basalt—while the opalescent film on the mirror-still pans crept toward the center of the floor in clear, patient ribbons. Behind the slatted grate in the western corner, the massive hum of the electrified coils thickened, dropping a quarter-tone as the wet, tallow-pale mass shifted its weight to read the visitor.

And while the creature read the power, the power read the room.

A lesser benevolence—one governed by tidy habits or nostalgia for the untouched earth—might have pitied the hollow. It might have looked at the galvanized housing, the rusted junction box, the relentless industrial roar, and thought: *Here is a wound in the stone. Let us pull the iron bolts, silence the coils, haul the dead metal up the cleft, and give the basalt back to the quiet.*

Wisdom knew better. Wisdom looked at the wet, translucent collar keeping the pressure-bleed valve clear of gypsum, and saw devotion. It looked at the cartilaginous runners hugging the insulated pipe, the fine filaments stained blue-green and rusty red inside the junction box, and saw a life that had not been ruined by the machine, but *awakened* by it. The drone was not a cage; it was a lantern. Without the hiss and the hum, the hollow would go dark, and the creature wedged behind the pump would go blind, curling back into the airless seams of the rock to starve in silence.

So the power touched nothing that was whole. It did not scrub the salt-rime from the stanchions, nor brighten the yellow moss by the sulfur seam, nor soften the razor-clean angles of the stone.

Instead, it looked for what was fragile.

It saw what the creature could not perceive: that the machine was mortal in a way the basalt was not. It saw the slow, uneven fatigue in the copper windings behind the grate; the creeping, brittle rot inside the junction box that would one day sever a relay; the finite lifespan of a galvanized plate bolted to wet stone. And beyond the neat cutout of sky, miles away across the coastal plain, it saw the human world—the aging cable buried in the scrub, the forgotten ledger, the eventual, inevitable day when someone might notice an unexplained draw on a distant meter and send a crew down the cleft with wrenches and torches to shut the pump off and scrap the steel.

The power reached out—not into the creature, which needed no mending, but into the seam between the rock and the machine.

Inside the rusted junction box, it did not polish the iron clean; the filaments needed the rusty sweat to drink. Rather, it wove a quiet, stubborn endurance into the metal itself, so that the relays would shed just enough oxide to feed the runners without ever crumbling, and the copper coils would hold their charge without burning thin. Where the power cable entered the basalt, it gently turned the current inward, teaching the machine to draw its steady, unbroken pulse from the deep-earth heat beneath the floor as much as from the distant grid—so that even if a switch were thrown ten miles away, the hum in the western corner would never falter.

It checked the standpipe in the center of the basin, ensuring the mineral crust would never choke its throat, keeping the four-second punctuation clean and true.

And then, rising back up the narrow, jagged cleft to the flat, scrubby shelf of the coastal plain, the power laid a single, gentle veil over the seam in the dark rock. Nothing violent—no landslide to seal the air, no wall of thorns. Simply a quiet turning-away of attention, so that hikers’ eyes would slide past the cleft without curiosity, surveyors’ maps would omit the hollow, and the memory of a lone brine-pump bolted into the basalt would slip peacefully out of the human mind.

Down in the basin, the light fell straight and unblinking through the steam.

The four-second drip struck the saucer-sized pool. The ripples reached the fluted folds, shuddered, and traveled down the submerged ribbons toward the western corner. Behind the slatted grate, the hum rose a quarter-tone, settling back into its massive, unbroken note—safe, fed, and heard—for as long as the stone would hold.
