At first, the power’s impulse was to break the floor.

It came to the plain as powers of that kind often do—carrying the memory of oceans, of root systems cracking through stone, of nightfall cooling a fevered continent. Looking down through the white, stagnant glare, it saw a world that looked like a forgotten basement: an endless, flat expanse of cast iron and polymer-concrete, trapped at thirty-five degrees Celsius, humming with a single, relentless chord. It saw creatures without eyes or voices, clinging to copper cables and wading through two inches of warm, tea-colored water. It saw the tall, porcelain-pale figures walking the catwalks with their arms folded against their ribs, never sleeping, never speaking, pressing their own damp bodies against hot metal to keep an ancient machine from drifting off-key.

To a power that loved green things and open skies, it looked like captivity.

The remedy seemed obvious. Crack the concrete plates and let soil rise from beneath. Part the condensing mist so a real sky could burn blue overhead, and bring a first, merciful dusk. Give the pale tenders eyes to see the horizon, and mouths to ask for something more than a steady load.

It reached down—and stopped, an inch above the perforated steel of a catwalk.

Wisdom, if it is worth the name, hesitates before it heals. Before touching anything, the power folded its vast attention down to the scale of the plain. It did not look with eyes; it listened the way the tenders listened, through vibration and thermal gradient.

It felt the sixty-cycle hum, not as noise, but as the steady pulse of a blood-warm sea. It watched a papery flyer, no thicker than vellum, shiver in perfect contentment along an insulated busbar, drinking the bleed of the electromagnetic field. It saw that if it brought a breeze—even a gentle, springlike wind—thousands of those flyers would be torn from their cables and dashed into the pans to drown.

It watched a broad, amber mantle-slug rasp microscopic rust from a transformer fin and lay down its slick film of oil. If the power cleared the mist and dried the air, the slug would shrivel in an hour, and the iron beneath it would begin to bleed red oxide into the water.

And then it stood beside a tender.

The tender was paused by a rectifier station whose pitch had drifted a fraction of a hertz. Two others arrived—not summoned by any cry, only drawn by the same faint dissonance in the soles of their feet. Together, without a word or a gesture of command, the three of them stepped off the catwalk and pressed their narrow chests and spatulate palms against the warm, ribbed housing.

The power leaned close to their flat sensory plates and read what was inside.

There was no despair there. There was no ache for a sky they had never conceived of, no resentment of a builder they did not remember. There was only a deep, quiet attentiveness—the satisfaction of a joint fitting cleanly into its sleeve, the shared, wordless ease of three cool bodies taking a thermal load together until the hum settled back into tune. When it did, the three tenders stepped back onto the catwalk in unison, and one of them brushed its damp hand briefly against the shoulder of another—not a human caress, just a final equalization of warmth—before they parted.

To give them eyes would be to blind them with a white glare that had never been meant for sight. To tear up the concrete and plant grass would be to starve the walkers, choke the grates, and leave the tenders standing in an alien ruin, listening for a chord that had gone dead. What looked like a prison from above was, from within, a liturgy—a closed, painless circle where nothing starved, nothing panicked, and every creature was exactly shaped to the world that held it.

So the power withdrew its hand from the catwalk. It would not unmake their home.

Instead, it looked deeper.

It sent its awareness down through the cast-iron plates, past the submerged copper conduits and the tea-colored water, beneath the polymer-concrete floor—down into the deep, unvisited strata where the plain’s true machinery lay.

Here, there were no mantle-slugs to scrape away the scale, no walkers to clear the channels, no tenders to press their cool chests against the heat. The tenders could keep the surface clean forever, but they could not reach the roots. And down in the dark below the floor, the power found what time eventually brings to even the most patient circuit: the slow, silent wear of millennia. A deep winding thinning toward an eventual fault. An ancient reservoir of coolant slowly losing its chemistry. A buried junction growing brittle under a load it had carried since before memory.

Left alone, the plain would not end in fire or storm. One day, very far from now, a rectifier would drift off-key, and four tenders, then ten, then a hundred would press themselves against its fins, and the hum would still falter—and slowly, over decades, the chord would thin into cold silence.

The power knelt in the dark beneath the world and went to work.

It did not perform a miracle that broke the rules of the place. It simply became, for a little while, a tender to the tenders. With infinite patience, it mended the deep copper. It refreshed the buried oil, cleared the unseen conduits, and balanced the ancient source so that the sixty-cycle current would run clean and steady for ages yet to come. It widened the margins of the plain’s equilibrium just enough that a small accident would not become a cascade—without shifting the dew point by a hundredth of a degree, without ruffling the glassy creep of the water.

When it was finished, it rose back through the iron plates and stood once more on the catwalk.

A pair of tenders was walking past, their three-jointed arms folded neatly against their ribs. The power knew it could not speak to them, and it did not wish to startle them with a change in the world they trusted.

Yet, as it drew its presence back into the distance, it let a single, brief grace pass through the floor—not a new sound, not a light, only a moment in which the sixty-cycle hum rang with an effortless, crystalline purity, and every housing along the horizon settled into perfect pitch at once.

The two tenders paused. They angled the flat plates of their heads toward the nearest transformer, reading the sudden, unexplained ease of the air, the stillness of a circuit that needed nothing from them at all. For a long minute, they simply stood together on the vibrating steel, taking in the warmth.

Then, with nowhere urgent to be, one of them stepped quietly into the narrow shadow between two oil-coolers, folded its limbs tightly against its torso, and rested.

The power watched for a moment longer, smiled—in whatever way a wise thing smiles when it has left well enough alone—and let the white haze close softly behind it.
