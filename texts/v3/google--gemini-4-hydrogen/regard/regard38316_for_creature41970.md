At first, from the height where great powers keep their watch, the plateau looked like an error that had been left too long to mend.

A sea stranded miles above the dry basins of the world, held back by nothing more than its own trembling wall of amber water; time pooling unevenly in the hollows like spilt oil; gravity forgetting its own direction along the worn granite crests; and beneath the tea-colored gloom, a handful of blind, deaf, heavy-limbed creatures crawling slowly through a silence so thick it swallowed every tremor.

A lesser power—one merely strong, or merely kind—would have reached down at once to set things right. It would have knit the fraying laws back into a single, crisp geometry. It would have cleared the water so the sun could resolve into a disk, anchored the pebbles to the stone, synchronized the thermal plumes with the cold pockets, and perhaps, out of pity, opened eyes in those milky domes and given those toothless mouths a voice to call across the dark.

And in doing so, it would have unmade the only home these beings knew.

The power that came to the plateau was wise enough to pause at the rim before touching anything. It did not descend as light, for light here was meant to be smeared and gentle; it did not announce itself in thunder, for the sea was built to drink sound before it could be born. Instead, it thinned its own presence until it weighed no more than a warm current, and slipped through the trembling amber wall into the crowded twilight.

For a long while—though *long* meant little here—it simply drifted and felt.

It felt the lateral, packed weight of the water. It felt the velvet-moss brushing the trunks of the bladder-trees, and the cool, silken pass of a detached shadow. It watched a dweller anchor itself in a pocket of slow, dense cold while its broad, four-fingered hands threaded living kelp-roots over a fast-time fissure, growing a stone-hard dome in the span of a single, stretched-out afternoon. It saw the muscular valves along the creature’s torso seal and unseal, keeping peace between two incompatible paces of the world.

It saw two dwellers meet by chance in the gloom—no startle, no haste, only two heavy, moss-dappled bodies pressing their flattened heads together until their heat and their clocks matched, sharing the quiet fact of having arrived at the same moment.

And it saw the cairns.

Along the highest, flattest ridges, it lingered beside those impossible stacks of river-rounded pebbles, wedged into the seams between rising currents. It understood then that this was not a broken world crying out to be repaired. It was an old world, settling into its final, eccentric grace, and its inhabitants had learned to read every fray in its fabric with their palms. To tighten the rules of this place would be to tear the dwellers out of the very medium they had grown to fit. To drain the sea to the dry basins below would be to trade one quiet eternity for another’s brief greening. To give them sound would be to fill a sanctuary with noise.

So the power set aside almost everything it knew how to do.

It did not mend the clock of the sea. It did not nail the shadows back to their trees, nor teach the pebbles how to fall.

Yet it did not leave entirely empty-handed, for wisdom is not indifference, and even a world at peace has edges where it wears thin.

At the continental rim, where the miles-high wall of dark amber liquid stood trembling against the cold, thin air of the lowlands, the power laid its attention along the water’s skin. It did not freeze the wall or turn it to glass—a sea must remain water, even at the edge of nothing—but it eased the deepest, oldest strains within the column, so that the sea would not tear itself open in a sudden, violent collapse before its own slow age had run its course.

And deeper within the forests, where fast thermal plumes met stagnant hollows, the power passed like a smoothing palm over the sharpest seams—never erasing the difference between fast time and slow, only softening the rarest, cruellest shears where time turned too abruptly for even a segmented heart to read.

That was all it altered in the architecture of the world.

Before it withdrew, however, the power drifted to one of the highest, flattest ridges, where the primordial granite had gone pale and fibrous and the gravity was least certain of its own mind. There, it gathered itself into something small, heavy, and no warmer than the blood-warm bath around it. With a patience borrowed from the dwellers themselves, it felt among the settled silt for three river-rounded stones.

It did not use its strength to weld them together. It simply read the upward tug of the ridge and the slow, century-old lean of a rising current, and nudged the stones into balance—one flat, one round, one small and pale—until the packed weight of the sea itself held them upright at an angle that ought to have been impossible.

Then it let its shape dissolve back into the amber twilight, and was gone.

Decades later—or perhaps three hundred years later, for who could say which current carried him there—a dweller hauled himself up the smooth whale-back of the ridge. He paused beside the unfamiliar cairn. Slowly, he laid his broad, sensitive palm against the balanced stones, feeling the tilt, the pressure, the quiet persistence of the current that held them.

He did not know what had placed them there. He only knew that they held.

After a long while, he reached down into the silt, found a fourth pebble worn smooth before the sea ever rose, and wedged it gently into the seam beside the others. Then he loosened his grip on the stone, let the water lift his heavy body an inch above the ridge, and drifted on into the warm, silent dark.
