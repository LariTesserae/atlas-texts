When the attention arrived, it did not break the water.

Nothing in the chamber had ears to hear a descent, and no current stirred the suspended mineral dust. Yet if the masons’ twelve thread-thin feelers had been tuned to something wider than salt and lime, they might have tasted a sudden, vast stillness—the kind that comes when something immense bends very close, and holds its breath so as not to crush what it has found.

For a long time, the power simply watched.

It read the city the way the city read itself: not in names or histories, but in pressure, temperature, and light. It saw the pale, thumb-length builders hanging from their jelly-like scaffolding, their flank-lanterns dimming from warm tallow to cold wax as their guts emptied onto the parapets. It watched the old, crusted pruners in the lower tenements, dutifully chewing back the living spore-flesh so the city would not heal itself into a tomb. It saw the cloak-shaped grazers lifting their edges over the bone-white algae, the rice-grain larvae fattening in the silt, the bone-armored scavengers trotting upside-down beneath the silk bridges with their neat spheres of salvage.

It saw, too, the slate-ribbon hunters folding themselves into the sharp angles of the upper scaffolding. It watched a hydraulic snap, a silent coil in the dark, and the brief, pale bloom of blood that followed.

A lesser kindness would have reached in at once.

A lesser kindness might have warmed the room to spare the brood-mothers their endless, shivering labor—and in doing so, rotted the cold-water fungi, boiled the copper-blue grazers in their own skins, and sent the symbiotic mortar swelling uncontrollably until every street sealed shut. It might have swept the slate-ribbon hunters out through the hairline seam—and left the vertical avenues to choke on too many spinners, too many masons, too little oxygen in a room with no tide. It might have whispered language into those braided crowns of feelers, or hardened the chalky spires into stone that would never need a pruner’s teeth.

Wisdom, looking at the damp bone-lantern cluster of the city, saw that it was not broken. It was complete, even in its sharpness. To rescue it from its own nature would be to turn a living metropolis into a paperweight.

So the power did nothing to the grazers, nothing to the hunters, nothing to the mute, rhythmic exchange of touch and taste in the narrow streets.

Instead, its attention rose to the ceiling, where the fresh crack was widening.

There, the math was different. The basalt shelf above was not a neighbor, and it was not a predator; it was blind geological weight, miles of cold ocean pressing down on a fault that did not know or care what had taken root beneath it. And the syrupy thread of super-cooled brine leaking through the seam was just a fraction too heavy, too bitter, too fast-chilling. Left alone, the fault would shear another inch within the hour. The brine would cascade down the vertical shafts like liquid glass, snapping the silk bridges and freezing the brood-mothers in their chalk alcoves before the masons could lay three rings of paste.

The power looked at the line of pale builders already marching up the silk ladder toward the drip. It saw their courage—though they had no word for it, only the chemical certainty that a leak must be met, and a wall must be raised.

They did not need to be saved. They only needed a fair chance.

Slowly, with a care finer than the legs of a spinner, the power laid its will against the fractured basalt overhead. It did not seal the crack—that would have robbed the city of whatever lay beyond, and left the masons with nothing to answer. Instead, it took the blind, crushing shear out of the stone. It eased the weight of the shelf until the fault settled into a slow, steady patience, widening no faster than a spire could grow.

Then, at the lip of the widening seam, where the lead mason’s feelers were recoiling slightly from the burn of the super-cooled drip, the power breathed the smallest possible mercy: a shift of temperature so slight no thermometer could have named it, a mere softening of the brine’s lethal edge. Just enough that when the mason opened its mouth and pressed its warm, living paste against the obsidian rim, the mortar did not shatter.

It caught. It held. It began, imperceptibly, to cure.

Around the lead mason, the others crowded in, their flank-lights glowing pale tallow in the gloom, laying ring upon ring of porous, breathing chalk around the leak. Inside the drip, the crystalline brine-creepers hung motionless like splinters of clear glass, neither banished nor destroyed—left as a question for the city to answer in its own time, whether to wall them out into the dark, or to build a street straight into the cold and learn a new kind of neighbor.

The power lingered a moment longer, watching the first faint updraft of filtered warmth rise from the lower tenements, carrying a newly molted spinner toward an unfinished arch.

Then it withdrew, leaving no monument, no sign, and no memory of its passing—only a ceiling that held, a ring of mortar that took, and a silent city continuing its work beneath the sea.
