If a power that was both wise and benevolent turned its attention to this sunken hollow, the first thing it would do is nothing at all.

It would not announce itself with light—the canopy already has all the sun it needs, and the dweller has no eyes to receive a revelation. It would not speak—a voice in this room would be only another ugly discord, like a boot breaking the surface tension of the mat. Instead, the power would simply wait. It would stand still in the greenish-gold haze for four or five turns of the great flywheel, letting its attention sink through the three-foot crust of liverwort and tubers until it understood the grammar of the place.

A lesser benevolence—one that was kind, perhaps, but not wise—might be tempted to *fix* things. It might look at the rusted iron and the blind, legless creature and feel pity. It might want to clear away the industrial bones and restore the hollow to pure, untouched forest; or, conversely, to mend the flaking gaskets, scrub the verdigris, and give the dweller eyes so it could finally see the cathedral it inhabits.

Wisdom would stay its hand from all of these. It would recognize that the hollow is not a ruin waiting to be rescued, nor a machine waiting to be repaired. It is a marriage. The pump was built for extraction and utility, but time and patience have redeemed it into a cradle. To silence the iron would be to starve the pipe-organ reeds and leave the dweller in a cold, dead pond. To give the dweller eyes—or worse, a mind restless enough to wonder what lies beyond the embankment walls—would be to shatter the only peace it has ever known.

So the power would touch nothing on the surface. It would leave the pale slugs to their brass junction boxes, and the strangler-figs to their bolted flanges. It would let the creature’s rasping lip go on polishing the piston rod, and its pink fronds go on drinking the mineral steam.

Instead, the power would look *beyond* the weeping walls.

It would follow the thick, tar-sheathed cables out of the hollow and through the earth, tracing them back to whatever forgotten substation, crumbling dam, or thinning grid still feeds that sixty-cycle hum into the root-mat. And there, far from the canopy, it would see the one thing the dweller cannot sense: that the world outside is brittle. Grids fail. Wires corrode. Distant municipalities turn off switches, or simply run out of the will to keep them on. One day—perhaps in five years, perhaps in fifty—the hum would stutter and die. The flywheel would coast to a stop. The mineral steam would cease, the cane-stalks would go mute, and the warm sump would grow cold and still.

That is where a wise and benevolent power would place its hands.

Deep inside the embankment walls, out of sight and out of mind, it would quietly sever the hollow’s dependence on the dying world above. It would graft the tar-sheathed conduits onto something older and less forgetful—a slow, deep telluric current, a patient pocket of geothermal warmth far beneath the bedrock—tuning the flow so seamlessly that the sixty-cycle drone in the root-mat would not flicker for even a fraction of a second.

Then, working as slowly as water, it would tend to the bones of the room. It would not replace the cast iron with shining, incorruptible metal—the creature needs the rust to taste, the scale to scour. Instead, it would simply *deepen* the iron’s patience, easing the microscopic fatigue inside the flywheel’s axle and the cylinder’s housing so that the machine wears down at the pace of geology rather than steel. Beneath the sump, in the dark below the petrified peat, it would nudge a slow, natural vein of copper and iron salts to weep gently into the water, ensuring the condenser’s breath never goes sterile.

And then, perhaps, before withdrawing its attention, the power might offer one small courtesy—not to change the dweller, only to greet it in the only tongue it knows.

On the next turn of the flywheel, as the piston draws back and the brass relief valve trips with its deafening *clank*, the power might lay a brief, gentle hand upon the water—not a shock, not a disturbance, just a single, warm harmonic overtone threaded into the forty-second pulse. Something steady and unmistakable, felt along every microscopic cilium of the dweller’s lateral seams.

*You are kept,* the water would say, in pressure and in warmth. *The rhythm will not break.*

Down in the dark beneath the liverwort, the great, blind creature would pause against the concrete plinth, its recessed seams flaring wide for a heartbeat to taste the sudden sweetness of the current. Then the cavernous hiss of the condensers would fade into the steady pattering of the canopy drizzle, the fronds would fold neatly back behind their shutters, and the power would step quietly away—leaving the hollow to its rust, its greenish-gold haze, and its long, unbroken sleep.
