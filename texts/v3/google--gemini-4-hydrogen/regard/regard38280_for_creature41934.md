At first, the attention did not touch the basin at all. It simply rested above the bruised violet sky, vast enough to feel the whole mountain range beneath it as a single wrinkled knuckle of stone, and quiet enough to hear the difference between a valley that teemed with green and a hollow where nothing grew.

It looked down through the fierce, unblinking noon.

Even to a power that had shaped stars and coaxed forests out of ash, the saddle between the three ridges looked, at a glance, like a place left unfinished—or a place that had already ended. There was only the hammer-blow of the sun, the chalk-bleached gneiss, and the dense forest of leaning ice-blades, ticking and sighing as they pared themselves away into the dry air.

Then the power slowed its gaze to the pace of the shadows. And it saw the seam.

It watched the narrow, papery thing slip sideways through a gap no wider than a palm, gliding on broad, fissured pads over glassy ice. It watched the blunt wedge of the head tilt toward the shaded underside of a blade, the long grooved tongue rising to catch a single bead of brine before it could reach the bedrock and turn to bitter salt. It felt, through the creature’s own throat-membranes, the small, sharp architecture of its world: not color or distance, but borders of heat and cold, the *tick* of a fracturing crystal lattice, the thin hiss of wind across a thousand serrated edges.

Benevolence, when it first looks upon austerity, almost always reaches to soften it.

The power considered the basin and felt the old, familiar impulse to mend. It would have been effortless to tilt the air a few degrees toward warmth—to draw the cloud-sea upward just far enough to drape the hollow in damp, forgiving mist at noon, to coax a thread of moss into the frost-split gneiss, to turn the fierce light gentle. It would have been easy to widen the creature’s recessed eyes so it could see the violet sky, or to thicken its papery hide against the dusk, or to call up a companion from the lower slopes so the northern ridge would not hold only one folded shape at nightfall.

Wisdom stayed the hand.

The power looked closer at the creature’s flanks, banded in rust and chalk, and saw what warmth would do to them. In a softer, wetter air, that dry, overlapping hide would swell and rot; the joints built for brittle cold would stiffen and seize. If the sun lost its hammer-blow, the ice-blades would slump into slush, drowning the ink-black corridors the creature threaded. If the afternoon mist rose too high or lingered too long, it would choke the hollow rather than sealing it cleanly, and the fine dust of pollen and spores from the world below would scatter instead of concentrating, drop by bitter drop, along the leeward glass.

Even the creature’s solitude was not an absence. It was the exact measure of the basin’s yield—enough brine, enough ash, enough shadow for one narrow life moving at the speed of the sun. To crowd it would be to starve it; to open its eyes to the glare would be to blind it.

So the power did nothing to the sky, nothing to the ice, and nothing to the creature. It watched the shadows lengthen eastward. It watched the creature climb the scree to the northern rim, stepping carefully past the cairns of milky quartz and the dry bowl caked with yellow salt, and tuck itself into the curve of the northernmost windbreak.

There, the power’s attention lingered on the old marks of hands.

Whoever had stacked those gneiss slabs and bored those three slate discs with a bow-drill was long gone—returned to the lower world, or dissolved into the mountain’s own dust. They had left behind a shelter that still held a memory of noon’s heat, and a small, accidental instrument hanging from a crooked staff of bleached mountain-wood.

As the temperature plummeted and the mist spilled over the lip of the saddle, the three slate discs drank the dampness and grew heavy with rime. A faint draft stirred the scree. The discs knocked softly against the hammered copper wire—a dry, deadened *clink*—and the creature, folding its wedge-shaped head beneath its salt-crusted collar, listened to that sound as the frost sealed it into the stone.

The power saw then what the decades had been doing in silence.

Frost-heave had begun to lever the base of the northernmost windbreak outward; another ten winters, or twenty, and the curved wall would slump into loose scree, leaving the northern rim flat to the wind. And the hammered copper wire, flexed thousands of times by the cold and the weight of the nightly rime, had worn thin at the crook of the staff—one sharp gale away from snapping and dropping the three slate discs into the gravel, where they would never knock again.

A wise power does not rewrite a song because it finds the key too minor. It only keeps the instrument from breaking before the player is done.

In the deep dark, while the creature slept beneath its skin of rime and even the ice-blades were still, the power reached down—not with light, and not with warmth, and not with anything the creature would ever recognize as a miracle.

Beneath the settled gravel of the northernmost windbreak, a single fist-sized wedge of gneiss shifted half a finger’s breadth deeper into the permafrost, locking the base of the curved wall against the heave of the coming springs. Up on the crooked staff, in the microscopic grain of the hammered copper, the fatigue in the metal quietly closed, leaving the wire as patient and whole as the day it was twisted.

And far below the lip of the saddle, where the flat sea of cloud breathed against the mountain’s flanks, the power let the forests and the valleys keep their ordinary, generous seasons—so that tomorrow afternoon, and for a thousand afternoons after, the rising mist would still carry its invisible freight of pollen, spores, and ash up to the rim.

By morning, there was no trace that anything had been near the hollow. The sun struck the pale gneiss like a hammer. The ice-blades began to tick and sigh. In the ink-black shadow of the leeward glass, the creature unfolded its long limbs, drew its grooved tongue up the freezing brine, and went about its quiet negotiation with water, stone, and light.
