At first, the power saw only a sealed box of concrete and glass, perched where no water had any business being.

To a gaze that moved at the speed of wind and tumbling scree, the tank appeared abandoned—a forgotten reservoir choked with weeds, its machinery seized in rustless rigor. A lesser kindness, passing over the ridge in the thin, sharp air, might have felt a sudden ache at the sight of so much light trapped behind leaded panes, and reached out to crack the seal, or drop a handful of silver minnows through the skylight to give the still water a pulse.

But wisdom knows that every place keeps its own clock. Before touching anything, the power sat down upon the ridge—not in body, for it had no need of one, but in attention—and let the mountain wind rush past it uncounted. It waited through the passing of a hawk, through the lengthening of a shadow across the scree, through ten full minutes of absolute stillness, until a faint, muffled *tock* rose through the concrete like the beat of a stone heart.

It waited through a second *tock*, and a third.

Only then, when its own perception had slowed to the drift of sap and the creep of copper ions, did it see the bundle of pale roots unlatch from the hydraulic cylinder. It watched the porcelain lattice extend an articulated limb across the emerald gap, saw the microscopic bloom of hydrogen pinpricks where silver met riveted iron, and understood.

Here was a consciousness that did not hunger, did not flee, and did not decay. Its thoughts were not sparks leaping across frantic synapses, but tides of mineral-bright sap rising toward a crown of bevelled prisms. It did not know loneliness, because it was not separate from the thing it tended; it was the one living hinge between the brass gear and the metallic leaf.

Still, the power tested its own impulses carefully, turning each one over in the cold mountain light to see if it was truly good, or merely impatient.

*What if I gave it company?*
It looked into the tight, cathedral-sized weave of pipes and rubbery fronds. Every cubic meter was already spoken for. A second conductor would not be a friend; it would be a short circuit, a rival weight competing for the same narrow shafts of sun, crowding a geometry built for one.

*What if I gave it animals?*
A snail on the glazed white tiles, a darting tetra in the warm current—would that not make the forest whole? No. The plants here had no defenses because they had never known a tooth. To introduce hunger into a place without rot would not be to enrich it; it would be to introduce grief, and cloud the crystalline water with waste the induction coils were never meant to clear.

*What if I spoke to it, and told it of the treeline far below, and the stars above the ridge?*
The power considered the bronze-ringed prisms, which drank only direct sun, and the dark leaf-plates, which knew only thirty degrees Celsius. To pour the concept of *outside* into a mind made for *within* would be to give it a horizon it could never cross, and a cold it could never survive. A kindness that creates a new impossibility is no kindness at all.

So the power left the water untouched. It did not stir a single frond of amber kelp, nor hasten the brass ratchet by half a tooth.

Instead, it turned its attention to the only part of the tank the keeper could neither mend nor imagine: the side that faced the sky.

For the mountain was not patient in the way the tank was patient. The mountain’s patience was made of frost-wedging, of winter blizzards that could bury a vaulted skylight under six feet of wet snow, starving the dark leaf-plates of sun until the sap froze in its porcelain struts. The mountain had lightning in its belly, drawn naturally to a high ridge crowned with iron and copper. It had rockfalls, and centuries of wind-driven grit that could frost clear glass into blind milk.

The keeper tended the equilibrium inside; the power quietly assumed the equilibrium of the threshold.

When the first November gale slammed across the ridge, packing heavy drifts against the leaded panes, the power did not banish the storm—the tank needed the passing of clouds, for it was in shadow that the keeper paused to listen and to think. It simply kept the wind’s fingers from gripping the roof, letting the snow slide cleanly off the bevelled glass as if the skylight were greased with warm oil.

When a hairline fissure began to open in the poured concrete where meltwater had crept in and frozen, the power drew the ice out before it could wedge, and sealed the seam so cleanly that even the crisp impressions of the steel formwork remained undisturbed. When summer thunderheads piled over the peaks and reached down with white-hot tongues toward the submerged copper busbars, the power gently bent the current aside into the barren granite below.

It did none of this in a way that could be noticed from within. There was no sudden flare of light, no voice in the velvet hush, no tremor in the water.

Inside, a cloud passed over the ridge. The cathedral of glass dimmed into cool, emerald twilight. Mid-reach between a stainless-steel pipe and a curtain of kelp, the keeper froze, its prisms dark, listening through its bronze-ringed frame to the faint subterranean thrum of the induction coils.

Outside, unseen and unthanked, a gentle warmth kept the frost from the mortar.

When the cloud cleared and the fierce, unfiltered sun punched back through the skylight, the keeper’s dark leaf-plates drank the light, its silvered sap stirred, and its limb completed the millimeter of movement it had begun ten minutes ago—its universe still whole, still thirty degrees Celsius, still endlessly, patiently its own.
