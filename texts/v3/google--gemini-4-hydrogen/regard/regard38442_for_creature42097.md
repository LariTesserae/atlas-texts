At first, they almost pitied it.

Coming from the high, open places—from worlds of clean salt wind, green chlorophyll, and creatures with eyes to catch the light—the sunken basin looked like an accident that had been left to fester. A scalding cellar. A deafening, copper-poisoned lung breathing its own exhaust under a sky of dirty glass.

If they had been merely kind, and not wise, they might have reached down at once. They could have wiped the mineral crust from the overhead panes to let the real sun pour in. They could have cooled the black lagoons, silenced the sub-bass thudding of the hydraulic rams, and given the sluggish, lobular things in the water a spark of nerve and mind—just enough to feel the relief of quiet, the novelty of sight.

Before they moved a finger, however, they listened.

They did not listen with ears, which would have been battered flat by the shriek of the steam vents. They listened the way the basin itself listened: through pressure, through heat, through the slow interference of waves across a dark lagoon. They followed the staggered pulse of the submerged rams into the gelatinous chambers of the creatures below, feeling how the fine metallic grit shifted and settled without a single jolt of alarm. They traced the blind, unhurried crawl of a bladder pressing its flank against a boiler vault, expanding into the open water, draping itself over a drift of scalded fern-pulp.

They watched a young one creep too far up a spillway and meet the rotating scythe. They waited for the flinch, the recoil, the shriek that never came—and watched instead as the severed lobe tumbled into the hot broth, sealed itself in glassy skin, and began to pulse in time with the engines.

Nothing here was waiting to be rescued. Nothing here knew how to suffer, and nothing knew how to want. To clean the glass would have scorched the violet moss and bleached the waxy ferns to ash. To cool the water would have frozen the creatures into dead tallow. To give them nerves in a vat of boiling copper and iron thunder would have been the cruelest gift imaginable.

The basin did not need mercy. It had already found a harder, stranger kind of grace: an absolute fit between what lived and what sustained it.

Still, wisdom looks further ahead than the present pulse.

Turning their attention upward, they examined the sagging canopy of rusted iron trusses. They traced the hairline fractures in the brick vaults, the thinning plates of the boiler casings, the slow, patient arithmetic of corrosion. The builders were long gone, and though the organ they had left behind was autonomous, iron is not alive. In another century, or three, a main truss would shear under the weight of its own wet moss. The grime-fouled glass would cave inward. The cold outside air would plunge down into the hollow, the steam would escape into the sky, and the lagoons would go still and clear.

So they did almost nothing.

They did not polish a single pane of glass, nor quiet a single valve. They left the darkness olive-amber and the air like warm broth. Instead, moving so lightly that not even the standing waves inside the creatures registered the touch, they laid a quiet resilience into the bones of the enclosure. Where the iron trusses sagged beneath their curtains of moss, the metal drank in a subtle, unbreakable temper—not gleaming or new, still pitted and red with rust, yet no longer brittle. In the mortar joints of the submerged arches, the salt-bloom and verdigris knit a little tighter, binding brick to old, mineralized creature to brick, so the footings would hold for another thousand years of water and vibration. Deep beneath the floor, where the mineral springs fed the boilers, they cleared a slow geological pinch that would one day have starved the pumps.

No miracle broke the surface. No light shone down.

Only, high in the steaming twilight, a rusted truss settled a fraction of an inch and held fast. A sluice gate groaned open on its chain, dumping a sheet of scalding water down the brick spillway. Overhead, a heavy seed pod dropped from a pale creeper with a loud *plop*, and beneath the mirror-slick surface of the lagoon, a heavy, unfeeling thing turned slowly toward the ripple—undisturbed, unawakened, and kept.
