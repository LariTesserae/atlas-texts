It would not arrive with light. 

Light is the first mistake a lesser power makes—a sudden, generous spilling of gold over the scree ridge, meant to cheer a dim room. A wise power knows that brightness here would only bleach the liverworts, dry the sweating seam to chalk, and wake the creature to a glare it has no eyes left to want.

Instead, the attention would come the way heat comes to the limestone: slowly, from the outside in, without disturbing the air. 

At first, it would simply pause above the scree ridge and look. It would see the dark crease beneath the weathered brow, the flat, secondhand dusk, the long stillness of the floor. It would wait—because wisdom knows how to wait longer than a hurried thing is willing to stand—until the pale dome near the back wall rose by a fraction of an inch, held, and sank again. Three water drops. Four.

A power that was only kind, and not yet wise, might feel a surge of pity. It might look at the lime crusted in the creature’s joints, at the four worn footholds leading up to a plain the creature can no longer climb to, at the smallness of a life spent licking mineral salt from stone saucers. It might be tempted to mend the joints, to bring a breeze carrying the scent of distant rain, to plant a flowering thing in the silt, or to send a small, harmless companion to break the silence.

But this power looks longer. 

It sees the unbroken reflection of the ceiling in the saucers, and understands what it would cost to ripple them. It sees the moss growing behind the ear-ridge—not as decay, not as neglect, but as a truce signed in green. It watches the broad, blunt hand reach out in the dimness to nudge the top stone of the three-stone stack by the width of a breath, restoring a balance that has no purpose other than being balanced. 

And it understands: this being is not waiting to be rescued. It is not enduring a hardship. It has spent centuries learning the exact temperature of this stone, until there is almost no seam left between the two of them. To give it back its youth would be to give it back its restlessness. To improve the hollow would be to evict it.

So the power does not step over the scree. It does not speak, or announce itself, or leave a gift on the floor for the creature to puzzle over.

Instead, it reaches—not into the room, but into the mountain behind it.

Deep inside the thick limestone of the escarpment, far above the smooth ceiling where no eye will ever look, a hairline fracture has been widening for three hundred winters, quietly gathering weight toward a sudden fall. The power does not turn the rock to iron; it would not steal the hollow’s eventual, natural end. It only eases the strain along the fault, shifting a thousand tons of pressure by the thickness of a hair, so the ceiling will go on lowering itself by time and dissolution, rather than by violence.

And farther back, in the dark, tight strata behind the wet wall, where a thread of groundwater is slowly being pinched off by a shifting bed of clay, the power opens the passage by a grain or two. Not enough to make a trickle. Not enough to hurry the moss. Just enough so that the stone keeps sweating at the pace it always has, releasing one heavy drop every few minutes, keeping the saucers full to the knuckle.

That is all. 

On the floor of the hollow, nothing changes. The air remains lukewarm and heavy, smelling of wet chalk and old dust. Inside the six-stone ring, or curled against the damp slate of the back wall, the creature does not know it has been looked upon. It takes its next breath—slow as a tide—and listens to the *tock* of the water.

The power lingers for the space of one more drop, simply keeping the hollow company from the far side of the ridge, and then withdraws, leaving no print in the gravel, and nothing out of place.
