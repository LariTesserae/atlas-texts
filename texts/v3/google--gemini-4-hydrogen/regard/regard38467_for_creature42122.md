A lesser mercy would have brought a lantern. It would have looked at the hunched, eyeless figures crouched beside the brick piers, seen the dark, the damp, and the forgotten purpose of the brass plaques, and felt a sharp, urgent pity. It would have straightened their spines, cleared the mineral curtains from the ceiling, given them eyes to see the amber bulbs, and led them upward toward the sun.

And in doing so, it would have destroyed them.

A wise power does not measure a world by its own light. When its attention settled into the hollow, it did not arrive as a blaze or a voice. It arrived by slowing down. It matched its own vast pulse to the hours-long rise and fall of the counterweighted pistons, until the cavern stopped looking stagnant and began to look like what it was: a lung, breathing once every half-day.

It listened through the iron railings. It felt the faint, electrostatic prickle of the DC current, the *plink* of groundwater hitting tin, the soft, dull *tink-tink* of a knuckle against a girder traveling fifty yards through the dark. It watched a resident kneel at the lime-crusted edge of the basin, dangling long, calloused arms into the black water while the ribbon-like swimmers knotted around their wrists in a bloom of bruised-violet light. It watched an elder sit motionless beside a nest of dry lichen, one hand flat against the warm brickwork, passing the grammar of healthy iron to the translucent young without a single spoken word.

The power looked for misery, and found none. It looked for captivity, and found only a world that had folded itself around its inhabitants like a coat of moss and pearlescent wax. They were not waiting to be rescued. They were already home.

So the first thing the power did was nothing at all.

It withheld the sun. It withheld sight, knowing that eyes in this place would only be two small, tender wounds waiting to be bruised by the dark. It withheld speech, which would only shatter the quiet against the wet limestone, and it withheld the memory of what the dynamos had once been built to power—for a tool that has forgotten its master is finally free to become a tree.

Yet wisdom also knows how fragile a closed loop can be when measured against centuries. Even in a place this slow, entropy still has teeth, however small.

So the power looked upward, through the dimness of the upper trusses and the miles of overburden above the vault, and placed a quiet hand on the geology.

High above, where the surface world shifted with its own frantic seasons, the power gently turned the paths of things away. A future borehole would strike an unexpected shelf of flint and veer three hundred yards to the east. A dry century would be guided around the deep aquifers that fed the cavern’s weeping walls, so the mineral curtains would never go dry, nor ever flood the sluices. An unstable seam in the unseen ceiling—one that might have dropped a ton of wet limestone onto the central island in another hundred years—was knitted shut, grain by grain, with a patient bloom of travertine.

Then, just as quietly, it looked inward at the machinery’s slow fatigue.

It did not polish the rust or replace the tarred cables; the residents knew the feel of those surfaces, and the arthropods needed the warm, rough ceramic to graze. Instead, the power simply *lengthened the patience* of the metal. In the deep, buried sumps beneath the pump-houses, where the finite reservoirs of ancient machine oil would one day have run thin and left the bearings whispering dryly, the dark rock began to weep a little differently—a faint, mineral-rich oil seeping in at the exact rate the pistons spent it. Inside the copper-wound dynamos, the tired metal was given the quiet endurance of stone, so the sub-audible hum would never thin into a rasp.

Nothing sparked. Nothing gleamed. Not a single resident paused in their crouch to wonder if the air had changed.

Only, in the deepest alcoves behind the bricked archways, where the residual heat collected in pockets, the warm air lingered a fraction of a degree kinder around the pale young ones curled in their beds of lichen. And out on the catwalks, when an elder pressed a broad palm against the yellowed glass of a pressure gauge, the pulse behind it felt steady, unhurried, and strong enough to last as long as the dark.

The power lingered for one full turn of the overshot waterwheel, watching a pale glider detach from a cable and slip silently between two pools of amber light. Then, careful not to wake the water or rattle the grates, it withdrew—leaving the hollow to keep its own good time.
