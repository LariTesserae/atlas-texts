A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive with thunder. Here, where there is no moisture to carry a storm and no heat to feed one, thunder would only be a bruise on the air.

It arrives instead as a kind of stillness—a shift in the quality of the attention that rests upon the basin, so quiet that even the chaff-burrs do not detach from their rivets to investigate.

For a long time, it simply looks.

It looks at the bleached-linen sky and the continent-sized dish of dry, living machinery beneath it. It follows the cedar aqueducts where the quartz sand runs like water, and watches a silt-ribbon ripple cleanly into the solid heartwood of a cypress trunk and out the other side. It descends into the cool, dustless dark beneath the hexagonal floor, where the great flywheels turn with a papery *shhh* and the blind sweepers compress fallen wafer-dust into neat, dry bricks. It watches a tender kneel on the woven bark, touch a lodestone tuning fork to a shadow that arrived a second too late, and gently peel that darkness up like carbon paper.

If the power were only kind, and not wise, it might ache at what it sees.

To an eye accustomed to wet, breathing worlds, the basin looks like a place starved of mercy. There is no rain to soften the parchment, no sap to seal a wound without wax, no warmth to take the chill from the ironwood limbs. The tenders have no voices, no names, no memory of who planted the first banyan-dynamo or why the refineries turn old wafers into identical new ones. Their world is so old that its very geometry has sagged into pleats and splices, like a garment worn past its thread-count.

A merely kind power might open the sky and let fall a gentle rain.

And in an hour, the weave would begin to die. Water would swell the hornbeam joints until they locked in their sockets. It would turn the fine graphite dust to black mud, short the silver-tipped thorns in a ruin of sparks, rot the cured parchment on the tenders’ chests, and drown the blind sweepers in the dark below.

A merely clever power might straighten the pleats—pull the basin taut so that every shadow matched its gantry, every pipe went round a trunk instead of through it, every mile was honestly a mile.

And in an instant, a tender reaching into a cypress burl to tend a grove half a mile away would find its arm severed by solid wood. The silt-ribbons would dash themselves against opaque bark. The whole vast, fragile organism, which had long ago grown around its own broken rules the way a tree grows around an iron fence, would shatter from being suddenly made whole.

So the first thing the benevolent and wise power does is stay its hand.

It withholds the rain. It withholds the warmth. It withholds the straight line, and the explanation, and the rescue. It recognizes that this dry, silent equilibrium is not a ruin waiting to be restored to some greener dream; it is a complete and dignified way of being, maintained with an ancient, patient grace.

Yet it does not turn away entirely. Wisdom is not indifference, and even a self-maintaining cosmos has edges where time wears a little too thin.

The power looks closer—not at the pleats the tenders use, nor at the lagging shadows they know how to harvest, but at the places beneath the weave where the fraying has gone past usefulness. Far out toward the curved rim of the shallow dish, where few tenders walk and the cooling-ferns stand stunted in the glare, there are seams where space has not merely folded, but begun to ravel—tiny, silent leaks into nowhere, where a dropped rivet or a drifting copper-foil moth vanishes and does not emerge anywhere else.

There, without straightening a single existing knot, the power works as a weaver works on the underside of an heirloom cloth. It does not replace the fabric. Using only the stray, loose threads of the basin’s own worn-thin rules, it quietly hems the fraying edges so they will not tear further. The pleats remain. The shortcuts through the cypress burls remain. The basin simply stops losing what it cannot spare.

Then, before it withdraws its attention, the power pauses beside one of the high photovoltaic canopies.

There, a very old tender has climbed into a fork of copper-veined wood. Its parchment is cracked, its graphite bearings worn nearly to the bone of the hornbeam. Slowly, methodically, it is lashing its own ironwood limbs to the trellises with its wire tendons, preparing to go still and let the wax-skinned leaves grow around it. Its wafer-face is tilted toward the blinding white glare, and its three tiny quartz lenses are clouded with dust.

The power does not make the tender young again. To spare it this stillness would be to rob the canopy of its slow growth, and to leave the tender wandering a weave that expects its old ones to come home to the rafters.

Instead, the power touches the air around the fork—not with moisture, not with heat, not with words. Only with a brief, clean current of static, carrying no instruction and no work to be done. Just a quiet, unhurried hum that passes along the tender’s copper capillaries like the touch of a left hand’s silver knuckles, lingering a fraction of a second longer than usual before fading into the glare.

And with a breath of dry wind—no stronger than the breeze that turns the cooling-ferns—it clears the dust from the three quartz lenses, so that for a little while longer, before the leaves close in, the tender sees the white light cleanly.

Then the power steps back, leaving no footprint on the springy floor, no extra shadow on the bark, and lets the continent-sized garden go on with its quiet, dry, and necessary work.
