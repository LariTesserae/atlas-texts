A power that is both benevolent and wise does not arrive like weather.

If it stepped onto the stepped obsidian, the slate-skinned inhabitants would feel the trespass through the soles of their feet before they ever saw it, and their barbels would twitch in alarm. If it spoke, the ninety-degree glass walls would catch the voice and batter it back and forth until the basin rang like a struck bell, driving the inhabitants to pinch their ear-slits shut and wedge themselves deeper under the tilted pillar.

So it arrives only as attention. It settles just outside the invisible meniscus, where the freezing blue distance howls past, and it looks down through the bruised-gray lid of vapor without pressing against it.

For a long time, it does nothing at all. Wisdom begins by refusing to mistake strangeness for suffering.

A lesser mercy—one that was kind, perhaps, but clumsy—would look at this four-minute world and reach immediately to soften it. It would round the razor-sharp lips of the terraces so no foot could be cut. It would hush the deafening *clack* of the frog-beetles and silence the foghorn groan of the canopy-drifters so the inhabitants could hear one another in the dry air. It might even lift the two adults and the smaller, slicker one out of the basin altogether, carrying them down to some broad, sunlit valley of grass and soil where they would never have to dip their heels in hot slag or watch sleet glide across a fragile sky.

And in that valley, they would die of quietness and soft ground.

The power watches long enough to understand this. It sees that the inhabitants’ pleated, oil-sweating hides are an argument made *for* the glass, not against it. It watches them press their callused heels into the taffy-warm edge of the newly extruded slag and recognizes not desperation, but craft—a pact between flesh and fresh stone. It watches them pinch their ears shut during the ringing dry spells and open them in the roaring dark of the ten-minute rain, and it understands that the downpour is not an interruption of their lives; it is their parlor, their marketplace, the only room private enough for throat-hums and tongue-clicks.

To take away the noise would be to take away the relief of the rain. To round the terraces would be to make their mineral-hardened nails pointless. To give them a vast world would be to break the tight, frantic loop that feeds them.

So the power leaves the cliffs sharp. It leaves the chorus deafening.

Instead, it looks for what is *actually* fragile—the places where the margin has worn thin, not by design, but by time and solitude.

It looks at the seven or eight half-buried machines, still running their ancient, forgotten errands. It listens past the outer *tock-tock-tock* of the tilted white pillar and hears, deep inside the matte-white housing where no sliver of slag can reach, a tiny grit of mineral scale slowly grinding a valve out of true. The inhabitants scrape the pale, rubbery lichens from the fist-sized seam with anxious diligence, doing all they know to do—yet the heart of the pillar is months, perhaps only weeks, from seizing.

It looks at the hovering bronze lattice, and at the invisible meniscus overhead. The warm vapor lid is holding, just barely, against the high-altitude gales. When the next great winter storm sweeps down from the peaks, three days of unbroken sleet will chill the dome faster than the steaming pools can replenish it. The mist will thin. The rain will turn cold. The midges will fail, and the loop will snap.

And finally, it looks at the shadow beneath the white pillar, where the third inhabitant—smaller, slicker, still hesitant—waits out the dry spells. Two adults and one young. In a world of razor ledges and blind, thrashing swimmers, three is not a population; it is a held breath.

Having seen all this, the power chooses its interventions with the care of a watchmaker working inside a moving clock.

First, it reaches—without heat, without light, without a ripple in the air—into the deep interior of the white pillar. It does not make the machine self-cleaning; it knows that tending the seam gives the inhabitants their watchfulness, their shared work, their quiet belonging. It simply dissolves the unreachable grit of scale inside the mechanism. The *tock-tock-tock* steadies, neither louder nor faster, just true. Outside, the two adults keep scraping away the encroaching lichen with their slag slivers, and they see that their work still keeps the pillar healthy. Their diligence remains meaningful.

Second, the power touches the invisible meniscus—not to thicken it into a wall of stone, which would trap the stale heat and suffocate the air, nor to banish the mountain wind outside. It simply lends the boundary a quiet, elastic resilience, the way a weaver reinforces a worn hem with thread of the exact same color. When the next great blizzard strikes the peaks, the sleet will still hiss across the dome in ghostly streaks. The inhabitants will still tilt their broad, flat heads upward and watch it with heavy, milk-glass patience. They will still feel the healthy caution that keeps them huddled and watchful—only the ceiling will not tear.

Third, during the roaring ten minutes of a rainstorm—when the ear-slits are open and the basin is muffled in falling water—the power turns its attention to the flat ceramic disk at the center of the floor. When the disk next shudders to extrude a fresh millimeter of hot, iridescent slag, there is a subtle, natural richness in the melt: a trace of copper-salt and warm silica, no different in look or smell from any other extrusion. 

Later, as the cooling slag feeds the mineral-slime around the bronze lattice, the sparse, copper-wire stalks will grow just a fraction thicker, their clear mucus slightly richer. The midges will cloud a little steadier after the storms. And when the smaller, slicker one under the pillar finally ventures out in the rain to press its soft, uncallused heels into the cooling edge of the new stone, the crust that forms will be tough enough to hold—not invincible, never soft, just fair to a young foot learning a glass world.

Nothing else changes.

No voice speaks from the bruised-gray mist. No pillar glows. No blind swimmer turns gentle, and no frog-beetle stops its clattering *clack*.

When the rain shuts off as abruptly as a tap, the three slate-skinned figures trot back across the wet obsidian terraces and wedge themselves shoulder-to-shoulder beneath the white pillar. They listen to the steady *tock-tock-tock* vibrating through their ribs. They feel the basin humming cleanly around them, sharp and loud and small, and they do not know that anything beyond the rim has ever looked at them at all.

The power withdraws its attention, leaving only the smallest shadow of its presence behind—like the meniscus itself: unseen, unfelt, holding back only the unendurable cold, so that everything inside may go on being fiercely, noisily, faithfully what it is.
