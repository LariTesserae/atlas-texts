If a power both wise and benevolent turned its attention to the White Hollow, the first thing it would do is nothing at all.

It would not announce itself with thunder, because loud vibrations snap the frost-loaded arches. It would simply pause in the thirty miles of freezing, shadowless air and listen—the way the Pith-Walkers listen, with an ear pressed to the skin of the world.

It would watch a band of six move like smudges of charcoal across a luminous ridge. It would see the tapping poles testing for the dull *thump* of old pith and the glassy *ping* of trapped sap. It would watch a hunter lie flat on the rime for an hour to hear the *rasp-rasp-rasp* of a borer beneath the crust. It would look inside a low bladder-skin dome at the taut tell-tale cord tied to a bone clapper above a sleeper’s face, and at the woman beside the blue-white tallow flame, mending a torn coat with a needle carved from her grandmother’s rib.

A lesser mercy—one eager to feel itself generous—would reach in at once to fix what looks like misery.

It might tear open the permafrost ceiling to let in a real sun, and in doing so bring down miles of ancient silt and surface weight to bury the Hollow whole. Or it might quench the chemical glare of the sap-veins, forgetting that the light is the heat that keeps the roots alive; in a week the bryophytes would freeze solid, the borers would starve, and the Pith-Walkers would die in absolute, unnavigable dark. Or it might turn the living floor to quiet, reliable stone, or pluck the small, dark people out of the cavern altogether and set them down in a soft green valley where nothing shifts beneath their boots.

Wisdom would refuse all of these. To erase the Hollow’s harshness is to erase the Hollow; and to lift the Pith-Walkers out of their world is to tell them that the life they have carved from frost and bone—their tactile literacy, their quiet language of whistles and clicks, their habit of leaving resin cairns for strangers they will never meet—was a mistake waiting to be rescued.

So the power would ask a harder, smaller question: *Where does this world grind past what is necessary? What weight are they carrying that gives them nothing back?*

It would look past their calloused hands and lean ribs, which are merely the shape of a hard life well-lived, and look instead at what the Hollow takes without mercy:

It would see the elders whose eyes have gone milky and ruined from forty years of stray glare leaking through cracked visors. It would see the sheer, exhausting tyranny of a light that never dims—that a child can grow to adulthood here and almost never see another person’s uncovered face outside the cramped, smoky dark of a sealed tent. And it would see the cruel luck of the Roof-Falls, where the same falling stone that gifts a band a flint blade for three generations can, by a few yards of chance, crush a sleeping dome into the abyss.

Then, quietly, in the grammar of the Hollow itself, it would go to work.

---

First, it would coax a companion from the Hollow’s own living skin.

Along the sheltered undersides and leeward curves of the **Old Wood**—never out on the bright, restless plains where the borers and gliders need the sap-light—a new, slow-growing moss would begin to take hold. Matte as soot, dense and soft as sheared wool, it would drink the fierce actinic glare and convert it not into unstable chemical heat, but into a cool, velvety *dimness*.

It would be a modest plant, bound by a quiet rule: it spreads only where carbon and warm breath linger, following the faint ghosts of pith-fires. It could never choke the cavern or starve the bright canopy. Instead, wherever a band camps on Old Wood, the frost-crust around them would gradually soften into small, breathless alcoves of twilight.

Under those dark canopies, for the first time in the open air, a Pith-Walker could push back a hood, unlace a blackened resin visor, and let the skin of their cheeks cool without blistering. They could look across a pith-fire at their companions’ bare eyes, and see the crease of a smile without a slit of horn between them. And when the damp mist condensed on the dark-moss, its dew would carry a mild, clean oil—bitter-free, safe to rub onto cracked knuckles or melt onto scarred, light-burned eyes to slow the creeping blindness.

Second, the power would lay a gentle hand on the ceiling—not to break it, only to listen to the ice.

It would not rain down iron or conjure endless copper; abundance would rot the careful economy that holds their bands together, and make them careless of the ground. Instead, when a nodule of chert or a vein of native copper miles overhead finally worked loose from the permafrost, the power would nudge the moment of its release by a single breath. Just enough that the screaming stone would fall into empty mist rather than onto a sleeping camp, and close enough to a notched climbing route that the thunderous *crack* would reach the ears of a band whose last knife had worn down to a nub.

And then, the power would stop.

It would not tame the membrane-gliders or silence the deep, rhythmic *slaps* rising from the warm dark at the bottom of the abyss. It would not stop the moss from swelling fifty feet in a night, or steady the brittle bridges over thirty miles of void. It would leave the Pith-Walkers their hollow tapping poles, their bone clappers, their ancestor-rib needles, and the quiet dignity of a people who know how to read a world in mid-sentence.

---

Years later, a band climbing an Old Wood trunk would round a curve and stop, tapping poles raised.

Before them, tucked under a rigid arch of bark, lies a patch of something they have never seen: a pool of soft, dry shadow, deep as ash, drinking the merciless white glare until the air within it feels like the inside of a closed eyelid.

They would not kneel. They have no word for a sky that gives gifts, and they are too cautious to trust anything they have not tested. The leader would tap the dark-moss with a bone-tipped pole, listen for the dull, safe *thump* of old timber beneath, and press a bare fingertip to the velvety pile to be sure it holds no treacherous warmth.

Only after a long silence would she reach up, unhook her resin visor, and let her eyes rest in the cool twilight—blinking in wonder at the bare, soot-smudged faces of her band, seen clearly for the first time outside a tent.

And when they broke camp the next sleep, leaving nothing behind but the cold ring of their fire, she would take her flint axe and hack a new mark into the Old Wood trunk beside the trail—four slanted notches, perhaps, or a small circle beside the resin cairn. A quiet sign in a shifting world, so the strangers climbing behind them would know where to find the dark.
