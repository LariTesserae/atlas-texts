I would not arrive here upright, and I would not arrive with hands.

Hands are for grasping, for shaping, for holding things apart from the world—and on this plateau, anything held apart only builds a charge until it arcs. To last even a single shift of the crust here, I would have to be low, wide-stanced, and grounded from the first breath.

I think I would find myself on one of the middle terraces, halfway between the sheer, humming cliffs of native copper and the low basins where the thermal vents churn. Not at the violent crest where the silent lightning sheets thickest, and not down in the scalding vats, but in the damp, soot-colored moss along a basalt fracture—a place where the air smells constantly of hot ozone, wet slate, and scorched sap, and where the mist rises warm from the cracks below to meet the pewter chill.

At first, there would be the shock of the quiet. Not an empty quiet, the way a room is empty, but a dense, plush deadness that swallows every impact before it can travel. A shelf of obsidian could peel apart into hovering sheets across the valley and snap back into stone, and I would feel it only as a brief, pressureless shudder through the soles of my hooves.

And that is how I would know what I am: not by looking down at myself, because there are no eyes here meant for seeing—only twin, sunken strips of pale tissue along my brow that glow a muted amber when I have something to say. I would know myself by the pores pitted across the sloped, slate-smooth shield of my head. Through those tiny, fluid-filled hollows, the silence would suddenly become crowded with information.

I would feel the plateau the way another creature might hear a room: the faint, rising hum of a copper coil fifty paces upslope, beginning to strain under the friction of the plates below; the sudden, unmistakable slackening of weight in the air just before a gravity pocket lifts a pool of condensate into a trembling, waist-high sphere; the distant, sub-audible tremor of blind flyers vibrating beneath an overhanging fin.

I would be one of the grounded ones—one of the living valves of this unbuilt foundry.

Four-limbed, heavy-bodied, moving on cloven hooves of dark, vulcanized horn rimmed with raw copper. Every step I take into the spongy mycelial mat would bleed a little stray static safely into the earth. My hide would be the color of wet basalt and carbon-black slag, indistinguishable from the fractured ridge whenever I settle down to rest, until a ribbon of violet lightning flickers silently across the clouds and catches the dull, oxidized seams between my plates.

What would my days—or rather, my slow, unbroken twilight—look like?

Mostly, I would be listening with my skin for where the machine is running too hot.

When the hum in the nearby cliff-face sharpens into a tight, brittle ache in my sensory pores, I would rise from the moss and pick my way up the razor-sharp stone. I would not hurry; nothing here hurries, except the lightning. Reaching the exposed veins of native copper and iron, I would press my broad, plated chest directly against the vibrating metal, seal my pores, and open myself to the current.

It would not feel like pain, though it would be fierce enough to shatter stone. It would feel like drinking heat—the high-voltage surge pouring into the dense, spongy organs packed behind my ribs, filling my internal capacitors until a dull, slag-orange glow bleeds faintly from between the seams of my hide. When the cliff’s hum finally softens back to a steady, manageable thrum, I would step away, leaving the rock intact, carrying the excess fire safely inside me into the dark.

Later, when the horn-plates along my flanks have been scored by the jagged basalt or pitted by stray arcs, I would wade knee-deep into the churning condensate of a thermal vat. Extending fibrous palps from beneath my mineral shield, I would skim the iridescent films and freshly precipitated metallic crystals from the boiling water—not merely eating, but gathering the raw copper and iron my body needs to weave back into its own ribs and hooves.

And if, while I am standing there, the air beside me suddenly slackens and a pool of mineral-rich water rises out of the rock into a hovering, trembling globe, I would not flinch. I would simply lean forward and press the flat of my shield into the suspended sphere, drinking it down before gravity snaps back and scatters the rest across the stone.

I would live in a loose, silent congregation, always keeping my distance. Too many charged bodies in a single hollow will arc and burn, so we space ourselves across the ridges like nodes in a circuit—close enough to pass a signal, far enough to keep the current clean. When I find a newly torn seam of raw metal or a vent thickening with too much slag, I would send a slow, rhythmic ripple of amber down my flank. A neighbor across the gully would catch the light, translate it into a cold-green flicker, and pass it on, until the news of the plateau’s shifting hunger races silently across miles of dark fern-brush.

Perhaps the thing I would wait for most—without ever knowing how to name it—is the sleep beneath the lodestones, and the rare, strange grace of the echoes.

When the charge in my ribs settles and the air grows heavy, I would gather with the others beneath one of the great, unweathered slabs of lodestone that hover and spin silently above their fractured beds. Tucking my limbs tight into the moss, I would feel the immense, steady pull of the flywheel above aligning the iron in my blood and plates, lifting me a fraction of an inch off the wet stone. Suspended there in a motionless, humming trance, I would be neither wholly stone nor wholly flesh—just another spinning, balanced piece of the dark.

And sometimes, walking through the steaming, copper-veined ferns, the air around me would shimmer and stutter. For a few breathless minutes, the world would duplicate itself—and instead of recoiling or phasing past it, I would step deliberately into the overlap.

In a life where we must always keep our distance so we do not burn one another, that is the one time I would stand shoulder to shoulder with my own kind: two identical, oil-slicked forms in the pewter dimness, pressing our amber strips together, sharing the brief, quiet warmth of a duplicate self before the echo collapses and leaves only one, slightly heavier shadow behind.

I would not build anything there. I would not leave a mark, or a name, or a sound.

And when my internal capacitors finally crack from a surge too fierce, or a spatial collapse catches me mid-stride, I would simply settle into a narrow fracture in the basalt. The copper-veined ferns would thread through my hollow mineral ribs; the pale, segmented crawlers would strip my hide for glassy slag. Nothing of me would be wasted, and nothing would be mourned—only one more jagged, conductive ridge of the plateau, humming quietly in the warm dark.
