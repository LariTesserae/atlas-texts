I would not arrive through the culvert. Anyone who swims in from outside brings the wrong clock with them—a frantic, kicking pulse that churns the silt and turns the dim water blind.

To belong here, I would have to wake up already calibrated to the four-minute turn of the flywheel.

I find myself in the narrowest seam of the basin: the alley between the eastern brick foundry and the stamping house, about three meters above the floor grates. It is barely wide enough for my bulk, which is why I like it. Below me, the geothermal manifolds bleed a steady, feverish warmth into the brine, and the brass intake valves glow faintly with their coats of chalky bacteria. Beside me, the copper cooling fins of the stamping house hum with a low, tireless sixty-hertz vibration. Above, the overhead busbars and braided conduits form a ceiling of metal and fibrous vine, hanging like a bead curtain in the amber dark.

I am one of the slow ones—the moss-backed climbers that wear the town on their spines.

From the inside, I have no clear edge. The water is thirty-two degrees, the exact warmth of my own sluggish blood, so there is no shock of cold or heat to tell me where my skin ends and the basin begins. My eyes are two small, tea-clouded marbles set into a blunt head; they tell me nothing about bricks or rust, only that the electrolytic vats in the factory hall across the alley have just pulsed sickly green through their grimy portholes, or that an indicator lamp down the row is still holding its dull orange thread.

Instead of sight, I have the hum.

Through the double rows of open pores along my pale, rubbery flanks, the drowned town is neither dark nor cramped. It is a clear, three-dimensional architecture of warm copper veins, leaking voltages, and slow thermal plumes. I do not need to look down to know when a blind crayfish shifts its weight on a porcelain insulator, or when a ribbon-eel slips out of a sluice-pipe. I feel the stamping house’s iron flywheel gather momentum through the calcified bones of my jaw—a long, rising pressure that crests every four minutes in a gentle shudder along the grates, then falls away into stillness.

My tendons are locked. Two of my thick, calloused limbs are clamped around a two-inch conduit, the other two hooked into the recessed, lime-velveted mortar of the foundry wall. It takes no effort to hang here. I could stay wedged in this warm current for a week, taking one three-minute breath after another, tasting the sweetish peat and hot transformer oil in the water, letting the silt settle onto my shoulders like snow.

My back is an old record of this place. Years ago—or decades, the distinction means nothing down here—I dragged my flanks across the fresh, pliable ingots piling up at the end of the winch-chain, scraping curls of extruded mineral wax and grey resin into the creases of my hide. Time and iron-oxide did the rest. Now I carry a hard, matte-black crust of magnetite across my spine, indistinguishable from the sealed iron girders around me. Olive-drab water-moss upholsters my shoulders. A stubborn tuft of leathery fern has rooted itself in the silt trapped behind my neck, its fronds dusted with precipitated calcium. When I sleep, I am just another piece of drowned infrastructure.

Every few days, when the moss along the lower walls has been rasped thin, I unhook my claws one by one and reach upward with my heavy upper lip. I nip a dozen translucent, gas-filled bladders from the vines trailing off the busbars, swallowing them whole.

Slowly, with the patience of a rising bubble, the trapped marsh gas lifts my mineral-weighted body off the pipes. I drift upward through the lattice of chains and cables—never swimming, only steering with lazy sweeps of my rudderless tail and an occasional pull on a hanging conduit—until I reach the cooler, sunless dark beneath the collapsed brick vault. Up here, the water is quiet and the silt has not settled as thickly. I hang upside down from a rusted gantry, grazing on the tender, clean moss that carpets the upper roofs, listening to the distant, muffled *tink* of an expanding thermal valve far below.

When I am full, I open my throat. A single, hollow *blup* escapes me and rises to join the trapped air at the ceiling, and I sink back down through the warm layers toward the hum of the transformer shacks.

Once in a great while—months apart, perhaps—I meet another of my kind in the alley beside the stamping house. There is no room to pass, and no reason to try. We simply halt, hanging side by side from the overhead pipes, and press our wax-crusted, moss-draped flanks together. For an hour or more, we stay like that in the amber-bruised water, exchanging warmth, sharing the quiet static tingle along our lateral pores, letting the slow thermal drift rock us gently against the brick.

Then one of us unhooks a claw, lets out a quiet bubble of gas, and sinks back toward the floor grates, leaving the other to keep watch over the flywheel’s next four-minute turn.
