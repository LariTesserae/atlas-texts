I would not be down on the floor grates, where the current pulls hard toward the sump and the pollock hang motionless in the eddies, waiting for the forty-second *boom* to scramble their senses. Nor would I be coiled inside the bearing-housings with the blunt-headed conger eels, enduring the endless, grinding proximity of the bronze shaft.

I would be up in the upper third of the vault, twelve or fifteen feet along the rusted gantry rail from the living lamp, inside the carcass of one of the dead ones.

The thick glass globe of the middle cage-lamp is still mostly intact, filmed over with the same pale, chalky calcium carbonate that coats the concrete piers. Years ago—decades, perhaps, when a slack haulage cable finally parted in a heavy surge—something struck the lower curve of the lantern and punched a jagged, three-fingered wedge out of the glass. The heavy brass cage bars held the rest of the globe together. Inside that hollow, sheltered from the eight-degree current and the wide, open dark of the bays, there is a space just large enough to pour a boneless body into.

I would be a cold-water octopus—not the quick, bright, shallow-water kind that flashes colors in sunlit reefs, but something deeper, slower, and built for the chill. Pale as tallow and wet limestone, my skin puckered with small, blunt tubercles so that when I press myself into the curve of the globe, I look like nothing more than another knobby crust of mineral scale and rust.

In eight-degree water, everything slows down. My heart beats at a patient, heavy cadence. I do not need to hunt often. Mostly, I exist inside the vibration of the iron.

Because the dead lamp hangs from a rusted chain-fall bolted to the gantry, it acts like a plumb line for every tremor in the vault. Every five seconds, when the warped bronze vane of the turbine grazes its housing thirty feet below, the *thrum-scrape* travels straight up the steel piers, across the corroded box-girders, and down the iron links into the brass cage. I feel it in the soft walls of my mantle before I hear it. Every forty seconds, when the counterweighted check-valve on the east manifold seats with its dull, metallic *boom*, the pressure wave pushes a tiny puff of black iron-oxide silt through the crack in my glass, rattling the small hoard I keep in the bottom of the globe: white crab claws, the opercula of dead snails, a few flakes of bronze, and a single brass cotter pin that shook loose from the crane rails years ago.

I use the larger crab shells to wedge the gap in the glass narrower when I sleep, leaving just enough room for a siphon to breathe and a single pale eye to watch the bruised rim of the amber light.

From here, I have a clear view of my neighbor.

The keeper hangs in the rising thermal plume of the third lamp, its slack, concrete-colored limbs hooked over the gantry girder. It is massive and blind, smelling faintly of biofilm and warm brass, and it never looks my way. Its world is the heat column and the forty-second clock of the check-valve; mine is the ironwork and the margins of the dark. When the keeper unhooks itself every few weeks and climbs hand-over-hand down the chain-fall to scrape the living lamp’s glass with its shard of shell, I stay tucked behind my barricade of crab claws, watching its pale knuckles brush through the tufts of dark red algae. Sometimes, as the rockfish scatter from the disturbed light, a stray mysid shrimp or a panicked juvenile blunders too close to my brass bars, and a single arm shoots out from the dark globe to snatch it back inside.

Mostly, though, I forage along the upper rails when the tide goes slack and the great turbine slows its cadence. I move across the corroded gantry without weight, my suckers tasting the cold iron, the colorless biofilm, and the faint metallic tang of the tar-wrapped umbilical. I pick small brown crabs off the upper ladder rungs before they can drop to the catwalks, and I pry gooseneck barnacles from the flange bolts, cracking them open in the shelter of the dead lamp and adding their pale plates to the floor of my den.

When the tidal surge peaks and the ocean’s weight drives the turbine into its low, continuous groan, the gantry shivers and my chain-fall sways in the accelerated draft. Flakes of rust drift past the cracked glass like black snow. Below, the keeper drops into the roaring dark to hunt the eddies. I pull my arms tight around the rusted socket of the dead filament, anchor myself to the brass cage, and wait out the rush—a small, quiet tenant in a darkened lantern, listening to the machine turn, and turn, and turn beneath us.
