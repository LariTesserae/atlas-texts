I would not be walking here. 

To walk through this basin is to be a noisy, temporary thing—a clumsy weight depressing the spongy bryophyte, a frantic heart thumping out its irregular rhythm, a pair of eyes straining uselessly against a dark that has no interest in being seen. If I arrived here with boots and lungs, I would feel like a static spark that forgot to go out: brief, sharp, and entirely out of place.

Instead, I think I would find myself already settled into one of the basin’s quietest folds, at a place where the equilibrium has had to work just a little harder to hold its shape.

Down where the living mat thins over a low, jagged ridge of fresh basalt, a mirror-still brine channel splits around the stone, forming a shallow, stagnant eddy. Above it, two of the fluted trunk-columns have grown slightly out of plumb, leaning toward one another over the years as the crust beneath them shifted by the width of a fingernail. Because of that lean, the braided tendon-vines strung between them hang a little lower than usual, crossing at an awkward angle to form a swollen, faintly warm transformer node right at chest height.

That is where I would be—not part of the machinery, and not separate from it, just a tallow-pale shape hanging in the crook of the margin.

I would have no face to turn toward the dark, no eyes to miss the sky. My world would be the diameter of my own waxy skin and the reach of the current. One long, mucilage-slick limb would be anchored down in the lukewarm eddy, its rubbery pad resting not on the razor-sharp basalt, but on a small, growing cairn of smooth, heavy pellets—copper and iron I have been quietly dropping into the dark for years, a slow, metallic record of every season that never happened. Another limb would reach upward, its broad pad pressed flush against the translucent casing of the crooked transformer node, drinking in the steady, high-amperage hum.

To be this thing is to know a kind of patience that has no name in a sunlit world.

Time here does not pass in days or rotations; it passes in resistance and load. When the planetary telluric currents swell slightly beneath the young crust, the node above me warms by a fraction of a degree. I feel it not as heat, exactly, but as a tightening in my pleated torso—a sudden richness in the dark. Without hurrying, I pump electrolytic fluid up from the channel into the chambers along my limbs, adjusting my own internal resistance to match the surge, keeping the circuit smooth so I neither starve nor burn.

Inside my translucent folds, the stolen current goes to work. It splits the heavy-metal brines I draw from the eddy, pulling the sulfur and carbon into my own soft tissues, binding me a little closer to the chemistry of the floor. The excess iron and copper gathers slowly, molecule by molecule, in the quiet dark of my lower chambers, until a nodule forms—dense, cool, and perfectly unoxidized. When it is heavy enough, a valve relaxes with a sound no louder than a wet leaf settling, and the pellet slips down into the brine, joining the others at my base.

That is my memory: a bed of smooth, unweathered stones beneath my foot, each one a century or a decade of quiet work.

And I would not be alone, though I would never see or touch another of my kind. 

Miles away—perhaps across a league of humming, copper-plated trunks, perhaps across twenty—another bridge is tapped into the same braided line. Most of the time, we simply share the load in silence, two tiny, steady draws on a continent-sized current. 

But every so often, when the vapor hangs especially thick and nothing in the basin has stirred for months, I feel a deliberate, rhythmic dip in the amperage. A fraction of a tone lower, held for the space of three slow sap-pulses, then released. 

It is not a warning, and it is not a question. It is just a way of saying: *Here is the weight of the line. I am still bridging it.*

I wait—because there is no reason not to wait—letting the current wash through my pleats, tasting the flat, mineral-sweet air with the copper-tipped filaments of my crown. Then, very gently, I constrict one of my hydraulic chambers, introducing a tiny, matching ripple of resistance back into the vine. 

Somewhere out in the pitch dark, miles from my crooked basalt ridge, another pleated body feels that faintest tug on the circuit, and settles a little deeper into its own waxy folds. 

Once, maybe, a strange noise passed near my eddy—a heavy, wet *thump-thump* on the moss, a warm cloud of salt-heavy breath, the erratic, chemical crackle of a nervous system flaring like a messy storm in the dead air. My crown would have tilted toward it for a long moment, filaments swaying an inch from that frantic, useless static, tasting something too hot and too fast to ever be part of the circuit. 

And then it would have moved on, and the silence would have closed back over the channels, and I would have turned back to the vine—to the steady hum, the slow electrolysis, and the endless, unhurried work of keeping the dark in balance.
