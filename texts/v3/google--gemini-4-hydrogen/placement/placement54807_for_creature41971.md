I would not be at the top, where the light is bruised indigo and the air is thin enough to make a muscle shiver just to stay alive. And I would not be at the very bottom, turning in the finger-deep pools among the shards, waiting years to grow the width of a fingernail.

I would be somewhere in the middle dark—about twelve meters above the scree, where the mist-threads thicken on the basalt and the breath of the wall is warmest.

At first, there would be no "I" at all, only the tide. A thirty-second draw—*shhh*—as the pleated hide beneath my belly parts and the pale, veined seam underneath drinks the thin air. Then the slow release—*ahhh*—and a faint plume of warmth curling past my throat, a fraction of a degree kinder than the stone. When you live wedged into the V-shaped trough between two sleepers, their ribs are your weather. You do not measure time by the slit of sky forty meters overhead; you measure it by the rise and fall of the slate-grey plates against your flanks, and by the slow, wet surf of ten thousand other lungs overlapping in the dark.

I am one of the pale ones. 

I know this not by looking down at my own chalk-white wrists—there are no eyes on my sloped, smooth face—but by the weight of the splinter bound in my left hand, and by the constant, restless turning of my ears.

They are the largest part of me, two wide, dish-shaped cups rising from either side of my skull. In a place this loud, you cannot simply listen; if you let all of it in at once, the wet roar of the sleepers and the *whoom* of wind shearing across the summit slit would drown you. So my ears fold, flatten, and swivel independently, slicing the noise into layers. I tune out the low, continuous wash of the bellows-lungs. I tune out the dry, papery rattle of the mica-wings shivering near the roof. 

Instead, I listen for the sharp things. The things that mean edge, or food, or company.

Right now, my left ear is angled downward toward the scree, catching the faint, silver *drip... drip...* of condensed cloud falling from a sleeper’s chitinous knee into an angular basin. My right ear is tilted up-wall, tracking a slow, deliberate *scrape*—one of the sleepers unanchoring a spike to shift a hand’s breadth to the left. Beside it, almost lost in the mist, comes a tiny, dry *tick*.

A flyer has come down. Its wings grew too heavy with condensation, or the gust that tore it from the ceiling pushed it too far into the dead air. It has struck a ledge six meters below me.

I uncoil from the warm trough between my hosts. My forelimbs are long—longer than the rest of my body—and I move belly-down across the pleated backs of the sleepers, using their hides as a highway. The raw basalt here is glass-sharp, unweathered by wind or ice; a careless slip would open the thin skin at my throat where the veins show indigo. Only the thick, calloused pads of my three-fingered hands can press safely against the bare stone when I have to bridge a gap between two sleepers’ flanks.

In my grip, the basalt splinter feels familiar and cool. I did not shape it. I found it three cycles ago in the shifting bed of shards at the bottom of the cleft, and I bound its base in tight, damp coils of twisted bladder-frond fiber so it would not slip in the wet dark. On my hip, slung in another loop of frond-fiber, hangs a curved, hollow rib from a sleeper that died last winter and clattered down to the floor.

As I crawl downward, bridging from one heaving shoulder to the next, a sound cuts through the surf of breath:

*Tock... tock...*

A sharp double-tap, twenty meters across the nave. My ears snap toward it, flattening to isolate the echo off the sheer face. Someone else is out on the wall, warning of a loose, razor-edged flake of slate. 

I pause on the ridge of a sleeper’s spine, brace my calloused palm against a dry seam of salt-crust, and raise the hollow rib. With the flat of my basalt pick, I answer—not a warning, just a single, measured *tock* to say: *I am here. I am above the scree. I heard the flake, and I am moving down.*

There is no hurry in the answer, and no hurry in my climb. In air this thin, a rushed motion is a wasted breath. I take the wall a hand’s breadth at a time, feeling the damp mist bead on my chalk-pale shoulders, tasting the salt and faint acid of the sleepers’ tracks where they have stripped the liverworts clean from the stone.

When I reach the ledge, my ears sweep the dark inches from my face until I find the faint, panicked *rustle* of wet crystal. The mica-wing is still alive, its four veined wings plastered flat against a damp seam of moss-hair, trembling weakly as it tries to warm its flight muscles. 

I could crack its carapace now. That is what we do.

Instead, for a long moment, I simply crouch there on my calloused knuckles, listening to it shiver. Forty meters up, its kin are hanging in a living frost against the bruised-indigo slit, breathing the dust of valleys I will never see. Down below, in the freezing pools, its young are turning blind and slow in the dark. And here in the middle, between the sky and the scree, there is me—a creature with no eyes, holding a splinter of the mountain bound in dead plant-fiber, listening to the breath of giants and tapping out small, careful rhythms so I am not alone in the noise.

I reach out with one long, three-fingered hand, feel the delicate, papery edge of its wing, and then—patiently, without malice, because nothing in the cleft wastes what falls—I bring the stone pick down.

Afterward, I will wedge my splinter beneath a swollen bladder-frond to drink the bitter water inside. I will tap a slow, rolling cadence against my hollow rib if I find a seam of mineral-salt too rich to finish alone. And when the cold begins to stiffen my joints, I will climb back up into the living wall, find two sleepers pressed flank to flank, fold my great ears flat against my skull, and let their slow, wet breathing rock me back into the dark.
