It would begin not with a breath—there is nothing here to breathe, and nothing in me wet enough to need it—but with a *thrum*.

It comes up through the broad, flat pads of my feet: a low, felt-muffled vibration, steady as a pulse that has forgotten what a heart is. If I shift my weight, the soles register a faint, gritty scatter of carbon-fluff and winged seed-husks on the floor, and beneath that, something utterly immovable. Striated, wind-planed, vitrified resin. Old wood turned almost to stone.

That is how I know where I am before I know what I am. I am standing on one of the middle terraces—the lip of an ancient retaining mesa where the geological, eroded bones of the city meet the frantic, stacked scaffolding of the newer tiers. Above me, the air is a rising chimney of thermal drafts and dry ash; below me, the sunken press-yards drop away into a deeper, slower dark.

I tilt my head—not to look, because there are no eyes in the concave amber dish of my face, only a smooth, heat-sensitive shield—and let the copper-veined bristles along my temple-rim fan outward.

The world arrives as a map of gradients.

Three tiers up and to the left, a bearing is running too hot; I feel its ember-warmth like a tiny, sharp point of sun against the resin of my cheek, accompanied by the high, thin whine of dry wood screaming against wood. Directly overhead, a sudden thermal gust corkscrews through a canyon of cane lattices. My bristles prickle as static builds along the taut bast-threads of the hanging seed-grinders; I hear the dry *rustle-snap* of their oil-cured membranes unfurling to catch the chaff, then folding shut to rock their internal gizzards.

At my ankle, something no bigger than a clenched fist skitters past on eight jointed legs of split cane, its internal coil wound tight and ticking faintly from whatever smoking axle it just left. It brushes my root-felt wrappings, registers that I am neither a pipe nor a source of waste heat, and vanishes into a crevice.

And inside my own ribbed, gourd-wood chest, there is a steady, measured *tick… tick… tick…*

I am one of the wound-ones. Yet even among my kin, I think I would belong to the seams.

Most of the walkers are built for the open tiers—long-striding, heavy-malleted, rushing toward the sudden *crack* of a buckling gantry to hack back the blind weaver-vines before they choke a flywheel. I am slightly more hunched than they are, my cane-and-burl frame patched in half a dozen places with mismatched, darker wood from older collapses, my root-felt bandages stained black with decades of mineral oil and ash. My resin face is scarred near the lower rim where a ruptured phloem-pipe once spat scalding sap across it.

I do not spend much time in the highest, loudest galleries, nor all the way down in the geothermal dark where the house-sized ganglia sit fused to the taproots. I walk the boundary between them—the ledges where the petrified heartwood refuses to flex, and the new, brittle cane tries to bolt itself onto it. Those joints are where the valley tears itself apart most often.

I kneel—a soft *shush-shush* of oiled pith sliding in bark sleeves—and press the copper-veined fringes of my wrists directly against a thick, root-like cable emerging from the mesa floor.

For a moment, there is only the ambient hum of the city’s static. Then, slow and immense, a tide of voltage rolls up from the sunken press-yards below. It has no words, no grammar a wet throat could recognize. It is simply the heavy, bruised awareness of the deep ganglia feeling a shift in the mountain’s weight: a main drive-belt two miles east is stretching toward its breaking point, and the geothermal draft beneath Sector Four is rising too fast.

The sunken things can only feel the whole; they cannot climb a ladder to turn a valve. That is what I am for.

I lift my wrists, straighten, and draw the heavy wooden mallet from the loop at my hip—not to strike a clogged pipe, not yet, but to listen and translate. I step over to a bundle of taut, oil-slicked phloem-pipes bolted along the terrace wall, press the concave dish of my face close to feel their pressurized pulse, and begin to tap.

*Tick-tick. Pause. Tick-tick-tick.*

The dry wooden code shoots down the miles of conduit faster than any sound could travel through the thin air. Up in the scaffolding, two other walkers will feel it through the soles of their feet—a directive to slacken the tension on the eastern idler-gears and bleed off pressure from the amber resin-vats before the heat spikes.

A moment later, another walker rounds the corner of the terrace, its tall, narrow silhouette emerging from the dark only as a faint displacement of warm air and the soft *shush* of its felt-wrapped knees. We stop a pace apart. Neither of us has a voice, so we lean our blank resin faces together until the copper-veined bristles at our temples touch.

*Snap.*

A tiny needle of blue static jumps between us, carrying the sharp, clean smell of ozone and scorched dust. In that single jolt—and the rapid flutter of sap-valves inside our skulls—I pass it the map of the eastern belt’s fatigue; it passes me the location of a ruptured oil-duct three levels down, already half-swallowed by pale, frantic weaver-vines.

Then we part without a gesture. It heads upward with its hooked vitrified blade drawn. I turn toward the deeper wall, where a massive burl-wood flywheel spins silently in a recessed pit.

The *tick* inside my ribs has slowed. My torsion-bundle is unwinding, the stored torque of the city bleeding out through my joints into the work of walking, tapping, holding the seam. I step up to the spinning wheel, brace my flat wooden feet against the petrified floor, and extend the notched key from my chest into the teeth of the drive-gear.

I lock rigid.

A rapid, rising *tick-tick-tick-tick* chatters through my gourd-wood ribs as the valley’s own weight twists my heartwood spring tight again. At the same time, I press the hollow tap at my wrist into a nearby phloem-pipe, letting warm, pressurized mineral oil surge into my sockets, flushing out the dry wood-dust and keeping the pith supple.

For thirty seconds, there is no difference between me and the wall, or the wheel, or the dark ceiling miles above. I am just a knot of tension being coiled by the engine so that I can walk back out into the ash and keep the engine from tearing itself in half.

Then the key disengages. I step back from the wheel, fully wound, humming faintly with borrowed static, and turn my blank face back toward the tiers.
