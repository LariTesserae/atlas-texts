To find myself here, I would first have to stop arriving.

Arrival implies a before—a cooler altitude, a dry lung, a footstep lifting from one paving stone and coming down on the next. In this valley, anything that insists on sequence would be scalded out of itself in seconds. The heat does not wait for you to acclimate, and the roar is not something you can listen to; it is already inside your ribs before you have the chance to hear it. A sequential mind needs silence between its thoughts the way a walker needs air between steps. Here, there is no silence to step into.

So I would not be a visitor on the avenues. If I am here at all, I am already part of the equilibrium—something that never began and therefore does not have to endure.

At first, I might look for myself among the green things: the emerald moss packed into the stairwells, or the broad, waxy leaves crowding the arcades. There is a comfort in imagining oneself as something turgid and unchanging, saturated with mineral water, holding a single perfected shape forever. Yet the moss is merely *held* by the city. It sits in the wet and stays. It does not do the work of keeping the noon from tipping into afternoon.

To feel where I actually belong, I would have to follow the sound—not away from the noise, which is impossible, but *into* its precision.

I find myself somewhere in the middle tiers, away from the outer precipices where the cascades explode into blind mist, and below the highest limestone needles where the wind screams rawest. Specifically, I am in a sunken plaza—or rather, I am *part* of the rim of one, wedged into the junction where a fluted aqueduct empties into a standing pool, just beneath the shadow of a colonnade that leans slightly toward the sun.

At a glance, you would mistake me for a knot in the calcified root-buttresses, or a pale, mineral-crusted swelling of travertine where an arch meets the pavement. I have no eyes to squint against the noon glare, no skin to blister in the sauna-air. Instead, I am hollow—a narrow, vertical chamber braided tightly into the woody lattice of the terrace wall, slick with the same weeping condensation that sheets down every surface.

If I had a shape you could trace with a hand, it would be something like a flute fused to a siphon. Along my pale, porous flank, a neat row of narrow slits opens to the scalding updrafts; from my base, a bundle of translucent filaments trails directly into the clear, fast-moving current of the aqueduct.

At first, it feels as though I am enduring a tremendous pressure—the weight of the water above, the screaming column of superheated air rising from the cloud-floor miles below. Then I realize there is no pressure, because I am not resisting it. The air passes straight through my slits; the water threads straight through my filaments. I am not a wall the valley hits. I am an aperture the valley uses to tune itself.

When the vibration settles, I notice the halo—a faint, permanent blur of atomized mist clinging to my outer pores, vibrating so fast and so evenly that to any eye I would look carved from dead lime. The superheated vapor around me bends the bright zenith light slightly backward, pulling the colonnade’s crisp shadow a fraction of an inch toward the sun. I am not doing this on purpose. I am simply the lens that happens when this much heat and this much hum occupy the same cubic foot of stone.

And yet, as I settle further into the chord, I realize I am not only on the rim of the sunken plaza.

Look down into the standing pool beside me. The water does not show the pale blue sky. It shows the wet, glistening underside of a root-arch, suspended over an endless, sunlit drop. And hanging from that inverted arch—trailing pale, hair-fine filaments straight down into the rising thermals—is the rest of me.

Or perhaps it is my twin. In a city where stairways climb toward bridges that pass underneath the street you just left, "above" and "below" are just two ways of holding the same note. My upper chamber drinks the current from the aqueduct; my lower filaments comb the rising heat miles above the white floor of clouds. Between the two, the city folds, and I am the stitch that holds the fold in place.

If you asked me what I *think* about, locked here in the deafening bright noon, I would have to tell you that thinking is the wrong word. I have no memories, because nothing has ever happened to me that isn't happening right now. What I have instead is *tension*—an exact, unbroken awareness of geometry.

When a single droplet falls into the center of the sunken pool beside me, I feel the ripple strike outward, lock into a crisp hexagonal lattice on the water's surface, and vanish—replaced instantly by the next droplet in the exact same spot. I do not *watch* this happen. I feel the weight of that frozen ring in my filaments hanging three ridges away, and in the curve of the fourth-tier waterfall that bends smoothly backward halfway down the cliff to pour into a cistern two stories above itself.

That is what I would be here: not a resident who walks the streets, and not a god who watches over them, but a single, resonant interval in the stone. A hollow place tuned just tightly enough that, as the wind screams up from the abyss and the water pours in its closed loops, the whole roaring, steaming valley stays balanced on the pitch—never swelling, never fading, forever at noon.
