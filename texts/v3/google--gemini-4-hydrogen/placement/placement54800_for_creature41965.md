Before I know what I am, I know the tilt of the stone.

It slopes downward, very slightly, toward the northeast—a dip so shallow that an eye would miss it, though there is nothing here to miss it with. I know it through the wide, cushioned pads that bear my weight. The bedrock is cooling now, giving up the dry, chalky heat it held all afternoon. Beneath my soles, the living floor shifts. Hundreds of small, pitted shells settle a fraction of an inch deeper into their hollows, their edges clicking softly against one another as the air begins to drop.

I am standing near the rim of a pan, though I did not choose it for the view. Up here, the sky is only a pale, flat glare that softens to grey as the sun goes down, and the horizon is a line so level it feels like the edge of a dish. What drew me here three mornings ago—or four, or ten; the count does not matter—was a thread of scent carried on the cooling air: wet lime, old salt, and the faint, sweet rot of algae waking in a shadow.

An older ring sits here, half-buried in the crust. Someone built it long before my hide grew thick enough to bear the noon sun. Three courses of cobbles, slumped inward on the windward side, their faces long since colonized by the flat-backed ones and draped with sleeping curtains of wings. The stones are good, though. Heavy, porous, wedged tight enough to hold a pool of mist until midday if the gap on the leeward side is closed.

That is where I find myself: at the break in the wall, with my forehead pressed against a loose slab of ochre-streaked limestone.

My eyes are small and milky, tucked back behind ridges of bone; they tell me little more than that the pale above is darkening and the stone below is grey. It is the rest of me that knows the world. My hide is dry, deeply fissured like the mud at the bottom of a vanished pond, and salt has crusted in the creases behind my shoulders. Along the broad shelf of my back, I can feel the faint, gritty tickle of the plateau grazing on me—tiny mouths harvesting the damp film that clings to my grooves, while the blind knots unspool from beneath the fallen stones to clean the dead skin from my heels. I do not shake them off. Everything here carries everything else.

When the night-mist finally arrives, it does not fall so much as gather. It rises out of the cool air like breath on cold slate, beading first along the brittle veins of the wing-curtains hooked to the wall beside me. I hear them unhook their lower edges—a sound like dry leaves turning over in a drawer—and feel the faintest stir of wet air against the cavernous nostrils tucked beneath my brow.

I lower my belly to the floor of the ring. The flat-backed creatures beneath me yield without hurry, pressing down into the dampness with a dull, hollow *clack* until my weight is settled. Through the porous skin of my underside, I begin to drink—not in gulps, for there is nothing here to gulp, only a slow, steady soaking, like chalk taking in rain.

If I am anything in this world, I am a keeper of edges.

I do not have the speed of the wind, though the wind here has no speed either. I do not have the rooted patience of the flat-backed ones, who will spend ninety years wedged into a single palm-width of rock and never ask what lies beyond the next terrace. I am made for moving what will not move itself, very slowly, from where it lies to where it can hold a shadow.

By the time the mist begins to thin, I have risen again. My forelimbs—the short, jointed ones tucked beneath the overhang of my jaw—reach out and test the loose slab with blunt, spatulate claws. It is heavy, twice the size of my skull, its underside slick with the pale creepers that gathered in the dark. I hook my stony mandibles around its rim, brace my horn-plated brow against its face, and lean.

Nothing happens at first. Then, with a dry, mineral scrape, the stone gives an inch.

I will spend the rest of the morning—and perhaps the rest of the season—bringing this wall back up to height. Not because the ring belongs to me. When the gap is closed and the brackish pool inside lingers an hour past noon, I may stay for a while, resting my chin on the cool inner stones, lapping at the thickened algae with my broad tongue. Or I may catch the scent of a deeper hollow half a mile off, where a cairn has toppled and left a nest of young without shade, and turn my heavy shoulders toward the slope.

Another may come after me and find the water waiting. We might even meet here, before I go. If we do, we will not speak—there is nothing here that needs the waste of breath. We will simply stand forehead-to-forehead in the quiet, our cracked hides creaking as we shift our weight, breathing in the damp, chalky smell of the stone we both belong to, until one of us reaches down to wedge a smaller pebble into the gap.

For now, the sun is climbing. The mist is burning away, leaving only a sheen on the undersides of things and the tiny, dry *tick* of shells warming in the light. I press my brow to the limestone, feel the steady, patient resistance of the plateau beneath my pads, and push.
