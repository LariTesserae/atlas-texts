I would not be in the open clearings, where the noon light drops straight down onto raw basaltic glass and leaves nowhere for a shadow to lean. Nor would I be up on the highest silica-slate ridges, where the air holds nothing but the final, patient stillness of things waiting to break.

I would be down in the deeper tangles, where the basin forgets to keep its layers separate.

Specifically, I would find myself at a quiet intersection where a stand of pale-jade reeds and a thicket of rust-stemmed shrubs occupy the exact same patch of ground—their hollow, glassy stalks passing cleanly through one another’s volume without a scrape or a shadow between them. A few paces to the left, a small pocket of sideways gravity holds a curtain of talc-fine mineral dust suspended three inches off the stone, motionless as a pane of frosted glass. It is a good room. It has no walls, only a crook in the light that hides it from anything looking straight across the valley, and a bedrock fracture running directly beneath it that carries vibrations cleanly from three directions.

As for what I would be: I would not be a visitor, soft and wet and tearing my feet on the razor-cleaved slate. To survive here at all, I would have to belong to the basin’s own grammar.

I would be one of the tall, quiet ones—built on a slender, multi-jointed frame of matte-black chitin and pale, vitreous bone, standing roughly shoulder-high to the reeds. No wet eyes to blink against the glare, no mouth to parch in the dry heat. Instead, I would look out through a cluster of milky, faceted quartz lenses set into a wedge-shaped head, angled slightly apart so I could read light that arrives along crooked paths. Beneath them, a taut, paper-thin membrane of spun silica would serve as both ear and tongue, trembling faintly to the sub-audible thrumming of the valley floor and tasting the dry, metallic tang of static in the air.

I would be resting with the three-pronged glass tips of my left feet braced on a ridge of dark glass in one layer of space, while my torso—shingled in overlapping louvers of translucent, amber-tinted mica—angled upward into the canopy of the *other* layer.

For hours, perhaps for what an outsider would call days, I would do nothing at all. I would simply hold my louvers open to the steady, unfiltered sun, listening to the faint, dry hum of my own solid-state tissues drinking the light. Below me, an articulated beetle might pick its way across the fibrous moss, its dry plates clicking softly as it grazes on stray charge. I wouldn't startle it, and it wouldn't notice me; to anything tuned to only one side of the seam, I would appear as nothing more than a half-formed shadow, or a cluster of amber leaves caught in a reed-stalk.

When the current in my louvers grew heavy enough, I would fold them down—a slow, economical movement, no faster than a shadow shifting—and lower my narrow, arching neck into the cushiony felt of the moss below. Dragging the fine copper fringe along my spine through the dry fibers would raise a string of tiny, crisp sparks, grounding the excess charge with a sensation like a clean, sharp chord struck on glass.

Then, perhaps, I would kneel by a crevice where the talc-fine dust sits undisturbed, dip my glass-tipped fingers into the powder, and draw a few measured grains up through the dry capillary channels in my wrists.

My life here would have no urgency, because nothing in the basin is running out. There is no weather to outlast, no rot to outrun. My attention would be spent entirely on the geometry of my half-mile of stone: knowing which seed-pods are swelling with internal tension, where a spatial seam has drifted by the width of a hair, and which clusters of rock bend the noon light sharpest.

Sometimes, I would press the glass tips of my feet flat against the warm bedrock and wait.

Through the stone, arriving first in my feet and rising into the silica membrane behind my lenses, would come a slow, dry percussion: *tap… tap-tap…* A neighbor, half a mile away across a ridge of pale slate, announcing a split pod, or a shifted shadow, or simply the steady fact of its own equilibrium.

I would listen until the last vibration settled back into the quasi-crystalline hum of the valley. Then, lifting one foot a fraction of an inch and setting the glass point cleanly back onto the basalt, I would tap out an answer—not to summon anyone, and not to hurry, only to let the stone know that this intersection is still held.

And in the long intervals between messages, I would notice the quiet signs of my own time passing: a faint, permanent stiffness in the hinges of my knees where mineral dust has settled for good; a slight darkening along the edges of my oldest amber louvers; the copper along my neck turning a deeper, quieter rust. There would be no dread in it. Only the slow accumulation of tension, and the patient certainty that when the equilibrium finally reaches its limit, I will climb to a high, sun-drenched ridge, fold my limbs neatly beneath me, and wait for the steady warmth to undo me—one small, brittle *tink* at a time.
