Down at the tree line, three thousand feet below the needle, you can tell the weather on the peak without ever looking up through the cloud-deck. You tell it by the pitch of the stone.

When the wind comes out of the west, the mountain hums. It is not a sound that travels cleanly through the air so much as a vibration that settles into the soles of your boots and the timber of your doorframe—a low, steady bass note that means the gale is funneling straight through the eye, lifting the mist, keeping the upper ledges wet and loud. It is a settled sound. Even in winter, when the spruce boughs are bowed white with rime, that deep thrum feels like a hearth burning in another room.

It is only when the wind swings around to the northeast that the note breaks. Down here among the twisted juniper and the last stunted mountain-ash, you hear it as a kind of stutter in the air—a flat, ragged slapping against the high ribs, followed by a thin, reedy chatter that drifts down in pieces when the cloud-deck parts. And if the chatter stops altogether—if the northeast wind blows for two days and brings nothing down from the eastern arch but empty, choppy gusts—then I know a binding has rotted through.

That is where I live, and that is what I am: a mender of small, high things, belonging neither to the valleys where the sheep are sheared nor quite to the bare gneiss above. My house is tucked into the lee of a granite shoulder just where the timber gives up, built low enough that the snowdrifts bury the eaves by December. I keep a few goats, I gather resin and juniper wood, I trade salt and copper wire with the pack-traders who come up the lower gorge in August. Mostly, I listen to the peak.

When the northeast voice goes silent, I wait for a morning with a heavy grey sky—never a clear day, because clear days bring the squalls of sun that blind you off the wet rock and set the falcons stooping too close to the arches. I pack light: a coil of waxed sinew, two or three spare lengths of hollowed goat-horn and swan-wing bone I’ve seasoned over the hearth, a handful of dry juniper twigs wrapped in oiled cloth, and a small hammer.

The climb takes half a day. You leave the trees behind within an hour, then the heather, then even the tough cushion-moss that holds the scree together. By the time you reach the narrow, curved sill of the western lip, your lungs are burning with thin air and your clothes are already soaked through from walking inside a cloud.

I never linger on the western sill. The forty-foot drop to the sloping floor is slick as grease, and the wind there wants to lift you by the ribs and drop you into the valley. Instead, I edge along the southern curve, keeping three points to the rock—both hands and a boot, or a knee wedged into a crack—until I drop behind the low dry-stone windbreak.

It isn’t much, that windbreak. Whoever stacked those flat stones before my time—my grandfather’s people, or someone older still—wedged the gaps with dried moss that has long since turned black and spongy. Still, it breaks the worst of the draft. You can catch your breath there, scrape the grit from your eyes, and listen.

Even in the lee of the wall, the chamber is deafening. Water slaps constantly from basin to basin. Swifts burst out of the upper pockets in dark, chattering ribbons, and somewhere out on the mossy shelves a marmot barks once, twice, before diving underground. Behind all of it is that low, trembling hum in the stone. Sometimes, when the gale drops for three or four heartbeats, the hum stays right where it is—a deep, wet, woody resonance that seems to come from the floor beneath your heels. I used to think the gneiss simply held the wind the way a struck iron pot holds a ring. Now I am less sure, though I have never gone looking down in the saucer-basins to test the boulders with my hammer. Some things in a high place are better left as stone.

The ladder is twenty feet up the southern wall, anchored with copper pins that have gone green as verdigris. I always test the lowest rung with my full weight before I trust it; the mountain-ash poles are weathered silver, and the blackened sinew swells and loosens in the damp. Halfway up, you pass the level where the beard lichen streams horizontally off the ribs like wet hair, brushing cold against your cheek.

At the top sits the niche. It is barely wide enough to sit cross-legged in, hollowed by frost long before anyone laid the hearth-ring of stones inside it. I never stay long—an hour, maybe two, just enough to strike a spark into the dry juniper twigs I carried up, warm my stiff fingers over a palm-sized flame, and melt the wax on my fresh sinew. The smoke doesn’t linger; the updraft through the oculus snatches it straight out of the niche before it can even smell up the rock. Still, I keep the fire small and brief. The place doesn’t like dryness. You can feel it in the way the moss around the sill seems to shrink back from the heat, and in the sudden, restless clicking of the air when a gust catches the flames.

Once my fingers can feel the thread again, I climb out of the niche and traverse east, working along the ribs just below the crown where the footing is narrow and the fleshy rosette plants wedge into every crack. If the sun happens to break through for a minute while I’m crossing, those translucent leaves warm up and snap like tiny wet blisters against my ankles, spilling cold mist-water down my boots.

High in the eastern arch, across a gap no wider than my arm-span, the line of bone and horn hangs green with lichen.

Usually it is only one or two of the bindings that have given way—frayed by grit-laden wind, or snapped clean by ice—leaving half the tubes dangling silent against the gneiss. I wedge my boots into the springy turf below the gap, lean my shoulder against the cold, weeping wall, and work quickly while the cloud-bank holds. You don’t tune the tubes to a human scale; you just leave enough slack in the waxed sinew that they can clatter freely against the stone when the northeast wind hits them, and enough tension that the air splits cleanly across their hollowed mouths.

Every time I work up there in the swirling grey, I get the strangest feeling that I am not alone on the eastern ribs. Nothing moves within arm’s reach—there are no swifts in the cloud-banks, and the falcons stay outside the arch when the visibility drops. Yet the stone behind my shoulder feels oddly deliberate, almost warm beneath its coat of mineral water, and once or twice I have sworn I heard a soft, wet click right beside my ear, like a throat clearing or a latch settling into place.

I don’t look around. If you turn your head too fast on a forty-foot wet ledge, you lose your balance, and besides, a mender doesn’t need to see who listens to the instrument.

When the last knot is pulled tight and the new horn-tube hangs level with the old ones, I test it with a flick of my thumb. It gives a thin, hollow *clack* against the gneiss, and a breath of northeast draft catches the lip of it—just a faint, reedy sigh, barely audible over the roar of the chamber.

Then I climb back down to the niche, scatter the cold juniper ash with my boot heel so the next rain washes it clean, and take the ladder down to the floor. By the time I reach the western sill and start the long descent toward the tree line, the cloud-bank has usually thickened again, swallowing the eye behind me.

Halfway down the scree, if the wind is still sitting in the northeast, I’ll stop for a moment on a dry boulder and listen back up toward the needle. Through the mist and the distance, I can just catch it: the thin, clattering chorus of the bones high in the eastern arch—and underneath it, steady and deep and holding the whole mountain together, a low, wet hum answering back.
