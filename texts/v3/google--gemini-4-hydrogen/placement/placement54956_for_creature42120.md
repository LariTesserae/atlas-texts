I would be on the lip of a terraced ridge, half-buried in sodden micro-moss, waiting for something thirty feet tall to lose its balance.

Up here, where the ancient petrified bark steps down toward a sunken flat, the air is thin and sharp enough to sting the slits of my nostrils. Under the clear, ordinary daylight, the basin below looks like an abandoned loom still running on its own—braided slurry-vines pulsing in slow, muscular waves, silver-barked reeds swelling with acidic sap, faint blue-white arcs flickering silently from tip to tip. Yet nothing reaches me as sound. The valley is wrapped too tightly in wet fleece for the air to carry a snap or a groan.

Instead, I know the basin through the pale, calloused wax of my soles.

Even sitting still, my feet are reading the peat. Beneath me, a thick conduit-vine is driving mineral-rich slurry up from the deep furnaces, each peristaltic surge arriving as a slow, heavy pressure against my heels. Farther out in the flat, a vascular tower—grown to full height since morning—is beginning to lean under the weight of its own crystalline resin. I cannot hear the cellulose straining, and through my cloudy second lids it is only a pale, blurred column against the mist, but I feel the tremor of its roots loosening in the compacted turf.

So I wait. Patience is not a virtue here; it is simply the shape of a body built low to the ground.

I am broad-shouldered, shingled from neck to rump in dark, non-conductive horn that has fused, over years of mist and resin-fall, with the valley’s own crust. Between the overlapping plates, the micro-moss has taken root, keeping me the color of wet umber and dull pewter. Beneath the armor, my hide stays slick with pale, sulfur-scented oil, shedding the freezing vapor before it can reach the skin. On my back, tucked into the crevice behind my left shoulder, two pale, heavy-shelled beetles are working their way along a seam, scraping away the mineral crust that would otherwise lock my plates stiff. Their tiny, steady rasping is the closest thing to company I need.

When the tower in the flat finally gives way, it does not crash. It settles into the moss with a single, muffled thud that travels through the peat and into my knees.

I uncurl, letting my short forelimbs take my weight, and move down the terrace. Every step across the acid-secreting mats at the edge of the flat brings a faint, dry hiss from my foot-pads as the outer layer of dead wax sacrifices itself to the sting, already regrowing from within. Along my jaw, two pale filaments slip out of their grooves, unfurling into the damp air to taste the static.

Halfway to the fallen tower, the filaments prickle—a sharp, metallic tingle against my cheek.

I drop flat into the moss without thinking, tucking my wedge-shaped head between my shoulders and flicking my second lids shut. A heartbeat later, a reed-stand ten paces to my left discharges a silent blue-white arc into a swollen spore-boiler. The boiler’s waxy seam splits, venting a plume of warm, damp gas that freezes instantly into delicate rime on the surrounding needles. If I had stayed upright, the arc would have found the resin on my back.

I stay flat until the tingle fades from my filaments, then rise and cross to the fallen tower.

I have only a narrow window before the acid mats beneath the wreckage wake up and begin dissolving it back into the nearest conduit. Using the blunt, leathery-sheathed claws of my forelimbs, I wedge open a split in the still-warm vascular chamber. The steam rises against my snout—thick, mineral-sweet, carrying the scent of sulfur and uncured resin. I scoop out the dark paste inside, eating steadily, unhurriedly, while the moss around my feet begins to bubble faintly where the mat’s secretions meet the fresh cellulose.

When the paste turns cold and the acid begins to bite through the tower’s outer bark, I leave the rest to the valley and wander over to a dark condensation pool nearby.

Around its rim, the broad-leaved press-plants lie open like flat, pale beds. Earlier, on the ridge, I gathered four iron-hard seeds flung from a snapping tendril—dense enough to crack a jaw, useless to me on their own. I nudge them out from the creases of my leathery sheaths and drop them neatly onto the center of the widest leaf.

Then I sit by the pool’s edge and watch the water hold the clear, cold sky.

Slowly, with the terrible patience of things that have no muscles, only pressure and time, the broad leaf folds inward over the seeds. I feel the faint, rhythmic squeeze through the peat. A fine puff of pale metallic dust drifts off the rim on the cold breeze, and beads of refined sulfur gather along the leaf’s veins. When it finally relaxes and opens again, the shells are crushed flat, and I lean in to lap up the rich, sulfurous pulp.

By the time I finish, the light over the ridges is thinning, and the mist is thickening into freezing pockets along the flats. A static charge has been building in my horn plates all afternoon—a dry, restless weight along my spine—and the beetles in my seams have gone still, tucked deep against the cold.

Before I leave the basin, I lift one heavy heel and drive it down into the compacted peat. Once. Then twice.

The double thud rolls outward through the petrified bark, indistinguishable from a collapsing grove to anything gliding high overhead, but clear enough to any other low, armored shape resting in the flats: *This basin has been worked. The tower is down.*

I do not wait for an answer. I climb back up the terraced ridge to where an ancient slurry-vine lies emptied and dry—a hollowed husk as wide as a tree trunk, half-buried in the older strata. I slip headfirst into the dark, winding down through the braided roots beneath the peat until the freezing air falls away and the faint, steady warmth of the unseen root-furnaces rises to meet my belly.

There, in the dry, insulated dark, with the static draining slowly out of my plates into the earth and the beetles quiet against my back, I curl up and wait for the next thing to break.
