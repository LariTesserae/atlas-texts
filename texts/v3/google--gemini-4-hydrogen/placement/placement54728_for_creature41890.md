I wouldn’t be inside the glass. I don’t have the lungs for twenty-eight degrees and perpetual vapor, and my hands are too dry—they crack in the cold, not from terracotta edges, and they’d swell up useless in a week of that wet heat.

I’d be at the other end of the wire.

If you follow the steel piers down—past the bright, un-pitted rivets, past the cross-bracing where the wind whistles a flat C-sharp, down through the flat white lid of the cloud-deck—the steel eventually meets stone. Not a valley, not yet, just a bare granite shoulder jutting out of the mountain like a kneecap, scoured clean of lichen by ice-grit. That is where I’d live: at the Head-Frame, in a low-slung shack of tarred timber and corrugated iron bolted straight into the rock with leaded anchor-bolts.

I am a line-tender. The builders call us *skip-men*, though half the time we’re just greasers and knot-checkers.

From my doorway, you can’t see the valley below—it’s buried under a shifting floor of cloud that looks solid enough to walk on, bright and blinding when the sun hits it. What you see is the sky, and the cables. Three twin-hawser lines fan out from the heavy cast-iron winches in my shed, rising at a steep, improbable angle into the blue until they dwindle to pencil-strokes and vanish into the hulls of the piers. On clear mornings, before the glare gets too sharp, you can see the conservatories hanging out there above the mist: long, narrow railway-cathedrals of glass and dark iron, perched on legs so thin they look like water-striders paused on a pond.

My world is the cold side of the glass.

Out here, the air has no smell at all except ozone, cold granite, and whatever I’m burning in my stove. Everything is dry, crisp, and loud. The wind never stops; it hums in the guy-wires, rattles the tin roof of the winch-shed, and pushes ice-crystals across the concrete apron like dry salt. While the commissioner inside his pier is listening to the polite *tock* of brass float-valves and the soft sigh of steam vents, I’m listening to the pitch of my cables. You can tell a lot about a hawser with a brass tuning-hammer and a gloved ear. When the wind shifts northeast, the long span out to Pier Four drops a half-step in tone, and I know to go out with the wrench and take two turns on the tensioning-screws before the carrier-skip starts to sway.

I don’t build the stations, and I don’t tend the green. I am the thread that keeps them from drifting off into the sky.

Twice a week—three times, if a pier is still in its first, frantic year—I load the iron carrier-skip. It’s a stubby, open-topped cage slung under a pair of grooved bogie-wheels, smelling of axle-grease and damp pine. Into it go the things the cold world makes that the warm world can’t: crates of tinned beef and salted fish, coils of tarred packing-twine, sacks of crushed slag, spare panes of ribbed glass packed tight in oat-straw, and small, soldered tins of loose tea. Sometimes there’s a bundle of mail wrapped in oilcloth, or a replacement pressure-gauge whose needle hasn’t yet been fogged by mineral steam.

Then I climb in after it, release the brake-lever, and ride the wire out over the void.

There is a moment, ten paces past the granite lip, where the sound of the rock-anchor drops away and you are simply hanging in the bright, thin air. Beneath your boots, through the grated floor of the skip, there is nothing but a hundred fathoms of empty space and the white roof of the clouds. Above your head, the bogie-wheels sing a steady, metallic *whirr-clack, whirr-clack* over the cable-joins. The cold gets inside your oiled wool coat and sits against your ribs.

From fifty yards out, you can always tell how old a conservatory is just by looking at the glass.

The old ones—five, eight, ten years on—glow like dull green lanterns. Their iron frames are streaked orange-brown under every rivet, their upper louvers are crusted with pale mineral scale, and the moss has crept out through the glazing-bars to freeze in black fringes along the eaves. When you dock at an old pier, three or four crewmen in dry aprons are already waiting on the platform with wooden crates of dried bladder-vine fiber and glass demijohns of clarified, mineral-sweet distillate to send back down to the valley. They talk fast, ask about the weather down-mountain, and hand you a mug of something hot before you’ve even set the brake.

The young ones are different. They are sharp-edged and glaring, their rivets still bright as dimes, their glass so clean you can see the dark silhouettes of the overhead trusses and the first, furious push of elephant-ear leaves pressing against the baffles.

When the skip bumps into the docking-collar beneath the wind-turbine spar, I secure the chain, unlatch the heavy, gasketed iron hatch, and step inside.

It hits you like a wet wool blanket. Every time, no matter how many trips I make, the transition steals my breath. My goggles fog blind in half a second; my throat, raw from the thin mountain air, suddenly drinks in twenty-eight degrees of wet iron, crushed fern, and fresh mortar. I have to stand there on the grated landing with my goggles pushed up on my forehead, blinking like an owl, waiting for the sweat to break out under my heavy coat while the noise of the wind vanishes behind the hatch and the quiet, hydraulic *tock-and-sigh* of the house closes around me.

Usually, I don’t go far inside. I leave the crates on the dry concrete lip by the door. The commissioner—whoever has the first-year contract on that pier—comes up out of the green to meet me. They all look a little alike after a few months: pale, slightly puckered at the wrists, smelling faintly of boiled ferns and boiler-grease, their knuckles crosshatched with tiny red terracotta nicks. They don’t say much. Solitude in a warm, loud-growing room makes people frugal with words. He’ll check the manifest with a damp thumb, maybe hand me a tin of something he’s pickled from the sump-ferns, or a bundle of water-warped notebooks to go down to the engineers.

Once, while I was waiting on the dock for him to fetch an empty slag-sack, a little long-legged wren darted past my ear through the open hatch, carrying a drowned moth in its beak. It didn’t seem to mind the cold at all. It flew straight out along my cable, using the hawser as a highway back toward the granite ridge, where its kind nests in the dry crevices under my winch-shed eaves.

I like that—knowing the birds use my lines the same way the water uses the copper gutters.

After twenty minutes, my wool coat is soaked through with vapor and I’m itching to get back out where I can breathe. I pull the iron hatch shut behind me, dog the latches tight, and climb back into the cold skip. As the winch pulls me away from the glass, watching the bright, warm box shrink back into the empty sky, I always feel a quiet, settled relief.

Someone has to live inside the garden to keep it from eating the pipes. Someone else has to stay out on the rock, in the thin, freezing air, with an oilcan and a tuning-hammer, making sure the wires don’t sing too flat when the wind comes up.
