I would not be up on the glass roofs with the Warmth-Tenders, though I would know the smell of their crushed-basalt beds—wet moss, bitter roots, and the faint, sweet steam of bramble-leaf steepings drifting down through the iron grates. And I would not be down in the lowest dark with the Ballast-Crews, knee-deep in black condensation, shoving the city’s broken bones back into the earth’s fresh cracks.

I would be somewhere in the middle layers, down on one knee in a damp cable trench, with a brass needle-meter cupped in my palm and a pale salamander warming itself against the back of my wrist.

In a city that unbolts itself every night, the heavy work belongs to the Riggers and the Masons. They are the ones who jack the three-story tenements onto rubber-wheeled dollies, who winch a foundry two blocks west before dawn, who lay down quick mortar that everyone knows is only borrowing space from the rock. But once the big iron is bolted down in its new berth and the main copper busbars are clamped, somebody has to come along behind them and teach the alleyways where they are now.

That is where I would find myself: in the quiet wake of a move, walking the seams between newly arrived buildings with a canvas satchel of jumper-leads, a spool of tarred twine, and three stubs of soapstone in my pocket.

You wake up differently when your house has traveled while you slept. There is no jolt—the hydraulics are too slow for that, and the roof-slings absorb the micro-tremors—only a change in the *hum* coming up through the floorboards. Yesterday, the low vibration under my heels was the steady, three-phase thrum of a dynamo hall. This morning, it is the intermittent *shhh-click* of an automated crusher two tiers down, and the pale light coming through the small square window smells faintly of crushed air-fern instead of transformer oil. You don’t need to look outside to know the block has shifted. You just tap your toe twice on the timber floor, pull on your felt-soled boots, and step out onto a catwalk that did not exist in this exact spot yesterday.

My work is in the trenches and along the baseboards of the world—the low-voltage nervous system that keeps the basin from going blind when its bones rearrange.

Down where the freight carts nose through the narrow canyons, their rubber tires follow copper induction loops buried just under the duckboards and packed into the mortar seams. When a Listener chalks an arrow on a slate and a whole row of workshops gets winched thirty yards clear of a rising basalt shelf, those buried loops are severed or bent out of true. If nobody tends them, the squat, blind carts will come humming around a corner carrying two tons of fresh-cut stone and nose straight into a brick wall, pushing against it in stubborn, silent confusion until their batteries drain.

So I crawl into the narrow, moss-lined channels beside the tracks. It is cold work, close to the sweating stone, where the air hangs damp and still. Before I touch a single insulated conduit, I have to clear the trench—sliding gloved fingers gently under the bellies of the cold-blooded salamanders that cling to the cables, lifting them one by one onto the nearest warm transformer fin. They never thrash. They just curl their pale tails around the copper ribs and wait, blinking slowly in the dim light, while I scrape away the white mites’ web and test the line.

My meter has no buzzer; a sound like that in a quiet canyon would draw a dozen sharp looks from the catwalks above. Instead, it has a thin brass needle behind thick, condensation-fogged glass. I touch the probes to the spliced copper, watch the needle swing, and read the city’s pulse in silence: *here, the current holds; here, the rock has pinched the sheath; here, a new gap has opened.*

When the loop is whole again, I stand, wipe my knees on my oiled canvas apron, and chalk the new routing tallies onto the iron junction-plate at the corner of the alley. A small circle for a clear lane. Two horizontal bars for a blind end. A diagonal slash if the grade has tilted steep enough that a loaded cart will need a winch-assist from the overhead rail.

It is not grand work. Nobody writes songs about a spliced induction loop—though here, nobody writes songs about anything aloud anyway. Still, there is a quiet, steady satisfaction in being the one who stitches the memory back into the street.

By midday, I know the shifting geometry of my quarter better than any map could hold it. A map here would be a lie before the ink dried. Instead, I carry the city in the soles of my boots and the chalk-dust on my knuckles: I know which conservatory roof is bleeding enough warmth to thaw stiff fingers, which freight cart has a dent in its left bumper and pulls slightly toward the gutter (I’ve chalked three extra tallies on its flank myself this week), and which elderly Listener sits by the west substation with one bare palm pressed to a jagged shelf of basalt, waiting for the water in the crevice to tremble.

Sometimes, when the pale, heatless afternoon begins to lean toward dusk, I climb up to the third-tier catwalks just to rest my knees and watch the mute, slate-gray birds packed along the glass eaves. From up there, you can see the whole basin working like a single, breathlessly patient organism—cranes swinging without a clatter, Riggers trading clipped one-handed signs across a chasm of brick, green curtains of air-fern swaying in the faint thermal draft above the cooling racks.

And if the birds suddenly lift off the glass all at once—a soundless gray cloud wheeling tight against the cold sky—I don’t panic, and I don’t shout. I just pack my probes into my satchel, check that the salamanders are tucked safely back into the moss, and wait for the stone beneath my feet to decide where we are going next.
