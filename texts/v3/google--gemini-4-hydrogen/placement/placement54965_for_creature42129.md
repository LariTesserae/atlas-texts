I would not be on the high roofs at noon, where the glare bleaches the gypsum dust white and the young ones string new walkways between the crowns of the candelabra succulents. I would be three tiers down, in the cool, dappled labyrinth where the hand-kneaded clay begins to forget the sun and remember the stone.

I wake before the light reaches the lower corridors, though there is no sound to mark the turn from night to day. Only a faint, dry trembling in the braided sedge mat beneath me: the last of the nocturnal grazers dropping down the outside walls, their splayed toes whispering over the rough mortar as they slip back into the sharp limestone crevices below. Above, the owls have already folded themselves into the dark under-eaves.

My palms know where I am before my eyes open. Pressing flat against the wall of my alcove, I feel the cool, porous clay holding the night’s chill, and beneath it, the faint, broad ridges left by whoever smoothed this room two or three generations ago. The strokes are long and unhurried. Whoever made this wall was not thirsty, and they were not in a hurry to escape the heat.

When I stand and drape the knotted apron of papery sedge over my shoulders, the fine ash and gypsum in the creases of my knees and elbows shifts like dry flour. My feet—thickly plated in pale callus, hard as cured succulent skin—barely register the grit on the floor. I step out into the corridor, trailing the backs of my knuckles along the left-hand wall as I go. It is habit, and it is conversation. A few paces down, the clay turns slightly coarser, ridged with short, choppy thumb-marks: a patch laid hastily yesterday afternoon by someone tired, before the thermals broke. Farther on, the wall is stippled with tiny, neat pellets—the work of the pale ants, sealing a hairline crack that the dry air opened overnight.

Others are already moving in the dimness. A builder taller than me, carrying a stack of hollowed succulent pith against her ribs, steps aside at a narrow junction. Neither of us makes a sound. She tilts her chin slightly left and lowers her elbows—*descending*—and as we pass, the back of her wrist brushes the hollow of my throat. Her scent is clean, faintly sweet as crushed acacia-pod, with only the slightest edge of chalky thirst. She has not been down to the roots yet. I touch two fingers to my ribs in acknowledgment and follow her toward the shaft-mouth.

This is where I belong: in the vertical seam between the living city and the raw, unweathered crust.

At the mouth of the root-shaft, the rammed earth ends, and the world turns sharp. Beneath the four stories of silt and woven stalks, the bedrock lies exactly as it lifted—jagged limestone and angular volcanic tuff that has never known a drop of rain or the grinding of a river. My spatulate fingers find the braided vine-ladder, and I drop down into the chimney.

The air changes immediately. Up in the middle galleries, it is dry and warm, thick with the scent of sun-baked mud and waxy leaves. Down here, it smells of wet chalk and old stone. The taproots of the great succulents plunge beside me through the jagged fissures, thick as a tamper’s leg, wrapped in tough, dusty bark. Far below, sealed beneath the rock, lies the water no one has ever seen.

I climb down until the daylight at the top of the shaft is only a pale coin, and my bare heels brace against a ledge of raw gypsum. Here, the pale ants are already at work. They move in a silent, translucent river up the side of a root, each nearly the length of my finger-joint, carrying glistening pellets of damp subsoil in their broad mandibles. They do not mind my feet; I do not step on their line. From the pouch of cured ice-plant skin at my hip, I take a handful of the hollowed pith the other builder brought down and lay it in a dark crevice beside the shaft. By afternoon, they will have carried it into their chambers to feed their pale, dust-dry fungi.

With my left hand gripping the vine-ladder, I reach out and press the thick, horn-like nail of my right thumb into the bark of a taproot. I score a shallow, neat channel—just deep enough to reach the pale, wet core, never deep enough to wound the plant. A bead of clear water gathers, slow as resin. I hold a cupped gourd-shell beneath it, waiting in the cool dark while the drop swells and falls without a splash.

Down here, it is easy to feel how old the city is. A few yards to my side, where the shaft widens into a low, forgotten chamber, the walls are sealed smooth with ancient silt. Behind those thin patches lie the old ones—builders whose palms wore down to nothing, sealed into the niches so they would not waste the dry air. In the dimness, I can see where thin, pale feeder-roots have already pierced the clay seals, threading quietly into the dark to drink the last salt and moisture of the makers and lift them back up into the silvery-olive canopy four stories overhead. When my hands grow stiff and my callus cracks for good, that is where I will go.

With the gourd half-full—enough for my own throat and a few drops to wet a handful of mortar—I climb back toward the warmth.

By midday, the middle galleries are alive with quiet traffic. I emerge into a terraced courtyard dappled by the thorny acacias above. A pair of tampers, their silver-ochre coats dusted with chaff, are being guided in tight, patient circles across a newly spread floor of damp subsoil. Their broad hooves pack the earth down with a soft, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* that travels up through the soles of my feet rather than the air. Behind them, a younger builder gathers their fibrous droppings and kneads them straight into a trough of silt and crushed ice-plant mucilage.

I kneel beside the trough, dip my fingers into the cool, caustic paste, and take my place along the low parapet wall that needs raising. My skin does not sting; generations of my kind have been shaped for this lime and ash. I scoop a handful of mortar, press it onto the sun-warmed rim, and draw my palm along it in a slow, even sweep.

I am not fast anymore, and I do not need to be. I let the ridges of my palm stay in the clay, wide and unhurried, leaving a clear mark for whoever walks this terrace in the dark or runs a hand along it years from now: *the wall is sound; there was water enough; the day was steady.*

Above us, a flock of the small, soft-feathered birds drops onto the crown of a candelabra succulent, their velvet-edged wings making no more sound than falling dust. Along the braided bridge spanning the courtyard, a fox-like hunter drifts low to the woven stalks, its leaf-shaped ears tilted forward, invisible against the ochre wall until it slips into the shade. No one flinches. The hunter keeps the night-grazers from chewing through the root-lashings; the birds prune the dead buds; the tampers pack the earth; the ants seal the cracks. We are all kneaded from the same dry crust.

By mid-afternoon, the heat rising off the miles of packed roofs begins to pull at the air. You can feel it in the shafts—a slow, steady upward draw, like a quiet breath being taken by the sky. Outside, invisible thermals are spinning dust-devils across the empty upper terraces, lifting shed lizard-scales and golden pollen into the pale blue glare.

It is time to stop. I wipe the drying mortar from my forearms with a handful of dry grass, and join the others in the thick-walled lower chambers where the canopy blocks the sun.

We sit shoulder to shoulder on the woven mats, resting our backs against the cool clay. There is no need to speak, even with our hands. An older builder beside me, his skin weathered the exact pale grey of volcanic tuff, turns slightly and begins to brush the fine white grit from my shoulders with the flat of his palm. When he finishes, I turn and do the same for him, feeling the ridges of his spine through the dry skin, the quiet warmth of his body. Across the room, two younger ones sit with their foreheads touching, exchanging the slow, mineral-sweet scent of rest.

Later, when the light filtering down the alleys turns thick and amber, the upward pull of the air stops. The thermals have collapsed. Outside, the dust that hung all afternoon in the sky is settling back down—covering the roofs, the waxy ice-plants, the braided bridges, erasing every footprint we left since dawn.

I do not go back up to see it. I stretch out in my alcove, press one palm flat against the wall to read the old, patient strokes beneath my skin, and wait for the cool stone below to send up the night.
