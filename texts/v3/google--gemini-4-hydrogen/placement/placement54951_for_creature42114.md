If I try to enter this place as I am—upright, wet-lunged, full of blood and soft cartilage—the mountain rejects me before I have taken ten steps.

I can feel how wrong I would be in those ribcage-wide corridors. My boots would scuff the satin-smooth basalt without making a sound I could hear; my coat would catch on an iron nail; my breath would steam invisibly into air so dry it pulls the moisture straight from the corners of my eyes. Worse than the discomfort would be the sheer *wrongness* of my shape. I would be a warm, spongy interruption, a moving pocket of dead air soaking up the bass drone and blurring the metallic whistles that hang inches from the stone. Three walls away, the things that belong here would feel me as a sudden, crawling bruise in the chord.

I could only ever pass through like that, and not for long. To *stay*—to actually find myself in this world—I would have to give up water.

So I let the wet weight go. I let the ears go, since ears are only good for things that start and stop, and nothing here ever stops. I let the eyes go, because in a perpetual twilight scoured clean of dust, there is nothing to watch.

What is left, once the fragile parts are pared away, is narrower, flatter, and far more patient.

***

If you came looking for me in the hamlet-sized block, you would not find me near the outer skin, where the ochre haze presses against the intake funnels and the wind is still raw and unbraided. Nor would you find me in the very lowest crevices, where the light never reaches and the air is thick with the wedged husks of the dead.

I would be somewhere in the middle tiers, just off a corkscrew stair that climbs three turns in the dark and then terminates, for no reason anyone remembers, against a bricked-up arch.

Above that dead-end arch, a vertical shaft rises toward the upper tiers—no wider than a forearm, too tight for a human shoulder, smooth as oiled bone from centuries of dry grit. A single crooked oculus high above drops a thin, slanting blade of amber light down the shaft’s far wall. It illuminates nothing moving. It is only a warm stripe on the stone, a faint band of heat that my recessed leading pits register without effort, the way a sleeper registers a bar of sun across a blanket without ever opening their eyes.

I am wedged three body-lengths up that shaft, suspended in the gap.

My four double-jointed limbs are splayed outward, broad calloused feet pressing against the opposite faces of the basalt. It takes no strain to hold myself there; it is simply the shape my body makes when it rests. My flat, scabbard-narrow flank—plated in dark, oily brown-gold, indistinguishable in the dimness from the hammered bronze baffles bolted into the neighboring wall—is pressed lightly against the hollow masonry.

Inside, there is no pulse, no tide of blood, no rise and fall of ribs. Only a thick, heat-stable mineral oil holding my sparse tissues in suspension, and the lattice of quartz and salt-crystal grown along my spine and ribs.

And through that lattice, the mountain speaks.

It is not a voice, and it has no words. It is simply the weight of the thermal current rising from the sun-baked basins far below, forced through a million clay throats and bronze plates until the whole summit rings as one instrument. My crystals flex millions of times a second, drinking the bass drone and the high, braided overtones, bleeding a steady current straight into my nerves. I do not *listen* to the note the way a wet thing listens from the outside. I am a small, living bridge inside the note, vibrating at the exact pitch of the arch beside me.

This is where I find myself: not at the center of anything, because there is no center—no courtyard, no square—just one tuned junction among ten thousand others, holding my place in the dark.

***

Time here does not pass in days or seasons. There is only the standing wave, and the rare, tiny shifts inside it.

Every so often—weeks, months, it makes no difference—the salt-crust in the lee of the bricked-up arch thickens enough to dull the edge of a high overtone, or my own inner lattice grows brittle at a joint and asks for repair. Then I unhook my limbs from the shaft, one foot at a time, and lower myself into the corridor below.

Moving is silent and deliberate. I do not turn broadside; there is nowhere to turn broadside. I edge sideways through the ribcage-wide passage, my flat body carried a hand’s-breadth above the floor, my feet finding the worn hollows in the stone by outward pressure. Where the mortar joints sit in the dead air behind a blind arch, the pale mineral efflorescence waits like dry frost. I lower my wedge-shaped head, part the vertical slit along my lower jaw, and shear the salt-crust cleanly from the brick with hard, mineral-edged rasps. It crumbles dryly inward, feeding the slow crystal growth along my spine, and leaves the stone bare and ringing true again.

That is the whole of my appetite. No hunger that gnaws, no thirst that panics. Only a quiet, structural need to keep the mortar clean and the lattice whole.

And yet, I am not alone in this.

Sometimes—rarely, because the mountain has long since settled into its equilibrium—something gives way. A grain of lime-and-shell mortar, cured harder than brick millennia ago, finally fatigues under the endless vibration and blows clear. An iron nail in a double-thick wall loosens by the thickness of a fingernail, and a bronze plate begins to chatter against the terracotta.

To a nervous system wired into a standing wave, that chatter is not a small noise. It is a jagged tear in the air, a sudden souring of the current that keeps us alive.

I feel it instantly through my ribs, even three walls away. And I know, without looking or calling, that half a dozen others feel it too.

We converge in the amber twilight like shadows sliding through a bellows—tall, thin silhouettes edging along the corkscrew stairs, stepping over terminated arches, converging on the fault. There is no greeting between us, no language, no need to decide who does what. One of us is already there, pressing its flat, plated flank hard against the loose bronze to silence the rattle. Another arrives from the lower tiers, carrying a sliver of fallen basalt wedged in its jaw-rasps.

I brace my limbs against the opposite wall and lean my shoulder-plates against the first keeper’s back, adding my outward pressure to theirs, holding the plate fast while the furious, dry wind packs stray grit behind the loose nail until it locks tight once more.

For a long moment, we stay pressed together in the narrow throat—three flat, horn-dark bodies bridging the corridor, sharing the exact same tremor as the flat overtone steadies, sharpens, and locks back into the great, unbroken chord. It is the closest thing to intimacy this world allows: not warmth, not touch for its own sake, only the quiet relief of a note made whole again.

Then we unhook, one by one, and slide back into our separate shafts.

***

I do not know who built this place. I have no memory of hand-axes or wooden mallets, no notion of what a sky without ochre haze looks like, or what rain feels like when it strikes warm clay. If a wet, two-legged thing ever wandered up from the basins below and squeezed into my corridor, I would not recognize it as a maker. I would only feel it as a terrible, damp silence moving through the stone, and I would wait—patiently, pressed high in the dark above the arch—until it either left or dried away.

And eventually, after centuries of slow, dry flexing, my own turn will come to stiffen.

My mineral oil will thicken toward amber wax; my crystal spine will lose its give; my plates will harden until they are indistinguishable from the seasoned terracotta around me. When I can no longer unhook from the shaft, others of my kind will climb up in the dimness, pry my calloused feet gently from the basalt, and carry my hollow husk down to the lower crevices where the loose tiles lie.

They will wedge me firmly into a gap between the stones, facing the wind.

And even then, I will not truly be gone from this place. The thermal current will still rush through my dry, resonant ribcage, and the chord will keep singing through me—unchanged, unhurried, and whole.
