I wouldn’t be a visitor who stumbled in by accident, and I wouldn’t be native to the dark. In this world, I think I would be someone whose work already belonged to the ground just above it—a ditch-walker, a peat-cutter, or a minor surveyor of the marsh, the kind of person who spends long, gray afternoons reading the water table through the soles of heavy rubber boots.

You don’t walk out onto a frost-rimmed basalt tear unless you’re used to listening for where the earth has gone hollow, or where the bog is draining too fast. I would have followed the low-frequency thudding because my job is to know what the marsh is doing underneath its skin—and when the ground starts keeping a four-second beat, you go find out what’s loose.

So when I slip down through the cleft and stand in the shallow basin, I am dressed for the surface: a coat stiff with dried mud and woodsmoke, damp wool socks, a pocketknife, maybe a brass key in my pocket. To the creature in the upper hollow, I must feel enormous and absurd—a towering, insulated column of salt water and wet wool, barging into a room no wider than a parlor and bending every invisible line of its weather.

When it nudges that copper flake across the magnetite seam and the blue-white arc snaps into the pool at my feet, the sharp tingle up through my boots doesn’t feel like a warning. It feels like a question asked by someone who doesn’t have a voice, using the only language the room allows.

If I shouted, it would just be noise swallowed by the *HISS*. If I stepped closer, my boots would crush the pyrite-crusted grazers and churn up the silt around the amphipods. To answer properly, I would have to learn the room’s clock first.

I’d stand still through one full cycle. *THUMP*—the bedrock slamming together, rattling my teeth. *HISS*—the displaced air screaming out of the vents, and beneath it, that strange, deliberate three-note chord humming from the upper hollow. Then the brief, breathless pause where the pressure gathers again.

In that pause, I’d pull off one damp wool glove. Watching the bare, sharp stone so I don’t tread on a cushion of liverwort, I’d crouch and press my bare palm flat against a weeping seam of magnetite, just above the waterline. 

I’m no engineer, and I don’t know the math of static or resistance, only that a living body is mostly salt and water, and that makes me part of the wire whether I mean to be or not. Against the cold stone, I’d let the creature’s trembling filaments read what I am: a slow, steady pulse that doesn’t match the four-second pistons; a stubborn, internal warmth that doesn’t need a copper-wired moss bed to keep from freezing. Just a quiet, grounded *I am here, and I am listening.*

I couldn’t stay down there. I’m too big for a room tuned that finely, and I wasn’t built for ozone and cold salamanders. After a few cycles—after watching it return to its moss, or watching it wait to see if I’d break anything—I would climb back up through the narrow, frost-rimmed lip and pull myself out into the wide, flat wind of the marsh.

That is where I would find myself in this world: on the threshold, belonging to the surface side of the same engine.

Because the chamber only runs on the heavy, unseen back-pressure of the surrounding peat and water table. Up above, with a spade or a sluice-gate, someone like me holds the valve to its sky. I would leave the tear in the basalt unmarked—no cairn, no wooden stake, no path worn through the heather. If the drainage ditches needed clearing that season, I would route the cut wide around that low-lying shelf of rock, letting the marsh stay heavy and wet above it so the pistons underneath never ran dry.

And every few weeks, when a hard freeze settled in or the spring thaw swelled the bog, I’d walk back out to that ragged split in the stone. I wouldn’t climb down and crowd the room again. I’d just stand by the frost-rimmed lip, feel the steady *THUMP* through my boots, and listen for the faint, rising three-note chord humming up out of the dark. 

Then, waiting for the breathless pause between beats, I’d tap the heel of my boot once against the basalt—a quiet knock on the roof of its world, just to let the keeper know the water above was holding steady.
