I don’t think I would be one of the bright-water people—not the young air-drawers with their fast, snapping lungs, racing the gut-bladders down from the surface, and not yet one of the elders soaking the stiffness out of their knuckles on the sunlit reed-rafts. 

I would find myself in the dim, near-freezing third of the world, down where the light has been strained through forty paces of green water and the pale-amber trunks of the bladder-cedars flare out into their braided floor.

I would be one of the deep-tier tenders—past the rush of youth, settled into the broad-shouldered slowness that the tarn teaches you once you stop fighting the cold. My hands would be pale and hard-palmed as peeled willow-bark from a decade of brine and wet cordage, and my sealskin tunic—traded up from the coast years ago for a bundle of hammered copper pins—would be patched at the elbows with sinew and slick with tallow.

My morning would start not with talk, but with the salve: rubbing the thick, pungent paste of rendered mutton fat and pine-resin into the hollows behind my ears, across my cheekbones, and over my wrists, sealing the skin against the bite of the hollows. Then a last breath of bell-air—tasting of wet wool, resin, and old salt—before slipping down through the hatch-hole into the green.

Moving through the middle tier is like crossing a quiet plaza before dawn. Above me, the shafts of pale gold slice down between the great trunks; beside me, the four central bells hang like dark hives, and I can feel the muffled *tok-tok-tok* of the copper-smiths’ stone hammers tapping against my ribs. If a neighbor glides past across the open avenue, a hundred paces off, I might raise a flat palm or tilt a wrist to ask about the weirs—nothing more. Words are for dry air. Down here, you save your breath and let the water carry you.

The deep floor has its own gravity. You don’t walk on it so much as lean against the drift, letting the cobble-weights at your belt keep you upright while your heels brush the springy, continuous mat of cedar-roots. No mud ever rises here. Everything is clean, slow, and loud.

That is what I would love most about being down there: the noise of it. Up in the domes, voices are low and careful. On the floor, the northern inflow *thrums*—a bass-note so deep it lives inside your sternum, vibrating through the mineral-crusted wood of the sluice-baffles. The hollow trunks knock softly as the current leans on them. The thumb-sized crayfish click in the moss-cushions like dry seeds in a gourd. 

My work would be small, patient maintenance. With a long-handled bone gaff, I’d clear drifted fern-fronds from the wooden baffles so the slow current keeps sweeping past the town’s doorways cleanly. I’d check the wicker anchor-cages packed with river-cobbles, feeling with my fingertips for any fray in the braided-fiber lines that hold the catwalks steady. Where a trout-kin shoal had torn a corner of the lower mesh weirs, I’d hang suspended in the dimness, stitching the soaked plant-fiber back together with a copper needle before my lungs asked for the bell again.

And I would know the salamanders. Not as beasts to be hunted, but as neighbors who keep the same hours. Down in the root-knots, tucked under the third cedar from the southern seepage, there would be the old ones—thick as a forearm, mottled like wet slate. You learn their spots the way dry-landers learn the faces in a village. While my hands work the mesh, I’d be listening for the dry, resonant *pop* of their throat-sacs. If they go quiet all at once, or if the deep hum of the snowmelt shifts pitch against my ribs, I’d be the one to swim up to the central swim-lines and give the braided cord the sharp, deliberate tugs that warn the town a storm is building on the peaks above.

I wouldn’t want the stone camp on the northern rim. I’d climb out only when it was my turn to tend the cauldrons of boiling pitch or anneal a fresh batch of copper ingots, squinting against the glaring, thin air, anxious to be done with the wind and the loose scree. The upper world feels unfinished—too bright, too sudden, too loud with nothing holding you up.

By dusk, when the mountain shadow drops across the tarn like a heavy blanket and the golden shafts vanish in a single breath, my shift would be over. I’d glide up through the slate-blue dark, past the newly unhooded wicker lanterns glowing pale green with marsh-fungus, and pull myself up into my family’s dome.

There is no better feeling in that whole drowned bowl than peeling off the dripping sealskin onto the drain-rack, rubbing the chill from my arms, and sinking onto a dry heap of sedge-straw under heavy wool. Eating cold, salt-cured trout wrapped in bitter-cress, passing the skin of fermented birch-sap, and feeling the entire bell rock half an inch on its lines as the northern inflow pours endlessly through the dark outside—held, unhurried, and exactly where I belong.
