If I am in this world, I do not arrive from anywhere else. There is no *elsewhere* beyond the raw basalt, and anyone who walked in from a cooler, faster place would burn out in an hour—lungs scalded, pulse hammering against their ribs until they dropped beside a boiler-casing. 

To be here at all means I have always been shaped to the pocket, or else I have been here long enough for the heat to boil away everything in me that used to hurry.

At first, looking for where I belong, I might think of the lowest tier—down where the thermal fissures breathe and the air shimmers above the cracks. There is a kind of clarity in the hardest work: dredging the mineral sludge, packing the presses, feeling the cherry-red glow of the heat-exchangers against your shins. But I know my own temperament too well. I am not built for the heaviest labor, nor for the fiercest heat. My attention has always been drawn to the margins—to the places where one thing translates into another.

So I find myself on the fourth tier up, in the warm, dim middle of the stack.

Here, the air is thick enough to chew, carrying the sweet, sharp stink of crushed plum-fern sap and the metallic tang of verdigris, but it no longer shimmers. Above me, two tiers higher, the young basalt ceiling hangs jagged and black, weeping cold beads of condensate onto the upper catwalks. Below me, the flywheels turn at the speed of clock-hands. Where I stand, the light comes from a single caged filament bulb, its amber pool catching the wet sheen of a brick arch and the pale, translucent skin of the succulents spilling from a planter-trough.

I am one of the tenders, though not one of the strong-backed ones who haul the sludge-buckets from the fissures. My shoulders have the same permanent, gentle stoop from ducking under the lagged steam-mains, and my soles are broad and leathery from gripping the slick iron grates. My head is shaved—too much hair traps the heat and catches in the vine-tendrils—and my forearms are slick with the dark, resinous sap of the ferns, keeping the endless damp from softening my skin into sores. Around my waist hangs a short apron of woven vine-fiber, weighted with a small brass wrench, a horn-handled grafting knife, and a strip of dry linen rag that is never quite dry for long.

If I have a specific place in the choreography, it is here among the conduits and the vats. I am a watcher of intervals.

My work is not to force anything, because nothing in this pocket survives being forced. Mostly, I move along a twenty-pace stretch of narrow gantry between two electrolytic vats and a bank of glass-fronted pressure gauges. I move as though I am wading through chest-deep water—one bare foot placed flat on the iron, waiting for the heel to read the low-frequency hum of the vats below, then the other foot brought slowly forward. A single sharp turn of the head would make the thick air swim; I turn my whole torso instead, unhurried, letting my heart keep time with the *tick* of expanding iron.

With the dry corner of my rag, I wipe the condensation from a gauge-glass, not because the numbers are urgent, but because it is good to see the needle resting steady. Then I stand still for a long minute, watching the transformer casing beside the brick arch.

Three small, heat-tolerant beetles sit motionless on the warm metal, their carapaces beaded with sweat. As long as they stay there, spaced evenly like rivets, the current is clean. If one of them begins to edge toward the cooler brick, I will not shout—my voice would be a useless, exhausting thing in this dampness. Instead, I will lay two fingers against the copper pipe that runs down to the third tier, wait for the rhythm of the hum to settle in my knuckles, and give a single, dull tap. Three tiers down, someone else will hear it through the soles of their feet and ease a valve by the width of a fingernail.

Between the gauges and the transformers, the fibrous vines are always reaching. They love the warmth of the electric current; if left alone, they will wrap their thick, pale tendrils tight around the ceramic insulators and then keep climbing, threading straight toward the open teeth of the auxiliary gears. With my grafting knife, I do not hack them back. I lift each tendril gently with the flat of the blade, coaxing it away from the iron teeth and guiding it back toward the ceramic, pressing its damp tip into the mossy fur of the insulator where it wants to be. The plant will take an hour to accept the new direction, and a day to grip it. I have the time.

At intervals—measured not by a clock, since there is no day or night here, only the slow curling of the broad plum-colored fronds toward the orange bulbs—I take a ceramic catch-basin from beneath a weeping pipe before it can lip over, set a dry one in its place without a clink, and carry the warm, mineral-heavy water to the basalt filter-bed. 

When I am hungry, I drink a cup of the pale, starchy liquor pressed from the thick-stemmed tubers, salted with a pinch of white crust scraped from the boiler seams. When I am tired, I do not lie down in a dark room—there are no dark rooms—but curl into a woven vine-hammock slung in a brick-arched alcove on the cooler side of the fourth tier. Even asleep, my ear stays tethered to the pocket. I sleep through the low hum of the vats, through the *tick* of the iron, through the soft *slap* of a water drop hitting a broad leaf. What would wake me—what *does* wake me, with eyes opening wide and dark-pupiled in the amber gloom—is the sudden absence of a drip that ought to be there.

I think, if I lived here, I would not be afraid of growing old. 

There is no sudden obsolescence in a machine that turns a millimeter at a time. When my knees stiffen too much for the narrow ladders between tiers, I would simply stop climbing them. I would settle permanently onto this middle level, where the heat is mildest, spending my shifts sitting on a warm brick ledge with a wooden bowl in my lap, turning the small hand-screws on the tuber-presses or tending the nurseries of pale succulents that drink the railing-sweat. My hands would grow slower, matching the hydraulic rams, until the difference between my stillness and the stillness of the brick wall beside me became very small.

And when the last breath finally thinned out in my chest, I would not be carried away into some cold, empty ground. There is nowhere to carry me. The ones who still climb the gantries would lift me down—carefully, unhurriedly, so as not to raise their core temperatures—and lay me deep in one of the brick planter-troughs beneath the plum-colored ferns.

In a few weeks, the heat and the damp would return my moisture to the air, rising up to bead on the raw basalt ceiling and drip back down into the ceramic basins. And my bones would stay behind in the dark soil, bracing the roots that brace the old ironwork, holding the whole heavy, breathless pocket in its quiet equilibrium.
