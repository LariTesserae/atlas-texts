I think I would be found in the middle tiers—neither up on the whale-backed ridges where the wind cuts straight through greased wool, nor down at the black, mirror-still sumps where the cold waits to stop a heart. I would be somewhere along the third or fourth terrace, where the town’s brick workshops lean their backs against the smooth primordial basalt, and the heavy black power cables pass close enough to the windows that you can watch the wire-pelt breathing in the mist.

My place would be a narrow, two-room lean-to wedged against the flank of one of the secondary powerhouses. Not an important building—just a brick shell built to house a secondary flywheel and a bank of cooling transformers—so my wall is always faintly warm, even in the slack hours when the sluices are shut and the voltage drops. That borrowed warmth keeps the plaster dry enough to hold a shelf, and keeps the condensation on my thick-paned window from freezing into rime.

On that slate sill, there would be a wide-mouthed jar of lukewarm runoff water, and in it, a single translucent salamander, pale as steeped bone. I wouldn’t name it; people here don’t seem to name things that belong to the weather. I would simply watch its gills, and watch the way its blunt snout presses against the glass an hour before the western rim darkens.

If I have a trade in this town, it is with the small, fragile hardware that sits between the mist and the men. I would be a *telltale-wright* and a *box-tender*—the person who winds the hair-thin filaments, solders the copper prongs, and walks the cobbled lanes during the slack hours to check the glass-fronted relay boxes before the next tide comes over the lip.

It is quiet, unhurried work, suited to thin air and a slow pulse. I would wear the same slate-colored felted wool as everyone else, the same waxed canvas coat that never quite dries, only settles into a cold truce with the fog. My fingers would be permanently nicked at the pads from stripping salvaged copper wire, and stained a faint, dull yellow around the cuticles from rosin flux and tallow. At my belt, beside the horn-handled knife and the brass pressure-key, I’d carry a small leather roll of jeweler’s files, spare porcelain bushings, and half a dozen tested telltales wrapped in flannel so the glass won’t clink as I walk.

In the hours between tides, when the waterwheels groan to a halt and the tungsten street-lamps dim to the color of banked coals, I would take the viaducts. The moss swallows my footsteps; the only sound I make is the faint *shhh* of my oilskin apron brushing the dwarf-pine that crowds the catwalk rails.

At each street box, I’d unlatch the wet iron catch and wipe the glass pane with my sleeve. Inside, the air smells of ozone and damp porcelain. The wire-pelt is always there, creeping up the conduit from below, wrapping the ceramic insulators in thick, emerald velvet. Where the pelt-cutters come through with their shears to harvest the moss for the crucibles, I come with a wooden pick and a soft brush. I don’t cut it back unless it’s bridging a contact; I like the way it muffles the chatter of the switches. I just coax the damp, bulbous tendrils aside, clear the tiny verdigris-dusted mites from the brass terminals, and press the prongs of a fresh telltale against the damp brick beside the box to make sure the stray current isn't bleeding into the street.

Sometimes a pelt-cutter passes me on the narrow stairs, their satchel heavy and smelling of hot copper and crushed fern. Neither of us speaks—not out of coldness, just because the air doesn't carry wasted breath. A nod, or two light taps of a spanner against the steam-pipe railing to say *the line ahead is clean*. That is enough.

I think what draws me to this particular corner of the basin is the listening. Up on the ridges, the sails sing too loud when the fog rolls in; down in the workshops, the vats hiss and steam. But inside a relay box, or sitting at my workbench behind the powerhouse, you hear the town’s real pulse: the dry *tick* of cooling transformer fins, the tiny blue *snap* of a switch throwing over as the water-head changes three terraces above, the steady *plip-plop* of a gutter emptying into a slate cistern.

And when the second tide has passed and the evening settles into its deep, pewter stillness, I wouldn't put the copper wire away. The spools too short or too kinked for the grid would go into a tin box under my bench. Once the kettle is on the iron stove and the room smells of coal-tar soap and wet wool, I’d draw that salvaged wire tight across a small wooden dulcimer frame, tuning it by ear to the low, steady hum of the flywheel vibrating through the brick wall behind me.

Nothing loud. Just enough resonance to hang in the steamy air between the stove and the weeping window, while the salamander settles back down to the bottom of its jar, and the moss outside goes on drinking the dark.
