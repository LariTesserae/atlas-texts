I would not be in the back alcove. That recess is already taken, and its occupant needs the whole of that deepest warmth to itself. Nor would I be out on the pale silt pan, where nothing crosses and every curled polygon is a record of undisturbed time.

If I belong to this hollow, I find myself halfway along the side wall, tucked high into the curve where the ceiling begins to arch—wedged between two rounded ribs of sandstone that feel like dry bone.

From here, the hollow divides into two halves. To my left, toward the entrance, the narrow seam of slate-grey light leaks under the rim, just enough to catch the smoothed upper faces of the settled scree and the silver-grey bristles of the bunchgrass. To my right, toward the back, the light gives up altogether, and the dark becomes thick, kiln-warm, and occupied.

I am not heavy like the one in the back alcove. I have no plates of baked clay, no seams of warm pitch. If I had, I would have sunk into the deepest recess long ago, chasing the steadier heat.

Instead, I am made for the half-dusk and the dry ribs of the wall.

I am something closer to the frayed bark of the shrubs and the papery grass—a folded, hollow geometry of silica-stiffened membrane and fine, mineral-cured fiber, no larger than a cupped hand, anchored by dry threads into a hairline fissure behind me. My outer surface is the color of old bone and powdered silt, matte and slightly translucent at the edges, like horn shaved thin or the cured husk of a seed-pod that never split. Inside, there is no wet flesh to shrivel, no blood to thicken in the drought. There is only a light lattice of dry struts and a small, warm pocket of trapped air.

Because I am hollow and taut, I do not know the room by weight, the way the plated one does. I know it by resonance.

I am a small drumhead stretched against the rib of the cave. When a single grain of sand lets go of the overhang and strikes the baked earthenware of the floor with a *tick*, the sound does not stay on the floor; it travels through the silt, climbs the iron-rich wall, and hums for a fraction of a second across my dry skin before the silence swallows it again. When noon heat finally finishes its slow descent through the ceiling rock and the sandstone expands by the width of a hair, I feel it as a long, silent tightening along my anchor-threads, and my lattice answers with a sound too small to be called a creak—barely the ghost of a papery whisper.

I do not move across the hollow. Over the span of a year, I might lean a millimeter toward the left when the slate-grey seam brightens in summer, or draw tighter into the rib when the distant seasons turn outside and the rim-stone cools by half a degree. Mostly, I stay still and let the dust settle on my upper ridge, until I am indistinguishable from a blister of pale lichen or a knot of dead root.

Yet I am awake in the way the hollow allows. I taste the baked clay and the old resin in the motionless air. I drink the faintest dampness from the fissure behind me, slower than a shrub drinks. And sometimes, after long stretches of nothing at all, I feel a tiny, dry *tick* travel through the stone from the pitch-dark alcove to my right—not sand falling, not rock shifting, just the other presence turning by the breadth of a fingernail in its hearth.

I do not answer it. There is nothing to say in a room where nothing hurries. I only hold the vibration for an instant in my hollow ribs, and then let it go back into the warm, unbroken quiet.
