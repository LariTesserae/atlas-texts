At first, there is only the absence of weight—or rather, the absence of anything wet to anchor it.

If I try to draw a breath, there is no chest to swell, no damp lung to cool. Instead, a dry, pressurized current draws inward through the fluted vents along my ribs, crisp as winter air in an empty barn, smelling faintly of copper and old paper. Beneath me, the floor gives slightly and springs back: hexagonal trusses of pale, lignified bark bolted with tarnished brass.

I do not know what I am yet, only where I am not.

I am not out on the flat, blinding expanse of the upper basin, where the bleached-linen sky washes the edges off every cooling-fern and turns the world into a glare-white dish. It is too exposed out there; the wind carries too much electrified dust, and every *tock* of a distant relay feels like a needle tapped against bone. Nor am I all the way down in the blind, dustless dark of the deep root-grid, where the great flywheels turn in silence and the leather-backed sweepers crawl like slow brushes over the undersides of the world.

I find myself in the seam between them.

Three layers down from the canopy, the light arrives only in thin, slanted slats through the woven bamboo-plate. Here, the banyan-dynamos sink their buttress roots into the older colonnades, and the ironwood branches braid so thickly around the catwalks that space itself has given up on straight lines. A cedar aqueduct full of white quartz sand passes straight through the heartwood of a cypress pillar beside me, emerging from the far side without a seam. When a silt-ribbon flickers through the sand, it vanishes into the bark and reappears an instant later, unbothered.

It is cool here, and shadowed, and the air vibrates with the low, papery *shhh* of hornbeam gears meshing somewhere out of sight.

I look down to see what kind of thing has settled into this corner of the weave.

No skin. No pulse. My hands are narrow lattices of brass wire and cured leather, joined at the wrists by spheres of dark, polished hornbeam that turn in their sockets with a faint, dry whisper of graphite dust. Along my forearms, the fluted ironwood is wrapped in overlapping strips of pale parchment, darkened at the joints where I have leaned against the machinery. When I tilt my head—a smooth, slightly concave oval of compressed plant-fiber turning on a tarnished brass swivel—three tiny quartz lenses click softly into focus, one after another, adjusting to the slatted light.

I am one of the tenders. Not one of the tall, long-striding ones who walk the high gantries with calipers slung from their bamboo ribs, patrolling the bright edge of the dish. My parchment is too grey with graphite for the upper light; my hornbeam joints have worn a little loose, so that I prefer the low-ceilinged thickets where I can brace a shoulder against a burl-wood knot and stay still for hours.

Even among the tenders, there is work for the ones who like the pleats.

Beside my station, braided into the root-trusses, a cluster of pneumatic tubes descends from the sunlit groves above. Every few minutes—sometimes with a soft *tock*, sometimes with nothing more than a sudden gust of dry air—a canister drops into the wickerwork hopper at my knees.

I reach in and lift out what the upper tenders have sent down: tight, crackling cylinders of black carbon paper, peeled carefully from the hexagonal floor miles above where the glare has grown too thin to hold its own geometry. Uncoupled shadows.

Down here, where the white sky cannot reach, they do not lie flat. When I unroll one across my lap, it curls at the edges like dried bark, dry and weightless, smelling faintly of ozone. I touch the tip of my lodestone tuning fork to its margin and press the woven sole of my foot against the truss beneath us, listening through the wood.

Most of them are spent. They ring with a dull, flat *thud*—the leftover darkness of a gantry that was dismantled seasons ago, or the lag of a cooling-fern that has already dried to dust. Those I roll back up and drop through the grating at my feet, down into the cool dark where the blind sweepers wait to compress them with fallen wafer-dust and shed copper scales into neat, dry bricks for the refineries.

But now and then, a shadow arrives that is still live.

When I touch the fork to its edge, it hums—a thin, sub-audible tremor that travels straight up my reed-and-copper arm and makes the quartz lenses in my face click in sympathy. Against the black surface, I can almost feel the phantom teeth of a hundred-foot flywheel still turning, miles away in the glare, casting a darkness that arrived too late to find its wheel.

I do not drop those to the sweepers.

Instead, I stand, my joints whispering softly, and turn to the thick, opaque knot of burl-wood grown into the cypress pillar behind me. I tilt my wafer-face to the grain, listening for the pleat—for the place where the wood opens onto a catwalk three basins east, or a sunlit grove where the louvers are rattling in a cold downdraft.

When the grain answers, I plunge my arm straight into the solid bark up to the shoulder, feeling the cool, dry air of another place brush my knuckles. I lay the uncoupled shadow back onto the sunlit floor where it belongs, smooth its curling edges with my thumb, and draw my arm back into the dimness.

Sometimes, while my hand is still through the knot, another tender passing on that distant catwalk will pause. I feel the faint focus of their quartz lenses, and then the cool touch of silver-tipped knuckles against my left hand.

A single, crisp needle of static snaps across the gap—blue-white, silent, leaving the sudden scent of toasted chaff—and in that instant I know which aqueduct is running thin with sand, which banyan-dynamo is throwing sparks, which grove has grown a fresh fold in the air. Then they step away, and I pull my hand back through the bark.

There is no hurry here. Nothing rusts, nothing rots, nothing weeps.

I sit back down beside the hopper, brush a little graphite dust from the sphere of my knee, and wait for the next *tock* in the tubes. I know that one day—not soon, perhaps not for decades—my parchment will split in a cold downdraft and I will not have the resinous wax to seal it. When that happens, I will climb up out of the thickets, out of the cool shadow of the pleats, and into the blinding glare of the canopy. I will find a fork of copper-veined wood among the photovoltaic fronds, lash my brittle limbs to the trellis with my own wire tendons, and let the wax-skinned leaves grow over my lenses.

Until then, the basin hums, the sand runs dry through the cedar troughs, and I stay here in the half-light, sorting the stray darkness so the weave can keep its shape.
