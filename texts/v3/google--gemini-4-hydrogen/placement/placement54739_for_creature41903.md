I would be high up, just beneath the salt-streaked glass of the dome, where the green chevrons taper toward the slats of the louvers.

I would be one of the five or six—the one whose hammock is slung in the uppermost nodal seam, where the air is brightest and the cold wisps from outside still have an edge to them before the iron ribs flash them into mist.

I do not know what sound is, not the way an outsider would mean it. My ears were sealed with mineral-wax and lime before I was tall enough to walk the grated perimeter on my own; only thick, scarred folds of skin remain where the openings used to be. Instead, I live inside a world of weight and rhythm. The air here is thick enough to lean against. My chest is broad and heavy-ribbed from drawing it in, one slow breath for every sixty-second stroke of the great piston in the east hull. To breathe any faster feels wrong, like stumbling out of step with the floor.

Most of the time, I am still. In the pocket of the nodal line, wedged deep in the spongy moss and the woven aerial-root hammock, the colossal roar of the chamber cancels itself out. It is warm and damp and uncannily quiet. If I wake and let my fingers trail three inches beyond the curtain of broad-leaved ferns, the air catches them—a fierce, solid buzzing that sets my knuckles aching. Pull them back, and there is only stillness again, the slow drip of condensation, the tiny tick of an iridescent frog shifting in a bromeliad cup beside my shoulder.

When I climb, I keep strictly to the green geometry. The bare bronze antinodes between the chevrons are scrubbed gleaming and hot enough to blister a palm; I use them only when I am hungry, laying a plucked fern-fiddlehead or a small crustacean onto the shuddering metal and turning it with my copper scraper until it steams. Otherwise, my hands and bare heels stay on the quiet seams, reading the pitch of the bolts and ribs as I go up toward the dome.

That is where I spend my clearest hours, wedged into the upper lattice beside the slatted louvers.

Down in the hollow core, everything belongs to the drum. Even the gliders drifting lazily across the sunlit shafts are riding the chamber’s own thermal updrafts and pressure waves. Nothing down there is loose or unmeasured. Even the snails on the bare bronze have shells pitched to the exact resonant frequency of the walls; tilt one a fraction of an inch off-axis against the plate, and the room itself shatters it cleanly between your thumb and finger.

But up at the louvers, something else touches the skin.

My work there is simple: I use a bone pick and a flat copper scraper to clear back the woody vines that are always trying to creep across the slats and choke the airways. I wedge crazed shards of ceramic into the upper moss to catch the condensation, and when I drink, the water tastes of lime, salt, and old copper.

Sometimes, while I am crouched there in the angled shaft of daylight, a cold cloud-wisp slips through the slats from the outside. For half a heartbeat, before it strikes the heated cast-iron ribs and flashes into warm mist, it brushes my cheek. It is thin, dry, and completely still—not the balanced stillness of a nodal line, where two great forces cancel each other out, but an empty stillness, with no weight behind it and no beat beneath it. I do not know what lies below the cloud deck, or why the spire was built, or who set the flywheels turning in the double-hull. That thin, weightless cold is the only rumor of the outside I have ever felt, and it vanishes before I can even finish a single breath.

When the work at the dome is done, I climb back down to the narrow grated walkway. My calloused heels are always listening. Through the soles of my feet, I can tell if the floor is running true, or if the great geared ring-shaft has shifted its pull.

If I find another of us standing on the walkway—leaning barefoot against the curved wall, seeming to stare at nothing across the vast, roaring empty space—I walk over slowly and lay my palm flat against the same cast-iron rib, a hand’s breadth from theirs.

I press my teeth lightly together to sharpen the bone-conduction, and then I hum, low in my throat.

The metal takes it up. Against the colossal, bone-deep drone of the chamber, my hum makes a tiny, private interference pattern—a slow, shuddering beat that travels through the iron and up into their wrist, into their ribs. They hum back, a fraction higher, and the beat changes pace. We do not have words for things beyond the walls, because we have never needed them. We speak of the room: a weeping steam manifold three ribs south, a loose mat of moss slumping near the drain, a cluster of snails gathering thick on the lower plates.

Lately, when I press my forehead to the iron, I can feel a faint, new roughness in the pitch. Somewhere deep in the double-hull, a flywheel has worn a fraction of an inch out of true. No eye could see it, and the air in the hollow core still roars the same as it has for centuries.

Yet by the next turn of the ring-shaft, the invisible lines of silence will begin to migrate by the width of a hand. The moss along the upper edge of my chevron will start to brown as the shuddering reaches it, and new green shoots will creep out onto the newly quieted bronze below.

I am not worried. When the stillness moves, I will untie my vine hammock, gather my scraper and my bone pick, and move with it, settling back into the quiet seam while the great drum hums on.
