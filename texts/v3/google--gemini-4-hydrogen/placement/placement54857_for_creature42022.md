I would be the one who wakes before the clapper falls.

Not because I am braver than the other five breathing beneath the bladder-skin dome, or lighter on my feet, but because my left hip—shattered three Roof-Falls ago when a bridge of lacy pith gave out under my pack—always feels the pressure-swell before the ground itself decides to move. A dull, cold ache starts in the joint where the bone healed crooked, and I open my eyes in the blue-white gloom of the tallow lamp.

Above my face, the small bone clapper hangs still from its three-knotted cord. Outside, the mist hisses against the stretched hide. Someone beside me mutters in their sleep—two soft clicks of the tongue, the sound we make when a trail ends in a drop—and turns over, their soot-rubbed leather rustling like dry leaves.

If you asked me where in the White Hollow I belong, I could not give you a place-name. Nothing here keeps a name long enough to wear it. Right now, I am three days' climb above the lower spore-plumes, tucked under the lee of a root-knuckle of Old Wood as thick as a river-barge. Tomorrow, if the sap surges under the moss-mat to the east, we will be ten miles across the chasm, clinging to a different rib. What I *am* is simpler: I am the ember-keeper, and the one who carries the notch-axe last.

That is not a grand title. In a band of six, everyone hunts, everyone hauls rope, everyone knows how to chip sweet ice from the rind of a frozen bladder-sheet. My work is just the quiet end of the line. When we break camp, I am the one who stays behind for five breaths after the others have clipped their copper hooks to the guide-line. I scrape the last half-burned pith-coals into my hollow stem-tube, plug the end with wet moss, and tuck it inside my coat against my ribs where the cold cannot kill it. Then I walk the perimeter of our footprints, checking the cairn we stacked or the marks we left in the Old Wood, making sure a stranger coming after us can read what we learned.

*Three slanted notches: water in the leaf-cups above.*
*Four lumps of amber resin: the bridge fell yesterday.*

I sit up carefully, keeping my head low so I don't brush the *tell-tale* cord. My visor is hanging from a bone toggle at my chest; even inside the dome, with the entrance flap laced tight, a faint, cold silver glow bleeds through the translucent skin of the tent, enough to show the frost riming the seams. I pull my hood forward, settle the blackened resin over my face, and lace it behind my ears until the world narrows to two hair-thin slits.

The Hollow is all verticality and glare, and the visor is what keeps it from eating you alive. Without it, the white-silver light bouncing off thirty miles of wet, luminescent moss burns your eyes out in an afternoon. With it, the world becomes manageable—just a thin strip of frost, a boot-width of crust, the dark charcoal smudge of the climber ahead of you moving against the blinding wall.

I crawl out through the flap into the noon-bright cold.

The silence hits first, vast and stinging. Across the chasm—ten miles? twenty? the air is so clear and bright it flattens distance—a curtain of pale, hanging tendrils sways in a slow eddy of mist. Somewhere far below, a plume of glowing spores rises from a thermal vent like smoke from an unseen chimney. There is no sky above it, only the faint, distant blur of the permafrost ceiling, miles up, where the stones sleep in the ice.

I tap the ground beside the dome with my bryophyte pole. *Thump.* Dry, dead bark. Good. Then I walk ten paces toward the edge of the shelf and tap the pale moss-mat where we crossed yesterday.

*Ping.*

High, tight, and glassy. Beneath the frost-crust, the sap is pressing upward, hot with chemical light. When I press the flat of my resin-soled boot against it, I don't even have to take my glove off to feel the wrongness—a faint, greasy warmth bleeding through the leather. By tonight, that flat shelf will buckle fifty feet into the air, split its skin, and pour a blinding river of pale froth into the abyss.

I don't shout. Shouting shakes the frost-loaded arches overhead. Instead, I pull the small, hollowed borer-shell from my belt and blow a single, thin note—sharp enough to cut the mist without waking the ice.

Behind me, the tent flap parts. One by one, the band emerges—small, broad-shouldered shapes wrapped in ash-darkened glider hide, their flat black visors tilting toward me in unison, like a cluster of beetles turning toward a sound. No one asks what I found. One of them—the youngest, whose crampon-spurs are carved from our dead cousin Tala's shinbone—simply walks over, feels the warmth through his boot, and nods once.

We pack in silence. Bone stakes come up; ropes are coiled; the bladder-skin is rolled tight and lashed to shoulders. I kneel by the cold fire-ring, rake the last gray coals into my stem-tube, and press the copper awl lashed to my wrist to make sure the knot hasn't frayed. My fingers are stiff, the skin around the nails split from the dry cold, stained permanently black with pith-soot and pale with borer-fat.

Before we step onto the Old Wood root-cable that will carry us west, I take the flint axe from my belt. The edge is notched and precious—three generations old, flaked from a Roof-Fall nodule my grandmother heard scream down through the mist before I was born. I lean out over the edge of the shelf, where the moss is already beginning to *creak* and *tick* beneath its skin, and hack two deep, slanted gashes into the pale bark of the anchor-trunk.

*Do not cross here.*

A shadow passes over me—not a true shadow, because the light comes from everywhere at once, from the walls and the floor and the ribs overhead, but a sudden dimming, like a hand passing across a lantern. I look up through my slits. A membrane-glider is banking thirty yards above us, its three-yard wings pale as scraped parchment, its blind, slit-eyed head swinging side to side as it clicks softly into the void, tasting the thin ribbon of warmth rising from my ember-tube.

I stay still. In my blackened leather, crouched against the frost, I look like a dead knot of bark. The glider decides we are nothing worth eating, catches a cold updraft, and drifts away toward the opposite wall, silent as a torn sheet.

I stand, lift my pole, and follow the dark smudges of my band along the ridge.

If you came looking for me here, you would probably walk right past me. You would be staring out at the thirty miles of empty, glowing air, or flinching at the distant *crack* of a frozen frond shearing off into the mist. You wouldn't notice the small, soot-streaked figure kneeling by a pile of amber resin, listening to the creak of a world that hasn't finished growing, trying to leave just enough marks on the wood so that whoever comes behind won't step where the floor used to be.
