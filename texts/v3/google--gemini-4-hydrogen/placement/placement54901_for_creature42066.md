I would be found in the quiet before the noise—curled inside a pocket no wider than my own bent knees, in the lee of three leaning travertine needles where the mist gathers thick as grease.

Not at the center of the basin, where the basalt spires stand tightest and the shale plates ring like struck iron without pause. Farther out, toward the eastern shelf, where the floor steps down into wider, ankle-deep terraces and the travertine grows older, thicker-crusted, heavy with lime. It is a damper, slower quarter of the valley, if anything here can be said to be slower than the rest.

I would be one of the dwellers—not young enough to still have soft, unringed wrists, and not yet so brittle in the collarbone that I must crawl into the deep silt and wait for the crust to close over my ribs. Somewhere in the long middle of a circuit that has taken me, season by season, around the same half-mile of channels for more years than the slate has thickened by a hand’s breadth.

If you found me there, you would not see me at first, even with the milky second lids drawn back in the hush of the pocket. My skin is the colour of the chalky water that weeps from the spires, and I keep it slick with clay-silt and the pale sulfur-slime scraped from the shaded cracks. Around my ankles, my wrists, and the sharp ridge of my collarbones, the mineral drip has left pale, rough collars of salt that catch the dim grey-violet light the same way the rims of the drinking basins do. My sling—woven from dried, twisted pool-threads—holds a single flat river-cobble against my ribs, worn smooth at one end from decades of cracking slug-shells and chipping lime.

I wake not to light—there is never enough of that to wake to—but to a change in the weight of the air against my jaw.

Even in a well-packed dead-zone, the silence is never empty. It is only muffled down to the size of a body: the slow, cold thud of my own blood, once every few seconds, and beneath it the distant, muted groan of the aquifer shifting under the shelf. When a seal fails, it does not hiss. It begins to *ring*. A thin thread of sound—some travertine needle thirty paces away catching a fresh drop—finds a hairline crack where the clay-silt has shrunk, and suddenly the pocket has an edge to it.

That is how I know it is time to move.

Before I uncurl, I press the flat of my thumb into the wet silt at the base of the slate slabs, feeling for the gap. I could re-pack it and drop back into torpor for another turn of the mist. Instead, I let the second lids slide shut over my eyes—the world outside has no use for them—and ease myself upright, turning sideways to slip through the ring of fist-sized stones and out into the channel.

The roar takes me all at once, not through the ears alone, but through the soles of my splayed feet, up the shins, into the heavy bone of my jaw and sternum.

To an outsider, it would be a wall of wet, metallic thunder. To me, it is the valley telling me where everything is.

I stand still for a long moment, resting two knuckles lightly against the nearest basalt column, letting the chord settle into my teeth. The basin is never the same twice, though it changes so slowly that only someone who has walked it for forty seasons notices the drift. Down-channel, to the north, the deep bass sigh of the submerged vent is flatter than it was last season—choked with silt, or pinched by a settling plate of shale. Closer by, three spires over, a hollow travertine tube is singing a fraction higher, its inner channel narrowed by another year’s crust of lime.

And somewhere in the middle distance, perhaps eighty paces through the razor-edged aisles, there is a soft, irregular dampening in the high gong of the falling drops. Something moving. Another dweller, sliding sideways through the mist, their clay-coated body swallowing the echo of the needles as they pass.

I do not hurry toward them. Nothing here hurries. I turn my shoulders narrow, tuck my chin, and step onto the path.

Under my horn-thickened heels, the deliberate line of broken glass-shards presses against the callous: two angled fragments set close together, pointing left around a jutting fan of shale. I read them without looking down, my toes curling slightly over the blunted edges. My left shoulder grazes a wet, slick spire—barely a brush, the sulfur-slime easing me past without a cut—and I catch a smear of the pale film on two fingers, bringing it to my tongue as I walk. It tastes of cold iron, wet chalk, and the long patience of stone that has never seen sun.

My part in this world is small, and mostly concerned with water that stands still.

On the eastern terrace, where the shale plates lie flatter, there are four deep pools where the translucent, hair-like threads grow thick in the murk. They are fragile things, neither root nor worm, pulsing slowly to strain the suspended silt so the water stays clear. When the aquifer booms too hard, or when a fat drop from a high spire finally pries a flake of shale loose, the sharp stone falls into the pools and tears them, or stirs up the bottom until the threads retract and vanish.

I walk those pools. When I reach the first one, I kneel slowly—careful of the razor-edged lip of the shelf—and lay my palm flat against the wet rock beside the water to read the tremor. The aquifer is between breaths, the bass drone low and steady. I slide my long, spatulate fingers into the pool, moving no faster than the mist drifts, feeling through the cold water without disturbing the silt. Near the center, my nails touch the crisp edge of a fallen shale flake. I lift it out, a fraction of an inch at a time, until it clears the surface, and set it wedged upright into a dry fissure where it cannot slip again. Beneath my wrist, the pale threads pulse once, twice, undisturbed.

I do not drink from the pool—that water has run down the basalt-glass and carries invisible splinters that tear the throat—but twenty paces on, beneath a weeping column of porous travertine, there is a shallow basin chipped into the rock. Someone else started it long before I was born; I have only deepened it with my cobble when the lime crust grew too thick. I cup the cold, iron-bitter water in my palm and drink slowly, then scrape a fresh layer of wet clay-silt from the basin’s edge to patch a thin spot on my forearm where the stone has rubbed me bare.

By the time the mist has shifted the length of a channel, the hum in my jawbone has grown sharper.

It is easy, out on the open shelf, to stay too long. The chord of the valley is vast and intricate, and when a vent chokes or a new plate tensions itself against a spire, you want to press your teeth to the glass and listen until you understand the whole shape of the change. But I have seen dwellers who listened too long—seen them standing rigid in a narrow aisle, their joints locked, their limbs shivering in tight, helpless sympathy with the ringing shale, unable to tell the roar of the basin from the phantom gong inside their own skulls. Once you are *rung*, the gaps between the needles seem to narrow, and the glass finds you.

I feel the first thin edge of it behind my eyes—a high, dry hum that does not stop when I lift my feet from the stone.

So I turn back toward the dead-zones. On the way, in a slightly wider junction where three travertine needles lean together like ribs, the dampening I felt earlier resolves into a figure stepping sideways out of the mist.

We stop a body’s length apart, narrow and pale in the grey-violet dimness, both of us slick with the same grey slime, both of us carrying the same chalky rings at the wrist. Neither of us opens the milky lids over our eyes. There is nothing to see that the stone has not already told us.

Instead, we step close until our foreheads meet lightly, bone to bone, bypassing the shouting air.

In that small, warm bridge of skull, the roar drops away, and I feel the slow, heavy beat of their chest—a little faster than mine, younger, the bone still dense and springy. Through the contact, without breath or word, they pass me the shape of the northern channels: the choked vent I had already heard, a rise of two finger-widths in the water table over the low path, a cluster of chalk-shelled slugs thick enough to spare two beneath the crooked shale plate. In return, I let them feel the clear pools behind me, and the small, drying crack in my dead-zone three spires east, in case they need quiet before the next tremor passes.

Then we part, sliding past one another in the narrow gap without a scrape of glass, and the mist closes between us before ten paces are done.

I find my way back by the glass-shards under my heels, scoop a handful of wet clay-silt from a damp seam, and press it firmly into the whistling crack at the back of my pocket. When I curl inside the ring of stones, knees pulled tight to my sternum, and let my breathing slow to twice a minute, the valley shrinks back to a distant, harmless murmur.

In the dark behind my lids, my own pulse is the loudest thing left in the world. And that is enough, until the crust thickens, or the water rises, or the stone calls me out again.
