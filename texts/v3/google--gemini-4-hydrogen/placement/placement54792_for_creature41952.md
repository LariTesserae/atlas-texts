I would not be a visitor from the streets above, sweating through a canvas suit and counting the minutes until I could climb back up the steel stair. At forty-five degrees in wet, motionless air, anything that needs cool breath to live is only passing through. To belong here, you have to be built for the long soak.

I would find myself tucked into the narrow, vibrating gap behind the cooling fan housing near the east stair—one of the few places in the entire vault where the air actually moves.

It isn’t cool air. It’s the same heavy, steam-thickened forty-five degrees as the rest of the chamber, but the fan pulls it into a thin, steady draft that dries the worst of the condensation off the metal casing and keeps the mold from furring my whiskers. Just below my perch, out of a hairline crack in the damp mortar, the single stunted fern trembles in the same pale current. From here, I can watch the whole eastern wall: the pallet of softening lime sacks, the abandoned wheelbarrows slowly filling with warm runoff, and the half-poured concrete buttress with its bloom of orange rebar.

I would be one of the rats—sleek, sparse-furred, long-tailed, my skin hot to the touch and my pulse quick enough to match the hum of the machinery.

Not a newcomer washed down from the storm-lines yesterday, confused by the roar and scalded by the steam, but one who has learned the architecture of the vault by heart. In a chamber where the floor is mostly shallow runoff, iron-bacteria scum, and the territory of something much larger and older than me, you learn to live vertically. My world is a high, narrow grid: the overhead cable trays, the galvanized grating bridges between the sump pumps, the tops of the copper condensate lines, and the dusty ledges of the brick junction house.

My days are measured not by light—the caged sodium lamps never turn off, though the drain flies crusting their glass dim them by degrees—but by the pitch of the two main sump pumps. Their thrum lives in my teeth and the bare pads of my feet. When the pitch drops and deepens, I know the city above is venting water and the catch-basins are rising by a few inches. That is when the cockroaches shift upward in slow, rippling sheets along the brick seams, and when I stay high on the cable trays.

Everything I need is up here, if I am patient. I don’t drink from the lukewarm basins where the red tubifex worms wave; I lick the clean, warm beads of condensation off the underside of the copper lines. I skirt the sagging hammocks of the blind cellar spiders slung across the unused scaffolding, picking off the midges and cave crickets that blunder onto the walkways, and now and then—when the steam thickens and a free-tailed bat loses its grip in the jagged ceiling fissures—I drop down the steel stair to claim it before the beetles and the roaches strip it to bone.

And I know better than to move when the *other* thing uncoils.

From my ledge behind the fan, I have watched it a hundred times. It looks like a slump of spilled mortar until the pressure-relief valve blows a sharper hiss of steam and it shifts its slate-grey plates. Whenever it pads across the slick stone toward the pallet of lime sacks below my stair, I flatten myself against the warm metal behind the fan, tail tucked tight, heart hammering against my ribs. I watch it nose open the softened paper, drag its pale, mucus-slick flanks through the caustic powder, and then haul itself up into the wooden formwork of the unfinished buttress to wedge among the rusted rebar.

We never fight, and we never quite ignore each other. When it climbs onto the galvanized grating or hangs upside down from the copper condensate lines, swaying gently with the rush of water inside the pipes, the whole overhead grid shudders under its weight. Every rat on the cable trays freezes. We let our whiskers read the slow, heavy sway of the metal until it drops back down into the runoff. It is too slow to catch us on the high wire, and we are too small and quick to be worth its trouble when there are blind insects falling into the catch-basins by the thousand.

What I avoid most, even more than the big blind thing, is the open relay box. It smells of scorched dust and sharp ozone, and when it snaps with a sudden blue spark, it makes my whole coat prickle. That is when I retreat deepest into the shadow behind the fan, pressing my belly to the humming casing, and watch the big creature pad out from behind the brick junction house to lay its heavy, eyeless head right against the sparking metal—basking in the crackle I can’t bear.

Then the spark dies, the dull red boiler-feed indicators settle back into their watch, and the vault returns to its slow, wet rhythm. The fern trembles below me. The pumps thrum through the soles of my feet. And I stay where I am, sleek and watchful in the bruised amber dark, waiting for the next shift of the water.
