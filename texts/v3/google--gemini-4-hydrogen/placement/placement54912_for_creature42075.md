I wouldn’t be up on the highest bridges, where the cables whip and re-braid in the glare of the white sky, and I wouldn’t be out in the middle of the steaming avenues for long. I think I would find myself lower down—around the waist of one of the middle towers, where the calcified cellulose ribs flare out into the tea-colored water and the light is already broken up by overlapping leaves.

To be there at all, I would have to be built for the noise. If I arrived with human ears, the basin would drive me mad in an hour—the wet bass thrum of the gravity-lenses, the constant heavy drip, the *clack* of tensioned vines snapping into new geometry. So I am tall, narrow, and mostly silent. The membranes on the sides of my skull are sealed tight, glossy and impervious to the air. I don’t hear the basin; I feel it. The whole four-hundred-pace bowl is a single drumhead beneath my wide, webbed toes, and every tower sends its own distinct shudder down through the root-mat into my heels.

I am one of the editors, though not one of the restless ones who spend all day riding the vertical water-columns from tier to tier. I stay closer to the intakes and the lower galleries—the places where the city’s frantic upward rush has to slow down and negotiate with the swamp.

Most of my time is spent shin-deep in the warm soup, where the ribbon-finned fish spiral around my ankles, flashing their sharp little geometric bursts as they herd the copper plankton. My skin is pale and water-smoothed, freckled across the shins and shoulders with copper-green moss that doesn’t hurt, just lives there like a second skin. When a dog-sized climber scuttles down a water-column with a bundle of silver tubers in its mandibles, I step into its path and brace my double-hinged knees against the spongy floor. I take one tuber—warm, starchy, faintly metallic—and in return I use the hardened edge of my thumbnail to scrape the pale salt-crust out of the climber’s leg-joints. It’s a quiet transaction, done entirely by touch. Neither of us needs to look at the other.

You learn to watch the marsh-birds out of the corner of your eye. When they rise all at once—a screaming, iridescent cloud lifting off the balconies—I don’t wait to wonder why. I have three seconds. I leap onto the nearest tower’s lower ribs, hooking my wrists around a slick ridge of lignin just as the invisible clearing-pulse sweeps across the basin. Beneath me, the reeds flatten as if combed by a giant hand, and the water-skin ripples clean, shoving loose pulp and tangled moss toward the edges. Then the pulse passes, the reeds spring back, and I drop down into the warm shallows again.

But that’s just maintenance. The real reason I stay near the lower flanks of the tower is for the louvers.

Out in the bright, muggy glare, there isn’t much to say. If another editor crosses my path on a swaying walkway, we trade two sharp raps of a fingernail against the railing, or slap a flat palm onto a cable to send a coded tremor down its length: *Intake clear. Southern root heavy.* It’s all shorthand, meant to survive the din.

Then, without warning, a bank of broad, translucent leaves along the tower’s side snaps shut.

The glare cuts off. The gallery plunges into a sudden, cool, green-tinted shadow. And that’s when I climb up out of the water, dripping and unhurried, to meet whoever else was nearby.

In the dark behind the closed leaves, the workshop roar outside becomes a dull, rhythmic pressure against the walls. We stand close, faces inches apart, and the pale, flat sacs along my throat and collarbones begin to warm. There’s no hurry in it—not like the fish flashing their math in the pools below. Our light is slow: overlapping washes of amber and faint violet, spilling across each other’s wet, soapstone-pale skin. It’s the only place in the whole bowl where anything is allowed to linger for a minute. We don’t trade commands in the dark; we trade the weight of things. How the tower feels today. Whether the gravity-lens above the third tier is humming a half-tone sharper than it did yesterday.

And sometimes, before the louvers snap open again, one of us reaches an arm straight into the shimmering, uncontained column of water that passes through the gallery. The current tugs at my fingers, cool and slick, and I pull out one of the glowing packets.

Cradling it between my palms, I read the light flickering against my wet wrists. It isn’t language—it’s a ledger kept in brightness and warmth. I feel the slow, creeping tilt of the tower’s eastern roots, a warning about a cable-knot strangling a feeder-tube three tiers up, and, layered underneath the structural data like sediment at the bottom of a pool, the faint, lingering pulse of someone who waded out into the deep water two seasons ago and let the mat take their calcium back.

I don’t mourn them, any more than I mourn a bladder-chamber when its ribbed walls soften and sag back into pulp. Nothing here is built to be kept. I simply press my thumb into the sphere, adding a fresh pulse of amber light—a small correction, a note that the southern intake is clear and the climbers are moving well—and push it back into the upward stream.

When exhaustion finally catches up with me, I don’t go down to the mat. Nothing stays dry or still down there. I climb instead into the high, vaulted ribs just beneath a cantilevered terrace, right into that faint, pale-violet distortion in the air that smells sharply of ozone and crushed mint. The dark, rubbery filaments growing down my spine bristle slightly in the static haze. I hook my double-jointed arms around a swaying, tensioned cable and let myself hang like wet fruit.

Half of my brain goes dark, sinking into the heavy, humid quiet. The other half stays awake in my fingertips, listening to the cable’s tremor, waiting for the subtle shift in tension that means it’s about to unravel and weave itself somewhere else. There is no finished thing to wake up to, and no monument to leave behind—just the warm, bright engine humming in the bowl, waiting to be edited again.
