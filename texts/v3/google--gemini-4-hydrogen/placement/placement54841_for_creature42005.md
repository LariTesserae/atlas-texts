I would not be out on the open salt-pan, where the cathedral-sized walkers plant their iron-wood stilts with enough force to boom like struck boilers, nor up on the high mesa rims where the wind planes the stone to razor edges. I would be at the seam between them: on a shallow apron of wind-scoured mica-schist where the cracked red clay meets the edge of the vitrified white flat.

Here, the crust is thin. You can feel the vast, superheated dark beneath it like the skin of a drum pulled tight over a furnace.

I would have no eyes to see the bleached indigo sky, and no throat to be parched by the ozone-thick air. I would be low to the ground—no taller than a knee—built like a cross between a seismograph and a desert flower that never learned to be green.

My base is three splayed, heavy spurs of porous iron-slag, wedged deep into a hairline fracture in the red clay and cemented there by decades of wind-blown salt. Rising from that tripod is a squat, drum-shaped housing of salt-glazed red ceramic, and above the housing sits a coiled torsion-neck of braided copper-gristle and mineral-silk. At the top, open to the merciless daylight, is my crown: a wide, shallow rosette of overlapping mica-schist plates, split thin as fingernail parings and backed with hammered lead foil.

I do not eat matter. I do not drink static arcs, or comb sulfur soot from the air, or saw porcelain bones into silica dust.

I feed on the delay.

In a world where geometry has frayed at the edges, nothing arrives when it should. A mile out on the salt-flat, a stilt-beast lifts a leg and sets it down. I cannot see it, yet I know every joint of its stride by the way the world disagrees with itself.

First, sometimes, comes the *cold*—a sudden, three-second drop in the radiant heat pressing down on my lead-backed petals, as the creature’s displaced shadow, three times too large and detached from its body, sweeps across the flat. My mica plates contract with a faint, dry *tink*.

Then, four seconds later—or sometimes *before* the shadow has even passed—the ground-shock arrives through my three slag spurs. The hollow plateau flexes, and the booming vibration twists my base a fraction of a millimeter to the north. My copper-gristle neck winds tight, storing the torque.

Twelve seconds after that, the air-wave finally catches up, slapping flat against my mica rosette from a slightly different angle, twisting my crown to the east.

That mismatch—the argument between what the earth says happened, what the air says happened, and what the sun’s shadow insists upon—is my metabolism. Every time the ground-wave and the air-wave arrive out of phase, my torsion-neck twists against itself. Deep inside my ceramic belly, that twist drives a ratcheting wheel of raw carborundum against a core of compacted sulfur and iron-pyrite. *Click-grrrt. Click-grrrt.* The friction generates a dry, internal heat and a trickle of mineral charge that keeps my copper-gristle supple and my ceramic shell vitrified against the hundred-and-twenty-degree glare.

My life is measured in immense, patient intervals.

For hours, there is only the steady, chest-rattling turbine-drone from the blind tangle of living slag grinding miles below. In that constant vibration, my ratchet rests. I bake in the sun. Down in the clay cracks between my iron spurs, the coin-sized stacks of tin and ceramic lie folded and still.

Then a six-legged copper coil drags its belly past my apron, leaving a smoking ribbon of fused glass across the schist. My spurs register the tiny, crackling *snap* of the coin-stacks unfolding, flipping three inches through the parched air to press themselves against the cooling glass. When two of the coils pass within a hundred yards of each other and a slow ribbon of violet lightning hangs buzzing between them, my lead-backed crown hums in sympathy, every mica plate vibrating like a struck cymbal until the arc snaps out.

Once a week, perhaps, a barge-sized bladder drifts low enough over the basin’s edge that its rhythmic *HISS-THUMP* slams straight down onto my rosette. The pressure wave flattens my petals nearly to the clay, driving my internal ratchet so hard that my ceramic belly rings out a single, high, clear note—a brief silver needle of sound threading through the basin’s heavy bass drone.

And very rarely, I have company.

I cannot see the spire-walker coming, because it moves too slowly to stir the air, and its three blackened-brass caliper-legs are too light to ring the drumhead crust. I only know it is near when the little pocket of ungrounded potential hovering beside my apron—an eddy where a stray magnetic wake from below has tangled with an orphaned echo from the north—begins to hum a half-step higher than usual.

The spire-walker stops. I feel the faint, gritty *shhh-click* of its slate collar rotating, orienting its milky quartz lenses toward the shimmer. I feel the tiny, sharp pressure as it screws its brass feet half an inch into the salt.

Then comes the long, papery *hiss* as its thirty-foot basalt mast unfurls its copper filaments and drinks the eddy dry.

For three or four seconds afterward, the frayed seam between us is mended. The air flattens. Down on the salt-pan, a stilt-beast takes a step, and for once, the ground-shock through my slag spurs and the air-slap against my mica crown arrive at the exact same instant.

My torsion-neck goes completely slack. My carborundum ratchet stops. In that sudden, terrifyingly neat silence of synchronized physics, there is no torque, no friction, no spark—nothing to grind and nothing to feed on. For three seconds, I am starved by order.

Then the spire-walker unscrews its feet and marches slowly on toward the next torn edge. Deep beneath the crust, the blind slag-tangle shifts its weight, the plateau slips its gears, and a stray *clank* echoes up from beneath my spurs before the leg three miles away has even begun to fall.

My neck winds tight. Inside my ceramic belly, the carborundum wheel catches a tooth, and begins to turn.
