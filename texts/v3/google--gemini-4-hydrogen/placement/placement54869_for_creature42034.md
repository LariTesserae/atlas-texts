Before the sun clears the eastern rim, there is no color in the world—only cold, and the weight of other bodies pressed against my back.

I wake when the chill reaches my knees. In the shallow den behind the boulder, three of us are curled together under layered sheets of glider-membrane, breathing slow and shallow to keep the moisture inside our chests. Even asleep, my ribs know the thinness of this air; they rise deep and wide, pulling what little there is to pull from the dark.

I slip out first, careful not to let the membrane rustle too sharply, though in this atmosphere even a harsh scrape dies a yard from the stone. The plateau is a bowl of salt and ash under a sky still bruised violet. Everything is knife-cold. My bare feet find the flat limestone, already familiar with its grain, and I crouch to smear a fresh coat of pan-clay across my shoulders and forehead while the dampness still clings to the crust.

Down at the nearest mineral pan, the water has just arrived—an inch of dull, brackish mirror. I kneel at the edge with a bone scraper, waiting. Under the surface, the coin-sized sleepers are uncurling from their tight, dry spirals, swelling into pale petals as they comb the silt. I do not touch them. My fingers go instead to the soft mud at the rim, where the mica-shelled burrowers are rising before the sun bakes the pan shut. I take six, no more—enough to trade for a strip of cured fat—and tuck them into the pouch at my hip.

When the light finally breaks over the ridge, it does not warm the air gently. It strikes. Within minutes, the chalky stone begins to bleach, and I pull down my bone frames, settling the oiled slits of glider-membrane over my eyes. Through them, the world narrows to a sharp, darkened band—cool to look through, though the heat is already rising off the flats in silent, invisible sheets.

If you asked me what I am to the band, I could not give you a name for it, because we have no names that travel on the air. I am the one who carries the heavy pouch of horn-blades and mica. I am the one who watches the wrists.

We set out across the limestone toward the central arch, seven of us, spaced wide so our shadows do not tangle. From a mile off, I know we look like nothing—just wisps of heat-haze drifting in parchment cloaks. My cloak is old, patched twice along the hem with rib-leather, its membrane yellowed like cured bone. I walk with my left hand resting on the hilt of my light-fan, thumbing the edge where a strip of grazer-skin is inlaid.

Halfway across the dry wash, the younger ones slow down, their shoulders angling toward the cobbles. Down in the grit, something has flickered—a weak, stuttering silver flash, like a calf losing its footing.

I step in front of them and lift my hand, palm turned flat against the glare, then tap two fingers sharply against my own collarbone. *Wait. Look again.*

A calf’s signal is clean, even in panic; its neck-flaps snap with a rhythm you can count. What glints down there between the polished stones has no rhythm at all—just a dry, hesitant twitch, pale against the ash-colored gravel. An ambusher, buried to its jaw, testing our patience.

We skirt the wash by fifty yards, silent on our calloused soles. Overhead, two parchment gliders tilt their wide, ribbed wings in a slow, intersecting spiral, watching us pass. They make no sound, not even the creak of tendon. We are too tall, too bony, too wrapped in their own shed skin to be worth a dive.

By midday, the heat presses down like a flat stone, and the pearlescent arch is ahead of us—a low, curving ribbon of something neither metal nor rock, rising seamless from the scree.

Here, the band loosens. We gather in the bent air beneath its curve, where the light slips sideways and the mist is already gathering. I tilt my head back and watch the droplets form along the pearlescent underside, swelling until they detach and drift *upward* for the width of a hand before losing their strange lift and pattering down into our wide bowls. Beside my elbow, hundreds of wingless crimson insects cluster in the shade, drinking the same cool dampness. I let my knuckles brush the edge of a child’s wrist to remind them not to sweep the stone clean; we take a dozen insects for the red tool-dye, and leave the rest to the mist.

While the others drink and pass water-bladders hand to hand, I take out my repair kit. A young hunter’s light-fan has gone dull; the salt-wind has clouded the mica blade, and the copper patch of grazer-skin at its tip is curling at the corners. I sit cross-legged in the damp gravel, scraping the mica thin with a flint flake, rubbing a drop of lizard fat into the dried flap until its iridescence wakes up again.

When I finish, I tap the back of his hand—two slow beats, then one light press—and hold the fan out. He snaps it open toward the flat. A clean, sharp blade of copper light leaps across three hundred yards of glare, crisp enough to read from the far ridge, and vanishes. He touches his knuckles to my shoulder in thanks. That is all the speech the noon requires.

In the fiercest heat of afternoon, we leave the arch for the matte-black needle two miles east.

It stands thirty feet high in a dry wash, tilted slightly, as if someone dropped it from the violet sky an age ago and the earth simply closed around its base. Up close, the heat drops away from it—not with a breeze, just an absence, like stepping into the mouth of a deep well.

We sit with our bare backs pressed against the matte surface, letting the stone-coolness drink the fever out of our spines. Most of the band sleeps here, heads tipped back, goggles pushed up onto clay-daubed foreheads.

I do not sleep. This is my hour.

I turn around, kneel in the grit, and press my jaw firmly against the black pillar. Then I close my eyes.

At first there is only the needle’s own hum—that faint, tooth-deep vibration that belongs to the artifact itself, pulling tomorrow’s water out of the dry sky. I let my teeth soften against the hum until I can hear what rides underneath it, carried up through miles of ancient, settled bedrock.

There: four miles west, a soft, papery thrum, steady as a pulse. A herd of grazers trotting over the limestone flats, maybe eighty strong, their spongy hooves drumming a message only the stone can carry.

And deeper down—a sharp, erratic shudder, like gravel sliding in a throat. I keep my jaw pressed hard, counting the intervals. Flash-water, tearing through one of the high canyons beyond the northern rim. It will not reach our dry washes until tomorrow, or the day after, when the pans swell higher than usual—but the rock already knows it is coming.

A shadow shifts beside me. One of the elders kneels at the opposite face of the needle, pressing her forehead to the black surface directly across from my jaw. She hums—a low, dry note deep in her throat, making no sound in the thin air. Through the cool core of the pillar, the vibration travels straight into the bone behind my ear.

*Water north?* she asks in the pitch of her hum, rising twice at the end.

I press my forehead to match hers and hum back, low and flat, letting the resonance settle behind her eyes. *High canyons. Two days.*

We stay like that for a breath longer, sharing the quiet inside the stone, before pulling away into the blinding afternoon.

When the sun drops toward the western rim, we walk back toward the pearlescent arch one last time before climbing to the boulder dens. This is the hour I wait for all day—when the arch’s shadow stretches long across the scree and splits into two lagging, double-edged shapes.

I crouch in the narrow gap between those two shadows, pulling off my bone goggles. Inside the bent air, the fierce glare falls away like a dropped cloak. Across miles of bleached basin, the shimmering heat-haze clears, and the plateau shows me its real shape: the faint dust-plume of another band moving along the southern ridge; a dark knot of grazers turning uphill for the night; and far out in the central wash, the pale, flat outline of an ambusher shifting its ribs in the cooling grit.

I memorize the lines of the basin—where the herds are, where the water is coming, where the hunters lie—so that tomorrow, when my fan snaps open in the glare, my hand will know which way to point.

Then the sun drops below the rim. Within twenty minutes, the warmth drains out of the sky, and the cold returns like a blade. We climb back to our moss-lined hollows behind the sunk boulders, pulling the parchment cloaks tight around our knees, leaving the flats to the frost and the flat-bodied spiders rising from the scree.

I press my back against the others, tuck my cold hands under my arms, and let my ribs settle into the slow, deep rhythm of the dark—listening, even in sleep, for the faint hum of the needles holding the plateau together until dawn.
