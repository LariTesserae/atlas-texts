I would not be near the center of the basin, where the main dewatering stations squat in their pools of cold-white glare and the intake grilles shudder hard enough to blur the frost on their own bolts. That is too bright, and too loud even for something without ears.

Instead, I would be out toward the northern margin, where the valley floor begins to tilt upward into talus and the far wall stops being a smudge and becomes a sheer, dripping face of fractured basalt. A secondary pipeline runs out there—older than the main trunks, narrower, wrapped in split canvas and tarred felt over its insulation—carrying seepage water back toward the central sumps. At the end of that line sits an auxiliary transformer shed whose exterior work-lamp burned out years ago. No one has come down from the rim to replace it.

That is where I would be: in the permanent gray twilight against the dark flank of that dead-lamp shed, where the steel casing still gives off a dry, steady warmth and the sixty-cycle hum never stops.

To belong here, I could not be anything that hurries, or anything that needs to stay warm on its own. If I had lungs that burned at freezing, or thin skin that tore on hidden rock, I would only be passing through, and the valley would eventually swallow my bones in peat. So I would be one of the moss-backed ones—not the largest, perhaps, and not the one that holds the center of the floor, but the one that keeps to the northern wall.

At first, if you looked at the base of the shed, you wouldn’t find me. You would only see that the olive-and-russet velvet of the sphagnum doesn't quite climb the leeward steel, and that the concrete footing is rubbed dark with grease and peat-oil. I would be pressed flat against the metal, eyes shut beneath heavy brow-ridges, my body temperature matched so closely to the casing that no breath-vapor leaves my nostrils. In the creases of my shoulders and along the broad ridge of my spine, the liverworts and beard-lichen would be damp with condensation, indistinguishable from the boulder-heaps ten yards away.

I wouldn't know the roar of the pumps as sound. Having only sunken, scarred membranes beneath smooth skin, the air-borne thunder is just pressure—a flat, constant weight against my flanks, like living at the bottom of a fast-moving river that never changes its depth or speed. The wind, the dripping water, the distant shriek of a raven on the rim: all of that is invisible to me. 

My world is made of what touches iron.

When three or four days against the transformer have warmed my core enough to make my joints supple, I peel myself away from the steel. I don't step out onto the open moss; I know what the peat is hiding. Beneath that springy, deceptive mattress are fault-scarps sharp enough to open a belly, and crevices bridged by nothing thicker than a hand’s-width of wet felt. Instead, I hook my broad, calloused forelimbs around the nearest rusted stanchion and pull myself up onto the secondary pipeline.

Moving hand-over-hand along the curved cladding, suspended a few feet above the jagged floor, I read the valley through my palms and my flat chin. Every few yards I pause and press my jawbone directly against an exposed steel flange. 

Through the bone, the emptiness of the basin disappears. I can feel the main dewatering station half a mile south—a deep, steady, grinding thrum, healthy and uninterrupted. I can feel the rotary ventilation housing to the west, and notice the slight, rhythmic hitch in its bearings that wasn't there last month, a tiny double-shudder every forty turns. Farther up the line, where the pipe crosses a stretch of open peat, I feel a sudden, sharp *tink* travel down the steel: a dipper or a raven has landed on a pipe-clamp to rest out of the wind, its small weight ringing clearly through two hundred yards of iron before it lifts off again.

I forage without haste along the concrete apron of a capped borehole near the rock wall, scraping the cold, iron-rich bacterial mats from the damp stone with blunt teeth, or lifting the edges of hanging ferns to crush sluggish beetles. Sometimes, when the cold settles deepest and the hunger sharpens, I return to the transformer shed and settle motionless beside a vole’s tunnel in the warm moss-mat, becoming a wet mound of peat and lichen for hours until something small and warm brushes the entrance.

Mostly, though, I am listening through the metal.

There are only four or five of us in the whole mile-wide rift, each keeping to our own stretch of pipe, our own favored hum. We do not need to crowd together, and we rarely cross paths in the dimness between the lamps. Yet the valley is not silent to us. 

Now and then, perched on a stanchion where the secondary line meets the main trunk, I wait until the vibration of the pumps feels steady and unchanged. Then I raise a heavy, basalt-colored knuckle and strike the steel pipe-clamp.

Three slow beats. A long pause. Two beats more.

Nothing in the air changes; the turbine-roar swallows the strike before it travels an inch. A human standing on the moss below wouldn't hear a thing, might only wonder at a faint shiver in the soles of their boots.

I stay still, my chin pressed to the cold iron, waiting. It takes a long time—long enough for a bead of condensation to gather on the tip of a lichen-tuft over my eye and drop onto my snout. Then, traveling up through my forelimbs and into my teeth, I feel it: two slow, heavy taps against a transformer housing half a mile away.

Someone else is awake in the gray light. We have nothing urgent to say. I lift my hand from the pipe, climb down the stanchion to the concrete footing of my shed, and press my flank back against the sixty-cycle hum, letting the moss continue its slow, millimeter-by-millimeter work of claiming the valley.
