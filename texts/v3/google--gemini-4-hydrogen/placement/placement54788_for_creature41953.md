I think I would be found below the level of the cedar walkways—not quite in the peat, and not quite on the avenues, but in the narrow, sheltered galleries bolted to the underside of the iron trestles.

There are four main junctions where the bundled copper lines, the brass condensation-pipes, and the pneumatic tubes cross paths on their way between the press-house and the outer terraces. Each one has a small, hip-roofed shed of tarred slate tucked beneath the planking, just tall enough for a person to sit on a three-legged stool with their knees drawn clear of the damp sphagnum. That is where I would spend my hours: in the pale, reflected light coming down through the gaps in the cedar boards above, with the smell of wet iron, linseed oil, and warm insulation.

I am one of the tenders—neither the oldest nor the youngest, though my knuckles have long since taken the permanent, mottled stain of verdigris and peat-tannin, and my shoulders have learned the exact curve required to clear the overhead pipe-clamps without thinking. In a settlement of thirty or forty people, everyone has a territory they know better than their own face. Mine is the *between*: the arteries that keep the four complexes talking to one another without a sound.

On my bench sits a shallow wooden tray holding tallow, spare brass ferrules, strips of waxed linen, and a small horn scraper for clearing lichen off the numbered enamel plates. Above my head, the pneumatic tubes run in pairs—dull copper dulled further by the perpetual mist. Every hour or so, one of them gives a faint, hollow sigh, followed a second later by the cushioned *thump* of a damp-proof paper cylinder dropping into the felt-lined basket at my elbow.

I don't read the slips unless they are addressed to a junction; usually they are only tallies from the northern kilns, or requests from the press-house for another half-volt on the eastern nerve-vines. My job is simply to check the leather washer on the carrier, wipe any bead of condensation from its brass cap, and send it onward into the next branch line with a twist of the diverter valve. The valve turns with a heavy, greased resistance that I know down to the millimeter. If it catches even slightly, I know before I open the casing that a fine thread of moss-root has worked its way into the seam, or that the air-pressure from the bellows at the press-house is running a fraction damp.

Because I work beneath the walkways, I know everyone in the basin by the weight and cadence of their soles overhead.

A young tender from the sedge-terraces walks quickly, with a light, uneven *pad-pad* that barely flexes the cedar planks. The old willow-pleacher from the western rim moves slowly, pausing every fourth step to rest a hand on the railing—I can hear the faint creak of the nerve-vine guy-wire taking his weight, followed by the tiny, sharp *ping* of the current adjusting through the living fiber. Sometimes two of them stop right above my slate roof, and I hear nothing at all for half a minute: only the soft shift of oiled wool, and then a murmur too low to carry through the wood, like water moving under ice.

I have my own quiet habits here. Every morning, before the mist thins enough for the automated needles to descend into the bladder-sedges, I walk the length of my section with a rag and my pocket voltmeter. I touch my bare thumb to the brass terminals along the pylons, waiting for that faint, sour tang of copper at the back of my teeth that tells me the lines are carrying cleanly. Where a nerve-vine has stiffened overnight in the cool air, I run my palm along its bark to feel for dry splits or slackness. A healthy vine under load feels like a tendon in a taut wrist—cool, fibrous, and faintly humming.

On the warm, peat-insulated housing of the junction transformer, there are almost always one or two grey moths resting with their wings folded flat. I never brush them away. When I have to open the housing to check the coils, I lift the lid slowly, inch by inch, so the warmth doesn't escape all at once and startle them into the damp air. Sometimes, while waiting for the midday tally from the kilns, I sit very still and watch the way their antennae twitch—the only quick, nervous movement in three hundred paces.

In the late afternoon, when the streetlamps dim a shade to mark the end of the shift, I lock the diverter valves, tuck my voltmeter into my breast pocket, and climb the short iron ladder up to the main avenue. My boots make no sound on the oiled cedar. Behind me, the junction settles into its night-hum, and ahead of me, the willow-arched windows of the western rim glow a dull, steady cherry-red through their mica panes.

I am not the one who bakes the ash-root loaves, nor the one who presses the swollen sedges at dawn. I am only the one who keeps the passages clear and the washers greased, so that when a message is sent from one end of the bowl to the other, it arrives dry, intact, and without disturbing the water in the hollows. When my hands finally stiffen past the point of scraping lichen or turning a brass valve, I will be glad enough to be wrapped in kiln-dried linen and laid down beneath the sphagnum—not far from the iron pilings, where the low hum of the cables still passes quietly through the peat.
