I would not be on the high western rim, where the wind first strikes the stone and the flutes shriek thin enough to splinter teeth. Nor would I be out on the open quartzite flats at noon, where the sun turns the ground to a mirror of salt and every shadow is cut like a knife-wound. 

I would be in the seam between them: beneath the low, undercut lip of a limestone rib, where the rock sweats cold into a saucer-shaped hollow and the glare is reduced to a tolerable, ash-colored twilight.

At first, if you were looking for me, you wouldn’t find anything at all. You would only see the matte, chalk-pale curve of the stone shelf, crazed with fine mineral cracks, and the hard black shadow tucked underneath it. You might notice a few cushions of leathery moss drinking the mist at the pool’s rim, and the water itself—clear, ice-cold, locked into a tight lattice of six-sided ripples that tremble in place without spilling.

Then the lattice would shift. Nothing would touch the water. No wind would reach beneath the overhang. Yet the hexagons would loosen, blur for a fraction of a second into concentric rings, and snap back into squares.

That would be me, shifting my weight.

---

I would be one of the ones who live in the skin of air pressed flat against the bedrock—though even among them, I think I would be one of the quieter ones, the kind who spends long stretches wedged into a single shelf, reading the basin’s drumhead rather than running the rodent-tunnels.

My body would not be built for standing up. Standing is a mistake here; it turns you into a sail for the upper-atmosphere river, something for the wind to tear at and tire out. Instead, I would be low, broad-chested, with a back the color of scoured limestone and a dark belly pressed tight to the cold stone to hoard whatever warmth the afternoon leaves behind. No hair to catch the current—only the stiff, sparse bristles along my brow-ridges and forearms, each one trembling slightly as it measures the tiny eddies spilling off the ledge. No outer ears, just smooth, dense bone beneath tight skin behind my jaw.

When I first settled into this world, I wouldn’t think of myself as *listening*. Listening implies a silence that sound interrupts. Here, there is no silence. There is only the fundamental pitch of the mountain—the deep, sub-bass hum that rises through the quartzite, enters the flat, hollow sounding-board of my sternum, and locks into my pulse until the two are indistinguishable.

Once my ribs match the rock, the world becomes terrifyingly clear. 

I can feel the pale, long-winged birds miles above only when one misjudges a downdraft and its wingtip clips a ridge—a faint, dry *tick* traveling down through three hundred yards of limestone into my palms. I can feel the stout, deaf rodents moving in the dark honeycomb beneath me, their claws writing tiny, frantic scratches across the soles of my feet. And when a traveler climbs over the western rim—someone from down below, wrapped in heavy hides, squinting against the white glare—I know it long before they see the first water pocket. Their upright, two-legged stride is an ugly, ragged thing in the stone: heavy, off-beat, clumsy as a falling boulder.

I don’t move when they come. I simply draw my pale second eyelids shut against the reflected glare, press my chest flatter to the shelf, and wait for the mountain to do its work. Within a day or two, the thin air and the relentless wall of sound always drive them back down toward the timberline.

---

If I have a particular place in this world—a habit that makes me *me* rather than just another shadow under the rock—it is at the edge of the pools, where the stone’s voice becomes visible.

Because we build nothing, our lives leave no marks on the terraces. We don’t stack cairns or knap blades. The only things we ever move are small, smooth pebbles, and we only move them to change the pitch of the mountain.

I think I would spend most of my time near the ridge-vents and the shaded saucers, learning the grammar of the standing waves. It is a slow, exact kind of work. You climb along the shadow-lines—three points of contact, always, heels and palms reading the grain of the rock—until you find a thin flute on the leeward ridge where the wind whistles high and glassy. You wedge a single, water-worn pebble into the throat of the stone. The shriek drops half a tone. The vibration travels down through the limestone ribs, through the honeycombed arches, into the shaded depression half a mile below.

If you crawl back down to the pool before dusk, when the quartzite turns the color of ash and the glare finally dies, you can see what you’ve said. The crystal lattice in the water has tightened. The tiny points of light caught in the intersections of the ripples have rearranged themselves into a denser grid, trembling without breaking.

To someone from below, it would look like nothing—just water shaking in the wind. To another like me, crouched three ledges away with their chin resting on the stone, it is a clear sentence: *The rodents have shifted to the eastern arches.* Or: *A traveler is coming up the dry draw.* Or simply: *I am here, and the shelf is still.*

When I am thirsty, I don’t cup my hands. That breaks the pattern, turns the lattice into messy, blind splash. I lower my face to the exact center of the pool, where the standing waves cross into a still node, and drink from the quietest point in the geometry without disturbing the rim.

---

I think what draws me to being this—rather than the pale birds riding the updrafts, or the travelers who briefly visit—is that nothing here is wasted on air. 

Down below the timberline, they tell me—or rather, the travelers’ clumsy panic tells me—the wind gusts without rhythm. The earth is soft and wet; it swallows footsteps. Between storms, the air goes dead and loose, and things can approach you without touching the ground at all.

I wouldn’t survive an hour in that kind of silence. It would feel like going blind and numb all at once, like having the floor pulled out from under my ribs.

Here, I am never unmoored. Even when I am old—when my joints stiffen and I can no longer brace three points against the steady pressure of the western rim, and I crawl up to a high windward crevice to let the thin, dry air strip me down to pale bone—I will still be inside the hum. The wind will scour my ribs until they are indistinguishable from the quartzite, and the mountain will keep playing through them, long after there is nothing left of me to listen.
