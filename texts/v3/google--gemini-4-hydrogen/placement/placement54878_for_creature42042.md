I would not be on the sheer cliff-face, hanging over the drop with a woven basket while the thin wind pulls at my fur. Others are better suited to that—younger ones, whose toes find the hairline seams without hesitation, whose shoulders can hold the weight of a full chalk-load while the sun climbs.

I would be three stories up, on the shaded side of an alley so narrow that I can touch the opposite tower’s eaves without uncurling my arm.

At first, if you were looking for me, you would mistake me for a bundle of dry bark wedged into the crook of a calcified vine. That is how I spend the middle of the afternoon: settled onto my haunches, my long, prehensile toes hooked loosely around the lip of a gypsum sill, my silver-grey coat fluffed just enough to hold a thin, private envelope of dampness against my ribs. When I breathe, it is scarcely a movement at all—a slow, shallow draw through the scrolled passages of my snout, which catch the warmth and moisture of the air before it can leave me. Every breath is a small ledger-keeping. Nothing wasted, nothing spilled.

If I lift my hand to test the plaster beside me, you would see the olive-dark leather of my palm, crosshatched with old white lines where the black basalt bit me in my first seasons of gathering stone. Those scars are smooth now, almost glassy themselves. My fingers are long, the pads thick and dry as cured hide. I press them lightly against the pale gypsum—not to push, only to read its temperature, to feel how much of the day’s glare has sunk into the stone behind it.

I am one of the climbers, though my work has grown smaller and closer to the ground-seams over the years. I tend the joints where the living city meets the stacked one.

In the late afternoon, when the shadows begin to sharpen into black blades across the alleys, I unfold myself—one limb at a time, letting the heat settle evenly so I do not sweat a drop. Beside me, along the sunlit lip of the parapet, a cluster of copper-skinned lizards lies overlapping like roof-shingles. They do not scatter when my shadow crosses them. One turns its broad, flat head by a fraction of a degree, its dull verdigris eye catching the slant of the sun, and then goes still again. Earlier in the day, I scored a thick, angular vine-joint two paces down the wall to coax a new shoot toward the neighboring tower’s bridge; a clear, mineral-heavy bead of sap has welled at the cut, and three of the smaller lizards are already gathered there, their blunt tongues working in silence before the dry air can crust it shut.

I cross the three-pace bridge to the opposite landing, taking the full minute it requires. Each segment of the woven vine is hard as coral and brittle if struck, so I test it first with the heel of my palm, feeling for the faint give that means a joint is loosening. Beneath my feet, forty feet down in the dead-air trench of the alley, the felt-like moss is thick and dark. Down below that, in the hairline cracks of the newly uplifted basalt where I will never go, the blind springtails and silver mites are turning yesterday’s shed lizard-skin and fallen bark-flakes back into dust for the roots. I do not see them, any more than the roots see the sky, yet I feel their work in the steadiness of the floor beneath me.

Halfway across, another climber emerges from a low woven-vine screen. We stop—not because the bridge cannot hold two, but because passing in haste is a kind of rudeness here, a spending of heat that neither of us can afford.

We settle onto our haunches facing each other, knees angled, elbows tucked close to conserve our warmth. Neither of us has a voice; our throats are bare, pale, and smooth between the parted silver fur. Slowly, I tilt my skull to the left and let my throat-pouch fill—not fully, just enough that the thin, pleated skin stretches faintly translucent in the amber light. It is a greeting, and a question about the upper terrace’s vines.

She holds my gaze, lets a long, quiet beat pass, then mirrors the tilt of my head and flattens her ears slightly against her pelt. Her pouch pulses once, shallow and unhurried: *the sap is holding; the north wall is dry.* We stay like that for several seconds, letting the shapes cure between us, before we lean slightly aside so the other can pass without brushing fur.

My real hour is the one just before dark.

I climb to the upper lintels, where the wooden bowls carved from fallen succulent trunks sit wedged into the niches. Inside mine is a dark, thick paste of stored resin-sugar, hoarded from the wetter months when the copper-green paddles were swollen and bitter-sweet. With the flat of a fractured basalt shard—unshaped, just a sharp edge I found years ago and keep tucked into a crevice—I lift a thin ribbon of the sugar and smear it along the underside of the gypsum eaves, just where the plaster meets the papery bark of a segmented trunk.

Before my hand has even drawn back, the wall beside me seems to detach itself from the shadow.

It is a moth, as wide across as my spread hand, so dusty white it looked like a rough patch of chalk-paste all afternoon. Its velvet wings lift without a whisper of air, no louder than ash settling. For a moment it hovers inches from my face, its huge, dark eyes reflecting the last pale strip of sky, and then its coiled proboscis unrolls toward the sweet amber smear I have left. I stay completely still until it finishes and drifts away down the canyon of the alley, following the scent of cinnamon and crushed stone toward the night-blooming crowns of the succulents.

There is a quiet satisfaction in this—not pride, which is too hot a feeling for the plateau, but the steadiness of a joint that fits flush. I do not own the moth, nor the lizard lapping at the vine-cut, nor the tall, hollow succulent whose base I will crawl into when the stars come out. I am simply the hand that scores the bark, the tongue that wets the chalk-dust when a wall needs mending, the weight that keeps the bridge-vines woven tight.

When the black basalt beneath the city begins to sigh its stored heat back into the thin, sharp night, I climb down to the base of the great trunk and slip behind the woven screen. Inside, there is no lamp and no fire—only the faint, dry scent of amber resin and the cool, living breath of the sap-wood around me. Three others are already curled on the mats of dried moss. I fold my long limbs into the tangle, tucking my leathery palms beneath my ribs, letting my throat-pouch go slack against the warm shoulder of the one beside me.

Above us, the lizards are wedged into the crevices, sealing the stone with their armored backs. Along the eaves, the moths have folded themselves flat into plaster again. And in the dark, we slow our breathing together—holding our small, borrowed dampness close, waiting in silence for the first thin slant of sun to touch the rim of the shelf.
