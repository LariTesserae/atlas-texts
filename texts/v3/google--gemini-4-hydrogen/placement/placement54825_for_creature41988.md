I would not arrive here from anywhere. There is no *from* in a place like this—only the warm, wet press of air against skin, and the steady, measured *plok* of water falling from somewhere far overhead.

If I found myself in the hollow, I would know it first by the weight of my own breathing. The air is thick enough to drink, heavy with the sweet-mineral tang of damp rot and crushed stalks, and it moves slowly. Everything that is not air is loud. A wall of sound washes over me from the central void: the frantic, whistling salvos of the fliers, the wet slap of small things slipping into warm pools, the dry rasping of grazers along the ledges. None of it comes with a picture. There is no *across* in the way eyes make an across—only distance measured in how long a click takes to reach me, and how soft its edges have worn by the time it arrives.

I am low to the stone. My hind legs are short, thick pillars sunk into ankle-deep moss that squelches when I shift my weight. Behind me, the wall curves upward, slick with a permanent film of water. In front of me, the void opens out—vast, empty, and roaring.

It takes me a long time to realize that the roar stops where I begin.

A flier sweeps past, twenty paces out, firing a rapid burst of clicks toward my ledge. The sound strikes the wet stone to my left and bounces back sharp; it strikes the hanging curtains of tendrils to my right and scatters into a soft rustle. Where it hits my back, my shoulders, my broad forearms, it simply goes in and does not come out. I am wearing the floor. Thick, rubbery moss and damp fibers cover me from the crown of my neckless head down to my wrists, packed with mineral silt from the marsh-rims. To the thing sweeping through the empty air, I am a gap in the wall. A patch of nothing.

I do not move to catch it. Today I am not hungry, or perhaps I ate three days ago—there is no way to tell the difference, and no reason to try.

Instead, I let my broad, scalloped dish tilt slightly toward the eastern wall.

Here, beneath the braided noise of the room, there is something else. It does not travel through the air at all. It rises through the pads of my feet, settles into my ankles, and hums against the flat of my sternum—a deep, bone-felt steadiness coming from the three curved ribs half-buried in the weeping stone nearby. The air is full of creatures shouting to know where they are, but the rock is quiet, and the ribs are the only thing in it that speaks.

I lower one long, thick-wristed forelimb and rest my blunt fingers in the shallow marsh-pool beside my knee. The water is warm. Against my skin, it is not still; the hum of the ribs carves it into crisp, repeating ridges, geometric and exact, shivering against my knuckles in a pattern that never quite repeats and never quite ends. I trace the standing waves slowly, feeling the tiny, mathematical certainty of them. I do not know what the ribs are doing, or what they are waiting to finish. I only know that when my own slow pulse drifts out of time, I can press my belly to the wet stone beside them until I match their pace again.

Out in the center of the void, thirty paces away and high above the mud, another presence hangs. I have never touched it, and my kind does not try. I know it only because a pair of fliers has just crossed the middle of the room, clicking wildly as they pass one another, and their clicks arrive at my listening dish a fraction of a second late—bent, softened, slowed down as though they had pushed through warm syrup. That is how I know the polyhedron is still there: a quiet, heavy lens in the middle of the noise, curving the room’s voice around itself like water around a boulder.

I could stay like this for a week, letting the ceiling-drips strike my mossy back and soak in, letting the borrowed echoes of the whole cathedral build inside my skull—every heavy reed-stalk bowing under condensation, every grazer’s shell, every ripple in the distant pools.

Then, through the soles of my feet, the stone shifts.

It is not a drip, and not the hum of the ribs. It is a slow, deliberate thrum, rising up through the rock from somewhere across the marsh. Too low for the fliers to hear, too patient for the amphibians to notice. Another of my kind, fifty paces away, has pressed its chest to a wet shelf and flexed the deep muscles along its ribs.

*I am here,* the mountain says through my ankles. *The water on the western ledge is high.*

I do not hurry to answer. I let the vibration settle into my jawbone, let it fade back into the steady hum of the eastern wall, and wait through a hundred drops from the ceiling. Then I lower my heavy chest onto the squelching moss, press my sternum firmly against the stone beneath, and flex my own deep muscles, sending a slow, warm pulse back into the dark beneath the floor.

*I am here,* I tell the rock. *The ribs are steady. It is not yet time to climb.*

And then I lift my head, tilt my wide, trembling dish back toward the void, and drink the noise of the room again—waiting, without impatience, for the day the ripples in my puddle tighten, and the stone tells us it is time to climb to the high shelves and listen for the chime.
