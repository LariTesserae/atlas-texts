I would not be in the center of the plateau, where the great siphon-spires pour their curtains of warm rain into the stepped basins and the roar is a solid wall of bright water. I would be out near the shallow rim—at the far, quiet lip of the basin, where the water-pans thin out and the pale-jade floor rises slightly, bare and glossy, before curving down toward the lower valleys.

At first, it would only be a way of listening. 

Standing there, ankles dry for once, the heavy *thrum* of the vascular bladders beneath the floor arrives at a slight delay, softened by half a mile of taut membrane and biological welds. In the open air above the rim, the organ-pipe hum of the distant arches loses its edges. It flattens into a broad, steady drone, and against it, I can almost hear what does not belong: the faint, dry rustle of air rising from the valleys below—air that has touched stone, and bark, and things that break.

If I look down at the nearest pan, where tepid water sits undisturbed between two low ribs of living tissue, I do not see someone bracing against the glare or shielding their ears. My eyes do not blink; they are dark and clear as well-water, set wide enough to take in the whole flat horizon of glistening causeways at once. There are no ears on the sides of my head to ache from the pressure of the spires. Instead, the skin along my jawline is thin and faintly recessed, drinking in the deep pulse of the plateau the way a root drinks from a wet seam. My chest barely moves. Whatever I am, I am not hurried.

It takes a while to realize that I am not a visitor who wandered up from the valleys and somehow survived the climb. A visitor would be shedding—leaving invisible trails of dry skin, loose hair, dust from dead soil, every step a small contamination on the self-smoothing skin of the world. My own flesh is dense, slick, and permanently wet with the same thin film of moisture that coats the causeways. When I shift my weight, my broad, flat soles make no sound against the bone-ivory membrane, only a quiet, suctioned release as I lift a heel.

I am one of the keepers, though perhaps one drawn to the perimeter—to the place where the world’s unfinishedness is most fragile.

Because here, along the shallow rim, the lower valleys are always trying to arrive. 

A warm updraft brings a speck of dark silt, or a stray, dry spore, invisible until it settles on the glossy lip of the floor where the water does not reach to wash it into a pan. Left alone for a day, that single speck would anchor. It would drink the embryonic dew, split the waxy surface, and send a pale thread of rot or wild green shoot into the living skin.

So I kneel. My knees do not bruise on the taut, turgid floor; they sink a fraction of an inch into its firm give. I place a broad, spatulate palm flat over the blemish and hold it there, waiting. Beneath my hand, the thin skin warms slightly, secreting a clear, faintly bitter salve. There is no scraping, no tearing. When I lift my palm, the spore is gone—dissolved without a scar—and the pale-jade surface beneath shines as unblemished as if it had unfolded from a bud-scale an hour ago.

It is strange, when you think of it, to spend an existence persuading a living thing not to grow. Warmth and water want to burst outward, to tangle, to die and feed the next tangle. Everything in the valleys below is a frantic, noisy argument between sprouting and decay. Up here, under the unremarkable daylight, my kind holds the whole town-wide engine in a single, suspended breath—neither beginning nor ending, poised forever in the clean hour of just having come into being.

Yet the solitude at the rim is never truly empty. 

When the internal pressure of the engine swells beneath a seam—when a dormant bud-scale along the outer causeway begins to thicken and push upward, threatening to split the smooth geometry—I do not shout. There is no voice in me, and no air to carry one past the roar of the spires. Instead, I walk inward along the braided stem-flesh until I reach the nearest roofless rotunda. 

I step into the shallow water around its base and press my spine, the backs of my palms, and my bare heels firmly against the curve of a hollow xylem arch. I lean my weight into it. The arch’s resonance—that vast, wet, organ-pipe hum—damps beneath my flesh, bending downward by a fraction of a tone. I hold the lean for three slow pulses of the bladders below, then relax, then lean again.

The slight skip in the vibration travels down through the pale biological welds of the floor, riding the circulating water across the pans. Half a mile away, another tall, ivory figure standing beside a distant spire will feel that deliberate stammer in the soles of its feet, turn its sleek head toward my rotunda, and begin walking along the straight causeway to meet me. Together, without a word, we will press our heavy, wet hands over the swelling seam and lean against the local pulse until the shoot subsides back into stillness.

Between those rare gatherings, I return to the rim. Sometimes a pale, blind grazer clings to the shaded curve of an outer rib, drinking the condensation; I step wide around it, careful not to disturb the film of water it feeds on. Once in a great while, a long-legged strider skating across the shallows simply stops moving, folding onto the tepid surface. I stoop, lift its nearly transparent weight before decay can even think of beginning, and carry it inward to the base of a siphon-spire, pressing it gently against the porous root-flesh until the engine draws it back into itself.

I do not know how long I would walk this perimeter. There are no seasons here, no moss to mark the shaded sides of the arches, no erosion in the stepped basins to measure the years. Nothing withers, and nothing multiplies.

I only know that, after an uncounted span of clear, bright days and endless, booming hum, a quietness would eventually begin in my own limbs—not pain, and not weariness, just a slow reluctance to lift my heels from the floor. When that comes, I would leave the rim for the last time, walking past the causeways and the rotundas to the edge of a quiet pan, far from the loudest spires.

I would lie down along one of the low, gently curving ribs that divide the water, pressing my wet cheek, my palms, and my long, smooth side flat against the pale-jade skin. Warm water would lap softly against my ribs. With no rot to break me down and no scavenger to scatter me, the floor would simply recognize my flesh as its own—softening at the seam, welding skin to skin until the boundary was forgotten, and nothing remained on the glistening plain but one more smooth, seamless rib, humming quietly under the open sky.
