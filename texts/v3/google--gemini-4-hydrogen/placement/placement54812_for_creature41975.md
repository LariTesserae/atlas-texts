I would not be inside the room. There is already a keeper in there, and a room thirty feet wide has only enough solitude for one.

I would find myself on the other side of the quartz—out in the thin, razor-cold blue that the keeper thinks is an empty cistern.

Up here, above the spilled-wool tops of the thunderheads, the air has almost nothing in it to hold a sound or a scent. It is all glare and wind. I would be something built for that emptiness: a high-altitude glider, narrow-bodied and deep-keeled, with long, scythe-shaped wings meant for riding the fierce updrafts that peel off the ancient spire’s flanks. My back would be the color of soot, packed tight with stiff, oiled quills to drink the unfiltered sun; my underside would be pale ash-gray, so that anything looking up from the cloud-deck below would lose me against the sky.

Most of the spire is dead stone and frozen iron. I know its ribs for miles—the dry, whistling vents choked with rime-ice, the shuttered embrasures where nothing has moved in centuries. But on the western wall, high above the weather, there is one place that is still warm.

You smell it before you see it: a sudden, impossible thread of wet copper and boiled stone, torn sideways by the gale. A faint plume of steam ghosts out from the iron casing, glittering with a fine, continuous snow of gold mica-scale that flashes in the sun and vanishes into the blue.

That is where I land, folding my wings tight against the wind and locking my long, blunt-clawed toes onto the pitted lip of the bronze sluice.

Out here, you do not hear the room’s *thrum-thrum-thrum* with your ears; the air is too thin to carry it, and the wind is too loud. You feel it through the soles of your feet, up the hollow bones of your shins, and into your keel-bone—a slow, heavy, rhythmic trembling in the metal, steady as a pulse. Every forty seconds, the bronze beneath my claws gives a single, sharp shudder, like a struck anvil heard through a mile of stone. A breath later, the narrow stream spilling over my toes surges warmer, steaming violently as it meets the freezing air, and flushes out a fresh scatter of mica, a torn shred of translucent fern-bladder, or a half-cooked siphon-shrimp.

That is what I wait for. I dip my hooked bill into the steaming mouth before the water can flash to ice, snatching the shrimp, drinking a drop of warm, mineral-rich runoff. And between the surges, I work at the rim with my beak, cracking away the crusts of amber-and-verdigris ice that constantly try to seal the drain shut. I do it for the salt and the trapped bits of kelp, not knowing that if I left the ice to grow, the little sunlit world behind the wall would slowly choke on its own water.

When the sun drops into the west, the convex quartz oculus above my head turns into a blazing, distorting eye.

From the outside, the window is a marvel—a thick, bulging lens of warm glass holding back an ocean of mustard-yellow light and swaying emerald curtains. The curvature warps everything inside. The bronze standpipes bend like reeds; the rising columns of bubbles look like silver trees blooming and shattering in slow motion.

I tuck my chin into my breast-feathers, puffed against the knife-edge of the evening cold, and watch the pale shadow uncoil from the catwalk.

Through the thick quartz, it looks immense and boneless, its tea-stained skin catching the golden shafts of daylight. It swims down to the base of the window—close enough that I can see the feathery gill-slits along its ribs pulsing in strict time with the vibration in my feet. A hooked, flat-nailed finger appears in the dark throat of the bronze sluice, raking away a tangle of kelp from the inside. I hop back an inch onto the frosted iron rivets, wings half-cocked, until the finger withdraws.

Then it rises and presses both palms, and the smooth curve of its forehead, against the glass.

Its round eyes are veiled behind milky membranes, squinted against the fierce western glare. It looks straight through me—or perhaps it only sees a dark, wind-tattered knot of soot clinging to the sill of its vast, empty, wall-less cistern. It does not know what feathers are, or wind, or frost. If it ever pushed through the glass into my world, its wet skin would freeze in a minute and its gills would collapse in the thin air. If I ever slipped through the drain into its world, the warm, loud water would drown me in three breaths.

So we stay on our own sides of the quartz—watching each other for the span of three or four valve-strikes, each of us tending our half of the same narrow bronze mouth, keeping the circle clear.

Then the metal shudders beneath my claws. Inside, the keeper pushes off the glass and slides back into the bright gyre. Out here, I open my wings to the freezing wind, catch the updraft off the spire’s flank, and rise into the blinding blue—with a dusting of gold mica frozen to my breast, and the taste of warm copper still on my tongue.
