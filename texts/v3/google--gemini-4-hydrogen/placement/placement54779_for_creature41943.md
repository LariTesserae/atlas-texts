I would be on the high western terrace, where the quartzite breaks into sheer, stepped shelves above the silver scrub, because that is where the light strikes first.

Before dawn, the cold there is absolute. I would be crouched beside a low concrete plinth—not sheltering from wind, since the air never moves enough to need shelter, but anchored to the stone, waiting for the eastern crest to break. In the dark, there is no difference between the chalk-white composite of my shoulders and the pale rock behind me. Inside my collar vents, the damp mesh holds the small, recycled warmth of my breathing, returning each shallow exhale as a cool, mineral weight against my throat. My blood is thick and slow from the night, moving with the reluctance of oil in an unheated sump.

When the sun clears the opposite ridge, it brings no warmth, only the sudden, surgical strike of white glare. Through my dark quartz visor, the basin below does not brighten so much as sharpen—every fracture line, every bruised-plum leaf snapping into razor-edged focus. All at once, the thin rime on the steel pylon beside me turns to a fine, clinging sweat.

That is when I stand. Not quickly—there is no use for quickness here, and a sudden motion would only tear the stillness—but with the steady, metered rise of a hydraulic piston.

I am one of the basin’s tenders, though up here on the rim, where the vegetation thins out against the raw talus, it feels less like tending and more like being a hinge between the stone and the current. I don't know where my own casing ends and the plinth’s housing begins; my matte ceramic plates share the same dust, the same static-charged sheen, the same refusal of the glare. I lay one gloved palm flat against the heavy copper busbar bridging the porcelain insulators. For several minutes I simply stand there, letting the first trickle of low-amperage charge from the waking condenser plates bleed into my core. There is no hum, no jolt—only a gradual loosening in my joints, a quiet thinning of the cold in my limbs, while the air around my wrists stiffens with static and lifts a few pale motes of pollen into a motionless ring.

Down on my shin, a mirrored beetle, roused by the dampness, begins its slow climb toward the pleated insulation at my knee, gleaning salt from the crease. I let it climb.

My work is not down in the deep bunkers, nor among the wide substations on the valley floor. My beat is the upper line: the single black armored conduit that surfaces from beneath the broad-crowned canopy and snakes three hundred yards up the fractured basalt to this last relay plinth.

I step off the concrete and place the grooved sole of my boot squarely onto the curved spine of the cable. Beneath me, the moss and pale lichen lie packed like a cold, unbroken sponge. I never touch them. To put a heel into that mat would be to break the seal that keeps the morning’s brief water from vanishing into the thin air. Instead, I walk the conduit the way a tightrope walker might, if the wire were thick as a thigh and the crossing took an hour. My arms stay close to my sides; my steps have no swing.

Halfway down the slope, where the waxy shrubs press edge to edge around the cable, I stop. A shard of pale quartzite, loosened by the night’s freeze, has slid from the upper cliff and lodged across the crown of a broad-leafed plant, pinning two of its fluted stems together so the morning’s condensation pools unevenly on the lip instead of funneling down to the roots.

I kneel on the conduit, bracing one hand on a sharp edge of basalt, and take hold of the stone. It takes the better part of ten minutes to lift it clear without brushing the neighboring leaves or tearing the waxy cuticle beneath. I set the shard aside on a bare, dry lip of rock where it belongs, then use a square of chamois—still damp from the dawn rime on my own shoulder plates—to wipe a faint film of mineral dust from the nearest parabolic mirror’s lower rim.

Around me, the valley holds its breath. Far out over the center of the basin, a grey glider drifts from one ridge to another without a single beat of its wings, passing through the aching pewter sky like a sliver of slate.

At midday, when the glare flattens every shadow to a thin black line beneath my boots, I climb back to the upper plinth. Across the valley, half a mile down the eastern slope—still pale and sharp as glass in the unfiltered air—another figure stands beside a transformer housing. I cannot see their face, only the chalk-white dome of their head and the dark horizontal slit of their visor turned my way.

I unclip the small, hinged mirror from my wrist and angle it into the white sky.

I do not send words. There are no words thin enough for this air. Instead, I hold a steady, razor-edged flash across the silver scrub for three slow counts, then drop the mirror, letting it tell the valley floor what I have measured: *The upper line is intact. The morning’s water held. The flywheel turns.*

For half a minute, nothing answers. Then, from the far ridge, a single pinpoint of white light leaps across the distance—crisp, soundless, and gone.

I return the mirror to its clasp and rest my hand once more against the copper bar, feeling the faint, patient pulse of the buried line beneath my feet. The afternoon will be long, and nothing in it will hurry. I will watch the louvered apertures above me widen by a millimeter over the course of an hour; I will wait for the sun to sink behind the jagged crest behind me, driving the heat out of the rock all at once; and when the frost begins to gather again on my ceramic shoulders, I will walk the black cable down into the dark, flush-sealed quiet of the bunker, to wait beside the slowing flywheels for the light to return.
