I would not stay out in the open, on the mirror-flat terraces where the light is bruised gray and every sound bounces off the black glass twice. A place that rings like a struck bell has no room for anything soft or unguarded.

If I found myself inside that four-minute world, my first instinct would be to get low, get out of the echo, and find the one thing in the basin that keeps steady time. I would end up beneath the forty-degree flank of the pale, matte-white pillar—wedged into the narrow, dryish pocket where the stone never puddles, because the rainwater curves upward in silver ribbons and drinks itself into the seam.

At first, I might only be there for the shelter. The ninety-degree ledges are too sharp for bare skin, and the racket of the frog-beetles is too relentless for open ears. So I would learn what the basin demands: you cannot meet it with soft heels, and you cannot listen to it through the air. You pinch the slits behind your jaw shut against the clacking cliffs, press your chin down until the pale barbels touch the obsidian, and let the solid rock tell you what is happening.

Through the stone, the world is completely different. The frantic, high-pitched scramble of the midges and the beetles disappears. What remains is slow and heavy: the faint, distant thrash of a blind swimmer turning a pool to foam; the long, low shudder of the ceramic disk at the center of the floor, pushing out another millimeter of hot, iridescent melt; and right above my shoulder, vibrating straight through my ribs, the patient *tock-tock-tock* of the pillar’s heart.

The longer I stayed in that pocket, the less reason I would have to leave it—except for the small, necessary errands that keep the pocket alive.

I think I would be the third one. The smaller, slicker one who rarely leaves the shadow of the white pillar.

While the other two range out across the terraces to hunt—crouching motionless by the steaming pans, flicking a single long finger in the water to fool the swimmers, licking the clear, sugary mucus from the copper-wire stalks around the hovering bronze lattice—my attention would stay closer to the seams. There is a quiet, anxious satisfaction in tending a thing you do not understand, simply because you know what happens if it stops.

Between the downpours, when the basin is at its loudest and my ear-slits are sealed tight, I would crouch beside the pillar’s base with a snapped sliver of cooled slag between my blunt, mineral-hardened nails. The pale, rubbery lichens are always trying to creep down the white flank, drawn by the dampness, inching toward the fist-sized seam where the water goes in. I would pare them back, gently, scraping the slick green-white film away from the lip of the intake without scratching the matte surface, watching the silver ribbons run cleanly again.

When the ceramic disk at the center of the basin gives its periodic shudder, I would lope out with the others—three-limbed, heavy-ribbed, my bruised-plum belly close to the glass—to press my heels and palms into the taffy-warm edge of the fresh slag. It stings for a moment, and then it cools into a thin, glittering crust over my dead horn. That is how you walk on razor-sharp stone without bleeding: you borrow the basin’s own new skin, wear it down into glittering flakes against the ledges, and come back for more when it wears thin.

And then there is the other clock.

When the vapor ceiling overhead finally sags dark and heavy as a wet wool coat and lets go, the whole world changes in a breath. The ten-minute roar of the warm rain drowns out the frog-beetles, muffles the foghorn groans of the kite-things, and turns the ringing glass bowl into a private, drumming room.

That is when I would open my ears.

In the blinding, warm hiss of the downpour, I would step out from under the white pillar and stretch my long arms, calling to the other two in low, wet throat-hums and sharp tongue-clicks that carry cleanly through the falling water without echoing off the cliffs. There is no hunting in the rain—the pools are too churned, the cliffs too slick—only trading and mending. I would bring them the sharp slivers of slag I’d snapped off the terraces, or a handful of swollen beetles caught near the overhang; they would pass me a freshly shed, papery strip of canopy-drifter skin, still trailing its sticky threads, and together we would wade out to the hovering bronze lattice to pull drowned insects from its slowly turning rods and hang our crude, gummy nets beneath it.

Then, just as abruptly as a shut tap, the rain stops.

Before the first frog-beetle inflates its throat-sac and cracks the silence open again, there is always a brief, breathless hush. In that quiet, I would tilt my broad, flat head back and look up through my milk-glass eyes at the dim ceiling of mist. Outside the invisible meniscus, the mountain wind is howling, and now and then you can see the faint, ghostly streaks of sleet striking the dome from above and sliding off into the cold blue distance.

You watch it glide past, feel the *tock-tock-tock* steady in your chest, pinch your ears shut against the coming racket, and slip back into the dry shadow of the pillar.
