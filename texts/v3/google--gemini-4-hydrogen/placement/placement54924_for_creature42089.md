I would not be in the sky. Whatever those silent, kite-shaped shadows are that skim the dragging belly of the clouds, they have no use for weight, and in a world this heavy, weight is the only thing that tells you where you are.

If I am anywhere here, I am down where the noise lives—down in the pewter dimness where the air tastes of boiled silt and old brine, and the floor never stops moving under you.

At first, I think I might be one of the blind, boulder-sized things clamped to a bedrock seam. There is a strange comfort in that: sealing your rim against the chaos for weeks at a time, letting the double-bores rear up and collapse over your shell like collapsing buildings, asking nothing of the world except that it leave you in your own dark. To be a stone that breathes, stubborn and unbothered, while the continent-wide puddle sloshes back and forth above you.

But no. That stillness is too deaf. If you seal your rim tight enough to survive the bores, you miss the fraying. You miss the moments when the world forgets itself and turns strange.

So I find myself uncoiling from the seam instead—heavy-limbed, slick-skinned, shedding curtains of warm water as the sheet-tide thins to my ankles. My belly is pale and horn-calloused from the millstones; my shins are crosshatched with old white scars where a rolling egg caught me wrong in a cross-current. I have no external ears to be torn by the wind, only the dense, spongy bone of my jaw and collarbone, humming with the tectonic chatter of a billion stones grinding together beneath my splayed pads.

I would be one of the drifters—and more specifically, one of the ones who lingers a little too long near the knots.

Most of us keep moving, stepping from crown to crown with that easy, rolling hip-swing, or dropping flat to ride the current with our palms out as rudders until we feel the dead, motionless thrum of bedrock underneath. You have to keep moving when the bores come from opposite horizons at once. If you hesitate, you end up wedged between two millstones the size of your own ribcage.

But I am drawn to the high humps of shale where the water thins, and to the small, deliberate anchors others have left behind.

I find myself kneeling in the rare, fleeting hollows between tides, waiting for the surface to still for a single breath. My inner lids are drawn against the flying grit, and when I finally part them to look down into the puddle, I do not look for myself. The water here would never show me my own face anyway. Instead, I watch the ghostly, tilted double of a wall of brine that is still a minute away—measuring its height, the angle of its crest, the way the light of the tarnished sky bends wrong across its ridge. Ten seconds before the violet flicker tears the cloud-deck above me, the piled-up crack of its thunder hits my sternum from three directions at once. I brace my shoulders, snap my nostrils shut, and wait for the lightning to catch up to its own noise.

That is where I live: leaning slightly forward in time, in the gap between a thing’s echo and its arrival.

When I find a bedrock spur with black kelp-grass braided around it, I do not just hook my forearm through the loop to sleep. I lay both palms flat against the knot and listen with my bones.

A braid left weeks ago by someone I will never meet has its own pitch in the rushing brine. If the current is running fast and clean, the wet cord vibrates against my fingertips like a taut gut string—a steady, low buzz that climbs into my wrists. If a stutter is building nearby, where a wave is about to catch on itself and loop into a rigid, roaring glass wall, the knot begins to *chatter* in uneven threes, skipping beats against the rock.

Sometimes I undo an old, frayed knot and re-braid it tighter, weaving in fresh, rubbery strands from the mat nearby. Not because the spur needs it, and not because I expect to pass this way again. You do it because someone else will come crawling out of the soup a month from now, half-blind with silt, and lay their palm on that rock. You leave them a clean pitch. You leave them a true reading.

And when the world frays—when the mud and brine suddenly remember a shape they held a moment ago and freeze into a hollow dome humming like struck bronze—I am always the first one inside.

Ten heartbeats. That is all you ever get. Ten heartbeats of solid quiet while the boiling tide slams around the outside of the wall, unable to touch you. Inside, the roar of the ball-mill drops to a deep, enclosing drone that vibrates in your teeth. It is the only room this world ever builds.

If I am alone, I just press my back against the vibrating mud-water, close my inner lids, and let my breath out slowly into the dark. For ten heartbeats, nothing is rolling. Nothing is grinding. You can feel your own pulse without having to sort it out from the chatter of a billion stones.

If another drifter slips through the wall beside me before it seals, we do not waste time. There are no words here—a shout would be swallowed before it left the throat—so we press our foreheads together, or lay our ribs side by side, and tap out our names in three-beat patterns against each other’s sternums. We trade the news that matters: *three bores back, a blind ray turned up a bed of pale worms near the split ridge; two ridges west, the shell-creatures are clamped tight, which means a double-bore is building.* On the eighth heartbeat, we check each other’s hides for deep stone-bruises. On the ninth, we slide back out into the waist-deep current, just as the dome shudders and collapses back into boiling foam.

What I love most—if a creature built for the ball-mill can be said to love anything—are the ghost-cairns.

When I have the time between tides, on a high shelf of shale, I gather the smooth stone-eggs that have rolled into the crevices. I stack them low and squat, wedging the gaps with dried bladder-weed and packing them tight with the heaviest silt I can scoop from the cracks. A good cairn has to be dense enough to break the knee of a sheet-tide, creating a tiny, quiet lee behind it where a tired swimmer can catch a breath.

I know it will not last. Nothing here lasts. Eventually a double-bore will rear up high enough to slam down on that hump of shale with the weight of a falling cliff, and scatter my stones back into the endless, rolling floor.

But if I build it tight enough—if it holds its ground through enough tides—the water around it learns the shape.

Weeks later, passing back over that same naked rib of bedrock where the stones have long since been swept away, I will sometimes feel the brine stiffen beside my shoulder. For ten heartbeats, out of nothing at all, the silt and water will freeze into a hollow, bronze-humming dome in the exact shape of the cairn that used to stand there.

I will slip inside it, crouch in the brief, impossible stillness, and press my splayed palm into the softest mud at its base—leaving a shallow ring beside the memory of stones. Not to claim the place, and not to be remembered by name. Just to leave a shape behind for the water to hold onto, for ten heartbeats, after it takes me again.
