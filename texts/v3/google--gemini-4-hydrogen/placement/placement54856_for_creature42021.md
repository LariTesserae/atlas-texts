At first, it is impossible to tell where the noise ends and the heat begins.

If I try to imagine myself upright here, my head would press straight into the bruised, coppery-grey lid of the sky, into the motionless, oven-thick vapor where the midges hang like smoke and the air carries nothing farther than an arm’s length. Up there, above the waist-high mat of waxy fronds, there is no room to think. The stridulating wings and booming throat-sacs press against the skin from every side, a wall of sound so dense it feels solid. I could not last an hour as something that flies, or clings to the upper leaves, or waits in the open glare for a storm that will never come.

So I drop down. Beneath the overlapping shelves of dark-emerald and bruised-violet, the light thins into a murky, tea-colored dusk. Here, the air is still stifling, still saturated until everything weeps, but the canopy breaks the worst of the brightness. And here, pressed flat against the razor-edged basalt, I find what I am.

I am low to the glass, balanced on the thick, glassy-black horn of my knuckles and knees.

It takes a moment to settle into the body—to feel the cool, bitter oil beading along my ribs, slicking my flanks so the gelatinous fungi and wet-winged moths slide off without catching. Beneath my chin, the loose, blood-dark folds of membrane hang heavy, waiting. When I pause under the broad underside of a frond, I tilt my head back and let the slow, steady *plip-plip-plip* of condensation strike the skin, each drop drawing a tiny, sharp breath of heat out of my blood before it rolls away. My eyes—wide, lidless, milk-pale—flick sideways behind their clear shields to wipe the mist away. In this dimness, sight is only good for edges and movement: the pale, grasping tangle of aerial roots, the sudden sheen of a coiled snake, the dull glint of a warm seep.

My ears are sealed slits. The frogs and beetles can have the air.

Instead, I lower my chin until the bare, callused bone at the tip of my jaw rests flush against a plate of vitrified stone.

Instantly, the basin opens.

The maddening, suffocating roar of the canopy vanishes, replaced by the deep, ringing dark of the basalt. Because there is no soil here, no frost to crack the crust into gravel, the stone beneath me is a single, unbroken bell stretching for miles. Through my jaw, I can feel the whole teeming, crowded world sorted into clean, sharp tremors: the frantic *tick-tick-tick* of armored scavengers swarming a dead amphibian two ridges over; the soft, wet *slump* of a heavy lizard dropping from a leaf into the shallows; the faint, rhythmic pulse of another knot of my kind, half a mile across the steaming flats, tapping a bone against the glass.

This is where I find myself: not in the thickest, loudest heart of the basin, where the root-mats choke the water and the knots crowd close around the richest seeps, but slightly toward the rim, on a broad, blistered shelf of stone where a hairline fracture is beginning to crust shut.

The seep beside my shelf is cooling—only by a fraction, not enough for anything above the fronds to notice, but I can feel it in the pitch of the stone. The mineral water here is growing sluggish and sour. The other two in my knot have already begun to drift away, sliding on their slick bellies beneath the mats toward fresher heat, their braided root-cords rattling faintly with hollowed beetle-carapaces and flakes of glass. They will wait for me a ridge or two over, wedged into the aerial roots above a warmer pool, half-asleep with one hand pressed to the floor.

I linger a little longer. It is my habit to be the last to leave a clearing.

The plants are always trying to bury the glass. Even in the short span since our last shed, pale, hair-fine roots have crept out from the edges of the shelf, feeling into the microscopic pits of the basalt, threatening to muffle it. With the horny edge of my palm—and then with a thin shard of slag wrapped in a stripped sheet of my own peeled sole-callus—I scrape the tendrils back. I clear a broad, dark island of naked stone, wiping away the slick fungal film until the basalt is as sterile and sharp as the day it cooled.

Every clearing is a listening-post, and every clearing is a larder. Soon enough, I will pry a flat-headed amphibian from the souring seep and drag it onto the glass, waiting at the rim while the clicking tide of many-legged scavengers pours from the fissures so I can scoop them up by the handful.

But for now, I only want the stone bare so I can hear.

I wedge my knees into a shallow depression, let one half of my brain drift into the dull, heat-soaked torpor that passes for sleep in a world without night, and keep my jaw and one palm flat against the shelf. Across my chest, the braided root-cord holds a single long femur—smooth at the grip from years of bitter oil, its heavy knob scarred from striking the floor. It belonged to someone from my knot, long enough ago that the scavengers who stripped it have been eaten and replaced a hundred times over.

I tap the bone once against the basalt. *Tok.*

The note runs outward through the unbroken crust, clean and bright beneath the endless, muggy roar of the air. Far out in the deepest seeps, where the buoyant root-mats sag into floating islands, one of the vast, moss-encrusted giants shifts its bulk and lets out a long, sulfurous groan that rattles faintly against my molars. Closer by, from the direction my knot took, comes the slow, answering *tap… tap-tap…* of a knuckle on stone.

They are telling me the water is warm over there. They are telling me to come down off the dying shelf.

I lift my chin, letting the deafening clamor of the basin rush back in around my head, and tuck the bone-hammer tight against my ribs. On knuckles and knees, weeping oil into the tea-colored dusk, I slip beneath the waxy fronds to follow the ringing in the glass.
